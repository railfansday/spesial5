David Baldacci, John Grisham, and Scott Turow Know How a Story Can End. They Say Losing the Rule of Law to Trump Would Be a “Death Sentence for America”

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

In July, for the first time in seven years, sitting justices of the US Supreme Court testified at the Capitol. Chief Justice John Roberts has been shy about letting members of the Court answer legislators’ questions, viewing it as a violation of the separation of powers. However, in this case, there was apparently no choice, since the Supreme Court requested millions of dollars to enhance its very own security in the face of sharply growing threats to lower court judges—threats that have grown exponentially over the last decade.

We are all lawyers as well as novelists. We have appeared in court on various matters before both judges and juries, and thus believe that respect for the rule of law is absolutely essential to have any hope for a civilized society. Recent events, like the reasons for justices Elena Kagan and Amy Coney Barrett to testify before Congress, have prompted us to write this, because we want all Americans to understand that we are drawing perilously close to the rule of law becoming optional. For any free and open democracy, not only is that unacceptable, but it is a death sentence for America.

Respect for the rule of law must come from everyone, and it most certainly should come from the highest officeholder in the land. Unfortunately, with the current administration, we have witnessed the exact opposite result.

Donald Trump with Supreme Court Chief Justice John Roberts, and Supreme Court Justices Elena Kagan, Brett Kavanaugh, and Amy Coney Barrett in February 2026.

Consider the fundamental text: The Declaration of Independence, which celebrated its 250th anniversary this year—and established what the framers viewed as the bedrock principles for a just government, starting with the declaration’s most hallowed statement, that all persons are created equal. But the document went on to list many grievances against the tyrannical rule of King George III, including the fact that the king had made “Judges dependent on his Will alone,” because it was solely the king’s grace that decided their continued employment or compensation. In response, our Constitution tried to insulate the federal judiciary from outside influence by providing for lifetime appointments.

But fast-forward to the present day, and President Donald Trump has repeatedly demeaned and insulted judges, variously claiming that a judge’s heritage, religion, or political affiliation can compromise their legal judgement. In February, he flagrantly labeled the members of the Supreme Court who had voted to overrule his tariffs as “fools and lapdogs,” claiming they were “swayed by foreign interests,” and attacking Neil Gorsuch and Coney Barrett, two of the justices he himself had appointed, calling them “an embarrassment to their families.”

Even more egregiously, the Trump administration has gone beyond schoolyard insults to taking actions that actually circumvent the rule of law.

For example, Trump’s use of the Justice Department to bring criminal prosecutions against those he thinks wronged him personally is one of his most brazen acts of defiance of established legal norms. The indictments of former FBI director James Comey, whom Trump fired, and Letitia James, the New York attorney general who brought a successful civil fraud case against Trump, his children, and Trump family businesses, were both thrown out of court because Trump’s selection of one of his former personal lawyers as the prosecutor was found illegal. The newest prosecution of Comey, because of a supposedly threatening arrangement of seashells on a beach, is equally unlikely to stand up under prevailing law.

The Ábrego Garcia matter is another instance of abuse, because it dramatizes the disobedience by the Trump administration with respect to immigration court orders. Kilmar Armando Ábrego Garcia was deported to the Salvadorean mega-prison despite being legally present in the US. The government first refused to return him, claiming, without adequate proof, that he was a member of the criminal organization MS-13. However, once the pressure became too much, he was returned to the US. Only then did the Justice Department charge him with human smuggling, claiming he had transported people within the US who were in the country unlawfully. His case was dismissed by the trial judge on the grounds of vindictive and selective prosecution and remains on appeal.

We are, without a doubt, in the midst of a veritable plague of looming dangers aimed squarely at the judiciary. Not only did an armed man attempt to assassinate Justice Brett Kavanaugh at his home in 2022 following a leak of the Dobbs decision, hostile threats directed at judges have more than tripled over the past decade, according to Chief Justice Roberts’s 2024 year-end report. Citing statistics from the US Marshals Service, the report said that in the past five years alone, more than 1,000 serious threats were investigated, and dozens of people were charged with crimes. In 2020, the US Marshals said it responded to over 4,000 threats or inappropriate communications against federal judges. In that same year, a disgruntled lawyer murdered US District Judge Esther Salas’s son and wounded her husband after gaining access to their home by posing as a delivery person. According to NBC News, in 2025, there were 103 unordered pizza deliveries to judges who had ruled against the Trump administration or spoke out about threats against the judiciary, and 20 were sent in Salas’s name.

As novelists, for decades, we have all explored themes of corruption, influence peddling, and pressure and threats against public servants in our fiction.

In Scott’s latest novel, Presumed Guilty, about a murder trial in a rural area in the upper Midwest, the longtime prosecutor, Hiram Jackdorp, and the trial judge, Wendy Carrington, have a contentious relationship. Jackdorp forced her out of his office when she was a young prosecutor, and later unsuccessfully attempted to prevent her election to the bench. But there is no moment during the trial when the novel’s narrator, the defense lawyer Rusty Sabich, sees Judge Carrington’s rulings as based on anything but a fair application of legal principles. She is a good judge whose fidelity to the rule of law prevents her from using her position for personal revenge.

In John’s 2008 novel The Appeal, an ambitious young attorney is unwittingly recruited by some nefarious special interests to seek election to the state supreme court. Their big money pours in and buys enough votes. He wins, unseats a legendary jurist, and takes office. But when the pay-off comes due, he realizes that his judicial independence is far more important than playing politics.

In David’s 1996 novel Absolute Power, an American president presumes himself to be above the rule of law and seeks to force his underlings to commit criminal acts that will benefit him. However, the institutions created to check and prevent just such abuses hold fast against these brazen king-like attempts to be immune from baldly unlawful actions. At the end of the novel the truth is revealed, and the criminal justice system holds the president appropriately accountable for his misdeeds.

The “rule of law” is not simply a slogan the three of us learned in law school, or a plot device we use in our fiction. It is what keeps our life in society from turning into the war of all against all, where laws that allow for a civilized way of life matter little and violence and chaos reign. It’s also what keeps our economy vibrant and growing.

Without the fair enforcement of the laws being sacrosanct, economic decline inevitably follows as investors decide against investing in American businesses, building plants or factories here, or purchasing American corporate or government bonds and other investment vehicles. If there is no certainty that commercial contracts or international trade agreements will be honored, this will also vastly diminish the potential of any economy. And if the environment is one where an unbiased court is not the final arbiter of legal disputes, our allies may well think twice before committing resources and lasting cooperation since the environment they sought to work and invest within could change without regard to legality. Economic decline invariably leads to widespread job losses, a lessening standard of living, a loss of confidence in institutions by the citizens, our allies stampeding away from us, and a dilution of every individual right outlined in the Constitution.

And it’s not just us who are worried. There are many other lawyers and judges who have expressed concern about the trajectory of this country to ignore court rulings if they collide with political expediency or partisan goals. Nonpartisan organizations like Keep Our Republic’s Article III Coalition initiative and the Democracy Rising Collaborative have brought together retired judges and advocates to sound the alarm over a country in peril.

What we are all advocating for is that the courts be given the respect they are due under the founding documents of this country. Article I of the United States Constitution established the legislative branch, the United States Congress. Article II established the executive branch, headed up by the presidency. Article III created the US Supreme Court, and granted the power to Congress to establish lower federal courts.

In the 1803 ruling of Marbury v. Madison, the Supreme Court asserted that the courts had the power of judicial review, and with that power came the ability to declare laws passed by the Congress as being unconstitutional. This one ruling lifted the judicial branch to full equality with the other branches, because their decisions would constitute the final ruling on whether or not something was legal or constitutional and in agreement with our rights protected under the Constitution.

In order to be fair and objective arbiters of the law, federal courts must be free from influence, bias, and manipulation from outside sources. There can be no middle ground here. The courts are either free to do their work or they are not.

Each time a court order is ignored or defied, a sliver of independence is torn from the judicial robe. If the executive branch’s answer to a ruling it does not like is simply to not enforce it, or to encourage disobedience through open disrespect, then the independence of the judiciary evaporates. Not only does the legal system itself suffer from this noncompliance, but every citizen does as well, because if one branch of government can defy and ignore court decisions, that means they can potentially defy and ignore the individual rights of any American citizen.

The rule of law must be respected, and the valid decisions of courts must be upheld by the government, even, and perhaps most importantly, when that ruling goes against the government. Only in that way can a free and independent judiciary continue to deliver the ideals outlined in the founding documents of what is the most durable democracy ever conceived and created. Without that, America can no longer claim to be the shining beacon on the hill, inspiring democracy the world over. We, in fact, will become the opposite of what this country has always stood for. Even with our combined imaginations, we can envision no greater tragedy.
