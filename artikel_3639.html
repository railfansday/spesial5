Is This the Last Dance for Spain’s Prime Minister, Pedro Sánchez?

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

On September 23, Maricarmen Abascal, a partially disabled 87-year-old woman, was evicted from the apartment she rented in Madrid, where she had lived her entire life. Suffering from exhaustion caused by the long struggle to keep her home and then her subsequent eviction, she was admitted to a hospital. That same day, Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez addressed the United Nations General Assembly in New York, an international forum where he has often promoted his foreign policy agenda, beginning with his first visit in 2018, when the politician who had been given the nickname Mr. Handsome met many of his counterparts around the globe. While Abascal would never return to her home (she died on October 7), and her eviction sparked widespread public outrage, Sánchez spoke of reforming international organizations, criticized the United States and Israel, and called for reining in the “tech oligarchs” at the helm of social media platforms and artificial intelligence companies: “The future of our sons and daughters must take precedence over the financial interests of these techno-oligarchs.”

No one in Spain paid attention to the speech: Abascal’s eviction sparked social unrest in a country that boasts of being the fastest-growing in Europe in terms of GDP, a fact on which the Banco de España and the International Monetary Fund agree. This has positioned the country as one of the leading economies in the eurozone, with unemployment having fallen to historic lows, according to official figures. In macroeconomic terms, the country has consistently appealed to international investors during Sánchez’s eight years in office, with foreign investment having grown 34% since 2018, according to calculations by the Ministry of Economy. It’s possible that the Spanish economy is experiencing its best period of growth since the country’s return to democracy in the mid-1970s. However, inflation, housing affordability, and low wages—the minimum wage has risen from 735 euros to 1,221 euros, but has failed to boost salaries broadly—are taking a toll on this economic boom.

And, among voters, there’s a sense of mismanagement during this legislative term. There was the migration crisis in Ceuta, a Spanish enclave on the North African coast that saw the sudden arrival of more than 72,000 migrants in late July. (Ceuta has just over 83,000 residents.) The crisis was not enough to get Sánchez to cut short his vacation. It revealed clashes between the ministers of defense and the interior, and it has taken nearly three months for the situation to be brought fully under control. Political scientist and university professor Lluís Orriols noted in El Periódico that the government had come out “badly” in its handling of crises such as the one in Ceuta and the eviction of Abascal—problems compounded by “legislative paralysis” (the government has managed to pass fewer than 40 laws in this legislative session, down from more than 100 in the previous one) and “corruption.” He asserted that only the strength of the economy had prevented “a government in shambles.”

After elections in 2023, Sánchez managed to secure another term, but without a clear left-wing majority, he had to rely on the support of several right or center-right nationalist parties without a legislative pact, which led to numerous parliamentary defeats. The coalition has failed to provide Spain with an effective government. The international landscape has not been particularly favorable either. Sánchez has gradually lost his key allies: That group of young, charismatic left-wing leaders who seemed to set the global agenda and had a special rapport with the Spanish leader have long since left power—the photos showing the admiration for Sánchez from Sanna Marin of Finland, Justin Trudeau of Canada, and Jacinda Ardern of New Zealand are now a thing of the past. His rapport with other European leaders such as Emmanuel Macron, a relationship marked by effusive camaraderie since way back in 2018, is of little use now that France is in turmoil and advocates for a return to austerity are gaining power in Europe. Even his relationship with Ursula von der Leyen, president of the European Commission and one of Sánchez’s main supporters in the European Union, despite their ideological differences, is now of little relevance.

To top it all off, the prime minister came under fire even at Harvard, where he went shortly after his speech at the General Assembly to discuss AI, only to face sharp questions about Spanish politics and corruption. Even his safe role as a statesman was being eroded by political reality. Upon returning from his trip, an attempt to pass legislation that would prevent future situations like Abascal’s (in 2025 alone, there were more than 25,000 evictions, according to data from the General Council of the Judiciary) ran into obstacles in Spain’s fragmented parliament, which rejected every measure, and the legislative session came to a close. Meanwhile, the streets of Madrid and other cities began to fill with tents as people camped out in protest in a country where housing prices are rising by more than 10% annually, well above inflation and more so than wages. Whether it’s a matter of buying (nearly three out of every four Spaniards live in an owner-occupied home) or renting, both the Ministry of Housing and the real estate portal Idealista (which typically lists nearly half a million homes) reported price increases ranging from 30%–40% nationwide, going up to 70% in high-demand areas, during the eight-year period of progressive government, exceeding even the real estate bubble that triggered a 2008 crisis coinciding with the global financial crisis. The country is only just beginning to recover from its impact.

The half-hearted measures approved in previous years to tackle the problem ran into resistance from Spain’s regional governments and city councils controlled by the opposition—the center-right Partido Popular (PP), which governs 13 of Spain’s 19 regions. “Exactly,” the mayor of Madrid, José Luis Martínez-Almeida, said on X in 2021, when opponents accused him of doing “nothing” to tackle the rental crisis in the capital, where the average rent already exceeds the minimum wage.

On October 5, following a tumultuous legislative session in a divided Congress (the more powerful of the two houses in Spain’s parliament), Sánchez announced a snap election. Two days later, Maricarmen Abascal died in the hospital.

If Sánchez loses this election—and even the most optimistic nongovernmental polls, just over a month before the election, give him only between a 5% and 10% chance of winning another term—one of the world’s last bastions of social democracy will disappear along with him. Perhaps the most significant one, both because of the influence Spain has gained within the EU in recent decades and because of the personal leadership Sánchez has exercised in global geopolitics, with the prime minister having spoken out on issues such as climate change; digital governance of AI; the war in Gaza; and, in recent days, European support for Ukraine and migration management. This last issue has even pitted him against another of Europe’s last remaining social democrats (who is also in a precarious coalition), Denmark’s Mette Frederiksen. “Sánchez is the last social democrat who defends a migration policy that is compatible with human rights,” explains political scientist Pablo Simón, a university professor and specialist in electoral systems and political parties, “and he is committed to feminism and environmentalism. Once he leaves office, Spain will be more in line with the majority of Europe and its governments.”

Policies that Sánchez has implemented domestically include granting ministerial status to the 2030 Agenda, elevating the energy transition and the fight against climate change to the level of vice presidency during his terms, and creating the first agencies in any EU country dedicated to AI governance. He has also boasted about these achievements in flawless English—still a novelty among Spanish prime ministers, who have not been particularly fond of languages—at numerous international events. All of this has taken place at a time when the established world order is collapsing, and he has emerged as one of the world leaders who most vocally opposes Donald Trump. When he denied Trump the use of Spanish bases to attack Iran, he directly accused the American president of using “the smoke of war to hide his failure and, in the process, line the pockets of a select few.”

But support for Gaza (not only does Spain recognize Palestine as a state, but Sánchez has also strongly denounced Israel’s actions and labeled them as genocide) and opposition to warmongering leaders like Trump do not win elections on their own. “The debates in Spain are quite similar to those across Europe and focus on the cost of living and immigration, which explain the discontent with governments in many countries,” Simón explains, painting a picture that affects social democrats like Sánchez, leaders further to his right like Macron, and even hard-liners like Giorgia Meloni. The problems of inflation, low wages, loss of purchasing power, and strains on the welfare state are taking their toll on nearly every government in Europe.

Sánchez is, in fact, something of an anomaly with his eight years of resistance—he even titled his book about his political life Manual de Resistencia (Manual of Resistance). Looking ahead to these elections, it appears, on paper, almost impossible for Sánchez to retain his leadership. But if anything has defined his political career, it’s his ability to survive at the last moment, aided by the sociological realities of Spain. Ignacio Urquizu, director of Metroscopia and an election expert, explained a few days ago on SER, Spain’s leading radio network, that it “is the most left-leaning country in Europe, surpassed only by Malta.” He also noted that, since the 1980s, “there has been a straight line” showing that 45% of Spanish society consistently identifies as left-wing or center-left.

Sánchez has always relied on that reality for his historic comebacks. He went from being forced to resign in 2016 as secretary-general of the PSOE (the Spanish socialist party), under pressure from the party apparatus, to regaining that position just two years later and becoming prime minister on the back of a no-confidence motion against corruption, the first of its kind to succeed in Spain. The then future prime minister denounced the corruption that caused “embarrassment, disbelief, and outrage.” He has since survived two elections with the odds stacked against him, without clear majorities, in a Spain that is no longer defined by parties but by blocs, where different forces compete for similar political spaces, leading to parliaments that the framers of Spain’s constitution never anticipated.

The most dramatic of these occurred in 2023, when the right was already uncorking the Champagne, and a single member of parliament tipped the balance in favor of the socialist coalition. It was a poisoned chalice, as Spain has seen: The fact that the PP leader did not become prime minister, due to his failure to reach agreements with several nationalist right parties, paved the way for an ineffective legislative session, with 11 parties and a minority government entangled in parliamentary gridlock and social measures often being blocked by the “no” votes of Sánchez’s supposed allies. This miracle of resilience, in any case, has somehow lasted until now. Even so, Narciso Michavila, president of the social-research firm GAD3, is not ready to declare victory for the right just yet, despite what the polls say: Sánchez, he stated recently, “still has appeal,” and Michavila thinks it is still possible that he will ultimately secure another term.

But there is no denying that in the last decade in Spain, electoral politics have changed. Some of his loyal supporters during the period between 2016 and 2018 are now in prison or awaiting trial for the very corruption that Sánchez denounced. Former minister José Luis Ábalos, once his greatest supporter and whom he even mentioned in the no-confidence-motion speech that eventually led to his becoming prime minister, has now been given a 24-year prison sentence for corruption. Santos Cerdán, the man tasked with reining in the Socialist Party, is awaiting his turn in the dock. The party leadership that came to tackle corruption has proven to be just as, if not more, vulnerable to the recurring cancer of Spanish politics as those they replaced. And although Sánchez boasted about how quickly these incidents were dealt with (there are ministers from the previous administration who are still under investigation for corruption), he has long been unable to boast of integrity as he once did, now that scandals have involved his close associates.

Meanwhile, alongside these proceedings, Sánchez, his party, and many of his allies are denouncing the “judicial persecution” that has extended to the prime minister’s family—his wife, Begoña Gómez, is awaiting trial on various corruption charges, which the prosecutor’s office has opposed on the grounds of insufficient evidence (she has denied the allegations); his brother, David Sánchez, is appealing a nine-year disqualification from holding public office imposed on him by a court in Badajoz for administrative misconduct, stemming from his holding a public position as a cultural manager that was allegedly created specifically for him (he has denied the allegations). Pedro Sánchez has claimed that his brother and wife are “innocent” and victims of a “harassment and bullying operation.”

The latest chapter in this series of trials and investigations surrounding Sánchez was particularly painful for his party and his base: Former Socialist Party president and prime minister of Spain from 2004 to 2011 José Luis Rodríguez Zapatero (who was very active in the last election campaign and served as a role model for Sánchez and his vision of progressivism) was indicted in spring 2026 over an alleged corruption scheme. Jewelry valued at more than a million euros, which Zapatero took months to clarify was supposedly a gift from the Saudi monarchy, was discovered during the searches and sealed his fate. (He has denied any wrongdoing.) The role model Sánchez had looked up to and who boasted of having led the least corrupt government in the history of Spain’s democracy suddenly became the first former prime minister in the country’s democratic history to testify as a defendant. The case is still in the preliminary investigation phase.

But at first, the PSOE came out in force, through press releases and statements from its leaders, to denounce the alleged act of lawfare with the phrase “Whoever can act, should act.” It was a direct quote from the former president of the Partido Popular, José María Aznar, when he attempted to rally opposition to Sánchez’s alliance with Catalan parties in 2023.

These tensions within his family and his party, and the blurring of the lines between the two that the opposition accuses him of, have also spilled over into his campaign. This has put his wife and the developments in her legal case in the spotlight. Sánchez declared he was “deeply in love with” Gómez when he threatened to resign in 2024, following the media harassment of her. “They’ve tried to break us in every way possible, and they’ve achieved exactly the opposite; today we’re more united and stronger than ever,” the prime minister told his wife. “Let them hear it loud and clear: I love you.” The prime minister and his wife’s campaign kisses date back to at least 2016, but this time the Socialist Party has taken up the cause: “We are the party of love,” declared Minister Óscar Puente, and the prime minister has even worn a T-shirt bearing the campaign slogan “La Fuerza del Amor” (“The Power of Love”).

Meanwhile, the far right is poised to become the country’s third-largest political force, now that measures to contain it have failed in Spain, as they have elsewhere in Europe. No measures have succeeded in curbing its rise—or, in cases like Italy, its success at claiming direct power. Spain has, however, long been a country that leans more to the left; it is perhaps the most socially left-leaning country in all of Europe.

“The truth is that the Iberian Peninsula as a whole leans a bit further to the left than its neighbors due to its historical legacy,” says Simón. “In fact, because of this history, socialist parties fare better in Spain and Portugal, and they also do well in the Scandinavian countries.” On the other hand, one need only look at France, where the once all-powerful Socialist Party is now politically irrelevant, to understand the situation in much of the rest of Europe, where centrist parties have lost their influence.

It was the mobilization of Spain’s historic left-leaning majority that saved the PSOE in 2023. This had repercussions in Europe: While other social democratic leaders were falling and the centrists or moderate right-wingers were losing momentum, Sánchez became the voice of a progressive Europe. Emmanuel Macron, who, with Sánchez, built a sort of Spanish-French axis within the European Union, now watches as France burns, six months before an election in which the far right is almost guaranteed to reach at least the runoff round. And all this is unfolding in the midst of what has been described as “Russiagate,” with “foreign interference in all democratic processes in the European Union, including disinformation.”

For Europe, the consequences of this electoral process have already become apparent: The Union had to decide on a far-reaching budget, which also includes the future of Ukraine—whose president has warned that the country needs an additional 70 billion euros that Europe does not currently have. This is especially true following the revelation by Poland’s foreign minister, Radosław Sikorski, that the EU has been left on its own in its contribution to Ukraine’s defense and rearmament: “Since January of last year, the United States has not contributed a single cent to Ukraine’s defense. We, Europe, are doing it.” But the elections in Spain are the start of a chain of electoral contests—France and Italy will follow—that push the possibility of an agreement further out of reach. European negotiations often drag on for years, and any electoral upheaval can derail those already underway. One of Sánchez’s most loyal ministers, Luis Planas, for example, will not appear on any electoral lists so he can devote all his time in office to these negotiations: “Between now and the end of the year, the future of the common agricultural policy and the fisheries policy is at stake,” which in Spain accounts for nearly 9% of GDP through both direct and indirect contributions.

The elections will be held on November 29 amid a tense atmosphere: The right is resorting to personal attacks and AI-generated deepfakes against Sánchez, his wife, and his cabinet. Left-wing forces have not found a figurehead like Jean-Luc Mélenchon of the France Insoumise (France Unbowed) party and are polling at less than 10%—a situation that would leave the PSOE without strong coalition partners even if it performs well. Sánchez’s international standing and the strength of the macroeconomy will likely have little weight in an election where the focus is on basic issues including the cost of living and specifically housing. But whatever the outcome, it will likely be the first in a series of elections that may lay bare the crisis facing liberal democracies in Europe. Now without the protection of the United States, it faces two existential crises. There’s an internal crisis, marked by the rise of the far right and its contempt for human rights, and an external crisis, with the Russian threat becoming clearer by the day and commercial and technological pressure from the United States and China, which has counted Sánchez among its most important advocates in its dealings with Europe.
