Tiny spider named for Stephen Colbert has more DNA than you do

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Scientists sequenced the genome of Stephen Colbert’s trapdoor spider, revealing repeated DNA and clues to spider evolution.

Stephen Colbert once used his TV show to demand that a spider be named after him. The trapdoor spider that got his name is about the size of a quarter, yet it carries more DNA than a person does.

The genome of Aptostichus stephencolberti, its full set of DNA, contains billions of base pairs.

Most of that length isn’t genes. It’s the same stretches of DNA, copied again and again.

A team led by researchers at UC Davis sequenced the genome. Jason Bond, a professor in the Department of Entomology and Nematology, is the study’s senior author.

Genome length is counted in base pairs, the paired chemical letters that spell out DNA.

“At 4.6 gigabases, or 4.6 billion DNA base pairs, it’s not the largest genome among the spiders, but it’s significant,” Bond said.

He said the spider is the first member of its family to have its genome sequenced. The team’s report was published in September 2026.

The team estimated that the spider’s DNA is split among 52 chromosomes, the bundles that hold a cell’s genetic material. A single set of human chromosomes has 23.

Repetition fills most of that space. About 77% of the genome is made of sequences that appear many times over, scattered across the chromosomes.

Genes take up far less room. An automated computer search, drawing on genetic material from whole adult spiders, identified 14,067 of them.

Future studies of this spider and its close relatives can use the genome as a reference for comparison.

In many animals, each chromosome ends in a short string of DNA letters repeated over and over. That repeated tip, called a telomere repeat, helps protect the end of the chromosome.

The team searched this spider’s genome for a five-letter version of that repeat and found none.

When the researchers widened the search to other spiders, the repeat was missing from every one they checked.

The findings suggest that this repeat disappeared in an early ancestor of today’s spiders.

“[The genome] lends itself to broadening our understanding of spider genome composition and consequently how genomes have evolved across the animal Tree of Life,” Bond said.

Female trapdoor spiders spend their entire lives underground. Each one lives in a burrow lined with silk and sealed by a camouflaged, hinged door.

A female waits for the vibrations of prey moving nearby, then comes out quickly to grab whatever wanders too close.

People walking through the spider’s coastal dune habitat often pass close to the burrows without knowing it.

The spider’s venom isn’t dangerous to people, though. Of the more than 50,000 known spider species, only 30 to 50 have venom known to be toxic to humans, Bond said.

Colbert’s connection goes back to 2008. Bond had named another trapdoor spider after rock musician Neil Young, and Colbert, playing his pompous pundit character on The Colbert Report, wanted one too.

He used a segment called “Who’s Not Honoring Me Now?” to demand a spider of his own, and Bond obliged.

As in the comedian’s own name, the t in stephencolberti is silent. Another spider species carries the name of Bob Marley.

The spider lives only in coastal sand dunes between the San Francisco Bay Area and Monterey. Those dunes are broken into separate patches, and trapdoor spiders rarely travel far.

Staying put has a cost. Spiders that stay on one patch can end up genetically distinct from the next patch over, and the team wrote that the same habits make trapdoor spiders prone to extinction.

The genome is part of the California Conservation Genomics Project, a state-funded effort to build genetic data for conservation.

Researchers can use data like these to spot distinct populations that may need protection.

“We Californians love our beaches and trapdoor spiders share them with us,” Bond said.

“We’re hoping to understand how things like climate change, fire and urbanization might be impacting the genetic diversity of species.”

Rising sea levels threaten this narrow strip of habitat. “They’re going to be incredibly sensitive to sea-level rise, but they’re also very sensitive to invasive species,” he said.

Genomes like this one could also help researchers find useful compounds in venom.

Some compounds that come from venom could work as painkillers, and others might help control pests, Bond said.

Scientists already study animals such as cone snails for the same reason.

“We don’t really know what’s out there in terms of untapped natural product diversity for venoms,” he said.

The researchers haven’t yet worked out which patches of dune hold genetically distinct groups of these spiders, or which of those groups are most at risk.

The team’s genome, which is publicly available in an online data archive, gives researchers the reference they need to compare spiders from one dune to the next.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Heredity.
