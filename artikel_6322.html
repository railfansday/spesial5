Vaping may disrupt the lung’s protective barrier after just one day

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Experiments in human lung cells and hamsters suggest brief vaping exposure can disrupt protective barriers and leave lingering signs of cellular stress.

Vaping can seem easy on the lungs. New research suggests that even brief exposure can disrupt the cells that help them function.

Deep in the lung, where oxygen crosses into the blood, a single day of e-cigarette vapor was enough to injure the cells that line the smallest airways and blood vessels.

In hamsters exposed for five days, signs of cellular stress remained 10 days after exposure ended.

A separate experiment found changes in how previously exposed animals responded to a respiratory virus.

The work was done in human lung cells and tissue and in hamsters, not in people who vape. Irina Petrache, a lung specialist at National Jewish Health in Denver, was the senior author.

She is chief of the hospital’s Division of Pulmonary, Critical Care and Sleep Medicine, and researchers at the University of Colorado and Colorado State University took part.

“Our findings suggest that even short-term vaping exposure can initiate injury in the deepest and most delicate regions of the lung,” Petrache said.

“Importantly, some effects persisted after exposure ended and altered the immune response to a subsequent viral infection.”

The deep lung works because two thin sheets of cells stay sealed. One lines the smallest airways, and the other lines the tiny blood vessels wrapped around the air sacs.

Together, these layers allow oxygen to reach the blood while helping keep fluid out of the air spaces.

Petrache’s team worked with human cells of both kinds, some of them grown from donated lungs. They also used thin slices of lung from donors who had never smoked and had no lung disease.

The vapor came from a commercial product, JUUL pods in the Virginia Tobacco flavor with 5% nicotine. A machine puffed on the pods for three seconds every 30 seconds.

The researchers tested the vapor, the liquid, and the condensed aerosol at doses meant to mimic casual vaping.

Within a day, the cell sheets had begun to leak. The more vapor a sheet got, the more it leaked, and the highest dose did the most lasting damage.

A stressed cell runs a recycling program, breaking down its damaged parts and reusing the pieces.

Biologists call it autophagy, and it normally helps a cell survive and repair itself.

Vapor switched that program on, but the cells didn’t finish the job. Half-processed material piled up inside them. Their growth slowed, and some of the cells died.

The team traced the trouble to a stress signal inside the cell known as JNK.

When they gave the cells a drug that blocks JNK before the vapor arrived, the sheets held together better and the cleanup went further.

To examine whether the effects persisted in a living lung, the team turned to golden Syrian hamsters, whose small airways and responses to respiratory viruses resemble ours.

Six animals per group, male and female, breathed a mist made from the condensed vapor once a day for five days. Then they breathed clean air for 10 days.

By the end, the hamsters looked fine. They had not lost weight or shown signs of illness. Under the microscope, their lungs had no inflammation or scarring.

Inside the deep lung, the changes had not cleared. The marker of unfinished cleanup had risen about fourfold, and cells lining the small blood vessels carried broken strands of DNA.

About 200 genes were more active than in unexposed animals and about 300 were less active.

Some of the less active genes were associated with Th1 immunity, a branch of the immune system that helps fight viruses.

The authors wrote that many of those gene changes passed a loose statistical test but not a strict one, partly because each group held so few animals.

In 2019, an outbreak of severe lung injury hit people who used e-cigarettes, and the CDC counted more than 2,800 people hospitalized or dead from it.

A respiratory infection on top of the vaping was one suspected trigger. That outbreak is one reason the team tested what prior vaping does to an infected lung.

So a separate set of hamsters breathed the vapor mist for five days. Three days after the last dose, they received SARS-CoV-2 through the nose.

Some also received influenza A virus between the final vapor exposure and the SARS-CoV-2 infection.

In the first three days of infection, the exposed hamsters carried more SARS-CoV-2 in upper airway swabs than the unexposed ones.

A week in, their lungs showed less activity in antiviral genes and more in genes tied to breaking down and rebuilding lung tissue.

Sex made a difference. Exposed females lost more weight after infection than unexposed females, while exposed males lost less.

The inflammation and injury in the lung tissue itself showed no clear difference between the two groups.

None of this was measured in a person. Human cells in a dish and hamsters in a chamber can show what vapor does to tissue.

Studies of people who vape would need to establish whether similar changes occur in their lungs.

The team used one product in one flavor, and the hamsters breathed a condensate rather than fresh aerosol, a choice forced by biosafety rules at the time.

A condensate lacks the short-lived compounds in a fresh puff. The team also did not separate what nicotine did from what the flavoring or the other ingredients did.

Long-term lung diseases such as COPD, which narrows the airways, and fibrosis, which scars the lung, involve the same kind of leaky barrier.

The study cannot establish whether these early changes lead to chronic lung disease.

Future research could test whether blocking JNK limits the injury and whether similar changes occur in people who vape.

The full study was published in the journal JCI Insight.
