How to Spot Employee Burnout Before the Holiday Rush Takes Its Toll

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

You're viewing the US website. Looking for the Site edition?

Here’s how small businesses can use workforce technology to prevent employee burnout and keep operations running smoothly.

Opinions expressed by Entrepreneur contributors are their own.

For a small business owner, a perfectly planned holiday schedule can bring a sigh of relief. Every shift is covered, employees are picking up extra hours, and the team is poised to keep pace with demand. But a schedule that works on paper can hide a business that is leaning too heavily on the same people.

Your most dependable employee may be covering their third extra shift this week. A manager may be staying late every day to finish work that customer demands pushed aside. Those efforts keep the business moving today, but relying on them week after week, especially at a small or growing business, leaves little room for employees to recover or for the business to handle another absence.

That is why burnout prevention belongs in holiday business planning and workforce strategy. Burnout is often treated as an employee well-being issue, but for small businesses it is also an operational challenge. When fatigue, overtime, absenteeism and scheduling pressure build across a workforce, they create risks that affect service levels, productivity and the ability to meet customer demand.

The right workforce technology helps owners see that pressure building. Connecting scheduling, timekeeping and payroll information makes it possible to spot recurring workload problems, understand who is affected and adjust coverage before another request to stay late becomes the only solution.

UKG’s 2025 Retail Workforce Report found that only 18% of retailers anticipated employee burnout as a major challenge. Meanwhile, UKG’s 2025 global study of frontline workers found that 76% of frontline employees reported feeling burned out at work.

Underestimating that strain can lead an owner to plan for holiday sales the team cannot sustainably support. If the staffing plan assumes everyone can keep adding hours, a single absence can leave the business struggling to serve customers and put even more pressure on the people who remain. Earlier visibility into workload gives owners time to arrange coverage before that cycle begins.

Small business leaders often work alongside their employees and know them well. Yet familiarity can make a staffing problem harder to recognize. The person who always volunteers becomes the first person asked, and a supervisor’s willingness to stay late gradually becomes part of the plan.

Disconnected systems make that pattern easier to miss. A schedule shows a filled shift, while payroll records the extra hours after they have been worked. Bringing those records together gives owners a clearer view of how often the business depends on the same employees to close its staffing gaps.

Overtime is one of the most important workforce operations signals because it shows where demand and workforce capacity are no longer aligned. Occasional extra hours may be welcome for some, but repeated overtime concentrated among a few employees warrants a closer look, especially when their schedules also leave little time between shifts.

Imagine a small retailer whose most experienced associate regularly stays an hour past her scheduled finish. Each request seems reasonable in isolation. Over three weeks, however, she also covers two absences and works several closing shifts followed by early openings. The owner sees someone dependable; the combined records reveal the true cost of how much additional work she is carrying.

Connected timekeeping and scheduling tools can make that cumulative demand visible without requiring the owner to reconstruct it from old schedules and messages. Alerts about approaching overtime or short intervals between shifts give a manager a chance to discuss coverage before publishing the next schedule.

Attendance changes add another piece of context. If absences rise on a team that has been working extra hours, the combination is worth exploring. While data alone does not establish that someone is burned out, it helps managers ask better questions about workload and support.

This is where workforce analytics earns its value for a lean business: It helps owners manage workforce operations proactively rather than reactively. Effective workforce operations requires connecting labor demand, staffing levels, scheduling, attendance, timekeeping and employee experience data to create a complete picture of how work gets done. When leaders can see those connections, they can intervene before a staffing challenge becomes an operational disruption.

Holiday staffing decisions often start with last year’s sales, but sales alone cannot explain why a team keeps falling behind. A store may have enough labor hours for the week and still depend on employees staying late because coverage does not line up with the work. That mismatch can turn an otherwise manageable holiday workload into a recurring source of exhaustion.

Consider a shop where afternoon pickup orders regularly arrive while employees are unloading a delivery. Associates leave stocking unfinished to help customers, then spend the end of their shifts catching up. To an owner reviewing weekly totals, the overtime may suggest the store simply needs more hours overall. Connecting order activity, labor demand, scheduling and timekeeping data can reveal a workforce operations problem that would otherwise remain hidden.

With demand forecasting and scheduling technology, that pattern can inform the next schedule. If the overlap is likely to recur, the manager can plan for stockroom coverage while keeping associates available to serve customers. AI-assisted scheduling can help identify an employee with the right skills and availability, reducing the time the manager spends working through possible changes.

The benefit is that the closing team no longer has to absorb the same unfinished work night after night. Addressing the bottleneck earlier can make finishing on time more predictable, giving employees greater confidence in their schedules. For the owner, it also creates a clearer basis for deciding whether to move existing coverage or invest in additional hours.

Better data also makes employee conversations more useful. A manager who can see repeated extra shifts can ask how that arrangement is working and what would make the coming weeks manageable. The answer may be a consistent day off, a different start time or continued access to additional hours.

That distinction matters during a season when employees may be balancing holiday expenses with family responsibilities. An automatic reduction in hours could create a different source of stress. Current availability and employee preferences help managers respond to what people actually need.

In UKG’s 2025 retail survey, 86% of retailers said frontline employees wanted more flexibility than their organizations provided. For small businesses, delivering that flexibility can be difficult when every change requires phone calls, group texts and a manager to coordinate the exchange.

Mobile self-service scheduling reduces that friction by letting employees view schedules, request time off and arrange shift swaps in one place. Scheduling rules can help check that replacement coverage meets role requirements and flag potential overtime. Employees gain a practical way to manage a conflict, while managers spend less time chasing responses.

The benefit goes beyond convenience. Someone who can resolve a scheduling conflict has more control over how work fits into life outside it. When employees see that technology makes those adjustments easier, they have a concrete reason to trust its role in the workplace.

Small businesses rarely have someone available to spend hours analyzing workforce reports. Technology is more useful when it brings a developing problem to the manager’s attention and makes the next decision easier, such as highlighting an open shift alongside qualified employees who are available to work.

Timing determines how many options remain. A coverage gap identified before next week’s schedule is published may be addressed through a voluntary shift change or additional staffing. The same gap discovered during a rush is more likely to land on whoever is already there.

Managers need that support themselves. UKG’s retail report found that 31% of retailers said managers worked additional shifts when understaffed. A supervisor covering the floor has less time to plan coverage or help a new employee learn the job, which can leave the team dependent on the same experienced people again.

Keeping demand forecasts and schedules connected also matters after peak sales pass. Returns and inventory work can continue into January, and a smaller team may still face a substantial workload. Visibility into that work gives owners a better basis for deciding when staffing can ease.

A successful holiday season depends on having a workforce that can sustain the work and the operational visibility to support it. Burnout, absenteeism, overtime, scheduling conflicts and staffing shortages are often treated as separate challenges, but they are all connected workforce operations signals.

When small businesses bring those signals together, they gain a clearer understanding of how work gets done across the organization. Workforce operations technology provides the visibility needed to identify strain early, align staffing with demand and respond before employee pressure becomes an operational problem.

Ultimately, organizations that manage workforce operations effectively are better positioned to support their people, serve customers consistently and perform through the busiest times of the year.
