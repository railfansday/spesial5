You Can See Everything on the Surface, Not What Lies Beneath

Posted November 6, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

I’m writing from the Hamptons. It’s the off-season, and the place feels like a small town, albeit a small town full of influential people, big thinkers, and, yes, some big egos. Almost everyone here is talking about You Can See Everything.

“Do you believe her? Aren’t her eyes crazy? Is she a narcissist? And what about her partner? Was he the real villain?”

It’s human nature to sort people into categories. We want to figure out what happened, so we won’t get fooled ourselves. We want a villain and a victim. We also want to feel better about ourselves, because at least we’re not “that crazy.” And it makes good theater.

If you haven’t seen the film: In the 34 days before Elizabeth Holmes reported to federal prison, she and her partner, Billy Evans, let a film crew led by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim record nearly every move. On the surface, the goal seemed to be showing them as everyday parents: raising their kids, walking on the beach, making eggs.

The title makes a promise: full access, nothing hidden, the real person behind the headlines at last.

The movie is disorienting. It took me a while to process. I won’t share all my thoughts here, but one has stayed with me: A camera can see everything on the surface. It can’t see the hidden drivers underneath.

A camera records behavior: what we say, how we stand, the stories we tell. It rarely captures the why. Under every polished performance and every bad decision, a hidden driver is steering. Unless you’re looking for it, you’ll miss it.

In my work as an executive coach, I use an assessment that uncovers these drivers and lays out the good, the bad, and the ugly in each one. I didn’t create it, and to be clear, it isn’t a personality test. It measures drive: the deep needs that push us forward.

I’ve never assessed Elizabeth Holmes, and I won’t guess at what drives her. But I have coached many founders and tech leaders, and her story reminds me of one of them. She happens to be a woman, but many men fall into the same trap. Her story shows what happens when hidden drivers go unseen.

From a distance, Laura had it all. She was brilliant, fast-moving, and, on paper, wildly successful. She carried herself as if she were the only one in the know and everyone else was slow to catch up.

When you asked her a question, she didn’t answer it. She used the moment to show how much more she knew. I could see why the people around her stopped asking questions. Nobody wants to feel stupid.

Her assessment results explained why. They showed two strong hidden drivers.

The first was going it alone. Somewhere in childhood, Laura’s brain had learned a rule: I can only count on myself. Needing anyone makes me weak.

The second was winning. Second place was not an option.

Put the two together, and her behavior makes sense. Being the smartest person in the room kept Laura in charge. If she always knew better, she never had to need anyone. And every conversation became a contest she could win.

As executive coach Marshall Goldsmith puts it, “Half the leaders I have met don’t need to learn what to do. They need to learn what to stop.”

Laura had built a life where she never had to need anyone. The cost was that no one could truly reach her: not her team, not even her best friend.

I see this pairing of drivers in high-performing leaders all the time. In many ways, it’s what got them to the top. But every driver has a shadow side.

People driven to go it alone tend to keep information to themselves, especially information that makes them look bad. People driven to win have trouble knowing a battle worth fighting from one that’s costing everyone around them.

I sat down with Laura and gently walked her through her results. I told her what her drivers were giving her and what they might cost her.

She listened. Then she said, “I’d like to take the test again.”

I told her the truth: It doesn’t work that way. A hidden driver isn’t a mood that changes depending on how you slept. It’s more like the foundation of a house. You can repaint the walls and rearrange the furniture, but the foundation stays where it was poured.

Here’s what I didn’t say out loud: Her request for a re-test was her drivers at work. To someone driven to go it alone, feedback sounds like “You need help.” To someone driven to win, a hard result feels like losing. So, she did what her drivers had always taught her to do. She tried to beat the test instead of learning from it.

That moment was a choice point: a small gap between what happened and what she did next. In that gap, Laura could have gotten curious. Instead, she reverted to the comfort of her drivers on autopilot.

A few years later, I heard that Laura had lost everything due to her own pride and arrogance: her company, her investors, her own money, and her reputation. I wasn’t surprised. Without awareness, there’s no way to change. Left unchecked, any of us can sabotage ourselves.

I’ve worked with other leaders who share Laura’s drivers and did change. They learned to reflect on themselves and take responsibility for their behavior. Laura never did.

Patterns begin in childhood. Attachment researchers have studied Laura’s pattern for decades. John Bowlby, the father of attachment theory, described people who become “compulsively self-reliant.” Adults with this pattern tend to hide or play down their feelings so they won’t look vulnerable, weak, or dependent on others.

Think of it like this: A child who learns that needing people isn’t safe builds a suit of armor. The armor protects the child. But decades later, the adult is still wearing it and wonders why no one can get close.

Fear makes everyone go quiet. Laura’s need to look smart came with a hidden price. Research on harsh bosses has found that abusive supervision makes employees afraid, and fear leads them to go silent. Harvard’s Amy Edmondson, who studies what she calls psychological safety, found that when it was missing, people were less willing to speak up, ask for help, or admit mistakes, so teams learned less.

Bad news stops traveling upward. Psychologists call this the “Mum Effect.” People tend to soften or hold back bad news, especially when it must go to someone powerful. Stanford professor Robert Sutton described a chilling example. After the 1986 Challenger disaster, physicist Richard Feynman found that NASA’s working engineers put the chance of losing a shuttle at roughly 1 in 100. NASA’s managers put it at 1 in 100,000. The truth had been softened at every step on its way up.

Now put the pieces together. Laura hid her own bad news about her failing company because her self-reliance wouldn’t let her look weak. Her team hid their bad news because she made them feel stupid for speaking up. She ended up leading from inside a bubble where nobody told her the truth, not even herself.

Her drivers weren’t what brought her down. Not seeing them was.

Let me be clear: Seeing someone’s hidden drivers doesn’t let them off the hook. Laura was responsible for how she treated people, and Elizabeth Holmes was convicted for what she did.

But labels like “bully,” “fraud,” or “villain” close the case. They let us feel safely different from the person we’re judging. Understanding drivers keeps the case open, and that makes change possible. You can’t change what you can’t see.

Seeing your hidden drivers isn’t a label or a verdict. It’s a mirror. And unlike Laura, you don’t need a re-test. You just need the willingness to look.

You Can See Everything promises full access. But none of us can see everything: not in a famous founder, not in the person across the table, and not in ourselves. Not without help and honesty.

All of us have a shadow side. Elizabeth Holmes’s story is an extreme one, but it’s a reminder of how much denial and ego can cost. Whether you’re in over your head at work or hiding the truth from people you care about, you're standing at a choice point. You can dig in deeper, or you can ask for help: from a therapist, a coach, or a trusted friend. Someone who can hold up a mirror and tell you what they see.

You can’t see everything. But you can learn to see yourself.
