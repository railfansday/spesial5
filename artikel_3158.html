Looking for a coffee machine to see you through all twelve months of the year (and then some)? The Smeg Espresso & Cold Brew Machine might just scratch that itch. This aesthetically pleasing machine can make genuinely cold cold brew — no lukewarm extraction here — and delicious, hot espresso. Although it's quite expensive in the U.S., it could be an ideal first machine for the budding enthusiast.

What do you look for when shopping for an espresso machine? Function? Coffee-snob-approved features? Affordability? Or style? If it’s the latter, may I introduce you to the Smeg ECF03 — one of the most aesthetically pleasing espresso machines I’ve ever seen?

Also called the Smeg Espresso & Cold Brew Machine, the ECF03 combines Smeg’s trademark ‘50s design with some seriously impressive functionality. The machine makes genuinely cold cold brew — not lukewarm like De’Longhi — and delicious espresso. While the steam wand takes a while to heat up and requires some patience for back-to-back shots, it’s a fun little machine for newbies.

However, its MSRP isn’t a fun little price tag for newbies — in the U.S., it’ll set you back $699. In the U.K. you can grab it for as low as £204. No, that’s not a typo. £204. So, is this enough to make it one of the best espresso machines? To find out, you’ll need to keep reading this Smeg ECF03 review.

Single shot portafilter (single-walled and dual-walled), double shot portafilter (single-walled and dual-walled), tamper, cleaning disc, brush, cleaning needle

The Smeg ECF03 is outrageously expensive in the U.S., and pretty par for the course in the U.K. On the east side of the Atlantic, you’re looking at $699 from Amazon, but over on the west, you’ll only pay £399 at John Lewis. It’s also on clearance at Curry’s for £204, which is utterly bizarre, seeing as this machine only came out last year, but I’d recommend buying there if you’re fast. I’m not sure how long that sale price will last as I write this.

In the U.S., this machine is really overpriced. $699 for a Thermoblock espresso machine with no grinder is steep. For $699, you can get the Breville Barista Express or the Breville Bambino Plus with the Baratza Encore ESP grinder.

You can also get the De’Longhi La Specialista Arte Evo for $699, which has a built-in grinder, and the Ninja Luxe Cafe Mini for $449 — which also has a built-in grinder.

See what I mean? You’d still have to buy a grinder separately to make the most of the ECF03. That’s an extra $200. An MSRP of around $400-$500 would have made more sense, putting the ECF03 in line with the best entry-level espresso machines, Breville Bambino Plus and Breville Bambino ($299).

Of course, the ECF03 can make cold brew, so you wouldn’t need to purchase a separate cold brew maker or French press for cold brew, but I’m not sure this is worth the extra $200-$300 over Breville’s options. On top of that, the aforementioned De’Longhi La Specialista Arte Evo can make “cold brew” for the same price.

I use quotation marks because De’Longhi’s cold brew comes out warm — but I’ll get into that later.

While the ECF03 has a 58mm portafilter — closer to the specialty coffee commercial standard than Breville’s 54mm — I’m not sure that’s worth the extra $$$.

The ECF03 looks like (almost) everything Smeg makes: retro, stylish, and oh-so cool. The only machine to forgo the trademark Smeg aesthetic is the $1,599 Semi-Professional/Mini Pro/EMC02 (the many-monikered machine), which was a collaboration between Smeg and La Pavoni.

If you like the Smeg aesthetic, you’ll be pleased. If it’s not your cup of tea, you won’t be pleased. It’s as simple as that.

For a $699 machine, though, the ECF03 is a touch more plasticky than I’d like. That gorgeous white chassis? Shiny plastic. The steam wand knob also feels like plastic. If it’s not actually plastic, color me surprised, but it sure does look and feel like it.

There’s a manometer, which is a nice touch, but it doesn’t actually show the specific bar pressure of each step. I assume anything in the perforated section on the bottom right means over 9 bars, but I’d like numbers on the next iteration of this machine.

But the accessories. Oh, the accessories. They’re beautiful. The 58mm tamper is super weighty, making it feel incredibly high quality, and the 58mm portafilter is just as professional. They actually feel like weapons. This is the mark of a high-quality machine. If you unbox a $500+ espresso machine and the tamper is plastic, run. Run fast.

Naturally, you get a pressurized (dual-wall) and non-pressurized (single-wall) portafilter basket. I only used the single-wall one as I have the best grinder in the world, Mazzer Philos, so I didn’t need to resort to the help-I’ve-used-pre-ground-coffee-and-now-my-espresso-tastes-bad dual-walled portafilters.

The Smeg ECF03 makes delicious espresso. As mentioned earlier, I used the non-pressurized portafilters and fresh Ethiopian beans ground on my Mazzer Philos. I recommend you also use freshly ground coffee and the non-pressurized portafilters for the best experience. Pressurized portafilters can destroy nuance and delicate flavors in specialty coffee beans, but are fine for pre-ground coffee if that’s what you like.

To make espresso, I ground 18g of my Ethiopian coffee beans to click 25 on the Philos, which is coarser than the Fellow Espresso Series 1’s ($1,499) optimal grind size, but similar to the Breville Bambino’s ideal range of 22-25. For context, I use the same coffee beans for every single machine.

Here’s a photo of a “perfect” espresso. This extracted 35.6g in 26 seconds, which is in the “golden window” of espresso extraction.

As you can see, there’s a lovely crema on top, with a delicate, fluffy texture and a nice color to the shot. While this might not impress serious espresso enthusiasts — the shot was a little bitter at the end of the mouthful — it’s definitely fine if you are less of a hobbyist and more of an appreciator.

Overall, the espresso tastes very Italian. Strong-bodied, rich, and dark, even when using medium-roasted beans. Again, if you’re an espresso enthusiast, you might want to invest a little more in the Fellow Espresso Series 1 or Lelit Mara X3 ($1,999) — or the Smeg EMC02, which I still dream about.

As an integral part of this machine’s marketing, it’s only logical I discuss cold brew in detail. You access cold brew via the “Blue menu”, which is activated by pressing the power button while the machine is already switched on. The buttons will glow blue, meaning you’re in cold brew mode.

According to the manual, you’re supposed to use “one or two” ice cubes per cold brew, but I ignored that. I filled my cup about halfway with ice cubes, dosed 18g of medium-fine ground coffee in the non-pressurized portafilter basket, and set it to pull a double shot of cold brew. Not sure how you can get a “double shot” of cold brew, but I assume that’s just the machine’s limited button options.

The cold brew took four minutes and fifty-three seconds to fill my glass. The machine is programmed to dose a certain volume of cold brew, but I used a 12-ounce glass filled with ice, so I didn’t need the “whole” serving.

As you can see, it’s relatively dark, with a strong body. The ice cubes aren’t all melted — can you guess what that means? Yes, it actually brews cold! This is fantastic news; De’Longhi machines tend to brew lukewarm, or sometimes even actively warm cold brew (which then requires ice to make it cold).

As for the taste, this cold brew was fine. I wouldn’t throw away my Espro P7 French press, which I use to make cold brew the traditional way (16 hours at room temperature), but I also wouldn’t turn my nose up at rapid cold brew from the Smeg. I think this will do if you want a cold coffee in a pinch, but serious cold brew lovers need not apply.

While the ECF03 makes a delicious espresso, its steam wand isn’t as powerful as I’d like it to be. Don’t get me wrong, this is nowhere near as egregious as machines like the Casabrews Ultra ($349), but for $699, I’d expect at least a Bambino Plus-level wand.

I was able to get perfect microfoam, but it took a while to steam an 8oz jug — around a minute. Here’s a photo of a flat white I made on the ECF03.

As you can see, the milk texture is perfect, with smooth, almost melted-marshmallow texture. There are zero bubbles and the milk is extremely creamy. This tasted divine.

However, it’s worth noting that I’m an ex-professional barista and an espresso machine tester of almost three years. I have a lot of practice. If this is your first espresso machine, you will need to baby that steam wand. Because it’s lower pressure than the Breville Bambino/Bambino Plus, you’ll need to learn how to eke out delicate texture even when you can’t achieve the perfect vortex.

At 14.3 x 8.1 x 11.6 inches, the ECF03 isn’t the smallest, nor the largest, espresso machine I’ve tested. As it has no grinder, you’ll need a separate one, so I recommend making sure you’ve got enough to room for both the machine and its grinder before purchasing.

The water tank is at the back of the machine, so you’ll need a decent amount of upper-cupboard clearance space — at least 17 inches, I’d say.

Speaking of water tank, Smeg provides you with water hardness strips, but no water filter. If you wanted to filter your water, you’d need to buy that separately. Smeg sells these for $25 on its website, which is pretty disappointing to see considering this machine is already $699.

When you do need to descale, Smeg doesn’t sell its own descaler. I’d recommend using De’Longhi’s or Durgol’s, which are $19 for 2 bottles and $15 for 4 bottles, respectively.

Last but certainly not least — warranty. Smeg offers a 2-year warranty if purchased from the Smeg store or a 1-year warranty from another outlet. This is pretty disappointing when De’Longhi, Breville, and Ninja all offer 2-year warranties as standard — and many of their machines cost less than the ECF03.

The Smeg ECF03 is ideal for espresso beginners. If you want to feel fancier than, say, a Casabrews (and you have the money to back it up, of course), then by all means you should invest in a Smeg. The ECF03 is really easy to use, makes actually cold cold brew, and has a mostly useful manometer for pressure-watching.

Although the steam wand means you need to really pay attention during milk heating, if you’re willing to put the work in, you’ll get barista-quality coffee out of it. And I mean… just look at it! Everything Smeg makes is an aesthetic marvel, and the ECF03 is no different.

If you’re in the U.K., you’re getting a much better deal, but if you find it on sale in the U.S., I’m sure you won’t be disappointed.
