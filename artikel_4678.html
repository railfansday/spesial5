A New Gut-Brain Pathway That May Affect Your Mood

“Tiny bubbles in the mind, make me feel happy, make me feel fine.”—with apologies to Don Ho

For the last decade or so, scientists have been aware of at least three ways that microbes can communicate with our brain. One is hormones, including the stress hormone cortisol. Another is through the vagus nerve that connects the gut to the brain. And the third is the immune system that constantly tracks microbes and their secretions. But a fourth method has slowly come to light: extracellular vesicles (EVs). These are tiny bubbles that bud off of bacteria and can travel seemingly everywhere through the body.

EVs have been known about for decades, although exactly what they are and what they do has been a little fuzzy. In 1967, Peter Wolf described particles that separated out with high-speed centrifugation of blood, which he called “platelet dust." In 1983, Rose Johnstone found EVs in immature red blood cells by serendipity. She called it an “Alice in Blunderland” discovery.

Since then, EVs have been found everywhere we look, in all kingdoms of life, including bacteria, fungi, plants, and animals. At first, it seemed like cells were producing the EVs as little trash bags. But more recent research has found that EVs are far more complex, carrying everything from proteins, microRNA, DNA, neurotransmitters, anti-tumor peptides, and even cellular organelles like mitochondria. They provide interkingdom communication, acting as messengers, repair modules, and yes, trash bags.

Their membranes are studded with proteins that serve as addresses, directing them to specific tissues where they can latch on and spill their contents. That mechanism is exciting to researchers who are starting to create EVs that can aim therapies at specific tissues.

They are small enough to pass right through an intact gut lining where they can enter the bloodstream. Some bacterial EVs can slip directly into our cells, which is usually not a good thing. Fortunately, most of those are summarily captured and dissolved on the spot. Surprisingly, EVs can also pass through the blood-brain barrier to send messages deep into the brain. That may be good or bad, depending on their origin.

A recent Korean study by Se-Young Choi and colleagues found that EVs released by Fusobacterium nucleatum contributed to neuroinflammation and caused anxiety and depression when injected into the brains of mice. The EVs acted to shift neurons from excitatory to inhibitory, likely explaining the low energy and listlessness of the affected mice. Although mice love to sip sugar, these mice couldn’t be bothered to leave their nest. This is classic sickness behavior, surprisingly similar to depression. The EVs also stimulated the brain’s immune cells, called microglia.

An earlier Korean study by Juli Choi and colleagues used an in vitro analysis to observe the effects of the probiotic Lactiplantibacillus plantarum. They started by stressing mouse hippocampal cells with the stress hormone corticosterone. This had the predicted effect of lowering expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which helps neurons to survive and grow and is associated with improved learning and memory.

When they added EVs from L. plantarum, BDNF levels were restored. They found the same thing in living mice that were exposed to stress: Their BDNF levels dropped and were restored with injections of L. plantarum EVs. Here, the injections were into the gut of the mice, not directly into the brain, demonstrating that EVs are likely traveling from the gut to the brain, sailing through the blood-brain barrier.

Similar results have been found in different studies for Lactobacillus paracasei, Akkermansia muciniphila, Bacteroides fragilis, and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus. They each have multiple methods of action, including butyrate production, tryptophan metabolism, and quelling inflammation.

That makes these important microbes psychobiotics: bacteria that can improve mood. The effect of these EVs is consequential: In some cases, they work as well as antidepressants.

Mice are the perfect animals for this research, as their plumbing is similar to humans. But mouse bacteria and human bacteria are different, even if there is a family resemblance. In general, the basic principles are conserved, but human studies are necessary before we can take advantage of this amazing science.

Fortunately, the human studies are starting to yield similar results. A review by Kuanjun He and colleagues describes dozens of studies linking bacterial EVs to schizophrenia, bipolar disease, and major depressive disorder.

We have long known that microbes can produce neurotransmitters like dopamine, serotonin, and GABA, all of which are favored targets of psychoactive medicines. But these molecules are excluded by the blood-brain barrier. That is why we don’t simply prescribe serotonin; it won’t pass the barrier. Instead, researchers create serotonin reuptake inhibitors that can enter the brain easily and, once there, can act to increase levels of serotonin in the brain.

But packaging these molecules into an EV lets them slide right into the brain. At some point, it is likely that formulators will take advantage of this packaging trick to get previously excluded drugs into the brain.

The truly exciting news here is that you can put EVs to work for you today. Start by avoiding the junk food that is destroying your gut microbes, and switch to something like a plant-heavy Mediterranean diet with lots of variety. Ecologically, diversity is a winning strategy for a harmonious microbiome. With a bounty of different microbes, it is difficult for a small number of bully species to dominate.

Intriguingly, the food you eat is also full of EVs. Natural foods like milk, grapes, broccoli, and ginger are packed with EVs that escape digestion and can slip directly into your bloodstream. Once there, they can change the way your stress genes are expressed, improve insulin sensitivity, and lower inflammation. These happy bubbles may be tiny, but they are potent.
