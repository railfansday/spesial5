10 Elements Metroidvanias Could Borrow From GTA 6

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Ronald is a video game journalist with more than 10 years of experience in the industry, working on several of the most important Spanish websites.

As someone who usually writes about Metroidvanias but has recently been engrossed in GTA 6, I haven't stopped thinking about the points where the two converge.

Rockstar's new work isn't an adventure like the series' previous iterations; instead, it boasts an even finer and more meticulous design that, at fleeting moments, makes me feel it hides lessons beyond the obvious.

That games in its genre will follow in its footsteps is logical, but I truly believe there's a sufficiently diverse level of excellence to consider that not only open-world titles will benefit from its influence.

Therefore, in order to return to writing about the type of games I'm passionate about while still fulfilling my communicative responsibility towards the interactive entertainment industry's latest titan, here are ten elements Metroidvanias could borrow from GTA 6.

Throughout its history, Grand Theft Auto has been a franchise of journeys, stories, and mechanics that unfold along the way rather than at the destination, giving vehicles a prominent role as in GTA 6.

In contrast, movement in Metroidvanias, despite the notable importance of exploration, is reduced to the characters and their abilities, limiting the worlds' scale to the equally slow speed at which their protagonists move.

Therefore, as the final stages of each campaign approach, fast travel becomes the key to backtracking, so the presence of manually controllable means of transport would not only add another layer of depth but also enable even larger worlds.

It doesn't have to be permanent, of course, as I personally love concrete maps that you can traverse on foot, yet the genre would benefit from a broader understanding of distance than it has been done so far.

Although I don't believe every game should have a plethora of minigames and side activities like GTA 6, I do think they highlight the narrow imagination with which Metroidvanias approach their gameplay concepts.

Speaking of verbs, fighting and moving are the only ones that can be attributed to their vast majority, instead of including more interactive elements that would help diversify the gaming experience.

Silver Pines, for example, does an excellent job in this regard by inheriting puzzles from horror games, but visual novels' decision-making, RPGs' morality systems, or stealth games' infiltrative sections could have a place in the right products.

Of course, it's easier to have diverse gameplay if you're an open-world game than if you're an action-adventure title, since the classification limits you, though I think it's a good time to break free from the shackles of labels.

Especially since FromSoftware's games have influenced the genre, it's become increasingly difficult to find a Metroidvania with a concrete story, told linearly and directly rather than through contextual and ambiguous elements.

These are rich and fertile narrative mechanisms for creating immersive worlds, no doubt, but the trope easily becomes exhausted after practically everyone resorts to it to tell their tales.

I'm not necessarily talking about cinematics or actors with impressive performances, but about campaigns with concrete beginnings, developments, and endings, as Prince of Persia: The Lost Crown and Ori and the Blind Forest have already done.

These examples demonstrate that it's achievable, and if we dare to go further and adopt a love story as emotional and sensitive as Jason and Lucia's, all the better.

Returning to the premise of gameplay variety, Metroidvanias have failed to convey the influence of their environments beyond the visual, as our ways of interacting with different biomes remain virtually unchanged.

You don't usually dive into aquatic biomes, just as you don't climb mountainous areas or sink into swamps, because the way we orient ourselves in space tends to be the same regardless of that location's unique characteristics.

In contrast, GTA 6 does a superb job of differentiating each of its regions, with activities adapted to the physical attributes of each one to truly convey the feeling that the world you're inhabiting exists.

I'm not suggesting the same level of depth or technological detail, but rather the same approach: to turn aesthetic beauty into playable immersion.

Find a fighter jet in GTA 6, learn how to enter Mile Zero Air Station, and escape the five-star wanted level after stealing one.

GTA 6, with its massive wardrobe and extensive customization system, reminded me of how special it felt to see Alucard's simple cape color change in Castlevania: Symphony of the Night depending on his equipment.

When you think of the greatest Metroidvanias, we all know and play the same protagonist, from their visual presentation to their gameplay styles, as the options are kept to a minimum to ensure precision and refinement.

It's a perfectly valid design sacrifice, and it has given us countless masterpieces, yet invalidating player expression by not allowing them to create their own unique gameplay and aesthetic combinations is a debt that has gone unpaid for years.

It's easier said than done, considering how much more difficult it is to conceive of an avatar that offers so many alternatives, but it's so captivating in GTA 6 that I can't help but dream with the possibility.

Even with the perpetual satire and exaggeration inherent in GTA 6's story, it reminded me how unusual realistic narratives have become within the video game industry, which is at the mercy of various forms of fantasy instead.

Thus, would playing a Metroidvania governed by our universal laws be even remotely as entertaining as one with an entirely new world? I don't have the slightest idea, precisely because this approach is so unexplored within the genre.

The conversations between Jason and Lucia feel so human, as do many of the events with NPCs and secondary characters, that you empathize almost instinctively, leading to a level of connection unusual in Metroidvanias.

Works like Shadow Complex or, to a lesser extent, Silver Pines itself, partially demonstrate this possibility, and I feel that exploring it would help open up a completely unique avenue for the genre, one that isn't limited to hand-drawn 2D side-scrollers.

Find a tank at Mile Zero Air Station in GTA 6. Learn where to enter the base, where the tanks are parked, and what to expect when you escape.

Among the most striking aspects of GTA 6 is the way every NPC reacts to your every move. Whether it's walking, wearing unusual clothing, drawing a weapon, or saying something, there's an interaction waiting for you around every corner.

In the context of Metroidvanias, games like Hollow Knight have already explored the idea of characters responding to our actions, and even with the system's restrictions, it's an integral element of its worldbuilding.

Accordingly, building on that foundation and with an eye on Leonida's organic world, campaigns can be created where we feel our influence isn't rigidly predetermined but rather gradually constructed on both a macro and micro level.

It's difficult to anticipate the rapid arrival of a structure with such ramifications due to the predominance of independent developers in the genre, though simply having the horizon in sight is enough to begin moving towards it.

Ever since I played The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim, I've realized that progression in video games is almost always extradiegetic—that is, it happens before the player's eyes, not so much the character's—and GTA 6 also rejected this principle.

Consequently, "finding" a skill instead of developing it is a statement in itself about how a character grows, which is quite functional for the Metroidvania philosophy but not so much for the feeling of evolving through our gameplay experience.

I doubt it's interesting to lock the double jump behind having to go to the gym and do jumping jacks for a whole week, yet a game where our combat and movement skills expand based on how we interact with its base mechanics is a future that excites me.

GTA 6 makes it very simple, and sometimes it's even annoying, but the fact that it works here for the sake of realism doesn't mean that Metroidvanias can't use the same methodology for the sake of fun.

In Metroidvanias, there's no such thing as approaches: you either play the way the game wants you to, or you simply have to give up on the possibility of finishing it altogether.

GTA 6, inevitably, is also a slave to determinism, yet it leans slightly towards immersive sim design, allowing you to choose how you're going to fulfill your unchangeable obligations.

There are fights you can avoid with dialogue, wars you can replace with stealthy takedowns, and conflicts you can relocate with distraction tactics, thanks in part to a level design that offers more than one way to handle its execution.

I would never ask a Metroidvania to be Dishonored, though I could respectfully request that it at least try to integrate the possibility that not every challenge is solved by depleting a boss's health bar or completing an artificial platforming challenge.

I'd be lying if I didn't say I'm quite a fan of the true ending trope hidden behind an obscure side mission that unfolds outside the main campaign, but it does sadden me that it's the only option Metroidvanias often display.

While GTA 6 emphasizes numerous decisions, circumstances, and systems that occur at pivotal points in the plot and throughout the game, Metroidvanias have a much more reductive model.

Regardless of how you play, games in this genre tend to follow the formula of "good ending, bad ending by changing a decision before the final boss, true ending behind an optional mission", and it's starting to lose its appeal.

Certainly, GTA 6's approach isn't necessarily revolutionary or life-changing, yet it's complex enough to make you think about how you interact with its systems, so it more than achieves its goal.

Of course, for there to be impactful decisions to make, it's necessary to apply several of the fundamentals mentioned in this list, as they can't be achieved in isolation. However, since Rockstar's game will surely be the benchmark for many years to come, I'm sure some lessons will sink in.
