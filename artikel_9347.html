Study: Menstrual Cycle Irregularities Linked to ADHD, Depression, Anxiety in Girls

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Menstrual cycle irregularities, like severe menstrual pain and premenstrual symptoms, are associated with mental health conditions in adolescent girls, according to a new study.

Several menstrual cycle characteristics, including timing of menarche, cycle regularity, menstrual pain, and severity of premenstrual symptoms, are associated with more common and severe symptoms of depression, anxiety, and ADHD in adolescent girls, according to a recent study published in European Child & Adolescent Psychiatry.1

The longitudinal study followed 5,673 adolescent girls, ages 9 to 11, over a seven-year period, with annual assessments up to ages 14 to 17.

Study participants self-reported the severity of premenstrual symptoms, such as irritability and fatigue, and the intensity of period pain (categorized as mild, moderate, or severe). Findings showed that severe menstrual pain was associated with higher anxiety, depression, and ADHD symptoms.

Premenstrual symptoms demonstrated a graded pattern in their association with mental health outcomes. Mild premenstrual symptoms were linked to higher anxiety symptom scores. Moderate premenstrual symptoms were linked to higher anxiety and depression symptom scores. Severe premenstrual symptoms were linked to higher anxiety, depression, and ADHD symptom scores.

The researchers offered several social and biological factors as potential explanations for these results.

“Psychosocially, menstrual pain and [premenstrual symptoms] may result in functional impairment – including missed school, disrupted sleep, reduced social participation – which may contribute to emotional distress,” they wrote.

The study explained that intense period pain and premenstrual distress may activate inflammatory and pain-sensitivity pathways, increasing vulnerability to anxiety and depression. Researchers also noted that the neurobiological mechanisms for processing pain may overlap with those involved in attention and focus, which could help explain the connection between menstrual pain and ADHD symptoms.

Researchers also suggested that fluctuating estrogen and progesterone may help to explain the connection between severe premenstrual symptoms and higher anxiety, depression, and ADHD symptom scores. These hormones heavily affect serotonin, dopamine, and GABA in the brain. Serotonin is linked to mood; dopamine controls motivation and reward processing; and GABA is crucial for emotional processing and regulation. When hormones fluctuate before a menstrual cycle, these neurotransmitters are impacted too, which, in turn, may influence symptoms of anxiety, depression, and ADHD.

The relationship between ADHD and hormonal fluctuations is a pressing subject for many ADDitude readers with lived experience. In an ADDitude survey of nearly 2,000 women with ADHD, almost one in two respondents said that hormonal fluctuations had a “life-altering” or “severe” impact on their ADHD.

“I know that the week before my period I am a complete mess,” Addison, an ADDitude reader, said. “I have to prepare for the fact that my world feels like it’s falling apart emotionally, and I hate the way that I look the most that week. These are weeks I often avoid being with people, as I know I am not the most positive and am the most sensitive.”

“I used to have lots of irritability, food cravings, tiredness, and was fuzzy-headed three to four days before my period started,” said Connie, another reader.

“I suffer from symptoms like uncontrolled fits of anger and crying spells and feeling extremely overwhelmed the days before my period. I’m even more moody, irritable and have more trouble concentrating,” said ADDitude reader Elena.

Moreover, women with ADHD are disproportionately affected by premenstrual disorders with more than 40% of women with ADHD experiencing premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) compared to 3% to 9% of women overall.2, 3 PMDD is also heavily tied to hormonal shifts and mental health.

“PMDD is caused by a high sensitivity to fluctuations in estrogen and progesterone, as well as decreases in serotonin – hormonal changes that happen during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle,” wrote Dara Abraham, D.O. in an ADDitude article. “During the luteal phase that follows ovulation, estrogen decreases, as do dopamine, serotonin, and eventually progesterone. These changes cause painful and distressing symptoms until the menstruation begins and hormone levels rise again.”

The study also found that irregular menstrual cycles were linked to higher depression and anxiety symptom scores. The longer a girl had been having her menstrual cycles, the stronger the link between irregular periods and depression and anxiety symptoms became. Likewise, earlier age at menarche was associated with higher depression symptom scores.

While the study focused specifically on early age at menarche, previous research suggests that early puberty more broadly may have an association to ADHD.

“Researchers are examining why girls with ADHD have about twice the risk for early puberty, and why autistic girls are up to four times more likely to experience early puberty compared to other children,” wrote Molly Nikolas, Ph.D. in an ADDitude article. “In addition to higher rates of obesity and social stress, which could accelerate the onset of puberty, neurodivergent girls may experience differences in dopamine activity, stress response, and immune and neuroendocrine systems — all of which may affect sexual development.”

The researchers listed several limitations to the study. Menstrual cycle characteristics were gathered through an annual self-report, and parents reported mental health symptoms. In addition, the relationship between menstrual characteristics and mental health symptoms may be bidirectional. The researchers also reported the associations were modest and that future studies are needed to further explore the potential social and biological mechanisms involved.

The study’s authors said they hope their findings will help healthcare professionals consider menstrual cycle characteristics as potentially meaningful insight when evaluating the mental health of adolescent girls.
