‘Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping’ Soundtrack: The Best Music Moments From Haymitch Abernathy’s Prequel Movie

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

The “Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping” album is an absolute behemoth befitting the blockbuster movie.

With 29 entries ranging from in-movie needle drops and ditties written for the dystopian movie to existing songs remixed for the occasion, it’s longer than any previous “Hunger Games” soundtrack album. (“Mockingjay Part 2” leads with 19 tracks, “Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes” clocks in at 17 songs, the original “Hunger Games” logs 16, and “Catching Fire” and “Mockingjay Part 1” both have 15.)

Noah Kahan joins Taylor Swift, Coldplay, Lorde and Olivia Rodrigo in a starry lineup of artists who have voiced the lead single for one of the franchise’s films. Kahan’s folk-Americana style blends into the rest of the soundtrack, which runs the gamut from country to indie. The soundtrack also includes songs from the movie’s stars, Mckenna Grace, Whitney Peak and Katniss Everdeen herself, Jennifer Lawrence.

Writing lyrics for characters with lived experiences different from your own (i.e., fighting to the death in a televised cage match against other children) is no small task. Many of the “Sunrise on the Reaping” tracks capture the struggles of Haymitch Abernathy’s (Joseph Zada) games and the overarching resistance movement in Suzanne Collins’ franchise. Others are good, but could have been left on the cutting room floor (or saved for the artist’s own project).

If listening to nearly 30 songs seems daunting, Variety picked the 12 best moments that Hunger Games fans must dive into.

I imagine this is what most District 12 tributes think when their name is called on Reaping Day. Perhaps during the Quarter Quell Games that Haymitch enters, with twice as many tributes to face, the fight to the finish is even fiercer. And let’s not forget that classic Abernathy wisdom: “Nobody ever wins the Games, period. There are survivors … there’s no winners.” Stephen Wilson Jr.’s voice has a hint of twang and enough rasp to give this song the grittiness the situation demands; the bridge’s refrain of “I’ll keep running” builds into a final repetition with a desperate cry into an edgy final chorus that hammers that “last place” refrain until the end.

This song feels inspired by Haymitch’s (Zada) promise to Lenore Dove (Whitney Peak) that he will eventually see the sun rise on his birthday without a Reaping. It also rings of Wiress, whose mentorship centered around the idea that “all machines can be broken,” and the role the other rebellious Victors played in Katniss’ eventual success in ending the Hunger Games for good. And Lizzy McAlpine nails the right tone of rebellion here, which doesn’t hurt either. Her voice bends, rather than breaks, to steal some of this track’s lyrics, which deftly handles the shift between the angstier chorus and the arching vocals of the verses.

Sydney Rose clearly knows “Sunrise on the Reaping.” This is the song that perhaps most directly and accurately references the text: The mention of Lenore Dove’s geese with the birds chirping on the backing track, the boy “sent to slaughter,” the phone line the lovers speak through while Haymitch is in the Capitol, the liquor he finds respite in after the “biggest loss” that comes after thinking he had finally escaped the Arena. This song’s strength lies partly in its accuracy to the story and also in how gentle Rose’s vocals are over these lyrics, down to the devastating final line. It’s haunting, beautiful and it’s Haymitch mourning his lost love, sung with an angelic lilt to boot.

This was the first song we got from the soundtrack, and while it’s good, it’s not Noah Kahan at his best — if that tells you anything about the strength of Kahan’s latest projects. That said, “Draw You Out” is beautiful and warbling, and most of all, it really nails what Haymitch must have been feeling after returning from surviving the Games only to lose his closest loved ones, too. Wrap that up with an Aaron Dessner production bow on top, and it’s the perfect song for the movie’s end credits. This song’s shining moment is its first verse. It’s beautifully wandering, and Kahan’s lyrical talent is undeniable on lines like “I’m in a song with one descendin’ note” and “I’ve been feelin’ like a wing-clipped bird.”

This track’s cinematic opening is beautiful, as are the lyrics, which poetically capture grief. I’m particularly partial to the line “Moments were stolen, put in the frame,” which lends itself to a prevalent theme throughout “Sunrise on the Reaping”: personal moments, like Haymitch’s goodbye to his family, being used for Capitol propaganda. It’s angelic, haunting and near operatic. The lyrics are succinct and effective, perfectly balanced between the “la la la” refrains and tugging on the listener’s heartstrings with lines like “Silt in the meadow, salt in your eyes.”

How can you not appreciate the work of the ultimate fangirl? From her press tour looks to her franchise knowledge to her portrayal of Maysilee (and Merrilee) Donner, Mckenna Grace has been at the top of her game. It somehow only gets better, with the second verse featuring the sucker-punch lyric “I was a sister once, I carry that love closely” and the warbling, defiant vocals of a girl who won’t be bowed on the final chorus and bridge. This is a character song executed to perfection by the actor in the role, and the overlapping dialogue that concludes the track is the brutal cherry on top.

This tune dances around the film’s title, with lyrics of “Open eyes are for the weeping/Let the sun rise, just for the weekend.” The haunting quality of Violet Grohl’s croon settles nicely on lyrics about Haymitch’s struggle to break the power generator in the Arena and his defiance when looking up at the “metal birds” coming to take fallen Tributes away. The vocals are patient, the lyrics are simple and poetic. There’s a dissonance in Grohl’s delivery and a gentle brutality in each line that makes this track an unassuming knockout.

Madeline Edwards’ song echoes elements of others that I like (i.e., the Haydove spirit of “Dove Song” combined with the grit of “First in Line”). I love the concept of Haymitch going toe-to-toe with Snow in a “wrestling with god” moment. And the contrast between the growl in Edwards’ voice on the final chorus and the gentler outro is incredible. The instrumental builds behind the bridge until she finally hits the growling final chorus and takes a deserved moment to rock out, giving this song an edge that she then reins in with the outro’s soft landing.

This District 12 ditty comes at a particularly emotional moment during the film’s final act, and it makes me cry every time. The only thing that hits harder than this rendition is the TikTok-edited version stitching it to duet Rachel Zegler’s take from “Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes.” Where hers is biting, this one is solemn but equally strong. Scot Greenan’s voice bears the kind of mournful restraint the song deserves in its scene, which lends this song the perfect near-tears tension that is bound to make any listener’s eyes well up when this track plays mid-album.

This is the darker sister song to Sydney Rose’s “Dove Song.” It’s more mournful, but it also has the beautiful references to the dove that Haymitch can’t let go. Carter Faith’s gentle twang adds to that heartachey feel with an Americana edge the “Hunger Games” franchise does so well. Faith effortlessly toes the line between classic country and lyrics that are undoubtedly rooted in “Sunrise on the Reaping.” Take a line like “I can taste you in the shine,” which nails the textual references to Haymitch’s drinking with a delightful vocal flip from Faith.

Another song with great “Hunger Games” lore woven into the lyrics. Reading between the lines, I see parts of it as a take on what Merrilee might have said before sending Maysilee to (almost) certain death in the Arena. “Who will I be when you leave me?” as a chorus to Maysilee asking Haymitch if Merrilee will still be a twin after she dies in the Games. Bella Kay’s mournful performance makes this one what it is: sweet high notes, tense vibrato on the post-chorus, plus she goes big on the bridge with the gut-wrenching lyrics of “Who will I be when it burns down?/You and I are out of control now/I don’t know now.”

To pull an old Stevie Nicks song for this ‘70s-inspired film is, well, inspired. Borrowing from Nicks’ discography makes perfect sense, though this 1989 track doesn’t have a novel take on the franchise. But still, it works in a way that emphasizes this franchise’s timelessness. I mean, “Fate calls this fortune and fortune takes it away” is just “May the odds be ever in your favor” to a different tune. And honestly, I’d accept more from Nicks and Fleetwood Mac on the soundtrack. “Hunger Games” to the tune of “The Chain,” anyone?
