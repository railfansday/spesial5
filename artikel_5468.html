From Gates to Gelsinger: 9 CEO exits and how investors read each one

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A look at what happens to a stock when the CEO quits, through nine exits from Bill Gates at Microsoft to Pat Gelsinger at Intel

A CEO exit is a message, and investors have to decode it before they can price it. The same news can signal a planned handoff to a longtime deputy, a founder moving into a new role or a board that has run out of patience. What happens to a stock when the CEO quits depends on which of those stories the market believes it is reading.

Several variables shape that reading. The first is where the successor comes from. Tim Cook at Apple $AAPL, Steve Ballmer at Microsoft $MSFT, Andy Jassy at Amazon $AMZN and Parag Agrawal at Twitter $TWTR were all insiders. Cook had been chief operating officer, Jassy ran Amazon Web Services and Agrawal was chief technology officer. Brian Niccol, who left Chipotle $CMG to run Starbucks $SBUX, was an outside hire.

The second variable is whether the departing leader really leaves. Bill Gates stayed at Microsoft as chairman and took the title chief software architect. Steve Jobs became Apple's chairman. Jeff Bezos moved to executive chairman at Amazon. Each arrangement kept a founder close to the company while someone else held the CEO title.

The third is the circumstance of the exit. Dave Calhoun announced in March 2024 that he would leave Boeing $BA at the end of the year. That announcement followed the January 2024 door-plug blowout on an Alaska Airlines 737 MAX 9. Pat Gelsinger left Intel $INTC in December 2024 following pressure from the board. Intel named Lip-Bu Tan CEO in March 2025. Disney $DIS's board removed Bob Chapek in November 2022 and brought back Bob Iger, who had handed him the job in February 2020.

Outside events can cloud the picture. Jeff Immelt took over General Electric $GE from Jack Welch days before the September 11, 2001 attacks. That timing makes it hard to separate investors' view of the handover from the shock that hit the entire market.

A leadership change can also touch more than one company. On August 13, 2024, Starbucks said Laxman Narasimhan was out and Niccol would take the top job. Starbucks shares rose sharply that day. Chipotle shares fell.

This is a selection of nine exits from 2000 to 2025, not a complete or ranked list. The slides run in chronological order, starting with Microsoft and ending with Intel. Each one looks at the conditions investors faced when the news broke and what came next for the company's leadership.

Microsoft $MSFT announced on January 13, 2000, that Bill Gates would hand the chief executive job to Steve Ballmer. Gates did not leave the company. He stayed on as chairman and took a new title, chief software architect.

That arrangement shaped how investors could read the news. A founder who gives up the CEO seat but keeps the chairman's role still holds a formal position at the top of the company. The chief software architect title pointed to where Gates would focus: technology and product direction rather than day-to-day management.

The choice of successor carried its own message. Ballmer came from inside Microsoft rather than from another company. An internal pick gives shareholders a leader the board already knows. An outside hire, by contrast, arrives without a track record inside the business.

The new structure divided responsibilities that had sat with one person. Ballmer took on the duties of running the company. Gates kept a hand in governance through the board and in technology through his new title. For shareholders, the change was less a founder walking away than a redrawing of who handled what.

The split also raised a question the announcement itself could not settle. Investors had to weigh how much authority Ballmer would hold while the founder still chaired the board and set technical direction.

Timing complicates any reading of the market's response. The announcement came as the dot-com boom was nearing its peak. Technology shares at that point were driven by forces much larger than one company's leadership plans. That backdrop makes it hard to isolate how investors judged the handover itself.

The Microsoft case shows one form of CEO succession: a change in title as much as a change in people. The founder stepped back from running the company without stepping out of it. That is a different event from a founder's full departure.

Jack Welch's retirement from General Electric $GE in September 2001 shows why a stock's reaction to a CEO handover cannot always be measured. Jeff Immelt took over days before the September 11, 2001 attacks. The market shock that followed makes the response to the leadership change impossible to separate from everything else.

Welch left after about two decades as GE's chief executive. A tenure that long changes what a succession means to shareholders. They are not just trading one executive for another. They are weighing how the company will run without the leader who led it for that long.

Welch's departure was a retirement, not a firing or a sudden resignation. A retirement is a planned event. That gives it a different character from a board-driven removal or an exit forced by a crisis.

The calendar is what sets this handover apart. Immelt had been in the job only days when the attacks took place. Whatever investors made of the new CEO, their view was quickly overtaken by a much larger event.

That creates a measurement problem. A clean read on a CEO change needs a quiet window around the transition. In that window, the leadership news is the main new information reaching investors. GE had no such window in September 2001.

Any move in GE shares in the weeks after Immelt took over carried the weight of the attacks and the wider market shock. A chart of the stock from that period cannot show how much of the movement belonged to the succession itself.

The GE case illustrates a broader point about reading CEO departures. A share price responds to all of the news at once, not to one headline at a time. When a leadership change lands alongside a market-wide event, the effect of the new CEO cannot be pulled apart from the rest.

Steve Jobs resigned as Apple $AAPL's chief executive on August 24, 2011, and Tim Cook, then the company's chief operating officer, took over the role. Jobs stayed on as chairman, a structure that kept his name attached to the company's leadership after the handover.

In the Apple case, the successor's résumé was part of the news. Cook was not an outside hire. He came from inside the company, from the job that oversees Apple's day-to-day operations.

That background shapes how shareholders can interpret a leadership change. An internal pick from the operating side can signal that a board favors continuity over a new strategy. A chief operating officer usually already works closely with a company's suppliers, product schedules and senior staff.

An outside hire can send a different message. It can suggest the board wants something to change. Apple's choice pointed the other way.

The chairman role added a second layer. Jobs giving up the CEO title did not mean he left the boardroom. As chairman, he led the board that oversees the chief executive. Shareholders could read that as a gradual transfer rather than a clean break.

That arrangement did not last long. Jobs died on October 5, 2011, about six weeks after his resignation. His death ended the chairman arrangement. Cook was left to lead without his predecessor on the board.

The short gap between the two events complicates any attempt to read the market's view of Cook on its own. The resignation and the death were separate pieces of news. Investors had to weigh each one as they judged Apple's future without Jobs.

What the episode shows is a sequence of conditions investors had to weigh. The successor was an insider. The departing leader stayed on as chairman. Then a second piece of news arrived before the new arrangement could settle.

Disney $DIS's board removed Bob Chapek as CEO on November 20, 2022, and brought back Bob Iger, the executive Chapek had succeeded. The reversal came less than three years after Iger handed Chapek the top job on February 25, 2020.

The two changes reached investors in very different forms. The first was a planned handoff, with the outgoing CEO passing the role to his chosen successor. A scheduled transfer like that frames the news around continuity rather than disruption.

The second came from the board, not from a CEO deciding to step aside. A board-driven removal is a separate category of exit from a planned succession. It tells shareholders that directors took the leadership question into their own hands.

The choice of replacement also shaped how the news could be read. The board did not search for an outside hire or promote a new executive. It reinstated Iger, whose record as Disney's CEO investors had already watched.

A returning CEO arrives with an existing reputation inside the company and on Wall Street. Shareholders weighing the 2022 decision were assessing a leader they had seen in the role before, not a newcomer.

A return leaves one question unanswered. Reinstating a former chief executive fills the top job, but it does not settle who will lead the company next. The search for a long-term successor, which the 2020 handoff had appeared to resolve, was open again.

The two dates bracket Chapek's entire run as Disney's chief executive. Within this selection of nine exits, Disney is the only case in which a former CEO came back to replace his own successor.

The sequence also shows that one succession can produce two separate moments for investors. The 2020 handoff and the 2022 reversal each presented a new leadership arrangement to assess, under different circumstances and for different reasons.

Jeff Bezos announced on February 2, 2021 that he would step down as Amazon $AMZN's CEO and become executive chairman. Andy Jassy, who ran Amazon Web Services, took over as chief executive on July 5, 2021.

The timing of the announcement shaped how investors could read it. Amazon released the leadership news alongside its fourth-quarter 2020 results. The succession plan and a full set of quarterly numbers reached the market at the same moment.

That pairing makes any stock reaction to the CEO departure hard to isolate. Quarterly figures and a change at the top are separate pieces of news. Investors weigh both during the same trading session. The share price then reflects one combined response rather than a separate verdict on each.

The role Bezos took on was the second signal. An executive chairman leads the board of directors and keeps an active hand in the company. Bezos founded Amazon in 1994. The new title meant the founder was stepping back from daily management without leaving. For shareholders, the person most closely tied to the company stayed inside its governance structure.

The choice of successor was the third signal. Jassy was an internal pick rather than an outside hire. He had led Amazon Web Services, the company's cloud computing business. That made the new CEO someone who had already run one of Amazon's major operations.

The length of the handover was the fourth signal. About five months separated the February announcement from Jassy's first day as CEO on July 5. That gap gave the board, employees and investors a defined transition period with a fixed end date.

Taken together, the early announcement, the founder's continuing board role and the internal promotion pointed to a planned succession rather than a forced exit. None of those features decides on its own how a stock trades. They shape the information investors have when they make that decision.

Jack Dorsey stepped down as Twitter $TWTR's CEO on November 29, 2021, and Parag Agrawal, the company's chief technology officer, took his place. The clearest price shareholders later received came from a takeover agreement, not from the leadership change itself.

The handover kept the top job inside the company. Agrawal was already a senior Twitter executive when he was promoted. With an internal pick, investors are judging someone who already knows the company's products and engineering. The open question is how that person will run the whole business.

A CTO in the top seat also says something about priorities. The chief technology officer oversees the systems a product is built on. Picking that executive puts a technical leader in charge of a consumer platform. It does not, on its own, tell investors what strategy will follow. That answer would come from his first decisions as chief executive.

The larger price event came later. Elon Musk agreed to buy Twitter for $54.20 per share. The deal was valued at roughly $44 billion. It closed in October 2022, less than a year after Dorsey left the CEO role.

That timing shortened the window for judging Agrawal on his own record. Once a sale price was on the table, the stock's moves were tied more to the deal's prospects than to the new CEO's choices.

A takeover works differently from an ordinary trading day. Buyer and seller agree on a fixed price for each share. When the deal closes, shareholders receive that price, whatever the stock had been doing before.

The two events are separate. Dorsey's resignation did not set the $54.20 figure, and the order of events does not show that one caused the other. Twitter's case shows a limit of judging a CEO exit by later share prices. A takeover can set the final number shareholders receive, whether or not it has anything to do with the succession.

Dave Calhoun announced on March 25, 2024 that he would leave Boeing $BA at the end of 2024. The decision came after the January 2024 door-plug blowout on an Alaska Airlines 737 MAX 9. That sequence made it a crisis-linked departure rather than a routine retirement.

The distinction matters for anyone trying to read the market's response. A planned handover asks investors one question: is the successor ready? A crisis exit asks two. Shareholders have to weigh the damage from the incident itself. They also have to judge whether a change at the top can repair it.

The order of events in Boeing's case shows how tangled those questions become. The blowout came first, in January. Calhoun's announcement followed in March. Any move in Boeing shares over those months would have reflected both the incident and the leadership news. Separating one from the other is not straightforward.

Calhoun also chose not to leave at once. He set his exit for the end of the year, months after the announcement. A long runway of that kind can signal an orderly transition. It also means the outgoing chief keeps running the company while investors wait.

That waiting period carries its own uncertainty. Until a successor takes over, shareholders cannot size up the person who will lead the recovery. They are pricing a departure without yet knowing what replaces it.

Kelly Ortberg started as Boeing's CEO in August 2024. That was months ahead of the end-of-year date Calhoun had given in March. Ortberg took charge about five months after Calhoun's announcement.

For investors, the Boeing case is a reminder that a CEO's stated exit date is a plan, not a guarantee. A successor can arrive well before the outgoing chief's announced departure. The stock's reaction to the original news is only part of the story.

On August 13, 2024, Starbucks $SBUX announced that Laxman Narasimhan was out as CEO and that Brian Niccol, then the chief executive of Chipotle $CMG, would take the top job. Starbucks shares rose sharply that day, and Chipotle shares fell.

That split makes this leadership change unusual. A single announcement involved two publicly traded companies. Their stocks moved in opposite directions on the same trading day.

A second company was involved because of where the successor came from. Many CEO changes stay inside one business. A board promotes an executive from within or names an interim leader while it searches. In this case, the incoming chief executive was already running another listed company.

The hire and the departure were therefore one event. Starbucks gained a new CEO, and Chipotle lost its sitting one in the same announcement.

For Starbucks shareholders, the news carried two pieces of information at once. Narasimhan was leaving, and his replacement was already named. There was no interim arrangement and no open-ended search.

For Chipotle shareholders, the same news arrived as a CEO departure. Their company's chief executive was moving to another restaurant chain. Chipotle's board now had its own succession question to settle.

Starbucks did not promote an insider to fill the role. Its board went outside the company and chose a leader who held the top job elsewhere in the restaurant industry. That choice is what tied a second stock to the news.

The case shows that the stock reaction to a CEO departure is not always confined to the company making the change. When the successor comes from another public company, investors in both businesses face a leadership change on the same day.

Pat Gelsinger left Intel $INTC on December 1, 2024, after pressure from the company's board. Intel did not name a permanent successor at the time. David Zinsner and Michelle Johnston Holthaus took over as interim co-CEOs.

A board-driven exit sends a different signal from a planned retirement. A planned retirement typically arrives with a successor chosen and a handover date set. A departure under board pressure tells investors that directors wanted a change at the top. It also shows they moved before a replacement was ready.

Other boards in recent years paired a removal with a named successor. When Starbucks $SBUX pushed out Laxman Narasimhan, it named Brian Niccol in the same announcement. When Disney $DIS's board removed Bob Chapek, it brought back Bob Iger at the same time. Intel's board announced Gelsinger's exit with no permanent replacement attached.

The interim structure reflects that gap. An interim title means the job is held temporarily while a search runs. Splitting the role between two executives adds a second question. Shareholders had to judge who was setting direction and for how long.

The interim arrangement ran from December 2024 into March 2025. That month, Intel named Lip-Bu Tan as CEO. The appointment returned the company from a temporary setup to a single permanent leader.

For anyone tracking a stock's reaction to a CEO departure, the Intel case contains three separate moments rather than one. The first is the exit itself. The second is the stretch under co-CEOs. The third is the naming of Tan as permanent CEO. Each step gave investors new information about who would run the company.

The sequence also shows how the cause of an exit shapes the way it is read. Intel's path ran from board pressure to shared interim leadership to a permanent appointment. That order points to a rethink of the company's leadership, not a planned handoff.
