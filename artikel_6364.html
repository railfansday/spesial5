Where children live may influence how well they focus

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Children in greener neighborhoods scored slightly higher on attention tests, suggesting nearby trees and parks may play a role in focus.

Children often do better on attention tasks after a walk in the park or time in a school garden.

But researchers have had mixed answers about whether living near trees and parks is also linked to better focus.

A large study of American children now finds a small connection between neighborhood greenery and attention.

Links with reading and vocabulary were less clear after researchers accounted for family income.

Carina Fowler led the study at Washington University in St. Louis. She is a postdoctoral researcher in the lab of Deanna Barch, a professor in the Department of Psychological and Brain Sciences.

“If a child lives closer to green space, and has more access to green space, does this help them pay attention on demand when it’s required, and do they do better at school on achievement tests?” Fowler said.

The researchers explored an idea called attention restoration theory. It suggests that nature gives the mind a break from the effort of staying focused.

The theory describes two kinds of attention: one we use to concentrate on a task, and another that responds automatically to things around us.

Phones, TVs and traffic can grab that automatic attention completely, while trees and parks may hold it gently enough to let the mind wander.

This may give our ability to concentrate a chance to recover.

Fowler said earlier research suggests people dislike having nothing to see or hear, or sitting alone with their thoughts in a quiet room.

“It seems people need something to be occupying them a little bit – but not too much – and that nature can provide that really well,” Fowler said.

The team used data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study, which recruited children near 21 sites across the continental United States.

The analysis included 11,868 children who joined between 2016 and 2018, aged nine or 10.

For each child’s home, the team studied the surrounding census tract, an area used in the national census that is roughly the size of a neighborhood.

Satellite images showed how much land trees covered.

Records checked by the Trust for Public Land showed how much land was parkland. The researchers averaged the tree and park percentages to give each child a green space score.

Across the group, the combined score averaged about 11%.

The children took five short tests that involved remembering things, switching between rules, ignoring distractions and processing information quickly.

The team combined the results into an attention score.

The score used a scale similar to an IQ test, with 100 in the middle and a typical spread of about 15 points.

Reading and vocabulary tests produced a second score that gave a rough picture of school skills.

The team looked at each child at one point in time. That means the results can show a connection, but cannot prove that greenery caused higher scores.

Children’s focus depends on many things. The researchers considered sex and how well off each neighborhood was.

They also included ADHD, stimulant medicines, sleep problems, weekly screen time and tobacco exposure before birth.

Other checks covered air pollution, physical activity and whether children felt safe from crime in their neighborhood.

According to the authors, no earlier study of green space and attention had included children’s own sense of safety.

“You have to feel safe in the green space to have an effect,” Fowler said.

After those checks, a 10-percentage-point increase in green space was linked to an attention score 0.76 points higher. Reading and vocabulary scores were 0.83 points higher.

A 10-percentage-point rise would mean going from 10% to 20%, for example.

The score differences were small: less than one point on a scale with a typical spread of 15 points.

Many children had little greenery nearby. About half lived in areas with a green space score below 10%, and about one in 10 had a score below 1%.

The authors suggested that even a small increase in greenery might matter in those places. Attention and school skills at this age can help predict later health, earnings and education.

Greener neighborhoods tend to have cleaner air, and studies have linked air pollution to attention problems. The team checked whether tiny pollution particles could explain some of the results.

These particles measure no more than about one ten-thousandth of an inch across, or 2.5 micrometers.

Lower particle pollution explained a small part of the link with both scores, which surprised the authors because they had expected it to explain none.

“There is something to the idea that air quality is partially explaining why green space is helping our attention,” Fowler said.

Exercise did not explain the link. Children in greener areas did not report more days with at least an hour of activity, although more active children scored higher on both measures.

The team then added family income to the analysis, which already included how well off the neighborhood was.

The link between greenery and attention became about half as large, but it remained.

The link with reading and vocabulary was no longer statistically significant. This makes it harder to tell how much greenery itself might matter for those skills.

The authors offered two possible explanations. Green space may have a weaker link with school skills, or it may help focus sooner while any effect on learning takes years to build.

The attention score combined several skills, so it was not a test of attention alone.

The school skills score covered reading and vocabulary, leaving out math, science and writing.

The study also included relatively few rural children because most of its brain-imaging centers are in cities.

And the team measured greenery at only one point in each child’s life, so it could not show what happens after a move to a greener area.

The authors suggested testing whether greener school grounds help children learn.

They also raised the possibility that keeping children inside during recess as punishment for poor focus or grades could make those problems worse.

Both ideas need testing, as does whether planting trees near homes or schools improves attention.

Experiments and studies that follow children over many years could help answer those questions.

The study is published in the journal Environment International.
