Astronomers discover a ghostly green star that shouldn’t exist

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Astronomers have identified a ghostly green white dwarf, along with dead stars that appear pink, purple, yellow, and cyan, challenging expectations about stellar colors.

Stars typically glow in shades of red, orange, white, or blue, with their colors revealing their temperatures. Even burned-out stars, known as white dwarfs, were thought to follow this rule.

But astronomers have discovered something extraordinary: a dead star that would appear green to the human eye.

The unusual color comes from sodium in the star’s atmosphere, which absorbs orange light and leaves behind a ghostly green glow.

Astronomers say no green star of any kind has ever been credibly reported.

The team’s research also revealed white dwarfs that would appear pink, purple, yellow, and cyan, with each unusual color linked to the chemical makeup of the star’s atmosphere.

Mark Hollands, a Warwick Prize Fellow in the Astronomy and Astrophysics Group at the University of Warwick, led the work.

Boris Gänsicke and Pier-Emmanuel Tremblay at Warwick, along with Detlev Koester at Kiel University in Germany, were his co-authors.

No white dwarf is close enough for its color to be seen directly. Instead, astronomers calculate the color from the star’s light, and the green star’s measurement is a noisy one.

A white dwarf is what’s left when a star like the Sun runs out of fuel and its core is all that remains. The coolest ones have atmospheres about as dense as water, and that density is the key.

Like a piece of iron heated to increasingly high temperatures, ordinary stars glow red, orange, or white depending on how hot they are.

Bright stars like Betelgeuse look redder than their neighbors, but they stay inside that range.

“Most stars, including most white dwarfs, behave like ‘perfect radiators,’ so their color is set almost entirely by temperature. But nobody had really looked into what else might be possible for white dwarfs,” Hollands said.

In an atmosphere that dense, a trace of carbon, calcium, or sodium absorbs light across a wider band of colors than it would in a thin gas.

That absorption can give a star colors that its temperature alone cannot explain.

A star’s spectrum is its light split by wavelength, the way a prism splits sunlight into a rainbow. Astronomers record spectra routinely.

The team ran spectra from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey and from their own atmosphere models through a mathematical description of human color vision that dates from 1931.

Each spectrum came out as the color a computer screen would need to show it.

The one thing a screen can’t show is brightness, because a real star’s surface is millions of times brighter than any monitor.

In an interview with Earth.com, Hollands said the calculation is more direct than a photograph would be.

Research telescopes record light through filters chosen for science rather than to match the eye, so their color images are false color.

A consumer camera on a research telescope could in principle show the same colors the team calculated, he said, but the spectrum already holds all of the star’s visible light.

“So this is the most accurate way to determine these colors short of actually going to visit these stars, which sadly won’t be happening at any time soon!” Hollands said.

The green star is WD J1330+6435, with a surface at about 7,200°F (4,000°C). Its helium atmosphere carries calcium, lithium, and sodium.

Those are the leftovers of asteroids or comets from its own planetary system, the same kind of debris seen falling onto other dead stars.

“The traces of sodium in the atmosphere of this white dwarf absorb the orange part of the spectrum, leaving behind a ghostly green glow,” Hollands said.

Calcium removes the violet as well, so the light that remains peaks near 500 nanometers. In the team’s model the star comes out chartreuse, which is a yellowish green.

Of all the unusual colors revealed by the research, one stood out to Hollands more than any other: the chartreuse green of WD J1330+6435.

“Green is generally considered an implausible colour for a star to have,” Hollands told Earth.com.

What would the sky look like from a planet around the star?

On an Earth-like world it would probably still be pale blue, in a slightly different shade, Hollands said. The star’s green would show up on you instead.

“If you were wearing a white shirt (or space-suit), then you would see your outfit illuminated the pale green color that we determined from its spectrum,” he said.

Two other white dwarfs in the sample came out a much paler green.

A cousin of the green star, WD J2356-2054, carries so much sodium that almost no light escapes from the middle of the spectrum.

At the same temperature, it comes out a vivid blue.

At the other extreme is WD J1011+2845, just 48 light-years away, with a surface near 7,300°F (4,000°C).

Movement within the star has carried carbon from its interior to the surface. There, the carbon forms molecules that absorb most of the blue, green, and yellow light.

Almost all of its visible light comes out beyond 600 nanometers, at the red end, and the result is a deep, salmon pink.

The team called it likely the reddest star known in terms of perceived color.

Among 36 carbon-rich white dwarfs the team examined, the cooler ones mostly came out in shades of pink.

Stars like it are hard to model, and a strong magnetic field may be distorting this one’s light in ways that the models don’t fully capture.

Hydrogen can tint a cool white dwarf as well, Hollands said. A trace of it forms H2 molecules, and their collisions with the dense helium can leave the star looking cyan or turquoise.

To find out how common the oddities are, the team took every white dwarf within 65 light-years (20 parsecs) of the Sun, 152 stars in all.

Five had colors too far from a heated-iron glow to be assigned a temperature: two yellow and three pink.

Five of 152 is about 3.4 percent, or roughly one in 30. For the rest, the team measured the color temperature, the scale printed on light bulb boxes, where 3,000 kelvin is warm white and 6,000 is cool white.

The median came out just under 6,800 kelvin, which the eye sees as white.

Gänsicke said the result corrects a habit.

“One in thirty might not sound like a huge fraction, but across the entire population of white dwarfs in the Universe, that is still a lot of oddly colored stars out there,” he said.

White dwarfs are usually drawn blue because the hot, blue ones are the brightest and show up most often in surveys. The nearby sample has no such bias. Within it, 95 percent of the stars fall between 3,800 and 18,700 kelvin.

The green star and the blue one are the same kind of object with different amounts of sodium.

The team therefore expects to find other white dwarfs with colors ranging from green to cyan and blue.

No other star quite like the green one is known yet, Hollands emphasized.

“Right now there are multiple spectroscopic surveys coming online which will be taking spectra of hundreds of thousands of white dwarfs, so hopefully we’ll find a few more, perhaps even more vivid green examples,” Hollands told Earth.com.

No white dwarf is bright enough to see without a telescope, but the team has developed a way to create color-accurate images of these stars.

As new surveys examine hundreds of thousands of white dwarfs, astronomers may soon discover even more unexpected colors hiding among the stars.

The full study was published in the journal RAS Techniques and Instruments.
