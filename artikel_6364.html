The prickly pear cactus has a surprising trick for surviving in the desert

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Scientists have discovered that tiny, short-lived leaves help prickly pear cacti capture carbon during the day and accelerate growth.

Cacti are usually described as plants that replaced their leaves with spines and thick green stems.

When rain finally reaches the desert, though, the prickly pear sprouts small green leaves on each new pad. Within a few weeks, these are shed again.

A team in Israel found that these leaflets behave like the leaves of a common garden plant. Carbon dioxide is taken in by them during the day.

The researchers estimate that they supply about a fifth of the carbon a young pad needs for growth.

So the cactus has two ways of gathering carbon rather than one. Older pads collect it after sunset, and the leaflets add to the supply once the sun is up.

The research was conducted by scientists at Ben-Gurion University of the Negev (BGU) in Israel. They worked with researchers from the Gilat Research Center of the Volcani Institute.

Potted plants of Opuntia ficus-indica were grown for the experiments. This is the prickly pear species that is cultivated on farms.

The plants were kept in a net house on BGU’s desert campus at Sde Boker. They were watered every day for a year.

Ilya Gelfand is a professor at BGU’s Jacob Blaustein Institutes for Desert Research and a co-author of the study.

“The cactus effectively has two gears,” said Gelfand. “Most of the year it runs in saving mode and breathes at night to hold on to every drop.”

“When water arrives, it switches on a second, faster gear: temporary leaves that work by day like a regular plant. When the good times end, it simply drops them.”

In most plants the tiny pores on the leaves are opened during the day.

Carbon dioxide enters through them and is used by the plant to build sugars. Water is lost through the same openings, and that loss becomes costly in hot desert conditions.

Cacti, agave and many other succulents get by on less water because their pores open at night instead. The carbon dioxide is stored as an acid until the following morning.

The pores then close, and carbon dioxide released from the stored acid is used to make sugars with the help of sunlight.

This strategy is known as crassulacean acid metabolism or CAM. Mature prickly pear pads rely on it so heavily that scientists classify the species as a full-time CAM plant.

The prickly pear is also an important crop that is grown on about 6.4 million acres (2.6 million hectares) worldwide.

Much of this area lies in drought-prone parts of Africa and South America. In these regions the plant is used for food and livestock feed and for slowing desertification.

Until this project, nobody had examined the leaflets as working organs. Each one measures only about 0.4 inch (10 millimeters) in length.

Around 150 of them can be carried by a single new pad, and they fall off after about six weeks.

The study was led by Patrick Mdemba, who is a doctoral student at BGU. Pores were the first thing he and his colleagues examined.

Clear nail polish was brushed onto pads and leaflets at five different times of the day. After the polish had dried it was peeled away and examined under a microscope. Each thin film preserved an impression of the pore openings.

The older pads opened their pores at night, which was the expected result for a CAM plant. The leaflets showed the opposite pattern. Their pores were opened in the morning and closed after dark, as in a typical leafy plant.

Young pads that were two weeks old did not follow either pattern. Their pores were found to be open at every time the team checked.

Acid build-up between dusk and dawn was then measured in each tissue. A large amount was stored overnight in the older pads.

The young pads stored more acid as they grew older. In the leaflets no significant difference was seen between the dawn and dusk readings.

Mdemba’s team also looked at the carbon inside the leaflets. Carbon in nature comes in a lighter form and a heavier form. Cactus pads that feed at night build up more of the heavier one.

In week-old leaflets the carbon looked much like the carbon in the pads. The mother pad had probably supplied it. Five weeks later the leaflets held noticeably more of the lighter form that daytime leaves collect.

For a second test the researchers sealed whole plants in a clear box almost 4 feet wide. They filled it with carbon dioxide made from the heavier form. One run lasted from 10 a.m. until noon, after the pads had closed their pores.

The marked carbon was found in the leaflets. The pads on those same plants gained no significant amount.

Next came leaflets with no pad at all. Mdemba cut them from pads less than a week old and kept them in sealed jars on a sugar-free gel.

In sunlight the carbon dioxide in each jar went down. Under aluminum foil it went back up. The cut leaflets also grew heavier until about day 15.

Mature pads in an earlier study took in carbon dioxide about twice as fast per square inch as the leaflets.

The team then worked out a carbon budget. A pad with about 150 leaflets gets roughly 0.12 grams of carbon a day from them. A 1998 study put a growing pad’s daily need near 0.6 grams. So the leaflets supply about 20% of it.

The leaflets covered their own growth first. What was left over went to the young pad.

Mdemba then took the leaflets away. All of them were cut off some young pads. On other pads they were left on but painted with clear nail polish, which sealed their pores.

Pad size was checked every five days. By day 25 both groups were about a third smaller in area than normal pads. Pads with no leaflets also weighed about 38% less at three weeks.

Without working leaflets, then, a new pad grew more slowly.

“That lets the cactus grow quickly whenever the desert briefly allows it, without giving up its ability to survive drought,” said Gelfand.

Desert rain tends to arrive in short bursts. For desert plants the timing of that water can matter a great deal.

Some desert shrubs drop their leaves when life gets hard. The prickly pear makes its leaflets in the wet weeks and drops them within about two months.

Every plant in the study got water daily. That may have made the leaflets look more important than they would be in a dry field. Well-watered pads may rely less on their night feeding.

The authors listed other limits too. They did not measure the two enzymes that capture carbon in each system. Gas exchange was not followed over a full 24 hours. Their conclusions come from pores, acid and carbon tracers instead.

They recommend measuring those enzymes in leaflets and young pads, along with gas readings around the clock.

Plants under drought stress should be tested as well. That work could show how much the leaflets matter when a wet spell is short.

The full study was published in the Journal of Experimental Botany.
