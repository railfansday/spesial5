Researchers designed a stiff new solid that keeps heat from escaping better than soft foams and aerogels

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

A solid crystal film with organic layers blocks heat nearly as well as still air, while staying stiffer than most plastics, a record for dense solids.

The best heat blockers are mostly made of air. Inside foam, it sits trapped in tiny pockets, which is why foam insulates so well and also why it squashes so easily. Now, engineers and chemists have made a solid crystal film that is stiff and has no air pockets at all.

It blocks heat nearly as well as still air, while staying stiffer than most plastics.

According to the team, no fully dense solid had ever been measured blocking heat this well at room temperature. The result suggests stiffness and insulation don’t have to be traded against each other.

Jun Liu, an engineer at North Carolina State University, led the work with physicist Dali Sun. Chemists at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill and colleagues in China also took part.

So far the stiff crystal film exists only in the lab. It is a layer far thinner than a human hair on silicon wafers, and it contains lead.

In most materials, stiffness and heat flow rise together. Strong bonds hold diamond together, and it is extremely rigid, and it carries heat very well. Plastics are made of loosely stuck chains, which makes them softer and poorer at passing heat along.

“Because, in general, stiff materials are good at conducting heat, and materials that are not stiff are good at insulating against heat,” Sun said.

That’s why most insulation is soft. Foams and aerogels get close to air by being mostly air. However, they give up almost all their stiffness to do it.

Better insulation is one way to save energy in buildings, but a coating that has to hold its shape on a circuit or a solar panel can’t be that fragile.

The stiff film belongs to a family of layered crystals called inorganic-organic perovskites that stack thin sheets of lead and iodine between layers of organic, carbon-based molecules.

In earlier work, Liu’s group found that swapping some of the carbon-carbon chains in the organic layers with benzene rings – stiff, six-sided rings of carbon atoms – made the crystals both stiffer and worse at carrying heat.

This time the team used molecules carrying two rings joined by a double bond. The rings make the layer rigid. They also vibrate mostly at high frequencies, which barely overlap with the vibrations of the lead-iodide sheets, so heat has trouble crossing from one layer to the next.

The longer molecules also jumble the way the sheets stack. That disorder slows the passage of heat even more.

The team compared four versions. The one with a plain carbon chain carried about five times the heat as the best version, which used a molecule called azobenzene ethyl ammonium.

To test the films, the researchers coated each one with a thin layer of metal. A short laser pulse heated the surface, and a second beam tracked how fast that heat drained away into the film below.

At room temperature, the best film carried heat only about 60 percent better than still air. That’s less than half as well as typical plastics, and one spot on one sample came in lower still.

The team got its best results from films about 6 millionths of an inch (150 nanometers) thick. Thinner and thicker films did worse, and only films in a narrow band of thicknesses stayed near the record.

Measurements at several spots on several samples gave consistent results, so the team doesn’t think it was a fluke of one sample.

For the second test, the team pressed a tiny vibrating probe against the film to measure how much it resisted bending. The azobenzene film came out stiffer than most plastics and far stiffer than foams and aerogels.

The films are made by spinning a liquid onto a surface and then heating it. The authors wrote that the method can be adapted to coat large or flexible surfaces cheaply. This is in contrast to earlier, ultralow-heat films that needed a high-vacuum chamber.

In an interview with Earth.com, Liu picked out two early targets for this technology. “Electronics and spacecraft would be the first series of applications, for the near-room-temperature thermal insulation in the form of thin film coating,” he said.

The team also sees it as a coating for use near room temperature, based on how it held up when heated.

The researchers see other possible roles too, such as shielding parts in fuel cells and solar collectors, or slowing the heat that spreads when a battery fails. None of those uses has been tested.

Like the materials in many experimental perovskite solar cells, the film is built around lead.

“Yes, the current formula has lead in it and there needs to be environmental considerations if the exact composition will be used,” Liu told Earth.com.

He called the tested film an example of a design idea, and he pointed to related crystals made without lead as the route to practical use. Handling lead safely is already a question for perovskite solar recycling.

The team also spelled out limits of the design idea. A near-twin molecule, with the two rings joined by carbon atoms instead of nitrogen, didn’t jumble the layers nearly as much and missed the record.

The team’s model accounts for that gap only loosely, and it can’t yet predict which molecule will work before someone makes it.

Nobody has shown whether a lead-free crystal keeps the same trick. By the team’s own estimate, a molecule whose rings twist more easily could push the film’s heat flow close to that of air itself. Making one is the next test.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
