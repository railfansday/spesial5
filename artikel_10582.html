When The Launch Becomes The Evidence: Why AI Adoption Isn’t Proof Of Business Value

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Steve Taplin is CEO & founder of Sonatafy Technology, a software consulting & engineering firm focused on delivery, quality & accountability

​Someone opened champagne near the conference rooms the day a lender my firm was working with put its AI underwriting assistant into production. I raised a glass alongside everyone else.

The achievement was real. The team had spent months connecting the assistant to a loan-origination platform so it could extract information from application documents and produce an underwriting recommendation in under a minute. Only applications with complete records and familiar financial structures qualified. Everything else stayed in the existing workflow.

The announcement left those limits out. Within days, an industry publication was describing the company as part of a new generation of AI lenders, and the board wanted to know how quickly the rollout could expand. A technical milestone had quietly become evidence of a business result nobody had measured.

That moment became the starting point for my upcoming book, The AI Validation Gap. Whether an AI system works is usually the easier question. Whether the business is better off because of it is the one that matters. Adoption rates, processing speed and launch dates are easy to count. None of them is proof of value.

Three months later, the same leaders returned for the first post-launch review. The figures below are illustrative to protect the client, but the situation was real.

The slide reported that analysts used the assistant on 81% of applications. The CFO read it and asked, “Eighty-one percent of what?” It was 81% of the applications the assistant was eligible to see, and those were only 64% of what came through the door. Measured against total volume, the assistant touched roughly half of all applications. Analysts accepted its recommendation without changes on 21% of eligible applications, about one application in eight overall. The rest of the time, they reviewed the source documents, adjusted the analysis, went back to the borrower or replaced the recommendation with their own judgment.

The headline number was accurate. It simply answered a different question than the one the board was asking.

The assistant made one task dramatically faster. That did not mean underwriting became faster or cheaper.

AI measures its performance at the point of output. Businesses experience its value at the point of completion. Between those two points sits the work that rarely appears on a dashboard: verification, correction, exceptions and rework.

To trust a recommendation, an analyst had to check it, and checking meant reading the same documents the assistant had read. When the analyst disagreed, the work became the original analysis plus a review of the machine’s version. The minute saved on drafting could easily be consumed, or exceeded, by the time spent verifying and correcting. Meanwhile, ineligible applications still ran through the old process, so the company now operated two underwriting paths instead of one.

Nobody could say which effect was larger, because measurement stopped when the AI returned its answer. The budget covered development and vendor costs but not analyst review time. The organization could report how fast the system responded. It could not report whether a loan decision cost less, took less time or carried less risk.

Adoption metrics win by default. They are available on launch day, they live inside the technology team’s tools and they tend to go up. Business outcomes require a baseline taken before launch, measurement across several departments and patience for results that take quarters to appear.

Adoption can also mislead on its own terms. A high usage rate might mean employees find the tool useful. It might also mean they are required to use it.

Ownership compounds the problem. At the lender, the CTO owned the model, product owned the workflow and operations owned adoption. Finance would verify savings, and legal reviewed the controls. Every one of those responsibilities was real, and none of them amounted to owning the business result.

When the review ended, the CEO saw no reason to change the language on the website, since nothing showed the system had failed. He was right. Nothing showed it had succeeded either. The meeting closed with a request for more data next quarter, and the launch stayed a success on paper.

That deferral has a cost that never appears in a budget: another quarter of engineers, product leaders and managers preserving a possibility instead of building something proven.

Closing the distance between a launch and a verdict starts with measuring the whole workflow rather than the model. Four areas matter:

1. End-To-End Completion Time: Measure how long work takes from the original request to the finished business outcome, including review, corrections, approvals and exceptions.

2. Total Cost Per Completed Outcome: Include software, infrastructure, implementation and the employee time spent reviewing AI-generated work. A lower cost per AI transaction means little if the cost per approved loan or resolved customer issue stays the same.

3. Quality And Rework: Track whether AI reduces errors or simply moves them downstream. Correction rates, escalations and reopened work show whether faster output is producing better results or more cleanup.

4. Realized Business Improvement: Compare results against a baseline taken before launch. Without one, any improvement is a guess.

Each of these should also account for the work AI cannot handle, which means reporting every percentage against total volume, not just eligible cases. And name one accountable executive with access to the outcome evidence and the authority to pause or scale the system. Committees can advise, but they should not stand in for a name.

At your next AI portfolio review, replace the usual status discussion with three questions:

2. What evidence would prove we achieved it?

3. Who has the authority to act on what the evidence tells us?

The companies that get real value from AI won’t be the ones that launched first. They will be the ones that can show, with evidence, what changed after they did.​
