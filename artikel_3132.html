How Reading The Room Can Help Prevent ERP Go-Live Delays

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Komal Goyal is CEO of 6e Technologies, a global IT advisory firm specializing in ERP implementation for commercial and government clients.

​Should a date decide when an ERP system is ready to go live?

ERP go-live dates are often tied to hard-to-move commitments, such as a fiscal year-end or a merger and acquisition (M&A) event. As the date approaches, teams focus on what dashboards can measure: testing, defects, integrations and training. But those measures do not capture every readiness risk.

Take Rhode Island’s payroll as an example. It went live as planned. Within weeks, unions reported missing wages, unpaid overtime and incorrect deductions. Root causes await an independent review, but the vendor cited the complexity of moving from fragmented legacy systems to a unified platform. However, technology rarely fails on its own; how people adopt, use and work around it often shapes the outcome.

Across more than 30 successful ERP implementations, I have found readiness gaps often appear first in behavior. Reading those cues early allows time to address problems while protecting the go-live date.

This is an important principle of ERP implementation assurance: independently testing whether an organization is ready to operate a new system, rather than relying solely on whether an implementation is on schedule.​

Reading the room starts with signals that never appear in test results or sign-offs. Here are five I watch for:

I have been left guessing in requirements discussions because only part of a process was shared. That under-sharing may be oversight or reluctance to expose informal work-arounds. Either way, missed details become missed requirements, which often surface after go-live as defects, delays or operational problems.

When a stakeholder stops discussing the future-state process to defend the current one, I pay attention. The objection may be valid or reflect concerns about changing responsibilities, authority or job security. I have seen people make a process sound more complex than it is or insist the new system cannot support it.​

Left unread, that defensiveness can lead to customizing around a problem that may not exist, adding effort and risk until go-live slips.

If a user engaged in testing suddenly gets frustrated, it’s easy to assume that perhaps the workflow is clunkier or a feature they rely on is missing. But sometimes the “missing feature” is lost authority, because a step they considered critical is no longer needed.​

If that goes unread, they may disengage from testing, and issues they would have caught surface later on.

Silence is harder to read. When someone who used to speak up goes quiet, I want to know what changed. They may feel speaking up is pointless, or fear what it could cost them. The concerns people stop raising do not disappear. They arrive at go-live.

Every go-live has a list of items to fix later. The list itself is not the warning sign; it is how difficult issues are handled. I pay attention when a question makes the room uneasy and is quickly deferred until after go-live without a clear owner or plan.​

Sometimes, a failed test is dismissed as minor because the launch date is approaching. Without assessing how it could affect daily operations, the team risks discovering the consequences after launch, when resolving them may be more disruptive.

Recognizing these patterns gives teams a chance to act early.​

I’ve seen readiness issues get harder to fix once go-live pressure builds. That’s why a strong change management lead should work closely with stakeholders to act on these findings in time.

However, more time does not inherently solve the problem. Idaho’s Luma rollout was delayed twice, yet an independent review found its change management left agencies and employees underprepared to work differently.

When a cue surfaces, look beyond the behavior to its cause.

If details are withheld, broaden the conversation until the full process, including informal work-arounds, is clear. If someone defends the old process or reports missing functionality, determine what need lies behind the objection before customizing or deferring a solution. If a workflow feels harder, assess whether the issue is design or user capability, then provide the necessary changes, training or support.

Where authority is changing, clarify decision rights. Retire work-arounds only when the new system meets the needs they served. If users go quiet, speak with them privately to understand why, then explain what concerns were addressed and what remains unresolved.​​

​​Importantly, while a change lead can drive this work, appointing someone outside of the program to independently challenge whether people are ready can help to make sure the pressure to meet the deadline does not overshadow unresolved readiness risks.​

As go-live nears, deadline pressure takes over. System integrators focus on delivery, internal teams manage execution, and leaders balance competing business priorities. Generative AI can accelerate this push, cutting implementation effort by up to 40% and testing time by 70%.

But faster delivery does not answer whether the organization is ready. That question needs someone far enough from delivery to challenge readiness. A dashboard or even GenAI can show participation dropped, but rarely why a respected user went quiet. Here, human judgment still has the edge over AI.

Emotional intelligence helps read that cue, and ERP experience tells whether it’s ordinary friction or a real readiness risk. Having the strategy in place to say “not yet” is how the date stops deciding the system change.​​
