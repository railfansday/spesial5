9 companies cutting jobs in 2026, from Amazon and UPS to Meta and Block

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Amazon, UPS, Oracle, Meta, and Heineken are among 9 companies cutting staff or planning cuts in 2026 as AI and weak demand reshape payrolls.

Opening a work inbox in 2026 can feel like a gamble, with the next restructuring memo as likely to come from a delivery service or a brewer as from a software firm. U.S. layoffs topped 1.1 million in 2025, and firms cited artificial intelligence adoption, economic uncertainty, and the push to trim costs. The new calendar brought no relief. Headcount reductions in the first weeks of 2026 spread across a wide range of industries and reached tens of thousands of workers. Anyone who draws a paycheck, manages a team, or owns shares in a public corporation has a stake in where the next round lands and what pushes executives to shrink their workforces.

The labor market and the broader economy told two different stories as 2025 closed. Hiring slowed in December, capping a year of weak payroll gains that left many people looking for work frustrated. Output kept climbing. Strong consumer spending powered a 4.3% annual growth rate in the July-September quarter, the best showing in two years. The Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta forecast a slowdown to 2.7% for the final three months of the year, and economists held on to their optimism through the hiring lull. The gap between expanding output and sluggish recruitment matters to job seekers weighing a career move. A healthy economy did little to shield wage earners from a slow job market, which suggests headline growth figures offer an incomplete read on individual prospects.

FOX 13 Tampa Bay has kept a running list of companies shedding staff since Jan. 27, 2026, updating it through July 6 with reporting drawn from LiveNOW from FOX, FOX Business, Business Insider, and The Associated Press. The roundup applies no scoring system of its own. Some entries rest on statements from the companies themselves, while others come from outlets citing unnamed sources. The compilation points to WARN Tracker, a website that monitors the WARN notices employers file before dismissing employees. WARN Tracker's tally shows more than 100 companies planning job cuts in 2026, though part of that total traces back to earlier announcements. This article draws nine companies from the list, a group that spans technology, delivery, finance, brewing, apparel, and manufacturing. The selection follows no order of size and makes no claim to completeness.

Amazon $AMZN announced on Jan. 28 that it is eliminating roughly 16,000 jobs, its second mass layoff in about three months. Beth Galetti, a senior vice president at the company, sent the memo that broke the news to employees.

The January announcement followed an October 2025 reduction that removed about 14,000 white-collar positions. The January figure exceeds the October total by about 2,000 jobs. Combined, the two waves reach roughly 30,000 roles.

Workers who survived the October cut had little time to settle before the next memo arrived. Two large rounds in quick succession, the second bigger than the first, point to a restructuring still in motion.

Amazon framed the downsizing as part of an ongoing drive to strip out management tiers, give staff more ownership, and clear away red tape. Thinner management fits the white-collar profile of the October cut. A campaign pitched as continuous leaves room for more job losses at Amazon.

UPS announced it would eliminate up to 30,000 operational jobs, a reduction aimed at the hands-on side of the delivery company's business. The words "up to" mark a ceiling. The final count could settle below that mark, and the announcement leaves the company room to stop short of it.

Operational roles set this plan apart from much of the 2026 list. Amazon $AMZN's earlier round fell on white-collar staff, and Meta $META's Reality Labs plan targets a division built around metaverse products. UPS is thinning the part of a package carrier that moves boxes from one place to another.

Scale gives the UPS ceiling added weight. At its upper limit, the plan would run nearly twice the size of Amazon's January round, the headline cut from the year's most prominent tech employer.

The announcement, as reported, offers no total headcount for UPS. Readers can't tell what share of the workforce the plan would touch, a gap that makes the cut harder to measure against companies such as Heineken, which framed its reduction as a portion of total staff.

The UPS plan carries the 2026 contraction past corporate desks and into the work that keeps goods in motion.

Oracle $ORCL may slash 20,000 to 30,000 positions, FOX Business reported, citing the tech magazine CIO. The range works out to 12% to 18% of the company's global workforce of roughly 162,000 employees. The two sets of numbers agree, with 12% of 162,000 coming to about 19,400 and 18% to about 29,200. The report gives no date, and nothing in it shows that any Oracle worker has lost a job.

Even the bottom of that range would place Oracle among the heaviest reductions in this group. Twenty thousand jobs would exceed Amazon $AMZN's January round of about 16,000, and the top figure equals the 30,000 operational jobs UPS has said it could eliminate. Measured as a share of staff, the plan would run deeper than the roughly 10% Reuters sources attached to Meta $META's broader cut. It would also land at four to six times Morgan Stanley $MS's 3%, roughly twice Heineken's share of nearly 7%, and below Block $SQ's 40%.

A 10,000-position spread between the two ends suggests the plan, as reported, has not settled into a firm number. At the low end, Oracle would keep about 142,000 workers. At the high end, about 132,000 would remain. The smaller of those totals would outnumber Heineken's entire pre-cut workforce of roughly 87,000.

Oracle has put no figures of its own on record. Amazon, UPS, Heineken, Nike $NKE, and Block each announced their reductions, and Meta disclosed its Reality Labs cut itself. Oracle's numbers reached the public secondhand, passing from CIO to FOX Business.

Meta's broader plan also surfaced through Reuters sources, yet that reporting came with a scheduled date of May 20. Oracle's report lacks even that, leaving its workers no sense of when notices might arrive. Until Oracle confirms the figures, the reported range describes a possibility, and readers should give it less weight than cuts a company has stated in its own name.

Meta $META plans to eliminate more than 1,000 jobs in its Reality Labs division as the company steers investment away from metaverse products. The move links the size of a team to the money behind its work. Meta is pulling dollars from the metaverse, and the staff who build those products stand to lose their positions. Meta announced this reduction itself, which places it on firmer ground than the wider plan that surfaced through anonymous sources.

A larger reduction appears in reporting from Reuters. Sources told the news agency that Meta planned an initial round of layoffs on May 20, one that would remove about 10% of its global workforce, or nearly 8,000 employees. The same reporting described the company as preparing to shed thousands of jobs, with deeper job losses expected later in the year. No update in the coverage confirms that the May 20 round took place, so the figure stands as a reported plan.

The reported round would dwarf the Reality Labs announcement. Nearly 8,000 positions would amount to about eight times the more than 1,000 jobs at stake in the metaverse unit. Pairing 10% with 8,000 implies a payroll near 80,000 worldwide. Against a base of that size, the Reality Labs trim reaches a little over 1% of the total. Neither account specifies whether the Reality Labs jobs fall inside the reported 10% or come on top of it. A downsizing of the scope Reuters described would stretch past any single division and extend across the whole company. Any deeper round later in the year would push the share beyond 10%.

A Meta executive has declined to rule out further departures. Janelle Gale, the company's chief people officer, told employees in an internal meeting that Meta could not guarantee an end to layoffs, according to People Matters. Staff outside Reality Labs have little reason to treat the metaverse pullback as the final word on Meta's headcount.

Heineken plans to eliminate up to 6,000 jobs worldwide, a reduction the Dutch brewer announced on Feb. 11 that equals nearly 7% of its roughly 87,000 employees. The 6,000 figure works as a ceiling. The final tally could land below it. Heineken expects its productivity program to reduce headcount by 5,000 to 6,000 positions over the next two years, according to Reuters.

Heineken expects its earnings growth to slow in 2026 and pins the forecast on weak demand across the beer industry. Reuters reports that the brewer's guidance calls for profit to rise 2% to 6% this year, down from the 4% to 8% it targeted for 2025. Tight household budgets and recent bad weather have cut into beer sales across the sector. Rival Carlsberg has announced job cuts as well, and it forecast the identical 2% to 6% range a week before Heineken, according to Reuters. Heineken brews Tiger and Amstel alongside the lager that carries its name. A slump that spans the industry leaves little shelter in a stable of several brands.

Heineken's stated reason for the cuts sets the brewer apart from much of this list. Block $SQ ties its staff reduction to a push into artificial intelligence, and Meta $META is steering money away from metaverse products. The brewer's explanation turns on its customers and their appetite for beer. Longer-term pressures add to the strain, with alcohol makers confronting more health warnings, competing alternatives, and disruptors such as weight-loss drugs.

The worldwide reach of the plan marks another contrast. Stanley Black & Decker $SWK's cut focuses on one Connecticut factory, while Heineken's job losses span its global workforce. The burden won't spread evenly, though. Finance chief Harold van den Broek said some of the cuts will center on Europe and on non-priority markets with weaker growth prospects.

At the full 6,000, the plan would leave Heineken with about 81,000 employees. Reuters reports that organic operating profit grew 4.4% in 2025, topping the 4% analysts had expected. The brewer faces unhappy investors who argue it has fallen behind on efficiency, and the cuts aim to free up savings as the company pledges faster growth with fewer resources. Heineken is paring its payroll early in a year it expects to bring slower profit expansion, a move timed to trim costs before the slowdown reaches its results.

Morgan Stanley $MS cut 3% of its staff, about 2,500 employees, and distributed the reductions through all of its business divisions. No single unit carried the burden on its own. Every part of the bank gave up some headcount.

The two figures together put Morgan Stanley's payroll at roughly 83,000 people before the layoffs. About 97% of them kept their jobs. Losing 2,500 positions at once still leaves a sizable group of people searching for new work, yet it amounts to a thin slice of an organization that large.

The firmwide reach sets this move apart from job losses aimed at one plant, one product line, or one division elsewhere on this list. Morgan Stanley spread the pain evenly enough that no corner of the firm escaped it. The pattern points to a bank paring each of its existing divisions and keeping its overall structure in place.

Nike $NKE announced earlier this year that it is cutting 775 jobs. The company attached an exact headcount to the reduction, with no range or rounding around the figure.

Several other cuts in this group surfaced as approximations or upper limits. Nike's precise tally stands apart from them.

Stanley Black & Decker $SWK said it will cut about 300 positions in New Britain, Connecticut, by closing a factory that builds single-sided tape measures. The toolmaker described the shutdown as one piece of a restructuring in progress.

The company traced the closure to what it called a sustained drop in demand for single-sided tape measures. Its explanation narrows the cause to one product category. Stanley Black & Decker cited no broad downturn and no companywide pivot. The stated problem is a shrinking market for a specific tool.

A single product's fading sales set this cut apart from several others in the roundup. Tech firms on the list frame their reductions around bets on artificial intelligence or shifts in investment priorities. The toolmaker offers a plainer account. Customers want fewer of one item it makes. The plant that builds that item will close.

Stanley Black & Decker's description of the decline as sustained signals that the company does not expect orders to rebound. A permanent closure fits that reading.

The ongoing overhaul places the New Britain closure inside a wider effort the company has not finished. The New Britain case shows the year's job cuts reaching past software and services onto factory floors, where weak sales of one product can shut down a whole facility.

Block $SQ cut its staff by 40% to expand its artificial intelligence business, a move that put two of every five employees out of work. Pinterest $PINS, the San Francisco company that also shifted resources toward AI-focused roles, announced it would reduce its headcount by just under 15%. Block's cut runs more than twice as deep, the largest proportional reduction among the companies in the roundup that disclosed a workforce share.

Block owns the payment services Square and Cash App. Both brands answer to a parent operating with three-fifths of its former workforce. Jack Dorsey presented the layoffs as a choice about structure. He wrote that AI tools and smaller, flatter teams are changing how leaders build and run a company. He added that the cuts did not stem from any trouble at Block.

Dorsey's account separates Block from employers on the list that tied their cuts to weak demand or a dimmer profit outlook. He describes a deliberate redesign, a wager that a leaner organization equipped with AI can do the work a larger one handled. Pinterest's move points in a similar direction. It is giving priority to AI-powered products as it trims staff. Together, the two cases show AI serving as a stated reason for cuts at companies that cite no slump in demand.

Tech experts told CBC News that pandemic-era overhiring and inflated AI promises have fed job losses across the industry. Their explanation complicates Dorsey's. He credits the tools themselves, while the experts point to the hiring binge and the hype that preceded them. If the experts have it right, some of the cuts at Block may correct past hiring as much as they prepare the company for AI. Either reading lands in the same place. Block is betting that a smaller staff, armed with new tools, can operate Square and Cash App.
