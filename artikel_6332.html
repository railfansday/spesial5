Five cows abandoned on a remote island in 1871 grew into a herd of about 2,000 despite extreme inbreeding

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Five cattle introduced to Amsterdam Island in 1871 grew into a herd of 2,000 before being eradicated in 2010.

In 1871, a French farmer from La Réunion brought five cattle to Amsterdam Island, in the southern Indian Ocean, and left them there.

Their descendants peaked at about 2,000 animals. A 2024 genomic study found the herd highly inbred, with average inbreeding of 33 percent.

The animals showed no significant purging of their most harmful mutations, yet the population persisted until it was eradicated in 2010. Their genomes point to a brief founding bottleneck and an ancestry already suited to the island.

Amsterdam Island covers about 21 square miles (55 square kilometers) and lies some 2,760 miles (4,440 kilometers) southeast of Madagascar. It belongs to the French Southern and Antarctic Lands.

Once ashore, the cattle roamed and bred freely. They “thrived within a few generations in a seemingly inhospitable environment,” the researchers wrote.

The herd reached peaks of around 2,000 animals in 1952 and again in 1988. A sharp decline in 1953 followed an outbreak of infectious disease, probably paratuberculosis.

Concern that the cattle threatened endemic species led to control measures from 1988, aimed at restoring the island’s terrestrial ecosystem. Culls took place in 1988 and 1993, and the herd was eradicated in 2010 following a decision by the authorities.

The herd was controlled by shooting and fencing to protect the Amsterdam albatross, a seabird found only on the island. The fences came down once they were no longer needed, according to the Agreement on the Conservation of Albatrosses and Petrels (ACAP).

Mathieu Gautier of the Centre for Biology and Management of Populations in Montpellier, France, led the research, published online in June 2024.

Laurence Flori, also in Montpellier, and Tom Druet of the University of Liège in Belgium were joint senior authors. The team genotyped 18 animals, 12 collected in 1992 and six in 2006, and sequenced the whole genomes of eight females from the 1992 group.

The herd’s ancestry mixed two cattle groups. About 73.1 percent came from European taurine cattle and about 26.9 percent from Indian Ocean zebu, with uncertainty ranges of roughly 71 to 77 percent and 23 to 29 percent.

All eight sequenced animals carried the same maternal lineage, mitochondrial haplogroup T1b1. That lineage is found in most African cattle, including African zebus.

The two ancestries mixed almost at the same time as the herd was founded. Assuming a six-year generation time, the estimate puts that mixing around 1860, give or take 15 years.

Genome-based models point to an intense but brief bottleneck in the late 19th century. Using a method called GONE, they estimate that about 20 generations ago, the effective population size fell to about 4.8.

Effective population size measures how many animals actually pass on their genes, not how many are alive. The same models put the herd’s later effective size at about 1,500, and they showed no sign of a decline tied to the 1950s disease outbreak.

Most of the inbreeding arose a few generations after the bottleneck, in ancestors living about 15 generations back. Because the population expanded quickly, little new inbreeding built up later.

The herd’s autozygosity, the stretches of DNA carried in two identical copies from a shared ancestor, was mainly related to the founder event.

Average inbreeding barely changed between the two sampling years, from 32.6 percent in 1992 to 32.9 percent in 2006. Genetic diversity fell only moderately despite the high inbreeding.

Inbreeding can expose harmful recessive mutations, which show their effects only when an animal inherits two copies. Natural selection can then weed them out, a process called purging.

Genetic drift is the random change in gene variant frequencies from one generation to the next, and it is strongest in small populations.

On Amsterdam Island, the genomes showed high drift and slightly weaker selection against mildly harmful variants.

Unlike some populations that have stayed small for a long time, the herd showed no significant purging of highly harmful variants.

The bottleneck also left a flatter spread of gene variant frequencies across the genome, a signature of strong genetic drift.

The authors attribute the herd’s success in a harsh environment to preadaptation inherited from its European taurine ancestors.

That suggests the island posed no strong climatic challenge. It also contradicts earlier claims, based on crude physical data, that the cattle had shrunk through island dwarfism.

A scan of the genomes for signs of selection pointed mostly to genes related to nervous system function. The researchers link those genes to rapid feralization, as domestic animals returned to a free-roaming life.

That shift was driven by changes in behavior and complex social restructuring, according to the study.

The herd offers insight into how populations can establish themselves quickly and adapt in challenging environments. Feral populations like this one also carry unique genetic legacies that raise ethical questions for conservation efforts.

The study was published in the journal Molecular Biology and Evolution.
