Corn fights back against a fungus – but its defense has a hidden weakness

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

A corn smut fungus uses the plant’s own defense enzymes to release a hidden signal that activates a receptor and speeds fungal cell division.

Corn, like every plant, fights back when something breaks in. When a fungus pushes into its tissue, the plant sends out enzymes that chop up proteins, including the ones the intruder secretes.

For the fungus behind corn smut, that defense turns out to be exactly the signal it has been waiting for.

The plant’s enzymes cut a protein secreted by the fungus, releasing a signal that helps the invader detect that it has entered plant tissue.

That cut frees a short fragment that was folded away inside. The fragment switches on a receptor in the fungal cell membrane, and the fungus starts dividing faster.

The plant’s defense, in other words, tells the invader it has arrived.

Florian Altegoer, who leads a research group at the Institute of Microbiology at Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, led the work with lead author Gabriel Mendoza-Rojas, a member of his group.

Colleagues at the University of Göttingen, NC State University, Imperial College London, and the University of Groningen took part.

The receptor belongs to a family of proteins called G protein-coupled receptors, which work as sensors in a cell’s outer membrane and relay signals from outside to inside.

Humans have more than 800 of them, and they are the targets of about 40% of approved drugs. Fungal versions are far less understood.

“We have identified a previously unknown fungal GPCR and uncovered an unusual mechanism by which it is activated,” Altegoer said.

Mendoza-Rojas put the twist more bluntly.

“The fungus thus ironically utilizes enzymes from its host plant to detect that it has entered the tissue successfully,” he said.

The fungus, Ustilago maydis, infects maize plants and causes large galls, especially on the ears, where it makes its spores.

Two of its genes were already known to be essential: delete either one and the fungus produces almost no galls.

One of those genes makes a secreted protein called Pit2, which corn enzymes cut into a fragment that blocks those same enzymes.

The other made a membrane protein of unknown function. When the team searched the fungal genome for proteins with the seven-helix shape of a receptor, that protein was one of 34 hits. They renamed it Gpe1.

They put the receptor into baker’s yeast engineered to glow red whenever it fires. Intact Pit2 produced a weak glow. Fragments of Pit2 fully activated the reporter at a tenth of the dose.

The team narrowed the active fragment down to nine amino acids.

The team also grew crystals of Pit2 and mapped its shape, which helped explain why the intact protein activates the receptor only weakly.

Two copies lock together, and the nine-amino-acid stretch is buried in the seam between them. Only the plant’s enzymes can release it.

“The peptide is hidden inside Pit2, like treasure in a chest that only plant enzymes can open,” Mendoza-Rojas said.

“However, opening this chest is more like opening Pandora’s box: It releases a signal that benefits the fungus at the plant’s expense.”

The team then watched living fungal cells under a microscope while the fragment flowed past them.

Soon after, the receptor pulled back from the cell surface into the cell’s interior, a common response of a receptor that has been triggered.

The cells also budded sooner. One round of division took about 226 minutes without the fragment and about 167 minutes with it, close to an hour less.

Faster division fits what the fungus has to do. It pauses its cell cycle while it grows a thread into the plant, then restarts division once it’s inside.

A regulator called Clp1 is part of that restart. Cells carrying extra Gpe1 made about five times as much of the Clp1 gene’s messenger molecule, and the fragment pushed it higher still.

“Our work establishes a mechanistic link between sensing the host environment and resuming fungal proliferation after entry,” Altegoer said.

The receptor mattered in whole plants too. The team changed single building blocks in the receptor’s predicted binding pocket and infected corn seedlings with the altered fungi.

Two of the changes, which also crippled the receptor in yeast, sharply reduced the galls on seedlings scored 12 days later.

Gpe1 has relatives across the smut fungi, but Pit2 shows up only in the corn fungus’s close relatives. The team tested whether each receptor would respond to the other species’ signal.

The signal from a related corn fungus switched on the corn smut receptor. The signal from a barley smut did not, and the corn fragments only weakly activated the barley receptor.

The nine-amino-acid stretch itself has barely changed across those species, which the authors read as the receptor and its signal evolving together.

A structural search also turned up a broad family of related receptors across the fungal group that includes mushrooms and smuts.

One patch of Gpe1 resembles a switch found in a large class of human receptors, including the light sensor in the eye.

The picture of how the fragment sits in the receptor comes from a computer prediction, which the team used only to decide which parts of the protein to alter.

The two weakest receptor variants might also be less stable rather than worse at binding, and the authors wrote that they can’t yet separate those explanations.

The restart of cell division is also only partly explained. The increase in Clp1 messenger RNA after receptor activation offers a possible explanation.

And the plant’s enzymes cut Pit2 into more than one fragment. One blocks the plant’s defenses; the other switches on the receptor.

How much each contributes during a real infection has not been tested, and that is the next experiment the authors wrote has to be done.

Whether other fungi that infect crops use the same mechanism is the larger open question, and the team plans to look.

“We want to find out whether other fungi use comparable receptors to sense their surroundings and adjust their growth,” Altegoer said.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Communications.
