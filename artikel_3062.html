3 Lessons From Berkshire Hathaway's Stock Portfolio That Work for Any Stock Portfolio

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Warren Buffett may have managed billions of dollars’ worth of investments, but the principles that Berkshire Hathaway’s holdings so successful aren’t size-dependent.

By James Brumley – Nov 27, 2026 at 6:51AM EST

The world is very different now than it was when Warren Buffett first took the helm at Berkshire Hathaway (BRKA +0.01%) (BRKB +0.27%) back in 1970. However, there's a reason that Berkshire and its stock have continued to perform so well despite never-ending change. That's because Buffett fully understood what really mattered for stocks in the long run, and what didn't. He remained focused on that and ignored the rest. He often even shared these nuggets of wisdom with the rest of the world.

For investors who want to distill the Oracle of Omaha's collective wisdom into something simple enough to apply in practice, though, three overarching lessons encompass most of the more detailed tips.

Warren Buffett. Image source: Getty Images.

Warren Buffett has voiced this idea in a number of ways over the years, such as suggesting you seek out companies with a wide competitive moat, reliable pricing power, and the high profit margins that should be evident with both. Or to put it another way, he argues, "the three most important words in investing are 'margin of safety.'"

The all-encompassing idea, however, is much more direct. Buffett's very first personal rule of investing is "don't lose money," followed closely by the second rule "never forget the first rule."

And there's little doubt as to what he meant by it. That is, never put yourself in a position where sizable setbacks can take a permanent toll on your portfolio. While subpar gains are no fun, it's much more difficult to recoup losses than it is to make up for merely below-average performance.

That doesn't mean Warren Buffett never lost money picking stocks for Berkshire, to be clear. He has. He was one of the key orchestrators of 2015's Kraft-Heinz merger, which turned into a disaster by 2019. Berkshire Hathaway also lost money on its stake in Paramount Global (now Skydance), which it exited in 2024.

These were legitimate misreads of the two companies' potential (it happens!), not losses suffered simply because Buffett got caught up in hype that was never going to deliver the suggested results.

Whether they realize it or not, investors who have embraced Warren Buffett's approach to picking stocks aren't merely stepping into specific tickers when they look as if they could move higher. As he penned in 2021's letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders posted in early 2022, "Charlie and I are not stock-pickers; we are business-pickers."

And it's an important distinction to make, if only because it forces you to mentally reframe your stock-picking standards. Given enough time, great businesses will drive their stocks higher simply by creating more long-term shareholder value. Not all stocks racing higher right now will be able to continue rallying, however, simply because their underlying companies aren't capable of sustaining bottom-line growth. It's just fear of missing out that's driving these names up.

Here's the litmus test: If it were a privately-owned business you were starting or funding on your own, would you be happy with its current, ongoing, and future results for the cost? If not, you probably don't want to become an actual shareholder either.

Finally, one of Buffett's most important pieces of investing advice isn't actually Buffett's at all. It's Benjamin Graham's, who was one of Warren Buffett's professors while he was a student at Columbia University. It was in Graham's book Security Analysis that he wrote, "In the short run, the market is a voting machine but in the long run it is a weighing machine." This is a crucial truth that allowed Buffett to comfortably ride out the market's rough patches that rattled plenty of other investors out of their stocks at the worst possible time.

But what does it mean? "Votes" reflect fickle, ever-changing, short-term emotions. Given enough time, however, stocks will eventually reflect their underlying company's value (which is why you should insist on buying quality companies in the first place).

That being said, there's an addendum to this tip. Buffett has also famously said, "Be greedy when others are fearful and fearful when others are greedy." Since fear and greed are emotions -- or "votes" -- that aren't built to last but instead ultimately cede to more sustainable valuations, periods of extreme fear or greed are also buying and selling opportunities, respectively.

This obviously isn't all of Warren Buffett's body of investing advice. If you can embrace just these three ideas, though, you'll be navigating the market in the same spirit in which he led Berkshire Hathaway to its superior performance.
