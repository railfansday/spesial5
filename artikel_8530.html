Forecast Your Business’s Cash 13 Weeks Ahead – Fix a Shortfall Before It Hits

Your business can be profitable and still run short of cash when customers pay later than your bills come due. Keep a forecast of the cash you expect in and out over the next 13 weeks, update it every week, and you’ll see a shortfall a month or two out, in time to fix it.

Your bank balance can’t show you that. It shows what cleared yesterday, not what customers still owe you or the bills due next month.

A 13-week cash forecast lines those up by putting each payment in the week the money will actually arrive or leave. If a customer you bill today has 30 days to pay but always takes 45 days, their payment goes in week 7, not week 5.

Take a landscaping company whose December forecast shows a $14,000 shortfall in February, as winter work slows but its equipment loan and insurance bill still come due. With two months’ warning, the owner puts the insurance bill on a monthly payment plan and covers the rest for six weeks with a line of credit, a borrowing limit the company draws on as needed.

Build yours in a spreadsheet with a column for each week, and start week 1 with today’s bank balance. Add each customer payment in the week you expect the money, and subtract every payroll, rent, loan, tax, and supplier payment in the week it’s due. Each week’s ending balance becomes the next week’s starting cash.

Every week, enter what actually came in and went out beside your forecast, then add a week at the end so it always looks 13 weeks ahead. When a customer keeps paying late, move their payments to later weeks, and give any bill you forgot a row of its own.

Any week that ends below zero is a shortfall to start closing now. Call customers with overdue invoices, delay purchases that can wait, or arrange a business line of credit while sales still look strong to a lender. A cash reserve covers any gap those moves can’t close in time.

Fill in your first one from your last three months of bank statements and invoices, which show how long each customer takes to pay. Then add the bills that come once or twice a year, like an insurance premium or property taxes, since three months of statements may not include them.
