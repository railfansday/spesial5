Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade: 5 Facts You May Not Know

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Every year, millions of people line the streets of New York City or tune in at home to watch the Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade, but few people know the ins and outs of the full-time, year-round work that makes the spectacle possible. In honor of the 100th Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade, Hoda Kotb, who has hosted the event since 2018, brought AD for a look inside the 72,000-square-foot studio where all of the floats, balloons, and costumes are designed and constructed. Read on to discover the most interesting behind-the-scenes tidbits that we learned.

Inside the Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade studio

“Everything you see in the parade is done right here in the studio,” says executive producer Will Coss, showing off the massive warehouse where more than 75 people, spread across costume, sculpting, metal shop, engineering, carpentry, and balloon fabrication departments, work a collective 50,000 hours each year. Before the Macy’s team moved into their current space, the magic happened inside a former Tootsie Roll factory in Hoboken, New Jersey.

Hoda Kotb and Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade executive producer Will Coss

The first parade in 1924 featured animals from the Central Park zoo, including elephants, lions, tigers, and bears. They were soon replaced with the giant character balloons that the parade is famous for today, but even that tradition used to look different. From 1928 to 1932, the balloons were released into the air and people were encouraged to find them for a cash reward. “That went on until things got a bit more challenging in the skies, because private aircraft would actually go and track them and try to grab them and bring them back down to Earth,” explains Coss. “But in 1931, there was a blue hippo that was released, never to be seen again. And this year, the blue hippo is making his triumphant return back to the Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade.”

In addition to the blue hippo, balloons of yore will be making a comeback for the 100th parade, including Felix the Cat, the first character balloon to appear in the parade, and Underdog, who hasn’t made an appearance at the parade in 42 years. Snoopy, the most frequently recurring balloon, will return this year as his alter ego Joe Cool for the first time.

Inside the Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade studio

“The 100th Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade has been in production since my first day here, five years ago,” says Coss. “This year the stakes are high; you only have one shot at the 100th.” To mark the occasion, there will be seven new balloons, six new floats, and one new “balloonicle,” or balloon vehicle.

Hoda Kotb welcoming AD to the Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade studio

From concept drawings to weatherproofing, it can take up to six months to complete a parade float. “While a lot of the work here requires a lot of planning, a lot of design, a lot of engineering, our end goal is to bring people together, to inspire joy, to create that new tradition,” says Coss, “whether it’s a family experiencing it for the first time or experiencing it for the 25th time.”
