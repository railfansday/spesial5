It's Time We Remember the Great Forgetting

Updated November 9, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

Why are we here? What is our purpose? How shall we live? We humans have probably been asking these big questions for as long as we have been human. Perhaps what makes us most human is that we all possess a consciousness that can ask them.

We understand our world through stories. Without meaning to, we built a prison for ourselves, and remembering who we truly are is what sets us free.

Here’s a Story of Us, and it begins with the Great Forgetting.

In his novel Ishmael, Daniel Quinn describes two stories humans have lived by. In one, the world belongs to us. In the other, we belong to the world. For nearly all of our history, we lived the second story. When we began to farm and settle down roughly 10,000 to 12,000 years ago, we started living the first.

So we moved indoors. We built houses, villages, and cities. Walls kept out the cold and the predators, and those dreaded “others.” They also kept us from seeing that we are part of nature, not apart from it. Slowly, our walls became a prison of separateness.

In a later book, The Story of B, Quinn named what happened next: the Great Forgetting. Our culture lost touch with the long human story that came before it, as if humanity began where civilization did.

Think about the last time you stood under a sky full of stars. Most of us have felt very small and very lucky at the same moment, an awe that is both humbling and connecting. How did we get here? What are we part of?

For a long time, the story of civilization ran in one direction. First came farming, then villages, and eventually temples. Göbekli Tepe threw that story into question. About 11,500 years ago, people who lived by hunting and gathering began raising enormous carved stone pillars on that hilltop, some more than 18 feet tall and weighing 10 tons or more. Archaeologist Klaus Schmidt put it simply: “First came the temple, then the city.”

That idea is mind-boggling. The quest to understand ourselves and commune with the divine may have helped lead us into civilization, which in turn made it easy to forget who we are.

Excavators have since found signs that people lived there, too, so Schmidt’s famous sequence is now debated. As psychologist Tracy Henley puts it, calling Göbekli Tepe a temple is “likely accurate, but perhaps also misleading.” For its builders, the line between sacred and ordinary was almost certainly blurred. That may be the first thing we forgot.

As people settled, stored food, and claimed land, leaving got harder, and the stories that once taught a band to share could be retold to explain why some deserved more. Over thousands of years, we built walls that protected us but also divided us, the haves from the have-nots, and all of us from nature.

The ladder of success in our ancient hunter-gatherer tribes was a step stool. At most, we might earn a little extra respect in our mostly egalitarian band, and life was hard enough that our end could come at any time. As we entered civilization, the ladder of success grew from large houses to castles to the tops of golden skyscrapers. As Lord Acton wrote in 1887, “Power tends to corrupt, and absolute power corrupts absolutely.” The key word is “tends.”

Nobody needs to be the villain here. We are victims of our own success. The drive for status lives in all of us. But when we left our tribes and nature to build civilization, the mismatch began to widen.

Over time, we stop noticing the walls at all. Winston Churchill put it this way in 1943: “We shape our buildings and afterwards our buildings shape us.” Not only that, we’ve forgotten that our true home had no walls at all.

Today we are running god-like software on Stone Age hardware. I call this Accelerating Evolutionary Mismatch. It's the Great Forgetting made visible.

And the clock is speeding up. On that graph, the time between our great revolutions shrinks from hundreds of thousands of years to a matter of decades.

That gap creates a prison that entraps us. Many of us feel anxious without a clear reason, overwhelmed by the sense that we can’t keep up with an accelerating treadmill. We feel lonely despite, or perhaps because of, our digital connectedness.

The Buddha taught that attachment is the root of suffering. Now, with our smartphones, we are attached to something we didn’t evolve to even hold. The walls that once sheltered us now imprison us.

Let’s reconsider the Garden of Eden. After Adam and Eve eat the fruit, “the eyes of them both were opened.” Perhaps that is the moment we became fully aware—conscious—of ourselves and began to wonder how we got here. With that awakening came the knowledge that we die and feed the trees. In the traditional reading, we were cast out of the garden.

Perhaps we walked out of the Garden on our own, seeking to connect with one another and find answers to the Big Questions. In seeking those answers, we left the Garden and then forgot where we came from. In trying to find our lives, we lost them.

“We are here to awaken from the illusion of our separateness.” –Thich Nhat Hanh

The first awakening, in the Garden, made us aware of ourselves. The next awakening is remembering that we all share the same home. As fellow Homo sapiens, we have always been part of the natural world.

Our fractured but interconnected world needs a story big enough to leave no one outside. I’m not talking about a new dogma. Our differences are real, and some are worth arguing about. But they aren't worth hating and killing one another over.

I don’t think we need a new story at all. As Quinn advocated, we need a Great Remembering.

“Out beyond ideas of wrongdoing and rightdoing, there is a field. I’ll meet you there.” –Rumi

We built the walls and forgot the Field was always our home.

We began our existence in nature, in a Garden, if you will. Perhaps the Field and the Garden are the same place, and the way back home is simply to remember. Long before anyone had the language of evolution, our greatest spiritual teachers had a simpler word for one human family. They called us neighbors.

We’ve all been in the Field, even if only for a moment. Think of the last time a storm or a power outage turned strangers into neighbors. Nobody asked who anyone voted for. For a little while, the walls weren’t there.

We built the walls together, so we’ll have to take them down together. When enough of us remember that we’re neighbors, we can connect and cooperate.

Our corridor to a thriving future is as narrow as our divisions and as wide as our cooperation.

We’re all neighbors in an interconnected world. Remembering who we truly are sets us free to love our neighbors as ourselves, and the Golden Rule, treating others as we wish to be treated, is how we practice that love.

Truth must be lived, just as oxygen must be breathed, to matter.

Our future will be revealed by the choices we make now.
