How to Trace AI Agent Decisions Across Blockchain Transactions

A blockchain transaction can tell us what happened. It cannot, by itself, explain why an AI agent decided to initiate it. Imagine an AI agent monitoring a decentralised finance application. It reads wallet balances, retrieves liquidity data, evaluates a possible transaction, calls a smart contract, and waits for confirmation. Eventually, the transaction appears on-chain. A monitoring dashboard shows its hash, gas consumption, status, and block number. Everything looks normal.

Then someone notices that the transaction moved assets into a position the user never intended to create. Where did things go wrong? Perhaps the agent interpreted an instruction incorrectly. Perhaps an RPC provider returned stale information. Perhaps the market changed after the agent calculated the expected outcome. Perhaps a policy check passed against an outdated configuration. Or perhaps the transaction executed exactly as designed, but the design itself failed to account for an important condition.

The transaction hash does not answer these questions. Neither does a conventional application log that says a tool call succeeded. To investigate the incident, engineers need to reconstruct the complete path from the original instruction to the final on-chain state. They need to understand which information the agent observed, how it interpreted that information, what action it proposed, which controls approved the action, and what happened during execution. This is where I believe observability needs to evolve.

My work and writing around production AI have repeatedly brought me back to a distinction that is easy to overlook: making a system capable of performing a task is not the same as making its behaviour understandable when something goes wrong. AI agents introduce additional uncertainty because they can select tools, generate plans, and change their approach based on intermediate results. Blockchain introduces another layer of complexity through asynchronous transactions, external data dependencies, network conditions, and state changes that may be difficult or impossible to reverse. Combining the two creates a system that needs visibility into both reasoning-related decisions and deterministic execution.

Blockchain applications need an observability layer that connects what an AI agent intended to do with what the infrastructure actually executed. That layer should not attempt to expose every internal model computation or turn private reasoning into an audit log. It should capture the operational evidence needed to explain decisions, enforce policies, investigate failures, and verify outcomes.

Traditional blockchain monitoring has a well-understood job. It tracks transactions, blocks, contract events, network health, gas usage, and other indicators of execution. These measurements remain essential. They tell us whether transactions were submitted, whether they were included in a block, whether execution succeeded, and how the network behaved. But consider what happens before an AI-generated transaction reaches the blockchain.

An agent receives a request, retrieves context, calls one or more tools, interprets their responses, evaluates possible actions, and produces a transaction proposal. That proposal passes through authorisation, signing, and submission before the blockchain processes it. Every stage can introduce a different failure. The agent may select the wrong contract. A retrieval service may provide outdated protocol information. An RPC provider may become unavailable. A policy engine may reject a valid transaction because it received malformed input. A signer may refuse an operation that exceeded their authorisation. The transaction may be accepted by an RPC endpoint but remain pending, or it may be included in a block and revert during execution.

A dashboard focused exclusively on the blockchain sees only part of this journey. Likewise, an AI application log may record that the model proposed a swap without establishing whether the proposal was authorised, signed, submitted, or confirmed. The problem is not that either monitoring system is inadequate within its own domain. The problem is that the two systems often describe different parts of the same operation without a shared way to connect them. An AI-native observability layer bridges that gap. It connects the original request, the agent's operational decisions, the data used to support those decisions, the policy checks, the transaction lifecycle, and the resulting on-chain state. The goal is not to replace blockchain explorers, node metrics, or application monitoring. It is to connect their evidence into a single operational picture.

Logs are useful for recording individual events. A service might log that it retrieved a balance, generated a transaction proposal, or received a transaction receipt. The difficulty begins when an operation crosses several services.

Suppose an agent retrieves data through an RPC gateway, obtains additional information from an indexer, sends a proposal to a policy service, and submits an approved transaction through a signing service. Each component produces its own logs, often with its own request identifiers and timestamps.

During an incident, engineers may have to search several systems and manually reconstruct the order of events. If an intermediate request was retried, if a response arrived late, or if the agent resumed a workflow after a timeout, that reconstruction becomes even harder. A timestamp alone is not enough to establish causality. Two events can occur close together without belonging to the same workflow, while events from one workflow may appear out of order because different services process them asynchronously.

This is why distributed tracing is a useful foundation. OpenTelemetry provides a vendor-neutral model for collecting traces, metrics, and logs across instrumented services. A trace can represent a larger operation, while individual spans describe the work performed by its components. For a blockchain-connected agent, one trace might represent the attempt to execute a particular user request. Its spans could include context retrieval, transaction planning, policy evaluation, simulation, signing authorisation, RPC submission, and receipt verification. The resulting trace gives engineers a structured way to understand how the operation progressed. However, a trace alone does not establish that the operation was correct. It establishes the relationships between recorded operations and helps reveal where failures or delays occurred.

The observability layer must therefore combine tracing with domain-specific information: transaction identifiers, chain and block references, policy versions, execution status, and the outcome that the user originally requested.

I would design the architecture around six connected layers.

The diagram represents a logical architecture rather than a requirement to deploy every component as a separate service. A small application might implement several layers within one process. A larger platform may distribute them across multiple services. What matters is that each stage has a defined responsibility and emits enough structured evidence for the next stage to be observed.

The runtime records when an agent begins a task, which tools it invokes, how long each operation takes, and whether the agent produces a proposal or requests additional information. For example, the runtime might record that an agent evaluated three transaction routes, rejected two under its configured constraints, and selected the third for further validation.

It does not need to capture the private chain of thought or every intermediate token. It needs a concise operational record of the decisions and outputs that matter to the workflow.

The context layer records where decision-relevant information came from. For blockchain data, useful attributes may include the chain ID, block number, block hash, source provider, observation timestamp, and indexing checkpoint, where applicable.

These attributes help engineers determine whether an agent acted on current and consistent information. A block number should not be treated as a universal freshness guarantee. The system must also consider the network's finality characteristics, the source's progress, and the requirements of the specific operation.

The policy layer records which rules were evaluated and whether the proposal passed. Useful fields include a policy version, the result of each relevant check, a machine-readable rejection reason, and whether additional authorisation was required. This allows an investigator to distinguish between an agent that proposed an invalid action and a policy engine that incorrectly authorised it.

Sensitive policy details should be access-controlled. The observability system should not become a convenient source of information for someone attempting to bypass security controls.

The execution layer records the transition from proposed transaction to authorisation, submission, inclusion, and verification. The distinction between these states matters. A successful RPC response does not necessarily mean that a transaction was confirmed. A receipt with a successful execution status is stronger evidence of execution, but the application may still need to verify that the resulting state satisfies the original goal.

The final layer compares the intended outcome with the observed result. If the agent intended to transfer a specified amount to an approved destination, the system should verify the relevant transaction and state changes rather than relying solely on a success message returned by the execution service. For more complex operations, such as adjusting a DeFi position, the expected outcome may involve several balances, contract events, and protocol-specific state variables.

The verification rules should be defined for the application rather than inferred solely from the model's natural-language summary.

Traces, metrics, and structured audit records feed dashboards, alerts, incident investigations, and recovery workflows. An alert might identify repeated RPC failures, a growing number of transactions stuck in a pending state, an increase in policy rejections, or a mismatch between confirmed transactions and expected business outcomes.

The objective is to move beyond reporting that something failed and toward identifying which boundary failed, what evidence supports that conclusion, and what safe action should happen next.

Consider a hypothetical agent that is asked to exchange a limited amount of a stablecoin for another asset. The agent retrieves the wallet balance, queries a liquidity pool, calculates an expected output, and proposes a swap. The policy engine checks the spending limit and slippage tolerance. A secure signer authorises the validated transaction, and an RPC endpoint submits it. A useful trace should preserve the relationships between these operations.

This trace is illustrative. In a real implementation, some stages may run concurrently, be retried, or occur in separate traces linked through workflow identifiers. The important feature is the relationship between them. An investigator should be able to start with a failed transaction and work backwards to the exact proposal, policy decision, and observations that supported it. Equally, the investigator should be able to start with an agent decision and follow it forward to the transaction's outcome.

A trace should also distinguish between attempts and logical operations. If an RPC request is retried three times, those attempts should remain visible without making them look like three separate user requests. This distinction becomes particularly valuable when agents resume long-running workflows. A workflow may survive a process restart, wait for a transaction to be included in a block, and continue later. Its operational history should remain connected even if its execution crosses several processes or sessions.

For a production system, I would use a durable workflow or operation identifier to connect the lifecycle, while creating spans for individual execution attempts. Where operations cross tracing boundaries, trace context can be propagated through service calls or linked explicitly. There is one important limitation: traces can be sampled, dropped, or retained for shorter periods than audit records. A security-sensitive application should not rely on sampled traces as its sole record of financial authorisation or execution.

Observability and auditability overlap, but they serve different purposes. Traces help engineers investigate behaviour. A durable audit record preserves the evidence required to establish what the system authorised and executed.

A trace describes how work moved through the system. A decision record describes the operational basis for a consequential action. Whether that difference matters depends on the chain, the indexer's behaviour, the transaction, and the application's consistency requirements. A reporting agent might accept the indexed observation. An agent preparing a price-sensitive swap might require a newer observation or an independent validation step. The decision record should make the distinction explicit. It should also be updated as the operation progresses, or linked to append-only lifecycle events, so that the system can distinguish between a proposal, an authorisation, a submission, and a verified outcome.

For high-impact operations, I prefer a design in which the authorisation event is separately recorded and bound to the exact transaction parameters. This reduces the risk that a proposal will be changed after it passes validation. A record should never be treated as proof that an action occurred merely because it says the action was intended. The execution evidence must be independently established.

One of the most common mistakes in monitoring is treating every unsuccessful operation as the same kind of failure. A failed RPC request, an agent decision rejected by policy, a transaction that reverts, and a successful transaction that produces the wrong business outcome are fundamentally different events. They require different responses. I would classify failures by the layer where they occur and the evidence needed to resolve them.

Refresh, reconcile, or reject the decision

The proposal does not satisfy the task constraints

Reject the proposal and investigate the planning failure

Block execution and record the violated rule

Apply bounded retry or use an approved alternative source

The transaction remains pending or is replaced

Reconcile the known transaction state before taking action

Inspect the receipt and relevant execution details

Transaction succeeds, but the intended result is not achieved

Trigger investigation and an application-specific recovery process

Required telemetry or audit evidence is missing

Raise a monitoring-integrity alert and apply the appropriate fail-safe policy

The category alone does not determine the response. A timeout might be transient, while a timeout during transaction submission may leave the outcome uncertain. A policy rejection may be the correct behaviour rather than an incident. The system should preserve the distinction between an expected refusal, a recoverable operational failure, and a security-relevant anomaly. This is particularly important for AI agents. If a system is evaluated only on its task-completion rate, it may appear that policy rejections are failures to be eliminated. In reality, a correctly rejected unsafe transaction can be a sign that the controls are working.

The right metric is not simply how often the agent completes a task. It is whether the system completes legitimate tasks while reliably preventing unauthorised or invalid actions.

The first view should show operational health: RPC latency, provider errors, indexer lag, agent task latency, queue depth, and transaction lifecycle distribution. The second should focus on decision integrity: stale-context rejections, inconsistent-observation detections, policy rejection reasons, simulation failures, and the proportion of transaction proposals requiring refresh or human approval. The third should focus on outcomes: pending transactions, confirmed execution results, reverted transactions, unresolved submissions, and mismatches between intended and observed state changes.

Observability is most useful when it helps teams identify a deteriorating condition before it becomes a serious incident. For blockchain-connected AI agents, I would start with a small set of operational indicators and expand them as the system's risks become better understood.

Context freshness. Measure the age of decision-critical observations at the time a proposal is authorised, not only when the data is retrieved. Where appropriate, measure the difference between the observation's block reference and the relevant indexing checkpoint.

Decision rejection rate. Track proposals rejected because of invalid assumptions, missing information, or policy violations. Break the metric down by reason so that an increase can be investigated rather than treated as a generic failure.

Transaction lifecycle latency. Measure time spent preparing, validating, signing, submitting, waiting for inclusion, and verifying outcomes. This helps distinguish slow agent reasoning from slow blockchain infrastructure.

Unresolved transaction rate. Track operations whose final state cannot yet be established. A pending or uncertain transaction should remain unresolved until the application has sufficient evidence to classify it.

Outcome mismatch rate. Measure how often an operation reaches a technically successful execution state without achieving its defined application-level objective.

Audit completeness. Measure whether consequential actions have the required decision, authorisation, transaction, and outcome records. Missing evidence is itself an operational concern.

These metrics should have explicit definitions. For example, an outcome mismatch rate needs a clear definition of success for each operation type. A token transfer may be verified through the relevant transaction and state changes, while a more complex DeFi operation may require several application-specific checks. Thresholds should also be established from the application's risk model and observed baseline rather than copied from an unrelated system.

I would avoid optimising the entire platform around one composite reliability score. Averages can hide rare but serious failures. A low average latency does not compensate for an unsafe transaction, and a high task-completion rate does not establish that authorisation controls are effective. For critical controls, direct measurements of violations and execution outcomes are more useful than a single headline number.

An observability platform can contain valuable information about how an AI agent operates, which contracts it interacts with, what policies constrain it, and how its execution infrastructure is configured. That information needs protection.

Logging every prompt, tool response, wallet identifier, and transaction parameter without considering their sensitivity can expose financial activity or reveal details that attackers could use to target the system. I would apply data minimisation at the instrumentation layer. Record the information required to understand a decision, but avoid collecting private keys, signing credentials, recovery phrases, or other secrets. Sensitive prompt content and user information should be redacted or access-controlled according to the application's requirements.

High-cardinality fields also deserve attention. Putting arbitrary wallet addresses, transaction hashes, or user identifiers into metric labels can cause monitoring systems to consume excessive memory and storage. These values are generally better suited to trace attributes or structured records than to unrestricted metric dimensions.

Audit integrity is another consideration. A system should make it difficult for an ordinary agent process to alter or delete the records of its own consequential actions. Depending on the threat model, this can involve append-only storage, restricted write permissions, integrity checks, and separation between the execution service and the audit service.

A hash-linked audit log can help detect certain forms of modification, but it does not automatically prove that the recorded events are complete or truthful. If the application fails to record an event in the first place, an integrity mechanism cannot reconstruct it.

Finally, observability must not silently become a dependency that fails open. If the system cannot produce a required authorisation record for a high-impact transaction, it may need to block execution or require a controlled fallback. For a low-risk reporting task, temporary telemetry loss might instead trigger an alert while the task continues. The correct response depends on the risk and on which records are essential to safe operation.

A full observability platform can become complicated quickly. It is tempting to instrument every service, define dozens of metrics, and build a large dashboard before understanding which questions the team actually needs to answer. I would start with one consequential workflow and instrument it end-to-end.

For example, select a transaction-preparation workflow that reads contract state, generates a proposal, applies policy checks, and submits an authorised transaction. Assign a stable workflow identifier, connect the relevant spans, and record the minimum decision and execution evidence.

Next, introduce controlled failure tests. Simulate an unavailable RPC provider, an indexer that falls behind, a policy rejection, a reverted transaction, and a submission timeout. Confirm that each scenario produces a distinct classification and that engineers can reconstruct the workflow without manually searching unrelated logs.

Then introduce the dashboard and define the expected behaviour for unresolved states. Ensure that an uncertain submission cannot trigger a duplicate transaction simply because an agent assumes the previous attempt failed. Only after these basics work would I expand the architecture to additional workflows and services. This approach makes observability an engineering capability that grows with the system rather than a separate reporting project. It also gives the team something measurable to improve: fewer unexplained failures, faster incident investigation, more complete decision records, and stronger evidence that authorisation and execution behave as intended.

As AI agents become more capable of interacting with financial infrastructure, the meaning of a successful operation will need to become more precise. A successful model response does not necessarily mean that a valid transaction was proposed. A successful RPC call does not mean that a transaction was confirmed. A confirmed transaction does not necessarily mean that the user's intended outcome was achieved.

Each stage needs its own evidence, and those stages need to remain connected. This is the architectural shift I find most important: moving from monitoring individual components to observing the complete lifecycle of an autonomous decision.

Blockchain explorers will continue to provide valuable visibility into on-chain activity. Application logs will continue to explain local execution. Distributed traces will continue to connect work across services. But AI-driven applications need an additional layer that connects those sources to the intent, constraints, and outcome of each consequential action.

That layer must also recognise its own limits. Observability can help engineers understand what happened, detect unexpected behaviour, and improve recovery. It cannot guarantee that an agent will never make a bad decision, replace a sound authorisation model, or reverse a transaction that has already been confirmed.

Its purpose is to make the system's behaviour sufficiently visible that problems can be detected, investigated, and addressed before uncertainty becomes an unmanaged operational risk. For me, this is one of the important engineering challenges in moving AI beyond demonstrations and into production. The more autonomy we introduce, the less acceptable it becomes to treat the final output as the only evidence that matters.

We need to observe not just what the agent did, but the path from its intended action to the verified result. In blockchain applications, that path crosses probabilistic reasoning, external data, policy decisions, distributed services, and deterministic execution. Making those boundaries visible is what turns observability from a collection of dashboards into a foundation for operating autonomous systems responsibly.

The next generation of AI infrastructure will need to make these systems not only capable of acting, but also understandable when their actions do not go as planned.
