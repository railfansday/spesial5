Lawmakers Criticize FAA Deploying New “Untested” AI Tool Days Before Nationwide Grounding

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

A group of 24 Democratic lawmakers has criticized the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) for prematurely deploying a new artificial intelligence (AI) tool across US airspace. In a letter to FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford on Tuesday, the lawmakers accused the agency of expanding a largely untested system nationwide before completing its promised 90-day validation period.

The nationwide deployment of the Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes, and Trajectories (SMART) system came just three days before a telecommunications failure brought US departures to a standstill. While the FAA has denied any connection between the two events, the controversy raises fresh questions about whether the Trump administration's ambitious air traffic control (ATC) modernization program is prioritizing the speed of implementation over adequate testing, safety oversight, and transparency.

Led by House Transportation and Infrastructure Committee Ranking Member Rick Larsen, the lawmakers allege that the FAA expanded the SMART system nationwide without notifying Congress in advance. The expansion came just 50 days after its initial deployment in the Washington area, despite the FAA previously outlining a 90-day validation period.

In their letter to Bedford, the lawmakers expressed frustration with the lack of supporting safety data.

“We have yet to receive any substantive information proving the system was properly validated before its nationwide expansion.”

Concerns surrounding SMART considerably predate the nationwide grounding. In September, the National Air Traffic Controllers Association (NATCA) told Flying Magazine that it had been excluded from the system's development, testing, and implementation. Lawmakers subsequently requested that the FAA suspend SMART operations and called for an independent Government Accountability Office (GAO) audit. More troublingly, the latest letter cites allegations that FAA employees who raised safety concerns about SMART's accelerated deployment may have faced retaliation.

The lawmakers have presented the FAA with 13 questions, demanding details on its safety assessments, performance metrics, controller training, cybersecurity protections, and contingency plans if SMART experiences an outage. They also question why the agency prioritized deploying SMART over essential infrastructure upgrades, including radar replacements. Reuters reported that the FAA has declined to address the allegations publicly, saying it will respond directly to lawmakers.

Developed by Air Space Intelligence (ASI) under an $875 million, 12-year contract, SMART is designed to make the increasingly congested US airspace more efficient. The cloud-based technology consolidates approximately 200 data streams, encompassing weather forecasts, flight trajectories, airport capacity, and controller staffing. Its predictive capabilities allow aviation professionals to anticipate operational bottlenecks and identify alternative flight paths before disruptions materialize.

Importantly, SMART does not autonomously direct aircraft. FAA specialists at a dedicated center in Washington review its recommendations before forwarding them to the agency's Air Traffic Control System Command Center in Warrenton, Virginia. Human controllers retain ultimate operational authority. The technology is similar to ASI's Flyways AI platform, which Alaska Airlines began using in 2021 to help flight dispatchers optimize aircraft routing.

The potential commercial benefits are substantial, and United Airlines, Delta Air Lines, American Airlines, and Southwest Airlines have all expressed support for the initiative. According to the FAA, and as reported by Aerotime, SMART's initial deployment delivered measurable efficiency improvements, potentially translating into considerable fuel savings and fewer passenger disruptions when replicated nationally. However, lawmakers argue that evidence of improved operational efficiency cannot substitute for comprehensive safety validation.

There is little margin for error in air traffic control.

The concerns come amid the shock of the November 13 nationwide ground stop, which demonstrated the vulnerability of America's existing ATC infrastructure. The FAA's preliminary investigation identified a telecommunications failure, with excessive data traffic overwhelming legacy equipment following routine maintenance work. The resulting instability affected communications and radar feeds across much of the country. Both the FAA and ASI have explicitly denied that SMART contributed to the disruption.

The incident was the first nationwide ground stop since the January 2023 failure of the FAA's NOTAM system. A September 2026 GAO report highlighted significant shortcomings in the FAA's modernization cost estimates and scheduling. Previous research found that 105 of the agency's 138 ATC systems were either unsustainable or potentially unsustainable, illustrating the urgency of replacing aging infrastructure while maintaining the integrity of existing operations.

Congressional scrutiny is set to intensify next week, with Bedford expected to appear before a House panel on December 1 and Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy scheduled to testify before the Senate on December 2. Duffy's request for approximately $50 billion in additional aviation infrastructure funding, including $600 million for strategic traffic flow management technologies such as SMART, already faces significant opposition. Securing that funding may increasingly depend on the FAA demonstrating that its accelerated modernization efforts are not compromising safety.
