Today’s better lounges offer actual meals and craft cocktails. When I visited Chase’s new Dallas–Fort Worth lounge, I found the best food inside a bank lounge that isn’t a Capital One Landing. Plus they have whiskey tastings, and facial treatments. The space is 18,000 square feet. These spaces are no longer just a room with seating, snacks and a fax machine. The same improvements help explain why lounges fill up. People go for the food People who might have stopped in to check e-mail now plan to eat a meal. They come more often and stay longer. That brings in more people, including travelers who wouldn’t buy a separate airline club membership. Uncertain security waits get people to the airport earlier. Even without an increase in the average security wait, greater uncertainty can mean more time in the airport. I laid out these problems for Delta years ago. Delta also limits entry before an originating flight to three hours prior to scheduled departure, with an exception for connections. You Could Buy Your Way Into Lounges In The 1960s, Too One commenter remembers the 1990s as a time when people earned their way in by flying. Comfort Can Spread Even When Status Can’t The discussion of aspiration is revealing. But you can also just aspire to a more comfortable trip, get it, and be pleased that other people can get it too. A product can also become less distinctive to its old customers while becoming an attainable aspiration for new ones. I don’t usually bother with Centurion lounges, they aren’t as nice as Capital One and Chase lounges in many airports and there are too many people waiting to get in. When there’s a choice, I’ll pick elsewhere. But some of those Chase (Las Vegas! Phoenix!) and Capital One lounges (Denver!) can make the wait not worth the squeeze, too. Banks are selling more access than they can deliver. Sometimes I’ll just take the restaurant in the terminal. The discussion of aspiration is revealing. Some people feel that accessibility is the inverse of aspiration – that’s true if what you’re aspiring to is being above other people. But you can also just aspire to a more comfortable trip, get it, and be pleased that other people can get it too.

A product can also become less distinctive to its old customers while becoming an attainable aspiration for new ones. The people entering and the people complaining aren’t necessarily the same people.

That’s why the comparison to remote work is revealing – someone getting to work from home doesn’t take away the time you save on your commute working from home. Treating it as a status symbol entails a tradeoff (‘when everyone has status, nobody does’).
