United Airlines To Finally Bring NEXT Cabin Retrofits To Its 21 Boeing 757-300s

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

United Airlines plans to modify its Boeing 757-300 fleet to the United Next cabin standard, bringing seatback entertainment, larger overhead bins, and refreshed interiors to 21 aircraft that average roughly 24 years old. The plan surfaced in a post on Tuesday by aviation analyst JonNYC, who quoted United’s modification notice describing the work. The 757-300 has been the most visible holdout in a program that has already transformed much of the airline’s narrowbody fleet.

The scope is broad. According to the notice, the aircraft will receive new Boeing Space Bins, Panasonic Audio Video On Demand, inflight entertainment at every seat, a full-cabin in-seat power system, new class dividers and cabin headers, and updated United branding, while all seats and monuments will be refreshed. United has not published a schedule, and travelers may continue to face the old cabin until modifications are completed.

Entertainment will be the most noticeable shift for passengers. The 757-300 currently relies on entertainment streamed to a passenger’s own device. That arrangement dates to an earlier reconfiguration, when DIRECTV seatback screens were removed in favor of streaming and power outlets were limited to rows 1 through 23. Installing screens and universal power therefore reverses a cost-saving decision, and one trade publication notes the plan reverses an earlier cabin approach that removed seatback screens from domestic aircraft.

Storage should improve as well. Existing bins are relatively small and often require passengers to place standard carry-on bags sideways. The replacement Space Bins are designed to hold more bags, although whether the 757-300 versions would match that capacity has not been confirmed. Cabin density is unlikely to change, since each aircraft is configured with 234 seats, a layout United adopted when it added 21 economy seats and kept 24 in first class under a plan reported in 2016.

Certification was the main obstacle. The larger bins were not previously certified for the 757, and United had to lead the certification effort because few carriers still fly the -300 model. Delta Air Lines is the only other US operator of the type. A small global fleet offers little incentive for a supplier to fund engineering work, which explains why the program reached the 757-300 only after the Airbus and 737 variants.

Retrofit economics also shaped the timing. United’s broader United Next program dates to a June 2021 announcement that projected 99% completion by summer 2025. That target slipped, and the airline reported in March that 70% of its narrowbody retrofit plan was complete. Against that backdrop, a 21-aircraft subfleet needing bespoke certification naturally fell to the end of the queue.

United has reshaped its premium transcontinental plans as the MAX 10’s role pivots toward a higher density domestic workhorse.

Spending on cabin hardware generally implies an airline expects to keep an aircraft flying. Some members of the aviation community will interpret the work as a sign that United sees a continuing role for the 757-300 for many years. That view sits somewhat uneasily with earlier expectations. The A321neo and 737 MAX 10 are intended to replace the type, so the retrofit may serve as a bridge while those deliveries arrive, rather than a permanent commitment.

The routes involved are significant. The 757-300s have flown San Francisco to Chicago, Los Angeles to Washington Dulles, and Los Angeles to Chicago. All those routes are long, high-demand domestic markets where inconsistent product is most noticeable to premium customers. Standardizing the cabin also simplifies the airline’s marketing, since customers booking a United narrowbody will more reliably receive the same amenities.

It is important, though, to treat the details as reported rather than formally announced, as United has not confirmed the work yet. The primary source is JonNYC’s post, which reproduces what appears to be United’s internal modification language, and the airline has not issued a public release with dates, aircraft counts per quarter, or final bin specifications. Until it does, the scope is clear, but the timeline is not.
