Bill for Birmingham's Oracle debacle reaches £128M

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

True cost may be much higher for delayed SAP replacement that helped bankrupt the city

Birmingham City Council faces a £128.1 million bill for its disastrous transition from SAP to Oracle – more than six times the original estimate for a project that contributed to its financial collapse.

Papers presented to its Audit Committee this week put the combined cost of the original implementation, which went live in 2022, and this year's reimplementation at £128.1 million, against £19.96 million forecast in the 2019 business case [PDF].

The report, from program director Philip Macpherson, said the project's funding allocation had previously reached £157 million.

The report said the new Oracle Fusion system went live in August without any priority-one or "showstopper" issues.

"The program has now moved into the Post Go Live Support phase where [it] continues to provide extra support but slowly transitions towards 'Business As Usual' operating procedures," the report said.

It is an understatement to say the same was not true of the first attempt to move the council's finance, HR, and payroll systems off its legacy R/3 SAP system to more modern software.

The first project started in 2019 and was supposed to go live in April 2021, but was delayed by a year. After the council started to use the system in April 2022, it soon became beset by a series of serious problems.

Although the council had planned to implement Oracle "out-of-the-box," it created several customizations, including a bank reconciliation system that failed to function properly. The council struggled to understand its cash position and was unable to produce auditable accounts. It has spent more than £5 million on manual workaround labor. The system move also contributed to it becoming effectively bankrupt, an event it failed to foresee due to the malfunctioning finance system.

It later emerged that officials ignored a "red" warning prior to the go-live date. They were explicitly told the system could cause late payments, a failure to collect debt, and incorrect accounting if it went live. Officials gave the go-ahead regardless.

The council decided to reimplement Oracle from scratch, while running the current system as best it could and implementing off-the-shelf software CivicaPay, now rebranded as Civica Income Management.

Despite the apparently trouble-free relaunch, the true cost to the council is likely higher than the projected £128.1 million implementation bill.

For a start, the original £19 million budget was supposed to support the software implementation for schools, but that was excluded from the reimplementation.

Secondly, the council expected significant savings from moving to Oracle in 2022 through greater efficiency. The 2019 business case booked £27 million in savings from 2019 to 2031, for example.

A 2024 analysis by Audit Reform Lab, based at the University of Sheffield, estimated that the disaster's direct and indirect financial impact, including lost savings and deteriorating tax collection, would reach £216.5 million by April 2026.

Council taxpayers can be assured the new system has gone live without major problems, but that is hardly cause for celebration. ®
