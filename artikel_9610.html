Verra Raises $5M From Kearny Jackson to Bring AI Agents to the $1.1T Consulting Industry

Global management consulting is a $1.1 trillion industry whose largest firms have committed billions of dollars to AI since 2023, from Accenture's $3 billion to KPMG's $2 billion. What those firms sell is expertise, yet a new engagement usually starts with a junior analyst searching old decks and past projects, because knowledge workers spend about 19% of their time searching for and gathering information.

Verra, a New York startup that builds an institutional memory layer for consulting firms, has raised a $5 million seed round led by Kearny Jackson, with participation from Petra, a16z speedrun and strategic angel investors. Founded in December 2025, the company reached seven-figure revenue within seven months of commercial launch and now runs across Berkeley Research Group, where 1,000 people use it to start client work from the firm's best previous thinking.

Consulting revenue has grown about 3.6% a year since 2020 to reach $1.1 trillion in 2025, while the industry is now rebuilding itself around AI. The Big Four have committed more than $10 billion to AI since 2023, with EY putting $1.4 billion into its EY.ai platform and PwC $1 billion into generative AI over three years.

The money is already reshaping the work. About 20% of BCG's 2024 revenue, or $2.7 billion of $13.5 billion, came from AI-related advisory, while McKinsey says 40% of its client work now involves AI. Firms that sell expertise are learning that their most valuable AI asset is the record of what they have already done for clients, which no outside model has seen.

A new engagement rarely starts from the firm's best work. Someone searches through old decks and previous projects, pieces together whatever turns up and rebuilds the deliverable for the new client, a process that takes about 80 hours to reach a first draft. Research from the McKinsey Global Institute found that interaction workers spend 19% of their week searching for and gathering information, or about 1.8 hours a day.

At firm scale, those hours add up fast. For a firm of 1,000 consultants working 2,000 hours a year, 19% comes to about 380,000 hours a year spent looking for information (our arithmetic). The first wave of AI tools helped with parts of the problem, since they could retrieve documents or generate text, but someone still had to turn that output into a working model or a client-ready deck.

Verra runs inside the firm's own cloud and learns from the proposals, models, reports and deliverables the firm has already produced. It distils how each practice works into reusable skills, which are packaged ways of doing a task the way that practice does it, then gives AI agents that context to produce new client work directly inside Word, Excel and PowerPoint.

An agent can find the relevant precedent, build the analysis, create the model and turn it into a presentation while linking every claim back to the page, cell or source it came from. Permissions mirror the firm's existing structure, so consultants only see files they could already open, while the platform carries SOC 2 Type II certification and offers a single-tenant deployment inside the firm's own cloud.

Across Verra's deployments, the time to a first draft of a client deliverable falls from roughly 80 hours to between 10 and 15 hours. That is 81% to 88% less time, or a draft five to eight times faster (our arithmetic), with the firm's best previous thinking built in from the first hour.

The saving matters because of how consulting is priced and staffed. Hours that used to go into rebuilding a starting point can go into analysis, client conversations and judgment, which is the work clients pay senior people for. The sources attached to every claim also shorten review, since a manager can check where a number came from without redoing the research.

Berkeley Research Group, a global consulting firm working in corporate finance, economics, disputes and investigations, rolled Verra out to 1,000 people within two months of a proof of concept, driven by demand from inside the firm. Verra now runs across BRG under the firm's own brand. Since August, its consultants have used it to generate 105,000 files and make 145,000 edits directly inside open Office documents.

That works out to about 920 files and 1,270 edits a day. Among onboarded teams, more than 87% of consultants use Verra every week, while BRG estimates the savings at thousands of analyst hours a week. The Keystone Group, one of Verra's first customers, has also deployed it firmwide.

Verra was founded in December 2025 by Kashyap Nathan, its CEO, who previously worked at OpenAI and Palantir, together with Christopher Farrington, who previously worked at Deloitte. The two started the company after watching consultants repeatedly search for work their own firms had already completed.

They built the product around three facts about professional services. Client data cannot leave the firm, so Verra runs inside the customer's cloud. Consultants will not trust an answer they cannot verify, so every output carries its sources. AI does not get adopted if it forces people out of the tools they already use, so Verra works inside Office files, while the company's engineers build practice-specific workflows inside each firm in days.

Their wider view is that AI will make raw intelligence cheap and widely available, which leaves a firm's own record of decades of client work as the advantage that is hardest to copy. Verra's goal is to make that knowledge compound, so each engagement leaves the firm smarter for the next one.

The $5 million seed round is led by Kearny Jackson, with participation from Petra, a16z speedrun and strategic angel investors. Verra will use the money to accelerate product development, hire and build integrations with the enterprise knowledge systems that consulting firms already run.

The round comes less than a year after founding and after the company had already reached seven-figure revenue, mostly through adoption that spread inside customers once it landed. That pattern, with a product pulled into new practice groups by the consultants using it, is the core of the case investors are backing.

Kearny Jackson is a San Francisco seed firm co-founded by Sriram Krishnan, a former product manager at Tinder and Spotify, together with Sunil Chhaya, previously at Menlo Ventures and Tenaya Capital. Its $65 million Fund III counts Marc Andreessen and Sequoia among its limited partners, while its portfolio includes MotherDuck, Cortex, Comulate and EigenLayer.

Krishnan, who has worked in consulting, points to how much consulting time goes into rinse-and-repeat work because a firm's knowledge is hard to reuse. What stood out to the firm was how quickly Verra became critical inside the firms using it, with consultants adopting it and asking for more. Kearny Jackson's view is that Verra is becoming the memory and operating layer for how consulting firms work, a position that grows more valuable with every engagement it records.

Verra plans to expand from individual practices into firmwide intelligence, then into adjacent sectors including accounting, advisory and investment research. It will also let customer engineering teams build on top of the platform without recreating the underlying infrastructure themselves.

If AI makes general intelligence a commodity, the firms that win will be the ones that remember best. Verra is building for that world, where every consultant starts each engagement with everything the firm has ever learned already at work.
