What's negotiable in a severance package: 10 terms to raise before you sign

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

What's negotiable in a severance package goes beyond the check, from COBRA premiums and equity vesting to release terms, references and payment timing

Private employers in the U.S. have no federal obligation to pay severance. That fact shapes any conversation about leaving a job with a package. When an employment contract, a written company policy or a union agreement sets the terms, the employer may be bound by them. When none of those applies, the package is an offer, and an offer can be answered with a counteroffer.

Employers usually attach a condition to that offer. Severance is typically paid in exchange for signing a release of legal claims against the company. That release is the main thing the employer receives in the deal. The employee is giving up something of value. As a result, the rest of the separation agreement becomes part of the exchange, not just the size of the check.

That is why the question of what's negotiable in a severance package reaches well beyond pay. A separation agreement can address how long health coverage is subsidized and what happens to unvested stock. It can settle whether a partial-year bonus is paid and how much time a worker gets to review the document. It can also set the wording of the release and the reach of any non-compete or confidentiality clause. Other terms can cover the reference a former manager gives and the way the money arrives.

Some of these terms rest on federal law. These include the WARN Act's notice rules and COBRA health continuation. They also include the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act's minimum review periods for workers aged 40 or older. Others depend on plan documents, award agreements or state law. Many are purely contractual, meaning they exist only if both sides agree to write them down.

The 10 items below are a selection of terms that commonly come up in severance package negotiation. They are not a complete list, and not every employer will agree to every request. Each slide explains what the term covers, what rules shape it and what an employee can ask for.

This guide is limited to the U.S. Severance rules and notice requirements differ from country to country. It is general information, not legal advice. An employment lawyer can review a specific agreement against the contract, policies and state law that apply to it.

No federal law requires private employers in the U.S. to pay severance. When no employment contract, company policy or union agreement fixes the amount, the figure in an employer's offer is a starting point that can be negotiated.

Severance pay is usually set in one of four ways. An individual employment contract may spell it out. A written company policy or severance plan may lay out who qualifies and how much they receive. A collective bargaining agreement may cover it for union members. Or the employer may simply make an offer at the time of separation.

Each source affects how much room there is to bargain over money. A contract or union agreement that names a figure creates an obligation the employer has already committed to in writing. A written policy can work the same way. It can also cap the amount the employer is prepared to pay. An offer made with no document behind it leaves the most room to move.

One practical step is to find out which source applies. That means rereading an offer letter or employment agreement for any clause on termination or separation pay. It also means checking the employee handbook, benefits materials or any separate severance plan document the company has distributed. Union members can look to the terms of their collective bargaining agreement.

If a written policy exists, compare the offer with it. An employee can ask directly whether the proposed amount matches the policy, exceeds it or falls short of it. A shortfall is a concrete point to raise. A match signals that the dollar figure may be harder to move. That can shift attention to other terms in the package.

Where none of those documents applies, the number in the offer reflects the employer's own business decision. An employee in that position can reply in writing with a different figure. The employee can also ask the employer to explain how it arrived at the proposed amount.

The federal WARN Act generally requires employers with 100 or more employees to give 60 calendar days' notice before a plant closing or mass layoff. Pay and benefits covering that period can stand in for the advance warning itself.

That substitution affects what a severance offer contains. Pay in lieu of notice under WARN is tied to a legal requirement. It is not the same thing as a voluntary severance payment, even when an employer provides both at the same time.

An employee can ask the employer to state in writing which part of the package covers the notice period and which part is severance. When the two are blended into one figure, it is harder to see how much of the offer goes beyond what the law already required.

WARN does not reach every exit. An employer with fewer than 100 employees falls outside the federal rule. So does a single termination that is not part of a plant closing or mass layoff. For those departures, notice is a matter of contract.

An employment agreement, an offer letter or a written company policy may already set a notice period. Where none of them does, the two sides can settle the length of notice in the separation agreement. An employee whose contract specifies notice can also check whether the offer honors it.

Garden leave is one way to structure a contractual notice period. The employee stays on the payroll through the notice period but is not expected to do any work. The details come from the agreement, not from federal law.

Several garden-leave terms are open to discussion. They include how long the period runs and whether benefits continue during it. Another is whether the employee may start a new job before it ends.

Pay in lieu of notice outside WARN follows the same contractual logic. The employer pays for the notice period instead of having the employee work through it. The job then ends sooner than it otherwise would.

An employee who is unsure whether WARN applies can ask how many workers the action affects. Another useful question is whether the employer treats it as a covered plant closing or mass layoff.

COBRA generally lets covered employees keep their group health coverage for up to 18 months after a job loss. An employer can agree in a severance package to pay part or all of the premium for a set period.

COBRA protects access to coverage, not its price. The former employee typically pays the full premium plus up to 2 percent for administration. That brings the total to as much as 102 percent of the premium.

The full premium includes any portion the employer covered while the person was on the payroll. As a result, the monthly bill after a layoff can be much higher than the health deduction that used to appear on a pay stub.

That gap is why a premium subsidy is a common request in a separation agreement. Under a subsidy, the employer pays some or all of the COBRA cost for a defined number of months. The worker keeps the same group coverage. What changes is who pays for it.

A subsidy's terms are open to negotiation like the rest of the package. A departing employee can ask for a longer subsidy period, a larger share of the premium or both. If more than one person is enrolled under the worker's plan, the request should spell out whose coverage the subsidy applies to.

Precise wording matters. A clear agreement states the start and end dates of the subsidy. It also states the dollar amount or percentage the employer will cover. It should say what happens if the former employee gains coverage through a new job before the subsidy period ends. Vague terms on those points can lead to disputes later.

Plan details also shape what a worker can expect. Coverage length and cost can vary with the plan and with the size of the employer. The 18-month period and the 102 percent ceiling are general rules, not guarantees for every worker. Checking the plan's own terms with the employer's benefits staff before signing shows what a subsidy would actually be worth.

A premium subsidy is a separate term from the cash payment. It can be requested even when the employer will not move on the severance pay figure itself.

Unvested stock options, restricted stock units and similar awards are governed by the equity plan and the individual award agreement, not by the severance offer alone. A separation agreement can change those default terms by accelerating vesting or by giving a departing employee more time to exercise options.

Vesting is the schedule that turns a grant into something the employee actually owns. An award that has not vested by the last day of work is handled however the plan documents say it should be. That makes those documents the starting point for any request. The plan sets the default rules. The award agreement sets the terms of each specific grant, including its vesting schedule and what happens when employment ends.

Acceleration is a request to vest some or all of the unvested portion as part of the exit. The ask can cover a full grant, a fixed number of shares or options, or the tranche that would have vested next. Whether an employer agrees depends on its own plan and on its willingness to depart from the standard terms in this particular agreement.

The exercise window matters only for stock options. An option is the right to buy shares at a set price, and the holder pays that price to exercise it. A restricted stock unit, by contrast, delivers shares or their value once it vests and does not require an exercise payment. Options left unexercised when the post-employment period closes are handled under the plan's terms. A separation agreement can extend that period beyond what the plan provides.

Incentive stock options (ISOs) carry a tax condition. They generally lose their favorable tax treatment if exercised more than three months after employment ends. A longer window still gives an ISO holder more time to decide whether to buy. Any exercise after that point, though, generally gives up the ISO tax advantage. A tax adviser can weigh that trade-off before the agreement is signed.

Non-qualified stock options do not have a single standard post-termination exercise period. Plans differ, and the award agreement is the place to find the actual deadline. An extension negotiated in a separation agreement can state the new deadline in writing. It can also specify which grants the extension covers.

Money already in motion at the time of a layoff — a bonus, a commission or unused vacation — can often be negotiated. Whether accrued vacation must be paid out depends on state law and company policy. Bonus and commission payouts depend on plan terms and contract language.

Accrued paid time off can carry a legal floor. Some states treat earned vacation as wages. Where that rule applies, a vacation balance is a pay question rather than a perk to bargain over. In other places, the company's written policy usually settles the matter. That makes the employee handbook and any PTO policy the first documents to read before a severance conversation.

Where neither state law nor company policy requires a payout, the unused balance becomes something to ask for. It can be raised alongside the severance figure itself rather than handled as a separate dispute.

Bonuses work differently. The answer usually sits in a bonus plan document, an offer letter or an employment contract. The exact wording matters. A plan might tie payment to company results, individual targets, a specific payout date or some mix of those conditions. Reading those terms closely shows whether a partial-year bonus is owed, partly owed or left to the employer's discretion.

Even when the plan does not require a payout, an employee can request one. The request can take two forms. A pro-rated bonus pays a share that reflects the portion of the year already worked. A full bonus pays the entire target amount, as if the employee had stayed through the bonus period. Either version can be written into the separation agreement.

Commissions follow similar logic. A salesperson may have deals that closed, or were close to closing, near the last day of work. The commission plan and any contract language determine whether those deals generate pay after departure. If the terms are unclear, the separation agreement can spell out which deals count, how much each one pays and when the money arrives.

Workers aged 40 or older must get at least 21 days to consider a severance agreement that releases age-discrimination claims. The minimum rises to 45 days when the offer is part of a group exit program. Signers then have seven days to revoke.

Those deadlines come from the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act. This federal law sets conditions on how employers can ask older employees to give up age-discrimination claims. The review period is a floor, not a ceiling. An employer can offer longer, and an employee who needs more time can ask for it.

The two lengths depend on how the separation is structured. An individual departure that is not part of a group program falls under the 21-day minimum. The 45-day minimum applies when the release is offered to a group of employees through an exit program.

Group programs carry an added requirement. The employer must disclose the job titles and ages of the employees selected for the program. It must also disclose the job titles and ages of those who were not selected. That information lets an older worker see how age lined up with the selection decisions before deciding whether to sign.

The seven-day revocation window runs after the signature, not before it. It gives the employee a chance to back out of the release after committing to it in writing. The review period and the revocation period are separate, and one does not replace the other.

Workers under 40 get no federal minimum review period. Any deadline in their offer is set by the employer. That makes the deadline a term like any other in the agreement. An employee can ask for an extension, especially when the document is long or touches equity, benefits and restrictive covenants.

A severance agreement often bundles many separate terms into one document. Time to read it closely, compare it against company policy and plan documents and raise questions comes before negotiating any of those terms.

Severance is typically paid only after the departing employee signs a release of legal claims against the employer. The wording of that release can be negotiated. It can be narrowed in scope, or made mutual so the employer also gives up claims.

A release is the clause in a separation agreement that gives the employer legal protection. In exchange for the severance payment, the employee agrees not to pursue claims tied to the job or the way it ended.

Release language is often drafted broadly. A standard version may cover every claim the employee has or could have, known or unknown, against the company. It may also extend to a long list of related parties. An employee can ask for edits that tighten that scope. One request is to limit the release to claims arising before the date of signing. Another is to spell out exceptions, such as the right to enforce the severance agreement itself.

A mutual release changes the balance of the document. Under a one-sided release, only the employee gives something up. Under a mutual version, the employer also agrees not to bring claims against the former employee. Some employees ask for this to reduce the risk of a later dispute over their conduct on the job. The employer may decline, and the request is a matter of bargaining rather than a right.

Some rights cannot be signed away, whatever the release says. These include claims for vested benefits, workers' compensation and unemployment benefits. A release also cannot remove an employee's right to file a charge with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. What an employee can give up is the right to collect money personally from such a charge.

The effect of a release depends on the facts of each case and the law that applies. Nothing here is legal advice. An employment lawyer can read the exact language before the agreement is signed.

Separation agreements often include restrictions on where a departing employee can work, who they can contact and what they can say or share. The duration, geography and scope of each restriction can be negotiated before the agreement is signed.

A non-compete limits where a former employee can work next. It usually sets a period of time, a geographic area and a definition of competing work. Each of those three elements is its own point of negotiation. A shorter period, a smaller territory or a narrower description of the competing business can each reduce how much the clause limits a job search.

Whether a non-compete can be enforced depends on state law. Those rules differ from one state to another. The same clause can carry different weight depending on where the employee works. An agreement may also include a choice-of-law clause naming the state whose law governs the contract.

The Federal Trade Commission issued a rule in 2024 aimed at non-compete agreements. A federal court blocked it. The rule never took effect.

A non-solicit clause works differently. It restricts a former employee from reaching out to clients, customers or co-workers to win their business or recruit them. An employee can ask to limit it to clients they personally handled. They can also ask for a shorter time frame. Another request is an exception for contacts who approach the former employee on their own.

Confidentiality terms cover an employer's nonpublic information. Some also cover the terms of the severance agreement itself. An employee can ask for a precise definition of what counts as confidential. They can also ask for written exceptions, such as conversations with a spouse, an attorney or a tax adviser.

Non-disparagement clauses bar negative statements about the employer. As drafted, these clauses often bind only the employee. One counter is to make the clause mutual. The employer would then agree not to disparage the departing worker. Some employees also ask the company to direct specific managers or executives to follow it.

Restrictive covenants in a separation agreement can stay in force after the severance payments end. Their wording shapes what a former employee can do in a next job, with past clients and in public comments.

An employee leaving under a severance agreement can ask for an agreed reference statement, an agreed internal and external announcement, and neutral wording of the departure. None of these is a legal entitlement. Each is a contractual ask that an employer can accept, modify or decline.

An agreed reference statement is a set text the employer commits to using when a prospective employer calls or writes about the departing worker. Its value lies in predictability. Both sides know in advance what will be said. The wording can also be reviewed before the agreement is signed.

A worker can ask who at the company will handle reference requests. Routing those inquiries to a named person or department sends them to someone bound by the agreed language. Without that step, a request may reach a former colleague who does not know the terms exist.

The announcement works the same way. An internal message tells colleagues that someone has left. An external one reaches clients, vendors or industry contacts. A departing employee can ask to see and approve the text of both before they go out, and can ask that they be issued at an agreed time.

Neutral wording covers how the exit itself is described. Phrases such as "terminated," "laid off" and "left the company" read differently to an outsider. A worker can ask that the agreement and any related documents use language that does not suggest fault.

Some employers will go further and agree to describe the exit as a resignation. That label can shape how the departure is characterized to future employers and former colleagues. It does not control the outcome of an unemployment claim, however. A state unemployment agency makes its own decision about eligibility. The wording in a separation agreement does not settle that question.

A worker who expects to file for unemployment benefits can weigh how a resignation label fits with that claim before agreeing to it.

These terms are only as dependable as the document that contains them. A spoken assurance about references or announcements is harder to point to later than language written into the signed agreement. Asking for the exact text, rather than a general promise, gives both sides the same words to rely on.

Severance can be paid as a single lump sum or spread out in installments, and an employee can negotiate that structure. The choice affects when the money arrives and how much federal tax is withheld from it.

A lump sum delivers the full amount in one payment. Installments divide the same total into a series of payments over a set period. These payments can follow the employer's regular payroll cycle. A series of payments spreads the income across a longer stretch. If the schedule runs long enough, it can extend into a new calendar year.

Federal income tax withholding on severance follows the rules for supplemental wages. An employer can withhold at a flat 22 percent rate on supplemental wages up to $1 million. Amounts above that threshold are withheld at 37 percent. Withholding is a prepayment toward tax, not the final bill. The amount actually owed is settled when the employee files a return. The flat rate can therefore end up higher or lower than a person's real tax liability.

Payment timing has limits of its own. Section 409A of the Internal Revenue Code sets rules for deferred compensation. Those timing rules can affect how a severance payment schedule is written. Anyone asking for a different schedule can have a tax adviser review how the change fits within those rules.

Severance can also interact with unemployment insurance. Whether severance pay reduces unemployment benefits depends on the state. The state unemployment agency is the place to check how a particular payment structure will be treated.

The agreement can also cover more than the payment itself. An employee can ask for outplacement services, which provide job-search support after the exit. A request for legal-fee reimbursement can cover the cost of having a lawyer review the agreement. Some employees ask to move the termination date later so it falls after an upcoming equity vesting date. None of these extras is guaranteed. Each is a request the employer can accept or decline.
