Staying properly hydrated has many health benefits—it promotes cardiovascular health, enhances skin elasticity, and eliminates waste from the body—and using a countertop water filter is an easy way to get safe, clean drinking water right in your kitchen. These user-friendly appliances either sit on your counter or attach to your faucet, removing common contaminants like chemicals, heavy metals, and bacteria without requiring permanent plumbing. Not only that, they help reduce the plastic waste associated with buying bottled water.

After selecting seven units for testing, we performed the same set of steps with each, starting with installation. Next, we performed water quality tests before and after filtration to measure contaminant reduction, followed by blind taste tests to rate flavor and odor. We measured flow rate by timing how long each unit took to process or dispense a quart of water, assessed how easy each unit was to clean and change filters on, and rated general convenience of extra features like filter life indicators and removable pitchers.

We used each model for a minimum of 2 days to get a feel for how much we liked them overall, and for the faucet-connected models, to see whether they got in the way during regular tasks like washing hands or dishes.

The best countertop water filtration system for your home will depend on the quality concerns you want to address, how much filtered water your household uses, and how much daily upkeep you’re willing to take on. It’s smart to request a copy of your local utility’s Consumer Confidence Report or use a home water test kit, then compare potential systems’ filtration claims, capacity, footprint, and replacement-filter cost.

Countertop water filters generally fall into two broad categories: reservoir-based systems and faucet-connected systems. Reservoir models don’t connect to your plumbing at all, while faucet-connected models attach to a standard faucet or use a diverter valve under your sink.

Reservoir systems hold untreated water in one tank or chamber and deliver clean water into another after it passes through the system’s filter(s). The simplest versions are water pitchers like the Epic Water Filters Pure XP Water Filter Pitcher or gravity systems like the Big Berkey Gravity-Fed Water Filter. Since they don’t connect to your faucet, they’re great for apartments, dorms, RVs, and kitchens with incompatible faucets.

Countertop reverse osmosis systems also use a refillable reservoir, but rather than relying on gravity, they have an internal pump that forces the water through their filters before delivering it to a pitcher or your glass. RO systems have the added benefit of reducing dissolved solids in untreated water, but that includes beneficial minerals like calcium and magnesium and also creates wastewater that must be dumped (or used for things like cleaning or watering plants).

Faucet-connected filters either attach directly to your faucet or connect via a hose and diverter valve. When the diverter is switched to the filtration setting, tap water filters through the unit on demand and into your glass, bottle, or pot. When the diverter is off, water flows straight out of the tap as normal.

Two major things to consider regarding faucet-connected systems are faucet compatibility and installation. Most aren’t compatible with pull-down, pull-out, spray, sensor-operated, or nonstandard faucets, and many also have small hoses that connect the faucet to the machine, which takes up a bit of space and can look cluttered. Some allow you to run the hoses from the machine to the plumbing under your sink, but that will require an adapter and a hole in the counter for the cords to route through.

While it can be tempting to choose the purification system for water that has the longest list of contaminants removed, it’s ultimately more important to pair the system you choose with what you’re actually trying to filter out of your water.

Most of the best water filters have activated-carbon filters that remove unpleasant tastes and odors and may reduce other chemicals or particulates. Some systems add sediment filtration or specialty media to target things like lead, fluoride, or PFAS compounds. Since performance varies by model, always check the manufacturer’s claims rather than assuming they all work the same.

Reverse-osmosis systems use a membrane designed to reduce dissolved solids, which means they generally remove more contaminants than basic filtration systems. However, they can also reduce beneficial minerals, which is why some manufacturers are now adding a remineralization stage to return the filtered water to a more alkaline state.

Many of the units on this list carry third-party certification to verify tested performance beyond manufacturer claims. NSF International’s drinking-water treatment standards are the most commonly used, although you may also see mentions of accredited organizations like IAPMO R&T or WQA. While our tests using the Varify Complete Drinking Water Test Kit for this guide backed up the manufacturers’ claims, it’s still a good idea to prioritize products that carry filter certifications and look up which contaminants the product is certified to remove.

Then, match the certification to your specific concern. For example, if lead reduction is your main goal, you’ll want a model with NSF 53 and 58 listings.

Capacity has two meanings: how much water a filter can treat before replacement and how much filtered water the unit can provide at once. For our purposes, we’re defining it as how much it can filter at once, as we discuss filter life in the next section.

Since reservoir models’ ready-to-drink supply is based on the size of their clean-water chambers, larger households or those with greater needs will want to prioritize a model that holds multiple liters or gallons. Faucet-connected models can also be a good choice in those situations, as they can deliver clean water continuously as long as the tap is on.

Flow rate—how quickly the filter provides clean drinking water—is another important consideration. However, many manufacturers don’t list this information, and it can also vary depending on water pressure, source-water quality, temperature, and cartridge age. If this is a major concern, you may want to reach out to manufacturers directly or read online reviews for mentions of filtration speed.

Every countertop drinking water filter system requires filter replacement on a set schedule, which means the true cost of ownership ends up being more than what you paid for it. When choosing between systems, compare the number of filters, their individual projected filter capacity, and each one’s replacement cost.

A basic pitcher may use a single cartridge that’s easy to change but needs more frequent replacement, while a multistage RO unit may have multiple filters that each have different change intervals and prices. While systems with filter-life indicators can make it much easier to know when it’s time to swap them, if your unit is noticeably slower or isn’t providing the same quality, you may still need to change filters even if it isn’t technically time.

Countertop water filters are generally pretty straightforward, but the following answers to commonly asked questions can answer any lingering queries you may have.

Water filters work by using a filtration mechanism to capture organisms and chemicals that lead to unsafe or cloudy water. A fabricated surface traps the larger contaminants, while a compound like activated carbon works as a magnet to trap smaller contaminants.

Installation depends on the type: A faucet-connected filtration system typically attaches to a compatible faucet aerator or diverter valve, while dispenser, pitcher, gravity-fed, and countertop RO models generally only require rinsing the parts, installing the filters, and filling the reservoir. Always follow the manufacturer’s instructions, especially for RO units that may also require an initial flush cycle.

No, countertop water purification filters do not work with every type of kitchen faucet. Specifically, more modern designs and detachable faucets with spray nozzles may be incompatible with faucet-connected countertop water filters.

Most filters can remove chlorine, fluoride, bacteria, and parasites. To know exactly which contaminants a specific filter can remove or reduce, consult the manufacturer’s information regarding filter certification.

Yes, bacteria can grow in a countertop filter, so it’s a good idea to flush the faucet for about 20 seconds after turning on the water and then for an additional 5 to 10 seconds to filter and flush out any bacteria that may be lingering in the faucet.

Yes, countertop reverse osmosis systems are worth it for households seeking to remove dissolved contaminants like lead and fluoride and who prefer a plumbing-free setup. However, they generally cost more and require more maintenance than pitchers or carbon-filter-only units, so they’re best when your water test or local report identifies concerns simpler filters can’t address.

Filter replacement timing varies by system and filter type, so it’s best to follow the manufacturer’s schedule for your specific system. Generally speaking, pitcher and faucet units may need new filters every few months, while countertop RO filters and membranes may last anywhere from 6 months to 2 years. You may need to replace filters sooner if the flow slows noticeably or your water develops an unusual taste or odor.
