Stop scheduling meetings over lunch

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Ever wonder what happened to the front porch?

The front porch used to play an important role in everyday life. Neighbors who weren’t necessarily close friends saw each other, stopped to talk, and maintained a sense of community. Then daily life moved indoors. Air-conditioning made staying inside more comfortable, television pulled families into the living room, and backyard patios offered more privacy so you didn’t have to say “hi” to the overly talkative neighbor.

There was no single moment when the front porch disappeared. It just gradually became less central to the way people connected, and faded away.

The lunch break is headed in the same direction.

Technology has made it possible to collaborate across locations and time zones, while AI is helping work move faster than ever. But constant connectivity has also weakened the boundaries around the workday. An open hour at noon can quickly become another meeting or a chance to catch up on work.

Peer pressure plays a role too. Employees see colleagues and leaders eating at their desks or scheduling meetings over lunch with a polite, “Sorry, it was the only free time I could find.” Eventually, skipping lunch becomes an unwritten expectation rather than an individual choice. And when that behavior is rewarded with recognition and career growth, the expectation becomes even harder to challenge.

Yet lunch is one of the few natural opportunities during the workday to step away and connect with colleagues without an agenda. It also gives employees a chance to decompress, take a walk, and mentally reset before tackling the rest of the day. Leaders who want sustainable performance should make sure that opportunity doesn’t disappear.

5 ways to protect lunch breaks 

You don’t have to mandate how employees spend lunch. Some people want to eat with colleagues. Others prefer to take a walk, run an errand, or simply be alone. 

The point is to create a culture where stepping away feels like a normal choice. And it requires looking at the signals your workplace sends about availability, performance, and connection.

1. Create space that can’t automatically become another meeting

If every open block on a calendar is considered schedulable, lunch will always be at risk. Consider establishing meeting-free windows during the day so employees have protected time to recharge.

Adapt that approach for global and frontline teams instead of assuming the same time works for everyone. Employees across time zones and operating environments may need different windows, but the principle is the same: Build recovery into the work day.

2. Model the behavior you want 

Culture is a collection of the norms, values, and behaviors that employees see around them every day. And nothing is more important for building culture than what employees see from their leaders. If leaders are regularly scheduling meetings during lunch, it’s a signal that the break isn’t protected. New employees in particular pick up such signals, and quickly learn which behaviors are associated with success (and failure) in their new team or organization.

Your own calendar also sends a powerful message. Protecting and taking your lunch break is critical, as is extending the same respect to your team during their breaks. These small, visible behaviors speak louder than words.

3. Treat skipped lunches as an important signal

Understanding and measuring workplace culture requires listening in different ways. Surveys alone don’t cut it. Paying attention to skipped lunches can be part of that strategy. And while working through lunch might ultimately be a personal preference, when it becomes the norm, it tells you something about your organization.

If employees feel they can’t step away for even 30 minutes without falling behind, that’s a strong signal that the always-on mentality is rampant in your organization. Burnout in these cultures can be prolific, and workloads, staffing levels, and meeting demands should be questioned.

But consider the unwritten expectations, too. What happens when employees say no? Is their commitment to the team or organization questioned? Are they no longer seen as top performers?

4. Use lunch to make human connection easier

Lunch offers us much of what the front porch did: unstructured time together. No agendas, no project tasks, no scheduled meetings. Instead, employees have space and time to build connections and relationships within and beyond their immediate teams.

That’s especially important for younger workers. Gen Z employees have 47% increased odds of saying they can’t find community at work, and 32% increased odds of saying technology makes it difficult to disconnect from work.

This research demonstrates that while technology makes it easier to stay in contact, it doesn’t guarantee genuine relationships. Employees can be constantly reachable and still feel they don’t know their colleagues at all.

Lunchtime gives employees room for conversations that aren’t tied to a project, deadline, or key performance indicator. When we protect this space, we create organic opportunities to build relationships and communities that are much more difficult to develop through structured interaction.

5. Reward outcomes instead of constant availability

Employees pay attention to who and what is getting recognized. If you want to shift behavior, change what you reward. Recognition is a powerful tool for change management.

Focus recognition on the outcomes and behaviors you want to encourage. Make it clear that high performance doesn’t require employees to prove they’re always available. When organizations reinforce behaviors through integrated recognition, they see a 43% reduction in early warning signs of burnout.

The focus on outcomes becomes even more important as AI increases the speed and capacity of work. When employees can accomplish more in less time, resist the temptation to fill every available minute with more work. Be deliberate about rewarding strong outcomes rather than constant activity.

The case for keeping lunch on the calendar

As technology makes it easier to stay connected and accomplish more throughout the day, protecting time for a real break gets harder. 

Push back on that pressure by establishing clear boundaries and modeling them yourself. Giving employees permission to pause creates room to recover and connect, while reinforcing that performance doesn’t depend on always-on expectations.

How you approach lunch reflects the broader expectations you set for your workplace. By making breaks a regular part of the day, you can help establish healthier habits that become a lasting part of your culture.
