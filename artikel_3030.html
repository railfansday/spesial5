Meta’s initiative to make Instagram safer for schools stemmed from efforts to increase user “acquisition,” “friending,” and “invites,” according to a report from NPR. Internal documents revealed as part of a group of child safety lawsuits against Meta reference a “Project Gryffindor” — a school program that would “fuel organic and unattributed acquisition” of users. One internal presentation from November 2023 says a school program would “give teens a new reason to join / find more value in IG [Instagram].”

The internal documents suggest Meta considered more goals than just teen safety when rolling out Instagram’s School Partnership Program, which launched touting features to fast-track moderation reports from participating school districts. Meta expanded this program in September by creating dedicated channels where schools can share announcements with students and parents. It also adds school banners to profiles, as well as directories for clubs and teams.

Meta spokesperson Liza Crenshaw told NPR that its story “completely mischaracterizes a positive program that helps thousands of U.S. schools protect their students online, communicate with them safely about extracurricular activities, and easily report safety issues.” Crenshaw adds that Meta doesn’t “want Instagram to be a distraction during class.”

The November 2023 document says that Meta’s “current strategy of relying on power user teens to get less active friends onto IG may not be effective if there are full communities of teens that have not yet joined IG.” Another 2023 presentation questions whether Meta can “use school networks as a lever for acquisition,” and says that structured school data could “help improve the ability to find friends and acquaintances,” for attracting teens who “don’t think their friends are on IG.”

In 2024, Meta started automatically putting users under 18 into more restrictive teen accounts while facing global scrutiny about the safety of its platforms. It later agreed to impose age verification, time limits, and an option to turn off personalized feeds to settle claims that its platforms are addictive.

Meta didn’t immediately respond to The Verge’s request for comment.
