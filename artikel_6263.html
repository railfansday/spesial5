A massive heatwave wiped out the snow crabs in the Bering Sea, now a rare hybrid crab species has taken over

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

A snow-Tanner hybrid crab became 13 times more common after the Bering Sea heatwave, and NOAA traced the surge to the snow crab collapse.

The Bering Sea warmed sharply in 2018 and 2019. Shortly thereafter, the crucial snow crab populations behind one of Alaska’s biggest fisheries vanished in one of the largest species die-offs ever recorded in the ocean.

Seven years later, a different crab is filling part of the gap, a cross between the snow crab and its warmer-water cousin, the Tanner crab. Last year that hybrid was 13 times as common as its long-term average.

Erin Fedewa, a fishery biologist at NOAA Fisheries’ Alaska Fisheries Science Center in Kodiak, Alaska, led the study with eight colleagues at the center.

Using the team’s models, the researchers traced the hybrids’ rise to the snow crab collapse itself, not to the warm water.

In an interview with Earth.com, Fedewa said the size of the jump surprised the team. She explained that hybrid crab have been rare in the Bering Sea historically, and not much is known about the factors that affect their abundance.

She emphasized that she “hadn’t expected hybrids to benefit from a marine heatwave and snow crab collapse”

Snow crabs (Chionoecetes opilio) and Tanner crabs (Chionoecetes bairdi) live on the Bering Sea floor in ranges shaped by winter sea ice.

The two meet along a boundary zone. A hybrid is the offspring of one of each, and surveyors recognize one by features that are intermediate between the two parents.

NOAA’s annual trawl survey has counted them since 1988, and before 2025 hybrids never made up more than 10 percent of the males large enough to catch. In 2025, they made up 41.7 percent, about 13 times the historical share.

Nearly half of the catchable males were suddenly not pure snow crab, and the total count of hybrids was nearly triple the survey’s previous record.

Tanner crabs, which do better in warmer water, also reached a record high in 2025. The snow crab population has rebounded quickly since the heatwave too, though the big males that the fishery targets have been slower to return, Fedewa told Earth.com.

Warmer water could have helped hybrids in two ways. The two parent species could have mixed more and produced more hybrids, or the heat could have helped hybrid crabs survive directly.

The team tested both ideas with statistical models built to separate cause from coincidence. Spring sea ice stood in for heatwave conditions, because the heatwave years were nearly ice-free in March and April, with less than 15 percent cover.

Neither warming idea held up. More overlap between the parent species didn’t explain it, and the ice had no direct effect on hybrid numbers. What did show up was a chain.

Less sea ice meant fewer snow crabs, and fewer snow crabs meant more hybrids. That one pathway accounted for 76 percent of the heatwave’s effect on hybrid numbers. The same chain explained the Tanner crab boom.

The likely reason is that snow crabs normally suppress both rivals – Tanners and hybrids – by competing with them or by eating them. When the snow crabs died, that pressure lifted.

Snow and Tanner crabs can’t be aged, so the team couldn’t say exactly how long the chain takes. They reran the models with plausible delays of up to six years and got the same answer each time.

Under current rules, a legal-sized male hybrid can be landed as a snow crab. But hybrids were always so rare that the federal plan behind crab catch limits does not treat them as their own category.

So there is no mechanism for taking hybrid numbers into account when quotas are set.

The Alaska Department of Fish and Game sets the annual catch limits for Bering Sea snow and Tanner crab. Fedewa told Earth.com it could raise the snow crab limit to reflect the harvestable hybrids.

She said that approach is generally not ideal, because it risks putting more pressure on a snow crab population that is still rebuilding. The other option, she said, is for the fleet to land more hybrids within the existing limit.

“If the snow crab fleet harvested and landed more hybrids during the snow crab fishery, this could have the potential to reduce fishing pressure on large male snow crab that we’ve seen much slower recovery of following the collapse,” she said.

The fishery is worth hundreds of millions of dollars a year, and it closed repeatedly after the collapse.

Fedewa was clear that the fishery questions reach beyond what the study tested. The ecological role of the hybrids remains highly uncertain, she said.

So does what their rise means for snow crabs, and for the harvesters, processors and communities that depend on them.

The bigger question is whether the Bering Sea is changing for good. The marine heatwave was part of a longer warming trend that scientists expect to favor Tanner crabs.

Fedewa said it remains uncertain whether the surge signals a lasting shift, from a seafloor community dominated by snow crabs to one that favors crabs suited to warmer water.

The survey that caught the surge runs every year. The next few counts will show whether hybrids stay common or fade as snow crabs make a comeback.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
