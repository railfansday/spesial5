Google broke its biggest promise about Gmail. It’s the one thing no company should ever do

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Promises, by their very nature, are inconvenient. You tell someone you’re going to do something, and then you have to do it. Or, you promise you’ll never do something, and now you’ve restricted your options.

Of course, the benefit of all that inconvenience is that people trust you when you keep your promises. And trust, as I’ve written before, is your most valuable asset, especially when you’re a massive company whose entire business depends on the willingness of your customers to give you their personal information. Like Google.

Which is why it was such a big deal back in 2017 when Google promised it would not scan your Gmail in order to show you personalized ads. Previously, Google would scan your email to decide which ads to show. People didn’t love that, so Google told customers their data was their own and wouldn’t be used for ads.

For almost a decade, that was the deal. You could use Gmail, Google Photos, and Google Drive without worrying that the information you stored in those services would be used as a source of ad recommendations.

Earlier this month, however, the company quietly updated its privacy policy to say that it would use information from Gmail, Google Photos, and Google Drive to influence the ads people see. Specifically, the policy—which took effect October 16—says that information from those services can be used to personalize advertisements when users choose to connect them to other Google products that have personalized advertising.

The most obvious reason Google is doing this is Personal Intelligence, which lets users connect their Gmail, Search, and Photos to Gemini. The reason you might want to do this is that it turns out that AI can be very useful at pulling information from your email, for example. Of course, that’s very different from wanting Google to then show you ads based on what it finds.

To be clear, it’s not that Google is suddenly scanning everyone’s inbox to sell ads. You have to turn on the connection—it’s off by default. In addition, Google isn’t giving your personal information to advertisers, and it says it doesn’t directly target ads based on the data you have in those services. Instead, Google’s AI can use information derived from connected apps to personalize ads.

I’m sure there’s some kind of technical distinction there, but I don’t know that it’s all that meaningful if you’re someone who has trusted Google with all your information. Say, for example, you are planning a trip, and you ask Gemini to help you plan activities when you’re there. You give it access to your email so it can find your flights and then track whether the price changes.

That’s super useful, and it’s the kind of thing that AI tools are actually really good at. Imagine, however, if suddenly you started seeing ads for things to do near your travel destination. Under Google’s new policy, information derived from that reservation could definitely influence the advertisements you see.

Look, it’s not that Google is doing something inherently wrong. You can certainly decide for yourself whether you want to connect your accounts at all. Also, to its credit, Google is disclosing how that information might be used. The problem is that Google is changing the terms of a promise it made in order to earn people’s trust in the first place. And you can’t connect Personal Intelligence and take advantage of its genuine benefits without opting in to the ad part.

Every company eventually faces a situation where keeping a promise it made to its users suddenly becomes inconvenient. Maybe it’s because the market changes, or because its competitors introduce something new, or there’s an opportunity to make more money.

When that happens, it’s tempting to find a technical reason for changing the terms of the deal. Google could reasonably argue that its new policy is different from its old practice because users have to opt in and their emails aren’t directly used to select advertisements. But you don’t build trust with technical distinctions in your privacy policy. You build it by being clear about what customers can expect, and then doing exactly that.

In Google’s case, the promise was simple: We won’t use the information you put in Gmail to personalize advertising. Now, if you want to connect your personal information to Google’s AI product, it will.

The most important thing a company can do is keep its promises, especially when doing so becomes inconvenient. The worst thing that can happen is that customers start wondering which promises you’ll keep and which ones you’ll change when you see a better business opportunity. Once that happens, you’ve already lost something far more valuable than whatever you hoped to gain.

—Jason Aten
