A 194-year-old tortoise has a remarkable anti-aging secret hidden in its DNA

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

At an estimated 194 years old, a giant tortoise named Jonathan has lived longer than any other known land animal alive today. Now, scientists studying his extraordinary lifespan have uncovered genetic clues that may explain how he has remained so resilient for nearly two centuries.

The findings suggest that Jonathan's remarkable longevity involves more than simply inheriting favorable genes. His body also appears to have avoided some of the genetic changes that normally accumulate with age, helping preserve important biological functions.

In a study published in Science Advances, researchers identified 287 distinctive genetic variants associated with protection against the effects of aging. These variants influence several essential processes, including repairing damaged DNA, limiting inflammation, controlling insulin activity, and suppressing cancer.

Jonathan is an Aldabra giant tortoise (Aldabrachelys gigantea) believed to have hatched in 1832. That means he was already alive during the lifetimes of Charles Darwin and Queen Victoria. Today, he holds the distinction of being both the oldest living giant tortoise and the oldest known living land animal.

A 194-Year-Old Tortoise With Remarkably Youthful Gene Regulation

One of the most surprising discoveries came when scientists examined Jonathan's epigenome, the system of chemical markers that helps determine which genes are switched on or off. These regulatory mechanisms typically change as animals grow older, potentially disrupting important cellular processes and contributing to age-related deterioration.

To investigate how Jonathan's biology has changed over time, the researchers compared his epigenome with those of younger giant tortoises belonging to the same species.

They found that the regulatory mechanisms associated with DNA repair and metabolism had remained remarkably stable. Despite Jonathan's advanced age, these genetic controls closely resembled those found in his much younger relatives.

The results suggest that maintaining stable gene regulation could be one of the factors helping giant tortoises withstand the biological stresses of aging for exceptionally long periods.

This research also represents the first investigation of the epigenome of a giant tortoise. The project was led by Kallel, a US nonprofit organization focused on longevity research. Its scientists believe that examining the biology of animals capable of surviving for centuries could reveal important new insights into the mechanisms of extreme longevity.

"We found that the gene regulators involved in energy production and DNA repair have remained incredibly stable in Jonathan over almost two centuries," said Dr. Justin Gerlach at Peterhouse at the University of Cambridge, who was involved in the study.

He added: "There are few creatures that could tell us more about aging than the giant tortoises of the Galapagos and Seychelles islands. Working with a really old giant tortoise is such a privilege: you get a sense of the tortoise being your collaborator, not your study subject."

Gerlach has spent several decades investigating giant tortoise ecology and conservation. However, this project marks his first examination of the biological mechanisms responsible for their exceptional lifespans.

Could Giant Tortoise DNA Reveal Secrets to Human Longevity?

The researchers hope Jonathan's genetic characteristics could eventually help scientists develop new approaches to slowing age-related decline and preventing chronic diseases in humans.

By identifying the biological systems that allow certain animals to remain healthy for extraordinarily long periods, researchers may be able to uncover protective mechanisms that could inform future medical treatments.

"Nature has already solved the puzzle of aging in remarkable ways, and Jonathan's genome provides a blueprint for cellular resilience," said Stephen Clark, MD, PhD, founder of Kallel and senior author of the study.

He added: "Our goal is to take these evolutionary insights and immediately translate them into practical, affordable treatments for everyday people. Aging is the greatest risk factor for nearly every chronic disease we face, and through the generosity of philanthropic partners, we can democratize access to longevity medicine."

Although the findings provide intriguing clues about extreme longevity, translating these discoveries into treatments for humans will require further research.

Nearly Two Centuries of Life on a Remote Atlantic Island

Jonathan has spent most of his exceptionally long life on St. Helena, a British Overseas Territory in the South Atlantic Ocean. He lives on the grounds of Plantation House, the official residence of the island's governor.

He arrived on St. Helena from the Seychelles 144 years ago as a gift to the governor. Jonathan was already fully grown when he reached the island, providing further evidence of his remarkable age.

The giant tortoises native to the Galapagos and Seychelles islands are among the last surviving representatives of a group of enormous reptiles that once played dominant ecological roles across many of the world's islands.

Today, these extraordinary survivors offer scientists a rare opportunity to investigate how evolution has equipped certain animals to withstand the effects of time. Jonathan's unusually stable genetic regulation provides a particularly intriguing window into the biology of aging and the possibility of extending healthy life.
