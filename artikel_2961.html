Why Portable Medical Technology Needs More Than Portability

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

​Making a medical device smaller changes where it can go, but it takes more than portability to bring that technology into a new care setting. It requires understanding who will operate it, what clinical need it addresses and how it fits into the delivery of care. Advances in hardware, software and AI are creating new possibilities, carrying advanced technology into places it rarely reached before. But portability alone does not ensure adoption.​

Primary care offices, wound clinics, ambulances and patients’ homes, and the people using medical devices in those settings, have different needs than a specialist department. These settings may offer less time for testing, fewer staff with device-specific training and tighter budgets. Users need information they can act on within those constraints.

The opportunity for developers is not simply to move existing equipment into new locations. Engaging care providers in new settings requires designing technology around those settings from the start. For example, what information or intervention does the clinician need? Who will operate the device? What happens after a result is available? Those questions should shape product development as much as size, cost or technical performance.​

A growing number of devices, both on the market and in development, illustrate how technology can be adapted to different users and care settings. For example, researchers are studying a portable, wearable device that uses light to measure blood flow in the brain to help ambulance crews identify the most severe strokes. In an early study, it detected large vessel occlusions more reliably than the symptom checklists that paramedics typically use.

Portability also matters for inpatient care, because it allows clinicians to bring the technology directly to patients who may be too unstable to move. One study of portable, low-field MRI in 50 critically ill patients demonstrated the feasibility of bedside scanning, with no adverse events reported, showing how inpatient settings stand to gain from advancements in medical device tech. ​

Outside the hospital, advanced diagnostics are reaching primary care offices and even patients’ homes. An AI-based eye exam allows primary care practices to screen patients with diabetes for eye damage without an eye specialist reading the images. In the study behind its FDA authorization, office staff were capable of running the tests after about four hours of training.

Older adults with a common form of age-related vision loss can also now scan their own eyes at home with results available for remote review by their treating physician. These examples address different barriers to access: where a test can be performed, who can perform it and how results reach the clinician responsible for follow-up. Manufacturers need to identify which barrier they are solving, rather than treating portability as the solution to all three.​

While advances in technology are largely responsible for bringing advanced devices to the point of care, healthcare policies and workforce needs add to the growing momentum. Earlier this year, Congress extended Medicare’s hospital-at-home program through 2030, and hundreds of programs across dozens of states have been approved to provide hospital-level care in patients’ homes.

One industry forecast projects that home-based care, outpatient services and clinics will see the fastest volume increases of any care setting over the next decade, signaling a need for more portable, low-cost technologies that require fewer specialists to operate them or interpret results.

The workforce and payment aspects of care appear to be moving in a similar direction. In radiology, for example, workforce researchers project that the supply of specialists will grow at roughly the same rate as demand for imaging over the next three decades, meaning the current shortage is unlikely to improve without changes in how imaging is delivered.

Payers, meanwhile, have started to recognize imaging done outside traditional settings. For manufacturers, the practical question is whether a device has a workable payment model or creates enough operational value to justify its full cost, including staff time, training and follow-up. Billing codes also now exist for the remote review of home eye scans mentioned above, showing a willingness from payers to consider nontraditional imaging.

With specialists in short supply and payers more open to new models, the decision to buy advanced medical technology is expanding to include the departments that will act on its results, such as emergency medicine, primary care and home health programs. For these buyers, technical performance is only the starting point.

They also need to know how much training staff will require, whether the evidence reflects the intended users and setting, how the device fits into daily routines and whether reimbursement or operational savings justify the full cost of using it. Manufacturers now have the opportunity to reach a new category of buyers by going beyond portability, making the technology practical for the team that will use it.​
