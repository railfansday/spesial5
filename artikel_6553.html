Coffee May Be Changing Your Gut in Ways Scientists Are Only Beginning To Understand

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

To find out what coffee might be doing deep in the gut, researchers sent a tiny sensor through the digestive tract.

For many people, a morning cup of coffee does more than wake up the brain. It also seems to get the digestive system moving, sometimes sending them straight to the bathroom. But what exactly happens deep inside the intestines after regular coffee consumption?

Researchers at the University of Copenhagen set out to investigate whether coffee drinking is linked to changes in gut function, particularly the muscle contractions that move food and waste through the digestive tract. Understanding this connection could offer new insights into digestion and potentially point toward natural ways to relieve constipation.

To see what was happening inside the digestive tract, the team asked Danish volunteers to swallow a small electronic capsule. As it traveled, the capsule recorded temperature, pH, and intestinal contractions, along with how long it took to pass through.

Over the eight-day study, the researchers compared volunteers who drank one to five cups of coffee on at least four days with those who drank coffee less often. The frequent coffee drinkers had a wider variety of gut microorganisms, consistent with earlier research linking coffee intake to differences in the makeup of gut bacteria.

“But perhaps more importantly, frequent coffee consumption appears to affect the physiology of the gut itself, in other words, how the gut functions,” says first author Francesca Bucci, a PhD student in the University of Copenhagen’s Department of Nutrition, Exercise, and Sports. Earlier studies have linked moderate coffee intake to lower risks of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, depression, and several cancers. Its relationship with conditions inside the gut has received less attention.

Frequent coffee drinkers had a significantly lower pH in the small intestine, meaning it was more acidic. Higher coffee intake was also associated with more contractions in the colon.

“Contractions in the gut can support healthy digestion, help prevent constipation, and keep the intestinal muscles in good condition,” says Bucci.

The implications of the lower pH are less clear. Digestive enzymes work best within particular pH ranges, so changes in acidity can affect how they break down food. The researchers still need to determine what the difference means for health.

Because participants followed their usual coffee habits, the study could only identify links. Other differences between the groups may have contributed to the findings. Senior author Henrik Munch Roager, an associate professor and research group leader in the same department, points to earlier evidence that coffee can influence digestion.

“For example, we already know from other studies that coffee can influence a range of processes in the digestive system, including acid secretion in the stomach. So there are good reasons to believe that what we are seeing is not merely coincidence. Anecdotally, many people also report that coffee affects their digestion,” says Roager.

Roager proposes controlling how much coffee participants drink in a follow-up study. Comparing periods of high coffee intake with periods without coffee could help researchers pin down which changes are due to coffee itself.

“A great many people struggle with constipation and irregular bowel movements. If we can understand how coffee affects digestion and intestinal contractions, it may be possible to use that knowledge to stimulate gut motility in a natural way. That would be extremely valuable,” says Roager.

Reference: “Habitual coffee consumption is linked to differences in gut microbiota and gut physiology” by Francesca Bucci, Marie-Luise Puhlmann, Paula Rodriguez-Garcia, Xu Yujing, Jan Stanstrup, Jeroen Raes, Giorgia La Barbera, Nicola Procházková, Lars O. Dragsted, Martin F. Laursen and Henrik M. Roager, 15 September 2026, Gut Microbiology. DOI: 10.1016/j.gutmic.2026.100018
