John Legend, Lynn Nottage, and Whitney White on Reclaiming an American Classic From a Black Perspective

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

The three artists have teamed up on a musical adaptation of Imitation of Life

In 2020, against the dual backdrops of the isolating Covid-19 lockdown and the righteous fury of Black Lives Matter protests erupting across the country, John Legend and Lynn Nottage began exchanging messages. Though the EGOT-achieving musician and the two-time Pulitzer Prize–winning playwright couldn’t meet in person, the pair quietly became collaborators on a long-gestating project, now finally reaching the stage: a musical adaptation of Imitation of Life.

For nearly a century, Imitation of Life—which began as a 1933 best-selling novel by Fannie Hurst, became a John M. Stahl melodrama the following year, then was adapted for the screen again by Douglas Sirk in 1959—has been a cinematic fixture in Black American households, though not an uncontroversial one. Stahl’s iconic telling centers on Bea Pullman, a white widow and single mother, who takes in Delilah, a Black single mother, as a maid in exchange for housing her and her light-skinned daughter, Peola (Sarah Jane in Sirk’s version). When Bea boxes Delilah’s family pancake recipe and puts a caricature of her likeness on the package—an unmistakable nod to Aunt Jemima—she becomes a wealthy businesswoman. But Delilah, content simply to care for Bea and her daughter, turns down any share of the money. Meanwhile, Peola, seduced by her adjacency to whiteness, wrenchingly disavows her mother to pursue a life of passing.

As Sterling Brown’s scathing and influential 1935 review for Opportunity: A Journal of Negro Life made plain, the novel and film are mired in racist tropes. Yet despite its flaws, the story has continued capturing Black audiences, perhaps for the complex humanity clearly latent within Delilah’s and Peola’s characters. Now the story is being retold in a limited-run production opening December 3 at the Shed in New York, this time with a fully realized Delilah as the lead and an all-Black chief creative team at its core. Nottage wrote the book, while Legend—who also recently released his 11th studio album, Muse—composed the entire score. Whitney White, of Jaja’s African Hair Braiding and Liberation, directs, with Adrienne Warren starring as Delilah and Jessica Vosk as Bea. Leading up to rehearsals for the show, Nottage, Legend, and White—so in demand that she was pulled into a production meeting midconversation—took time to discuss their own relationships to Imitation of Life, their experience working together, and why the story’s themes remain as relevant as ever.

Lynn Nottage: I come from a generation where both films, Imitation of Life from the 1930s and 1950s, were fairly ubiquitous on television. I ended up seeing the films probably once or twice a year and found myself in sort of a tense, love-hate relationship with Imitation of Life, in that I was frustrated by the treatment of Delilah and Peola in 1934, then once again by Delilah and Sarah Jane in the 1950s. And yet I was fascinated by the racial politics and this young woman who had a really angsty relationship with her own identity. So I felt, when I was invited to work on this, that I had an opportunity to delve deeper into the character of Delilah and right some wrongs. She is so fascinating; she’s a mother, she’s an entrepreneur, she’s a woman who defies the expectations of the time. And yet throughout history, she has not been given her just deserts.

John Legend: I didn’t grow up knowing this film, but my parents and their generation know it very well. So when I told them that I was involved in this musical, they were so excited. And it made me realize how important this work was in our culture. But like Lynn, it was a fun challenge to reimagine the storytelling, where Delilah was centered, her emotionality was centered, her humanity was centered, and Peola’s humanity was centered as well.

And then, of course, I’ve never written a musical before. So part of the joy and educational experience that I’ve gotten from doing this is working with people who have so much brilliance and experience and understanding of a medium that I didn’t bring to the table. It was the perfect introduction, for me, to writing a musical. It’s been such a welcoming and edifying way to enter into this new branch of my career.

Nottage: John is a natural in this medium. It’s almost as if he had gotten a master’s degree in musical-theater writing. I think that, John, you have so many musicals in you and the world is going to be blown away.

Legend: Thank you. It really did make me think, why hadn’t I done this before?

Whitney White: For me, I was raised by a single mom, and she raised me at great personal cost—spiritual cost—in Chicago, on her own. So the 1930s film, I oddly was more familiar with it because I liked it more. I would sit with my grandmother when she was having her coffee, like, at whatever-o’clock in the morning, because neither of us would ever sleep. And for some reason, I attached myself to the 1930s film. The visuals of that film are so iconic. It’s like the foundation of Americana as it exists in global culture is visualized in that film. But also the story of Delilah echoes in so many ways. It’s almost like touching a stove that’s too hot for me. Because I’ve watched, my whole life, the world not see my mother in this way: her brilliance, her intelligence, her spunk, her grit. So I just knew that I could bring something to it. But I also knew that John and Lynn were going to give that character the eulogy and the eternal life that she’s always deserved.

Legend: Yeah, I think because of Lynn’s perspective and storytelling, we see other sides of Delilah. We see her being romantic. We see her being a brilliant businessperson, making a way out of no way—something that a lot of our ancestors, especially, had to do. They did what they had to do to realize the American dream.

Nottage: At the core, this is a story about the American dream, with all of its contradictions and complications. It’s a story about motherhood; it’s a story about friendship, but it’s also a story about passing. It’s what we are forced to do to negotiate and survive in a world that is sexist, in a world that is racist. Why, in America, do we constantly have to make compromises in order to reach for the things that we desire? Those sacrifices, most often, people of color have to make, women have to make. We are often asked to reshape our identities to fit into some sort of template that’s not necessarily comfortable for the shapes and forms of our bodies and our ideas and our identities. I think that’s why we keep coming back to this story, because the struggle remains real.

White: And I think, in the midst of having to constantly reshape our identities and bodies as Black folk, at the same time, we got the goods. We make culture. Here you have this character who saves this business, and what does she make? Delicious food that is worth millions of dollars. So while we’re forced to be at the bottom of the totem pole, we also make the goods that make capitalism work. That’s something I’ve really been sitting with: We’re so often plundered and taken from the most, and yet we are the foundation of capitalism.

Nottage: Can I ask you, Whitney, you’re looking particularly luminous today. Where are you going?

White: Girl, I’m sweaty. I just walked my child [to school]. My child is very heavy. I have this giant toddler; maybe he’s going to be LeBron. He didn’t want to walk to school, so he made me push him in his stroller.

Legend: I met your husband last week, so I see where he got it from.

Nottage: I totally digress, but I was like, “Look at you, you have, like, a rosy green glow.” [Laughs]

White: [Laughs] That beat that just happened is so beautiful because it’s so indicative of the show. Like, we’re talking about this heavy stuff, and then we all have this beautiful laugh. When I read the text and hear the music, it’s like I go through 10 cycles of emotions. I laugh, I cry, I fall in love, then I laugh and cry and fall in love again.

Legend: One of my favorite things from the workshops is watching the music come to life. You have all these amazing performers embodying all the things that I wrote in my little piano room at home.

Nottage: Theater is often so difficult and fraught to make because it is collaborative. I think one of the things that’s been really lovely about this process is the ease with which we, as collaborators, are capable of having conversations, because there’s so much mutual respect and appreciation. The bottom line in making a musical is that trust is necessary. It’s a tremendous leap of faith.

White [as she is pulled into production meeting]: I just want to add one last thing. Oh God, I don’t want to get too emotional. Never in my wildest dreams did I imagine that I would be sitting here with two of my favorite living artists. When I started in this business, this kind of makeup for a musical team was impossible. I can’t tell you how much pride and excitement that brings me, to be working alongside John and Lynn.

Nottage: Thank you. We love you, Whitney.

White: I’m going to go lovingly yell at people—lovingly, lovingly. Bye.

Legend: Our writing process started—we were meeting during Covid, so a lot of our first interactions were on Zoom. Lynn would send me pages with story dialogue and possible lyrical ideas. I would sit in my piano room and sketch out an idea with maybe a couple lyrics, but mostly just mumbling musical ideas around the theme.

Legend: One of the things that happens more in musical theater than I’m used to is the revision process and the workshopping process. … I’ve definitely done more revision in this show than I’ve ever done in my pop-music career.

Nottage: John, do you remember one of the very first conversations that we had? I said, “We’re probably going to write that opening-number song at least 15 times.” You’re like, “Oh, I don’t think so.” Here we are!

Legend: Here we are. We’ve rewritten it multiple times. [Laughs] I don’t have an extensive knowledge bank of musicals that I’ve seen and been a part of. So it was interesting, thinking about how to write for one, because part of it was like, “Well, let me just bring my own musical experience in the church and popular music and, you know, singing in a jazz a cappella group and singing show tunes in the choir in high school.” I just brought my own experience and said, “Let me just write the songs that I can be proud of and that I believe will work.” And then Lynn really taught me how to incorporate the storytelling into the songs.

Nottage: It’s so interesting to hear you say that, John, because I think one of the things that made the process so smooth is that you are a natural-born storyteller. There’s something that you understand instinctively and fundamentally about dramaturgy that I didn’t have to explain to you. I would venture to say that perhaps musicals are the hardest form of theater-making just because it is so layered.

But I also want to circle back to something that you said earlier, John, about the beginning of the process. It was Covid, and we were all thinking and making in isolation. There was something really lovely about reaching across the country to John and having these conversations about a book, even though we didn’t really, at that point, know each other. We spent a lot of time talking about ideas. It was absolutely thrilling. When I got that first mumble, I was like, “Oh my God, it’s John Legend mumbling, and it still sounds beautiful.” It was a song that we never, ultimately, ended up using. I think that eventually you should do an album of the outtakes.

Nottage: I just want to add, because we’ve been talking about some heavy themes, that the musical is not delivering spinach. The musical embodies all of the complexities of a beautiful woman like Delilah, a beautiful woman like Bea, a beautiful woman like Peola. It really is, in some ways, a celebration. It is a love letter. And it is a gift to be able to be in dialogue with these women.
