How Hugs Help Combat Anxiety and Pain

Psychological research has shown that hugs can positively affect both physical and mental well-being (Ocklenburg, 2024). For example, they have been shown to reduce stress, increase bonding between people, and are associated with reduced feelings of loneliness and depression. One question, however, that has not been answered yet in psychological research is whether hugs can combat anxiety and pain.

A new study, just published in the Journal of Integrative and Complementary Medicine, aimed to answer exactly this question (Aydın and co-workers, 2026). The study entitled “Preoperative Family Hugging: Associations with Anxiety and Postoperative Pain: A Randomized Controlled Trial” was a randomized controlled trial. This is a strong form of scientific experiment in which volunteers are randomly assigned to two groups that are compared. In this study, 150 patients who were about to undergo surgery were randomly assigned to a hug or a no-hug group. Patients in the hug group were instructed to hug family members for at least 20 seconds about 30 minutes before the start of the surgery. Patients in the other group did not hug anyone.

About 30 minutes later, patients’ anxiety was measured in the operating room using standard anxiety questionnaires. Afterward, patients in both groups were anesthetized, and surgery was performed. After they woke up again, their pain levels were assessed using standard pain questionnaires 30 minutes and 6 hours after surgery.

The results of the study revealed a clear and statistically significant difference in state anxiety before surgery between the groups. Patients in the hugging group reported lower anxiety before surgery than patients who did not hug anyone before the surgery. Further analyses also showed that the patients in the hugging group showed lower pain levels after surgery than patients who did not hug before the surgery. Six hours after surgery, no pain differences were found between the groups.

As the randomized controlled trial is a strong form of experiment, these results can be interpreted as a sign that hugs indeed may help reduce pain and anxiety in the context of surgery. While more psychological experiments are needed to explain this effect, one idea could be that the bonding hormone oxytocin could play a role. Hugs have been known to lead to oxytocin release, and oxytocin has been known to interact with the pain system and the function of the amygdala, a central brain area for feelings of anxiety and fear.

The study shows strong empirical evidence that a hug before surgery can help reduce anxiety and pain. So, the next time you visit a loved one in the hospital: Hug them!

Aydın A, Kutlusoy S, Gökdere OG. Preoperative Family Hugging: Associations with Anxiety and Postoperative Pain: A Randomized Controlled Trial. Journal of Integrative and Complementary Medicine. 2026;0(0). doi:10.1177/27683605261505158

Ocklenburg, S. (2024). The Psychology and Neuroscience of Hugging. A Multidisciplinary Perspective. Springer.
