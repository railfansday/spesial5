Anker’s Prime Power Bank (26K, 300W), model A110A, is the one power bank we’d buy if we could only own one. It’s expensive, at $229.99 at Anker as of October 26, 2026 (a sale price; Anker shows $30.01 off), and Macworld found it for $199.98 at Amazon in July. What you get for that is a battery with no real ceiling for everyday gear. Anker rates each of its two USB-C ports at 140W and the bank at 300W total, so it can fast-charge a 16-inch MacBook Pro and a second laptop at the same time and still run a phone off the 22.5W USB-A port.

Most banks make you wait as long to refill them as they took to drain. Anker rates the Prime at 50 percent in 13 minutes and 80 percent in 40 minutes, Anker’s page doesn’t say which setup those figures assume, but its fastest refill uses both USB-C ports at once with two 140W chargers, so expect slower results from a single charger. A built-in display shows the remaining charge and what each port is drawing, and the capacity is tuned for flying. At 26,250mAh, it comes in at 99.75Wh, just under the 100Wh line airlines use for carry-on batteries that don’t need approval. Expert Reviews, Engadget, and Macworld all ranked it at or near the top of their 2026 power bank roundups.

The trade is size. At 1.32 pounds, per Anker, it’s a bag battery, and if you only ever charge a phone, the UGREEN 20,000mAh bank below does that job for a quarter of the price. Check the model number before you buy, too. Anker also sold an older Prime 27,650mAh 250W bank (model A1340), which shows as sold out on Anker’s site, and the A110A is also sold in a bundle with a charging base. This recommendation covers the A110A power bank on its own.

A 20,000mAh battery is the size that covers the most situations without becoming a burden. It’s enough to recharge most phones more than once, top off a tablet, or buy a laptop a couple more hours, and it still slides into a backpack’s laptop sleeve. UGREEN’s Nexode Power Bank 20,000mAh 130W (model PB721) is the one we’d buy at that size. It costs $59.99 at UGREEN as of October 21, 2026, a sale price against a $89.99 list price, and UGREEN rates it for 130W of total output across two USB-C ports.

This is the successor to the 20,000mAh, 100W UGREEN bank that earned our travel award last time, and the predecessor’s results are why it’s back in that slot. In our testing, that model took an iPhone 16 Pro Max from empty to 55 percent in 30 minutes and to full in about an hour and a half, with 66 percent of the bank’s own charge left afterward. It also fully recharged a 2024 iPad Pro in two hours with 36 percent to spare. We haven’t run the same tests on the 130W version yet, so treat those as results from the previous model, not this one.

The feature we’d miss most on any other bank is the screen. It shows the remaining charge plus the voltage and current going to each device, so you can tell at a glance whether your laptop is actually fast charging or trickling along on a bad cable. The bank weighs about 480 grams, per UGREEN, and there’s no built-in cable, so pack a USB-C cable rated for 100W if you plan to charge a laptop with it. UGREEN’s US page names two USB-C ports; some international listings add a USB-A port, so check the listing if you still need one.

A magnetic battery snaps onto the back of an iPhone and charges it with no cable at all, which is why it’s the kind of power bank people actually carry every day. Anker’s MagGo Power Bank 2 Pro (model A110R) is the one to get right now. It costs $109.99 at Anker as of October 21, 2026, and it was $87.99 at Amazon during October’s Prime Big Deal Days. It’s built on Qi2.2, a newer version of the magnetic wireless standard that grew out of Apple’s MagSafe, and Anker rates it at up to 25W wirelessly and 45W through its USB-C port.

That 25W figure only applies to the iPhone 16 lineup and newer. Per Macworld, the iPhone 15 and older, plus the iPhone 17e, charge at 15W and the iPhone Air at 20W, and Anker excludes the iPhone 16e, which lacks MagSafe magnets. Macworld’s reviewer charged an iPhone 16 Pro to 50 percent in 30 minutes with the fan running. Faster wireless charging means more heat, and Anker’s answer is a small built-in fan with Cool and Silent modes. Reviewers at Macworld and Digital Camera World both found it audible up close, and Digital Camera World’s tester noticed the phone still got warm. If it bothers you on a nightstand, Silent mode turns the fan off.

The rest of the package earns the price. A fold-out kickstand props the phone up for video calls or watching something on a flight, the display shows battery level, temperature, and battery health, and 45W input means the bank itself refills quickly. Anker says it’s designed for Apple devices, so Android owners should look elsewhere, even though one reviewer did get a Pixel to charge on it. If you’d rather have a built-in cable than a kickstand, Ugreen’s MagFlow Pro is the rival Macworld compared it against. Our previous multipurpose pick was the Anker PowerCore Fusion 10000. Anker’s current US lineup leads with a newer version, and the idea is unchanged. The Anker Power Bank (10K, Fusion, Built-In Cable), model A1637, is a 10,000mAh battery with folding wall prongs, so it works as a wall charger when you’re near an outlet and a power bank when you aren’t.

The new version adds a built-in USB-C cable, which fixes a familiar power bank problem: having the battery but not the cord. There’s also a second USB-C port for charging two devices at once, and Anker rates the output at up to 30W. That’s plenty for a phone, earbuds, or a tablet, but slow for a laptop. It costs $52.99 at Anker as of October 21, 2026, on sale, and B&H listed it for $44.99 the same day.

Like the model it replaces, it’s heavier than a normal wall charger, and in a loose hotel outlet, the weight can pull it partway out. If that happens, plug it into a power strip instead. For anyone who travels light, losing a separate charger from the bag is worth the trade.

If you work from coffee shops, libraries, or airport gates, the power bank that matters is the one that keeps your laptop alive until an outlet frees up. UGREEN’s Nexode 25,000mAh 145W Power Bank has been our pick for that since we tested it, and it’s still on sale at $79.99 at UGREEN as of October 21, 2026. UGREEN rates the battery at about 90Wh, which leaves it comfortably under the 100Wh limit for carry-on bags.

After using it regularly for months, it never let us down. It extended a 16-inch MacBook Pro by a few hours and fully recharged an iPhone 15 Pro Max and a Nintendo Switch OLED. It did run warm when a laptop drew power from it for long stretches, though never hot enough to worry us. A side display shows the remaining charge, so you know whether to plug it in before you leave.

One thing to know about the numbers: UGREEN’s product name says 145W, while its own product images list 140W, 65W, and 22.5W for the three ports. Either way, the main USB-C port has enough power to fast-charge most laptops, and the other two cover a phone and earbuds at the same time. The one thing it can’t do is run a laptop that only charges from its own AC adapter. For that, see the Goal Zero below.

Some gear still only charges from its own power brick: older laptops, camera battery chargers, a drone charger. The Goal Zero Sherpa 100AC gives you a real AC outlet, rated at 100W, in a bank that stays under the 100Wh carry-on limit. It costs $249.95 at Goal Zero and B&H as of October 21, 2026. Goal Zero shows that as a discount from $274.85.

The current model is the fourth-generation Sherpa 100AC, part number 22200. Beyond the outlet, it has two USB-C ports rated at 60W and 100W, two USB-A ports, and a 15W wireless pad on top. Goal Zero has sold several Sherpa generations under nearly the same name, and the previous one, model 22050, had a much weaker 5W wireless pad. Make sure the listing says 4th generation or 22200.

Know a couple of things before you buy. The AC outlet is modified sine wave rather than pure sine, which is fine for most chargers but can make some motors and audio gear hum or run poorly. Owner ratings are also mixed: REI showed a 3.9-star average across 63 reviews in October 2026, with some owners describing port and display failures. Goal Zero is now owned by BioLite, which bought the brand in April 2025 and has kept its products on sale. If you need more than about 100W of AC power, a portable power station is the better tool.

Our previous outdoor pick, Goal Zero’s Venture 75, was sold out at Goal Zero and Backcountry when we checked in October 2026, with no successor announced. For hiking and backpacking, we’d now carry the Nitecore NB10000 Gen 4. It’s a 10,000mAh battery that weighs about 5 ounces and carries an IPX7 rating, meaning it’s built to survive brief submersion, so rain, a creek crossing, or a soggy tent won’t kill it. It costs $83.95 at Garage Grown Gear as of October 21, 2026.

Weight is the whole point here. Most of the other 10,000mAh banks in CleverHiker’s 2026 testing weighed between 6.6 and 8.5 ounces, and on a multi-day trip, those extra ounces add up. Nitecore gets the weight down with a carbon fiber composite shell and silicon-carbon cells, and the bank has two USB-C ports with pass-through charging, so you can recharge it and your phone from one wall plug at a trailhead motel. It was the top backpacking pick in CleverHiker’s power bank testing this year.

It’s not a fast charger. Nitecore rates it at 22.5W, which is fine for a phone, a headlamp, or a GPS messenger but slow for a tablet. Like every power bank, it also delivers less than its label suggests, because energy is lost converting the cells’ voltage up to USB levels. A power bank is a battery plus the electronics that turn its power into something your devices can use. These are the specs that separate a good one from a disappointing one.

Capacity is listed in milliamp-hours (mAh), measured at the cells’ own voltage, about 3.7 volts for most lithium-ion banks. Your phone charges at 5 volts or more, and converting between the two loses energy as heat. That’s why a 10,000mAh bank won’t charge a phone with a 5,000mAh battery twice. In Macworld’s 2026 capacity tests, 10,000mAh banks averaged 174 percent of a phone battery, not 200 percent.

Watt-hours (Wh) are the more useful number, because they don’t depend on voltage and they’re what airlines use. Multiply mAh by the cell voltage and divide by 1,000: a 20,000mAh bank at 3.7 volts is about 74Wh. As a rough guide, 10,000mAh covers a day of phone use, 20,000mAh covers a weekend or a tablet, and 25,000mAh or more is for laptops.

Output, in watts, decides how fast a bank charges your devices. Most current phones fast charge at 20W to 45W, tablets want 30W or so, and laptops need 45W to 140W depending on the model. Look for USB Power Delivery (USB PD) on the USB-C ports, since that’s the standard laptops and iPhones use, and check whether the advertised maximum comes from one port or is split across several. A bank rated for 100W total might only give 60W to each of two laptops.

Wireless power banks use the Qi standard. Qi2 added MagSafe-style magnets that line the phone up with the charging coil, and Qi2.2 raised the speed to 25W. Every iPhone since the iPhone 12 (except the 16e) has the magnets; Android support is spotty, and some phones need a magnetic case. Wireless charging is convenient but less efficient than a cable, so it gets more heat and fewer total charges out of the same battery. Our explainer on wired vs. wireless charging goes deeper, and our MagSafe accessories guide covers the cases, mounts, and wallets that use the same magnets.

Power banks have to go in your carry-on, never checked luggage. The FAA allows any number of batteries up to 100Wh, and up to two between 101Wh and 160Wh with airline approval. Airlines added rules of their own this year. Southwest allows one power bank per passenger as of April 2026, American and Delta cap passengers at two banks of 100Wh or less as of May 2026, and several airlines now bar power banks from overhead bins. International aviation rules adopted in March 2026 also limit passengers to two and ban recharging a power bank in flight. Rules are still changing, so check your airline’s page before you fly. Every pick in this guide is under 100Wh.

Power bank recalls have become common enough that checking is worth a minute. In 2025, Anker recalled more than 1.1 million PowerCore 10000 banks (model A1263) in June and about 481,000 more banks across five models in September. INIU recalled about 210,000 BI-B41 banks in December 2025. Recalls usually cover specific model and serial numbers, which are printed on the bank itself. Search the Consumer Product Safety Commission’s recall site for your brand.

Whatever you own, stop using a bank that’s swollen, cracked, or too hot to hold, and don’t charge one on a bed or couch where heat can’t escape. Buy from brands that sell under their own name and have a working support page, since they’re the ones that issue recalls and replacements when something goes wrong.

MagSafe is Apple’s magnetic charging system for iPhones. Qi2 is the open standard built on it, and Qi2.2 raised its top speed to 25W. A Qi2.2 power bank like the Anker MagGo 2 Pro charges an iPhone 16 or newer at up to 25W and most older iPhones at 15W.

Warm is normal, because some energy is lost as heat while charging, especially wirelessly. Hot is not. If a bank or your phone gets too hot to hold comfortably, unplug everything, move the bank away from anything flammable, and stop using it if it happens again.

Most power banks keep useful capacity for a few years of regular use, and many makers rate their cells for several hundred full cycles. Storing a bank about half charged in a cool place helps it age more slowly. Replace one that swells, no longer holds a charge, or gets unusually hot.

A dead phone at the wrong moment is more than an inconvenience. It can leave you unable to call for help, find your way home, or pull up a boarding pass. Pick a bank sized for what you actually carry, check its model number against recent recalls, and charge it before you need it. Then stop worrying about the battery icon.
