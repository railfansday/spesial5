How to prepare before you gather with loved ones, what to say, and what to do next.

Holiday gatherings have a way of stirring up difficult and downright uncomfortable conversations — and not just about politics or cousin Derek’s love life.

Relatives and friends may also bring up their money troubles and financial plans. That might involve asking you to lend money or your credit.

It’s easy to feel obligated or pressured to help out. But it’s important to establish boundaries so you don’t end up making a decision you’ll regret.

If you suspect a money request is coming your way, think carefully about what you’re willing — and able — to offer. You might choose a specific dollar limit or to give no financial help at all.

“Only lend what you're truly OK losing,” says Alvin Carlos, a certified financial planner at District Capital Management in Washington, D.C. “Look at your budget and decide what you could hand over and never see again.”

Choose the language that best fits your situation. Adapt your responses as needed to guide the conversation in a direction that works for you.

“I understand that you need help. I just need to review my own finances before I can give you an answer. I’ll get back to you by [day].”

If the request comes up at a holiday or family gathering:

“I’d rather discuss this privately. Let’s find another time to talk.”

“Here's what I’m comfortable with, and here's what I have to protect to be financially safe, mentally safe, and to continue to be there for you in the ways that you need me.”Or, if you want to be more direct:

Sometimes, there’s an unspoken expectation to give money because of how much loved ones think you earn, says Ashley Agnew, a certified financial therapist in Boston.

Getting ahead of it before there's a tipping point can help with tricky dynamics, she says.

A recent Edward Jones report found that only about one-third of financial caregiving arrangements were planned and discussed in advance. The report also found that nearly half of financial caregivers (49%) make financial sacrifices, such as reducing or stopping saving, or tapping into their retirement account.

Factor your financial health, your values and emotional well-being into your decision:

Review your income, expenses and savings. If you provide financial help and don’t get the money back, would you struggle to pay bills or reach financial goals? Prioritize building an emergency fund, saving for retirement and paying off high-interest debt first, Carlos says.

Consider your relationship. Would you feel resentful if you aren’t repaid? How do you think your friend or relative would react if you decline to help? Could it damage your relationship, and if so, is it worth it?

Understand the credit risks. Co-signing or allowing someone else to use your credit can leave you responsible for payments and expose you to potential credit damage.

Know the tax implications. If you’re considering a substantial gift or loan, check with a tax professional about any reporting requirements and tax consequences.

Consult your partner. If you share finances, make sure to include your partner in your decision-making process, Agnew says. If you have a parent asking for financial assistance, for example, but your cultural view is different from your spouse's, that’s a big opportunity for a rift.

Aim to be clear and direct. When in doubt, keep your answer concise, Agnew says.

“Keeping it short and sweet will communicate that you've made a decision, not that you've requested a negotiation.”

Depending on where you left the conversation, you might have more steps to take.

Stick to your boundaries and promises. Did you offer to contribute money? Pay as agreed. Did you say no? Don’t let your friend's or family member’s persistence, or the opinions of other friends and relatives, change your mind.

If you offered to provide nonfinancial support, Carlos recommends pointing your loved one to local government and nonprofit resources. That might include emergency rent assistance, cheap or free financial advice, food banks and career services.

Document the details and set the plan in motion. If you agreed to loan money, send your relative a quick text recapping the terms, Carlos says. He also suggests setting up a simple repayment system together, such as a recurring Zelle or Venmo transfer.

“Fewer reminders from you mean less tension,” he says.

Get outside help, if necessary. Despite your best efforts, it can be difficult to handle the situation on your own. If you’re feeling lingering stress or your family can’t reach a resolution, you might want to bring in some help, Agnew says.

She recommends working with a financial therapist or advisor, or using a financial wellness app.
