$1,000 Invested in Eli Lilly 5 Years Ago Is Now Worth Over $5,000. Could It Soar That Much Again by 2031?

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Eli Lilly's GLP-1 drugs have been a huge success, but drug patents eventually end.

By Reuben Gregg Brewer – Nov 27, 2026 at 6:18PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Eli Lilly ›

Eli Lilly (LLY +0.01%) is executing at the top of its game right now. Wall Street has recognized the pharmaceutical giant's success, leading to a material advance in its stock price. In fact, if you had invested $1,000 in the stock five years ago, it would be worth $5,300 as of this writing. Can the good news keep flowing?

It is entirely possible, but investors need to go in with their eyes wide open to the risks. Indeed, at this point, Eli Lilly's stock may be priced for perfection. Here's why you probably shouldn't bank on Eli Lilly's stock rising as much over the next five years as it has over the last five.

Before getting into the actual business issues involved here, the bigger issue is investor sentiment. Eli Lilly's success with its GLP-1 weight-loss drugs is widely known, and Wall Street has acted accordingly. After the stock's massive rise, its price-to-earnings ratio sits at 45x. You can argue that 45x is actually below the stock's five-year average of 58x, which it is. But that doesn't mean the stock is cheap. It is just cheaper than it used to be. The P/E ratio of the average pharmaceutical stock is 27x, a number that makes 45x look very expensive.

Meanwhile, Eli Lilly's dividend yield is a miserly 0.5%. The S&P 500 index (^GSPC +1.60%) yields a bit over 1%, and the average pharmaceutical stock yields 1.3%. Again, you aren't getting any bargains when you buy Eli Lilly. And given the lofty valuation, if anything goes wrong, there's a material risk that mercurial investors move on to other stocks. That could result in a material decline in Eli Lilly's stock price rather than a continued upward climb.

So how likely is it that Eli Lilly stumbles? It is difficult to answer that question, but the risks are material. Yes, the company has done an excellent job with its GLP-1 drugs, and that's impressive. But drug patents eventually expire, so by design, this success comes with an expiration date. Which is why the healthcare giant's GLP-1 success is both good and bad.

The bad is twofold, with patent expirations merely setting up the larger problem. While the company's GLP-1 drug revenues are still growing rapidly, the company's weight-loss portfolio now accounts for more than 66% of its revenues. Eventually, Wall Street will start to worry about the size of the patent cliff Eli Lilly faces.

To the company's credit, it is using its GLP-1 success to fund acquisitions that are expanding its portfolio into new treatment areas. So it is building a pipeline that may be able to offset the revenue hit that is on the way. However, there's no guarantee of success in that endeavor. Exciting new drug candidates fail all the time.

Meanwhile, Eli Lilly isn't guaranteed to remain the GLP-1 kingpin it is today. Novo Nordisk (NVO +0.24%) has seen material success with its GLP-1 pill, and other companies, including industry giant Pfizer (PFE +0.21%), are hard at work on their own GLP-1 offerings. The competition in the GLP-1 space is going to be intense. The weight-loss market is likely to be huge, so there's room for more than one competitor. But Eli Lilly's current dominance may not last, even though continued dominance is likely being priced into its stock.

Nobody knows what the future holds on Wall Street, so it is entirely possible that Eli Lilly's stock continues its ascent. But given the stock's valuation, the nature of the drug industry, and the company's current reliance on GLP-1 revenue, investors should probably adopt a cautious stance here.
