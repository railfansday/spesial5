The Railroad Bubble (The Panic of 1873)All the steel rails! 35,000 miles! Dark railways...

Between 1866 and 1873, America laid 35,000 miles of new railroad track. The ensuing financial crisis drove roughly a quarter of the nation's 364 railroad companies into bankruptcy. Jay Cooke & Company's bankruptcy, precipitated by its investment in the Northern Pacific Railway, triggered the panic.

Railroad magnates and financiers acquired distressed railroads, reorganized them, and consolidated their operations. The resulting national logistics network helped fuel the American Industrial Revolution and enabled businesses like Sears Roebuck, Standard Oil, and US Steel to operate at unprecedented scales.

The railroad bubble burst. Railroads did not become irrelevant.

The Dot-Com Bubble (The Telecom Crash of 2000–2002)

All the fiber-optic cable! 39 million miles! Dark fiber...

Telecom companies took on massive amounts of debt to lay fiber-optic cable under oceans and across continents. Famous bankruptcies included Global Crossing, WorldCom, 360networks, and AboveNet. Worldwide telecommunications debt was estimated at $1 trillion.

By April 2001, utilization of the long-distance backbone had fallen below 3%. By 2004, bandwidth prices had fallen by more than 90%.

Companies like Google, Amazon, Netflix, and Facebook subsequently benefited from that ridiculously inexpensive bandwidth, making data-intensive services like streaming video, social media, and cloud computing economically viable.

The Internet bubble burst. The Internet did not become irrelevant.

CoreWeave alone has $47.5 billion in debt. Venture-backed AI startups are burning through VC cash. Billions of dollars in AI hardware are being purchased ahead of the electrical infrastructure necessary to operate it. Power grids, transformers, and substations cannot keep pace.

If the bubble bursts, who inherits the infrastructure?The better-capitalized hyperscalers, surviving AI companies, and potentially entirely new businesses.

But perhaps the biggest beneficiaries will be the non-tech Fortune 500 companies: banks, logistics networks, healthcare providers, manufacturers, and retailers. As the cost of compute collapses, they will be able to integrate AI into their existing systems and pursue actual productivity gains, much as businesses eventually embraced the Internet when bandwidth became inexpensive.

GPUs admittedly have a shorter economic lifespan than railroad tracks or fiber-optic cables. But the data centers, electrical infrastructure, cooling systems, and networking infrastructure will remain.
