7 Sci-Fi Books That Are Better Than 'Neuromancer'

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Cyberpunk wouldn't be what it is today without Neuromancer. Long before computers and the Internet became part of everyday life, sci-fi writers were already imagining what the world might look like with such technology, but more importantly, how it would affect humanity. Neuromancer is one of those books, imagining a dystopian society controlled by global computer networks and introducing hackers like Case, who push the boundaries of artificial intelligence.

However, Neuromancer can be a difficult and disorienting read for some. While it's an essential part of the genre, other books explore similar ideas in ways that some readers might find more engaging. From a cyberpunk reimagining of an American classic to stories about mind-controlling viruses, here are sci-fi books that are better than Neuromancer.

Set in a dystopian 21st century, Snow Crash depicts a Los Angeles that is vividly different, one where corporations rule and governments falter. By day, Hiro Protagonist is a Mafia pizza delivery driver. By night, he's a freelance hacker. When a mysterious digital drug called Snow Crash causes a fellow hacker to suffer brain damage, Hiro gets to the bottom of it—only to discover a sinister conspiracy that could endanger not only the lives of Angelians, but the entire world.

Casual sci-fi fans who find Neuromancer a tough read may prefer Snow Crash. Neal Stephenson's novel is more accessible and entertaining than Gibson's noir-inspired classic, but still offers plenty of investigative intrigue. Its cyberspace is also easier to picture, with virtual worlds, customizable avatars, and interconnected networks that feel familiar today. Published over three decades ago, Snow Crash remains relevant, especially in its depiction of a world where corporations control entire territories.

Forget F. Scott Fitzgerald's The Great Gatsby—K.M. Fajardo gives the American classic a cyberspace twist in Local Heavens. The year is 2075, and Filipino-American Nick Carraway has moved to New York City, now dubbed the Fractured New Americas. Towering skyscrapers loom over the city, while the slums of the working middle class lie beneath them. But despite cynicism over capitalism, Nick can't help but be charmed by the charismatic new-money Jay Gatsby.

Bringing hacking into a familiar tale of admiration, love, and jealousy might seem like an odd choice, but Local Heavens makes it work by giving Nick's mission a personal angle. Unlike Neuromancer, where hacking serves a larger heist, it becomes a source of emotional conflict here. Nick is a corporate-sanctioned hacker assigned to investigate Jay, but the deeper he gets into Jay's world of wealthy people using technology to cheat death, the more he finds himself drawn to Jay, putting his mission at risk.

In The Shockwave Rider, America has become a dystopia where government agencies and corporations have near-total control over computer networks. Not everyone is willing to play along, especially Nick Haflinger, a gifted programmer on the run from Tarnover, a secret program that trained him to serve the state. Armed with a stolen access code, he manipulates the network to stay one step ahead of his pursuers. But one wrong move could expose him and put him right back in their hands.

For a novel published more than half a century ago, it's mind-blowing how prophetic The Shockwave Rider is, especially since the internet was still in its infancy. While Neuromancer imagines cyberspace as a high-concept medium accessible only through neural interfaces, The Shockwave Rider shows how cyberspace is much closer to our personal lives, with computer networks connecting homes, offices, and public terminals. It also offers a sharper look at surveillance culture, showing how easily institutions can access and exploit our personal data.

If you've watched the Blade Runner movies, it's imperative that you read the original novel it's based on. Philip K. Dick's Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? is set on a post-apocalyptic Earth where radioactive fallout has driven most animals to extinction. Rick Deckard, a San Francisco bounty hunter, hopes to earn enough money to replace his electric sheep by hunting six rogue androids that have escaped from Mars.

People often talk about artificial intelligence as if artificial beings are nothing more than machines. While that may be true in a technical sense, Dick challenges this assumption by exploring the philosophical implications of AI rather than just its mechanics. He questions whether artificial beings deserve the same empathy, freedom, and dignity as humans, especially when supposedly empathetic people demonstrate cruelty and indifference toward other living beings. As AI becomes increasingly humanlike, Dick's questions about what separates humans from machines feel more relevant than ever.

Nobody is safe in Gnomon. In an alternate, futuristic Britain, an all-seeing AI keeps the population under constant surveillance. Government investigator Mielikki Neith is assigned to investigate the death of Diana Hunter, who died during a forced extraction of her memories. But as Neith examines Hunter's recorded thoughts, she uncovers something far more disturbing than a suspicious death: a hidden danger that could expose the surveillance system's nature and threaten the society built around it.

In a technologically advanced future, artificial intelligence has evolved beyond human comprehension in Void Star. Only a select few can interpret its thoughts, making them valuable assets. Told from three perspectives, the novel follows Irina, a corporate consultant who translates AI-generated thoughts; Thales, the son of a Brazilian politician; and Kern, a streetwise thief raised in the favelas. Their lives collide when Irina and Thales discover that someone can remotely extract memories stored in their cranial implants.

Zachary Mason's Void Star is essentially Neuromancer for readers who want cyberpunk that feels closer to the world we live in today. Building on the ideas established by Gibson, the novel brings them into a future shaped by climate change, mass displacement, autonomous weapons, and extreme wealth inequality. Although the story follows three main characters, the shifting perspectives are easy to follow and add nuance by showing how each experiences the same technological and social problems.

Death is no longer an issue in Altered Carbon. Set 350 years in the future, Richard K. Morgan's novel imagines a world where cortical stacks implanted in people's spines store their consciousness, allowing them to transfer into new bodies called sleeves. But immortality doesn’t come cheap. The wealthy can afford replacement bodies and backups, while poorer people still risk dying for good. In this world, staying alive even depends on how much money you have.

While Neuromancer explores cyberspace, corporate AI, and crime through an investigative plot, Altered Carbon brings those technological anxieties into the human body. Its technology can preserve consciousness, but it can't resolve the moral questions that come with it, from bodily ownership to the privilege of living forever. Morgan takes Neuromancer's criticism of capitalism and makes it more personal, turning it into a dilemma about bodily autonomy and how far people are willing to go to preserve their lives.
