Movie Theaters in 2026: Filmmakers and Exhibitors on What’s Good, What’s Bad, and What’s Changing

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

In 1948, movie studios held a monopoly in entertainment, owning the means of producing and distributing films, and the theaters themselves. The 1948 Paramount Decrees (named after the studio that pioneered the vertical integration) required studios to give up one of the three options. They decided to give up exhibition. The choice was easy: With low margins on ticket sales and the need to constantly bring audiences in, who wanted to run a movie theater?

70 years later, movie studios again faced the possibility of giving up on exhibition. The rise of streaming was said to bring about the end of theaters as we know them. But the COVID-19 pandemic brought exhibition to its closest brink of extinction yet. “COVID allowed people to acknowledge their inner recluse,” Tim League, the co-founder and former CEO of Alamo Drafthouse, told IndieWire.

After the global pandemic, lockdown protocols forced movie theaters to close for months. The luxury theater chain Arclight shuttered all its locations within weeks. Locations closed at a variety of companies, including Alamo Drafthouse. Some theaters survived off of private rentals. Even as theaters slowly reopened, COVID protocols required leaving some seats empty, meaning not all tickets could be sold.

“I think there was no right move,” League added. “We laid most of our people off. We spent a lot of energy trying to build up a TVOD streaming platform which didn’t entirely work for a lot of reasons… And so we went through bankruptcy, and it was rough. We just ran out of money.”

“There really was no strategy,” said Greg Laemmle, owner of the California-based independent theater chain Laemmle Theatres. Since Los Angeles did not allow private rentals, the company stayed afloat via concessions, pop-up events, and some virtual cinema opportunities. “It was all just trying to understand how we can maintain contact.”

Exhibition became such an unappetizing business that many of those who jumped on board the irony-fueled GameStop stock inflation of 2021 also bought shares of AMC Theatres, bringing the company to an all-time high in valuation.

But years later, the theater market is experiencing a rebirth. Driven by new theater operators and new kinds of theatrical experiences, the contemporary exhibition landscape looks very different from 10 years ago.

Theater operators paint a picture of an industry that is no longer weathering a storm. Instead, they reveal the trends driving theaters to thrive and the issues with the model that won’t seem to die.

The first high-profile artist-led theater acquisition was Quentin Tarantino’s purchase of the New Beverly Cinema in Los Angeles. The director had previously helped fund his childhood theater to keep it afloat before buying the cinema outright and taking over as programmer in 2014. The purchase of the 300-seat New Beverly was partly fueled by Tarantino’s desire to preserve what the digital era sought to make extinct: celluloid projection. Since the director’s acquisition, the theater proudly boasts that it screens only on film, usually 35mm.

Tarantino declined to comment on this story.

A few years later, COVID-19 brought a new threat to movie theaters. A similar mission-driven attitude took hold in a new crop of directors and artists: to protect not just how movies are screened, but to ensure audiences can still see them in communal spaces.

Independent film director Kevin Smith bought his hometown theater in Atlantic Highlands, New Jersey, in 2024. The Atlantic Twin Cinema was a five-screen multiplex Smith grew up going to, housed in a building over 100 years old. But over time, nearby multiplexes threatened to drive the theater out of business. When Smith’s friend Ernie O’Donnell told him the theater would be torn down to build condos, he resolved to help save it.

“Anything I ever learned about myself I never learned in a classroom, and I certainly didn’t learn it sitting in a church,” Smith told IndieWire. “But mostly everything I know about myself I learned sitting in the darkness of that theater, looking up at the great white mirror and seeing life reflected back at me.”

Smith invested $450,000 and partnered with several friends to buy the theater, rebranding it as Smodcastle Cinemas. While he mostly lives in Los Angeles, Smith travels to Jersey every month to work on the theater.

Smith described how the finances of owning a theater in a small town may not always reflect larger box office trends. “Avatar: The Way of Water” opened in 2024 and eventually grossed over $2 billion, making it one of the most successful films in history. But at Smodcastle Cinemas, they only sold 12 tickets opening weekend.

At Smodcastle, the theater has leaned into what Smith calls “The business of Kevin Smith.” This includes Q&As of his films featuring Smith and his collaborators like Justin Long for a “Tusk” screening, and Tracy Morgan for the 15th anniversary of “Cop Out.” The theater started a film festival to help showcase local New Jersey cinema. Smith was working on selling a reality show about the theater’s day-to-day operations, similar to the seven-season run of “Comic Book Men” filmed inside the comic shop he owns, but said progress has stalled.

“I love calling up Ernie and being like, ‘How many seats [have we sold] so far?’ And he’ll be like ‘120 out of 230,’ and I’m like, ‘All right man, I gotta put 90 more asses in seats. Let me see if I can jump on a radio show… go over to Manhattan and jump on “Good Day New York” and try to get the word out,’” Smith explained. “It’s fun, but it is definitely fucking work. I have a feeling Quentin don’t work nearly as fucking hard at his theater.”

Smith elaborated on the work it takes to keep the theater running, “We all got together and saved the theater, man. But it was the worst financial fucking decision I ever made,” Smith said. “Owning a movie theater in the 21st-century post-COVID [world], it’s a dicey proposition at best… We became a nonprofit theater because the movie theater’s never going into fucking profit.”

The nonprofit model has become an increasingly popular option for keeping theaters alive. In 2012, Los Angeles video rental store Vidiots transitioned to a nonprofit to stay afloat, recasting its mission around film preservation, protection, and education. The organization later received heavy support from Megan Ellison’s Annapurna Pictures, which kept the company running for two years after it shuttered its store in 2017.

“If you go across the country, you’re seeing a real boom in nonprofit or community-supported movie theaters,” said Laemmle. He highlighted the Tasveer Film Center in Seattle, which reopened the former Ark Lodge Cinemas in 2025 due to support from a local nonprofit that had run a South Asian film festival.

“Why not form a community organization that augments ticket sales with other sources of revenue, or that can take advantage of their not-for-profit status and do the enhanced kind of programming that you can’t do at a for-profit business?” said Laemmle.

The high costs of running a movie theater are also evident in one of the latest major theater purchases by an artist. Director Jason Reitman led a group of 30 filmmakers — including Christopher Nolan, Guillermo del Toro, Steven Spielberg, and Chloé Zhao — to purchase the historic Village Theater in Westwood, Los Angeles, in 2024. Since rebranding as Directors Village, the theater has partnered with the film nonprofit American Cinematheque, which will handle its programming.

The $12 million acquisition in 2024 rescued the historic theater after its lease with its former owner expired. The filmmakers will pour an additional $25 million into renovating the theater, which was open for programming in summer 2026 but closed in October and will reopen in 2027. The project required an alleged six-figure minimum buy-in from the artists involved.

Jason Reitman declined to comment through a representative, citing that it is too early to discuss plans for the theater’s renovation. American Cinematheque did not respond to requests for comment.

Jason Reitman and Co. also looked into purchasing the adjacent Bruin Theater but were ultimately deterred by the extensive cost of renovations, as previously reported by IndieWire.

The list of artists owning theaters is only growing. Kristen Stewart purchased the historic Highland Theatre in Los Angeles in February 2026 and plans to restore the space into a hub for the L.A. film community. Tarantino bought a second LA theater, the Vista, in 2021. Martin Scorsese, Ethan Hawke, and Frances McDormand joined the advisory board of the Upper West Side Cinema Center to turn the former Metro Theater on Broadway in New York City into a five-screen arthouse theater, funded by New York City grants and donors like the Kate Capshaw and Steven Spielberg Hearthland Foundation.

The trend highlights a new form of altruism by creatives seeking to keep theaters alive. But even if the business isn’t financially valuable, there is an argument for artists to be seen as patrons of their craft. “As more and more movie theaters start closing down, every filmmaker is from somewhere, and every filmmaker has their hometown theater,” said Smith. “And if you have a degree of success where you’ve got some side hustle loot and whatnot, what a great way to spend your money.”

In 2019, the Department of Justice marked the Paramount Decrees for termination, citing changes in the studio system structure and film market that rendered the law obsolete. In August 2020, while most theaters in the United States were shuttered, the Department of Justice ended the decree.

In November 2019, Netflix announced it would lease New York City’s historic Paris Theater, three months after a notice of closure was posted. In May 2020, the streamer bought the closed Egyptian Theatre in Hollywood to be co-programmed between the studio and its previous operator, American Cinematheque. In October 2021, Netflix also announced it would take over the lease of the Bay Theater in the Pacific Palisades.

These acquisitions had a touch of irony considering Netflix made a name for itself by rejecting theatrical exhibition. The streamer did not abide by the then-standard 90-day exhibition window before making its films available on the service. The Academy Awards require week-long theatrical runs to make films eligible for awards. Major chains like AMC Theatres and Regal Cinemas refused to show Netflix films (and still do on occasion; Regal and Cinemark recently announced they will not screen “The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth” due to the shortened theatrical window). But just as it became legal for Netflix to enter the theater market, it did. By becoming an exhibitor, it no longer needed major theaters to show its films.

“Netflix needs screens in the L.A. and New York market,” said James Huntsman, the co-founder of distribution company Blue Fox Entertainment Group, which purchased two former iPic theaters in June 2026. “I don’t care if you are Netflix, it’s really hard to get a screen in both of those two markets… So if you’re Netflix and you have six movies that are coming out between October and December and they all need qualifying runs of seven days, full runs in both markets, now you start seeing why they’re interested in screens.”

Amazon Studios opened The Culver Theater — the former Culver City Arclight location, which shuttered in March 2020 after the company went bankrupt due to closures from the COVID-19 pandemic — in December 2022. The theater screens a mix of new releases, repertory screenings, and hosts premieres for Amazon MGM Studios’ television shows and movies.

In October 2026, Paramount Pictures announced it took over the lease for the Bay Theater, which has been closed since the 2025 Palisades fires. The new theater, opened in November 2026 and operated by The Lot, similarly offers first-run theatrical releases from all studios, along with dine-in food.

“We’ll see if it really develops into anything more than just a novelty for them,” said Laemmle about studios purchasing individual theaters. “[They’re] one-offs and just being able to want to say that they’ve got something. But I don’t sense there’s going to be a more prolonged investment, but if it happens, it’d be great.”

The most high-profile studio entrance into the movie theater market was Sony Pictures Entertainment’s acquisition of theater chain Alamo Drafthouse in 2024. Founded by Karrie and Tim League in 1997, the company made its name as an offbeat theater option with dine-in service and strict rules against talking and phone use in its theaters.

“I think we came to it from people who loved movies but didn’t really love our options with chain theaters that we had around,” said Tim League. “We were always looking at how we can build this and build a community around sharing movies we love.”

Alamo Drafthouse began talks with Sony Pictures Entertainment in late 2023 before completing the sale in 2024.

“It was kind of essential to find a new partner as soon as we ran out of money,” said League, who has since stepped back from daily operations at the company and remains involved as Strategic Advisor.

“Why it was appealing for us as a company with our board prior to deciding that Sony was the right acquirer for us is partly because of what that also meant in terms of resources that Sony could offer… Not only in terms of Sony [being] a very big, well-capitalized company but reputationally, very financially stable, [and] very well respected in the space,” said Michael Kustermann, CEO of Alamo Drafthouse and head of Sony Pictures Experiences.

Sony is not involved with day-to-day operations of Alamo Drafthouse. But the parent company’s ownership is evident in occasional special programming, such as airing the Season 4 finale of “Breaking Bad,” which Sony’s TV division produced, in Alamo theaters in September 2026 to celebrate protagonist Walter White’s birthday.

But the largest initiative made possible by Sony’s ownership was the July 2026 announcement that Alamo Drafthouse would purchase the historic Cinerama Dome theater in Hollywood and the attached 14-screen multiplex.

“This has been the white whale for Alamo for a long time,” said Kustermann. He explained Alamo was interested in the location before the Sony deal but didn’t have the financial means to acquire a new flagship location. But after a couple of years of interest and conversations that did not progress, Alamo reached back out after the Sony deal and began serious talks in 2025.

The company plans to operate the Cinerama Dome screen unlike traditional Alamo theaters. Dine-in service will not be offered. Kustermann said that the renovations will be minimal, aiming to keep a similar seat count. The iconic curved screen that defined Cinerama projection will remain, and reprints of films screened in Cinerama such as “It’s a Mad, Mad, Mad, Mad World” will be part of the programming slate.

Renovations to the multiplex will include adding one or two 70mm projectors, karaoke rooms, an updated bar, and an aesthetic redesign inspired by the theater’s 1960s decor.

Blue Fox Entertainment was founded in 2015. The North American distribution and film sales company distributes over a dozen films a year across a variety of genres.“Exhibition is where all the glamour is, right?” said Huntsman. “Distribution is very labor-intensive; you’re supplying content to a theater, to a platform, to DVD. It’s very back office, administrative.”

“I only got into distribution as a way to get into exhibition,” Huntsman explained, adding that Blue Fox Entertainment bought its two theaters to combine the company’s passion for film and enter a new market. “I make no qualms. I’m in the money business, but I’m using movie theaters and film distribution and film sales to do that.”

When the dine-in theater chain iPic went bankrupt in 2026, Blue Fox bid on six of the 12 available locations and ultimately purchased the Westwood, Los Angeles, and New York City theaters. The dine-in service was removed, but each location has a full-service restaurant attached that does not require a cinema ticket.

By rebranding as The Cinemas, Huntsman aims to turn the theaters into viable assets that can make money beyond box office revenue. “I have private events, I have two restaurants at these locations, I do advertisements around the exterior. So if you put all that together, it’s not a bad business. If it was just a standalone straight movie theater, it’s a tough market.”

The economics of why so many new independent theaters are popping up can be traced to a basic reality of the business, according to Laemmle: the simplicity in bringing a theater back to life. “You don’t need to pay anything to acquire inventory. It’s not like opening a bodega where you’ve got to fill it with stuff. You get a film, you promise to pay a percentage of the box office to the studio, and suddenly you’re in business.”

Ticket split rates can vary per title. The rate can be anywhere from 30% for repertory screenings to over 60% for a blockbuster Disney release. Some studios send a range of split rates that can be negotiated; others (like Disney) set a non-negotiable rate.

A film’s success can also affect how much a theater owner makes per ticket sale. Speaking to this summer’s releases, Huntsman said that the underperformance of films like “Supergirl” and “Moana” meant he received a larger portion of the box office. But for big hits like “Toy Story 5,” theaters get a smaller piece of the action.

Studios can also control exhibition through clean policies: the practice that if a theater licenses a certain film, it must be on screen when the theater is open. These policies can be especially detrimental to single-screen theaters in rural areas, effectively limiting the variety of films a community can show.

At Smodcastle, Smith planned special programming with Vulgathon, a marathon of all the films in his View Askew universe from “Clerks” to “Clerks III” in order. But while ticket sales were tracking to sell out all 900 seats across five screens, Disney said that if the theater did not leave a screen open for the second weekend of “Deadpool and Wolverine,” the studio would not send them the film to screen for its opening weekend.

“So we had to leave one screen open, for fucking ‘Deadpool’ and shit, that did not sell out,” Smith said.

Advertising revenue also dropped dramatically post-COVID, according to Laemmle. He estimates that the company currently makes 25% of its pre-pandemic revenue through theater advertising. “Ad agencies had a budget that they allocated for in-theater advertising in the pre-pandemic world. All of a sudden, movie theaters were closed so they just took those same ad dollars and moved them somewhere else…That was a significant source of contribution to the bottom line that’s really gone.”

Loss of advertising revenue forces theaters to find new revenue streams. For some major chains like AMC and Regal, that includes pay-for-play trailer programming: where studios can pay to put trailers for their upcoming releases in front of new titles. This also incentivizes longer trailer blocks that can run up to 30 minutes.

Other major sources of revenue for theaters are concessions, which can offer high margins for cheap snacks like popcorn and pre-packaged candy. Beer and wine sales have also been a major boost of revenue for Laemmle Theatres.

The other option: Lean into alternative programming that may not incur as high ticket splits. The Cinemas has been experimenting with testing various repertory titles across different genres to figure out what titles will draw people back into theaters. Their upcoming lineup for November and December 2026 includes “Boyz in the Hood,” “When Harry Met Sally,” and “The Lord of the Rings” trilogy.

For Huntsman, the need for theaters to diversify revenue reflects the current model for how studios release films. “When I grew up seeing movies in theaters primarily in the ‘80s, there were like three studio releases every week…There was always the rom-com, the horror film, the action film, the indie film…Now it’s ‘Fast and the Furious’ comes out, and nobody releases anything for three weeks.” Huntsman added, “So in terms of profitability, [that’s] part of why we’re making the theater a destination.”

Rising labor, rent, and electric bills over the last few years have made it harder for theaters to improve their margins. In a search for more profits, theater operators have taken on new initiatives to upgrade the technologies and amenities associated with theater-going to bring in crowds looking for a more premium experience, with varying degrees of success.

For Alamo Drafthouse, the Sony-owned company received complaints from longtime fans in 2026 after introducing mobile ordering to its theaters, replacing the original pen-and-paper method that involved more engagement with servers. Instead, guests scan QR codes to place orders that are sent directly to the kitchen.

This move drew ire from the Alamo community, who saw it as hypocritical to the company’s prominent No Phones policy. The company’s subreddit became flooded with users saying they wouldn’t be returning to the theater amid the changes. Actor Elijah Wood, an Austin local who frequently appeared at events put on by the chain, criticized the new system, calling it “truly awful” in a post on X.

The company described the QR code ordering system as part of a larger plan to ensure long-term stability and keep innovating the experience, citing past changes like introducing reserved seating and ending its adults-only policy.

An Alamo spokesperson said that the company has not laid off any staff due to introducing QR codes and that it allows servers to handle more tabs. They added the core of the decision was to protect wages and jobs for the company’s staff working 12 months a year, but that QR codes will allow them to hire fewer seasonal staffers.

Tim League declined to comment on the QR code system.

While Alamo Drafthouse pioneered the dine-in model for theaters, League’s newest business venture is to bring exhibition into the luxury space. He launched Metro Private Cinema in 2025, a 20-screen theater in New York City’s Chelsea neighborhood that specializes in small, private theater experiences with fine-dining menus to accompany the films.

Theaters range from four to 20 seats. Bookings start at four hours, and food packages are added to the price. Guests can choose between new releases, special holiday-themed programming, or a specific movie, plus a la carte food.

“Since the dawn of cinema, it has been being tightly packed into a large room with a bunch of strangers,” said League. “So what if we kind of flip that around, and it’s you’re surrounded by friends. And how does that change the experience?”

Pricing for the experience can vary. Example packages on the website list costs starting around $200 per person. When asked about introducing a luxury price point into the theater market, League explains that costs align with the high prices commonplace in the city. “Our meals are $115 for a 13-course meal, which is very much in line with New York pricing.” League added, “We have built a more higher-end, expensive model, but I think what we’re offering is not ridiculous.”

If companies are not expanding the price-point of concessions, the other option is to expand the theatrical viewing experience itself. Sony Pictures Entertainment launched Cosm, a 364-seat immersive theater with an 87-foot curved screen, in 2024. Tickets are roughly $50, with some including themed food options. And massive Las Vegas entertainment complex The Sphere received over $400 million in revenue from sales of its multisensory AI-edited run of “The Wizard of Oz.”

In recent years, audiences have shown growing interest in premium theater presentations. Major studio blockbusters are expected to have exclusive IMAX runs. Films like “Sinners” and “The Odyssey” have driven a rise in interest in IMAX 70mm screenings, letting audiences see movies in the format directors intended.

CJ 4DPlex, the developer of premium screening formats ScreenX and 4DX theaters, reported a 26% increase in revenue in the first half of 2026 compared to 2025. Their technology includes 270-degree panoramic presentations or immersive 4DX theaters with moving seats and water effects. Ticket surcharges can range from $3 to $7 for ScreenX showings and up to $10 for 4DX tickets, compared to a traditional 2D ticket.

But the desire to jump into premium formats is not universal. Laemmle said his theaters have no plans to offer advanced presentations. “We’re not necessarily engaged in the large format with the higher ticket price because we believe that movie-going should be something that’s done regularly…Moviegoing historically was the cheapest form of out-of-home entertainment, and we believe it should stay that way.”

The movie theater industry has always existed on a wire’s edge. Owners and monopolies have come and gone. Projection trends like Cinerama and Vistavision had their moments before becoming obsolete. Television, VHS, DVDs, and streaming have all carried the threat of being the final innovation that would put theatergoing in the past forever.

“The death of our industry has been predicted since the ‘50s with every new turn,” said League. “I just think there’s [now] pressure not to lose sight of the fragility of being an awesome third space.”

2026 is currently on track to have the best box office performance globally since 2019, with the year expected to end with $35.7 billion in global ticket sales.

For Huntsman, the increase is corollary to the output the studios offer: that the box office success of “The Odyssey” and “Spider-Man: Brand New Day” is more tied to quality than any other metric. “Anyone that says, ‘Oh, movie theaters are back,” they didn’t go anywhere. There just weren’t any good movies playing.”

At Laemmle Theatres, the biggest draws of the year have been indie success stories, with films like “You Can See Everything,” “NAZA,” and “Filipiñana” bringing the theater some of its largest ticket sales.

Speaking with a variety of theater operators, the common thread in running a successful theater wasn’t what was on the screen. The key was understanding each theater’s audience. For Laemmle Theatres, it’s typically older people who go to the movies often and have a wide variety of tastes. At Metro Cinema, it’s New Yorkers with the funds to splurge on an elevated experience for special occasions. And at Smodcastle, it’s Kevin Smith fans looking for programming only Kevin Smith can provide.

The full scale of how COVID-19 changed culture has yet to be fully realized. But the renewed investment in owning and innovating movie theaters speaks to a broader desire to safeguard community spaces where people can exist between work and home. The theater industry’s revenue streams are more diversified than they’ve been in years. The allure of owning a movie theater isn’t just about making money; public goodwill has added intangible rewards for exhibitors for keeping theaters alive.

Or as Smith said, “It’s good for the heart, good for the soul, good for the optics, good for the brand.”
