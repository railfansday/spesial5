UK government rejects national consultation on AI datacenters

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Ministers point to project-level environmental checks as petition passes 39,000 signatures

The UK government has rejected calls for a UK-wide public consultation on the environmental impacts of AI datacenters, despite a petition attracting more than 39,000 signatures.

Campaign group Protect the Wild launched the petition last month, calling for a national consultation that would consider whether further expansion should be restricted, paused, or halted until those impacts are properly understood and addressed.

The nonprofit says the petition smashed the 10,000 signature threshold above which a government response is required in just three days, and the total stood at over 39,000 as this article was being prepared.

In its response, published on November 23, the government ruled out the requested UK-wide environmental consultation, saying individual projects' impacts should be addressed through the planning process. It pointed instead to a separate consultation on its draft datacenter planning policy, open until January 8, 2027.

"Datacenters are vital to the UK's digital economy. They underpin the digital services that people rely on every day, including public services, healthcare and the way people work. They also provide the infrastructure needed to develop and use AI," the Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade (BIST) says.

"Building more capacity in the UK will support economic growth and innovation, while strengthening national resilience and ensuring the UK is well placed to realize the opportunities presented by AI."

But it adds that the government recognizes the environmental impacts of bit barns are real and must be understood and mitigated, and this is one of the reasons why it established the Data Centre Covenant tests.

This Covenant, published last month, sets out tests all compute sheds "are encouraged to meet," while "strategic datacenters" are required to meet them to qualify for additional government assistance.

The government has yet to define which datacenters qualify as "strategic," saying it will publish further details and eligibility criteria in due course. Qualifying projects could receive help securing power and accelerating planning.

Briefly, the Covenant tests are that operators cover any energy costs they create, so that communities do not have to pick up the bill; that they invest in local communities; adhere to the highest environmental standards; create jobs and drive reindustrialization; and provide secure and reliable access to their compute resources.

If this has a ring of familiarity, it is because President Trump earlier this year introduced a Ratepayer Protection Pledge, under which hyperscalers and AI companies agreed to protect US consumers from any bill shocks due to the energy and infrastructure requirements of their datacenter projects. It seems that where the US leads, the UK still often follows.

The Covenant sets out efficiency targets and reporting expectations, but does not explain how compliance will be enforced or specify penalties for failing to meet its tests.

Protect the Wild notes that it "barely acknowledges wildlife, habitats or biodiversity. In fact, it doesn't mention any of those things at all."

The Covenant does, however, call on operators to "protect nature," without spelling out specific safeguards for wildlife, habitats, or biodiversity.

While the government encourages all server farms to meet the tests, only those it considers "strategic" are required to do so, as we noted above. And even that is only in order to qualify for extra government support, not in order to gain approval for a project.

Further, the campaign group contends that having environmental concerns addressed on a project-by-project basis at the planning level is a major barrier to tackling greenhouse gas emissions at a national level.

It cites the draft National Policy Statement for Data Centres, under which operational emissions from nationally significant projects in England, including those from on-site power generation, would be managed against national climate commitments at an economy-wide level rather than assessing each application's contribution to those targets.

Protect the Wild also argues that project-level assessments do not adequately capture nationwide habitat loss, even though the planning process requires consideration of cumulative environmental effects.

Datacenters have become a growing public concern in the UK, although opposition has not reached the level it has in the US, where anti-datacenter sentiment played a major part in political campaigns for the recent midterm elections.

But there were protests around the country at the start of November, such as in London and at Great Torrington, a town in Devon where a proposed 1.5 GW data campus and battery energy storage facility would cover 850 acres of nearby farmland.

Celebrity TV cook Delia Smith recently joined protests over a proposed datacenter on the edge of Norwich in Norfolk over concerns it may threaten a nearby nature reserve.

Protesters are unlikely to find much sympathy in Westminster. The government has already pinned its hopes for growing the economy on AI, as detailed in its AI Opportunities Action Plan last year, and it sees building more datacenters as key to achieving this.

The Guardian reports that Natural England, the government's own advisory body for the natural environment, is being threatened with abolition weeks after raising objections to a large AI campus near Bristol because of the threat it poses to protected wildlife and habitats.

A separate report found that the government met with 137 different businesses to discuss datacenters between July 2024 and June 2026, but did not meet a single community group or environmental organization during the same period. ®
