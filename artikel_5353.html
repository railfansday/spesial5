9 things airline passengers can and can't recover after long delays under new U.S. rules

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Cash refunds hold whatever caused a delay, but meals and hotels depend on blame. New DOT limits and an FAA rule shift that line. Here's what travelers can still recover.

Anyone who has spent an afternoon stuck at a gate tends to assume the airline owes them something for the lost hours. What a waiting passenger can count on is care rather than cash, and only when the airline is to blame. When a large carrier causes a delay, it usually offers help in kind, such as a new seat, food and sometimes a hotel room, but not one U.S. airline promised passengers money for a disruption in 2024, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. Each airline writes those promises into its own customer service plan and can strike them out whenever it chooses, so a meal voucher or a hotel room is a favor rather than a right. The one protection that holds no matter what is the right to walk away from a disrupted trip and get the money back.

Because care depends on blame, the question that decides a stranded traveler's night is who labels a delay and how. Airlines make that call themselves. In 2024, airlines used differing methods to decide whether a disruption with several causes was within their control, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. New federal limits that take effect Feb. 1, 2027, and cover flights from Jan. 1, 2027, will stop airlines from counting 10 causes of delay as their own fault. The likely result is fewer delays that come with meals and hotels, while the right to a refund stays exactly as it is.

InsideFlyer laid out what travelers can recover when their flights are delayed or canceled, from turning down vouchers to the Montreal Convention, the FAA's line-up-and-wait rule and credit card insurance. Some of these are firm rights. Some are airline promises that the carrier no longer owes once a delay is ruled outside its control, and others pay only with proof.

A delayed traveler can get cash back without anyone deciding who caused the trouble. A passenger who turns down the trip after a cancellation or major delay is owed the ticket money, according to InsideFlyer. A storm, a crew shortage and a broken plane all count the same. After the traveler turns down rebooking and stays off the plane, the airline must issue the refund automatically, with no request from the passenger, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. A stranded traveler has two choices: keep flying on the airline's terms or take the money.

The automatic payment spares a traveler any argument with the airline, but the price of the refund stays the same. "Passengers deserve to get their money back when an airline owes them — without headaches or haggling," U.S. Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg said in 2024, as reported by the U.S. Department of Transportation. A passenger who wants the cash still has to give up the flight, and in return gets back the full amount paid, including taxes and fees.

Before accepting a rebooking or airline extras, a traveler should compare them with the full price of the ticket returned.

A delay qualifies for a refund at a fixed point on the clock, not when the wait starts to feel unbearable. For a trip within the U.S., a traveler can claim their money back only once the delay reaches three hours, InsideFlyer reports, and on an international flight the wait has to reach six hours. A traveler who gives up and goes home before then has lost the trip without getting back the fare.

Most delayed domestic flights never reach the three-hour mark. Carriers that report to the Bureau of Transportation Statistics logged 85,892 domestic flights in 2022 that ran three hours or more late, a small share of all their delayed flights, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. A traveler who leaves after an hour or two at the gate has no right to a refund. The hour that matters is the one when the flight passes the mark, because that is when the traveler's options change.

A traveler whose flight passes three hours may be owed more than a refund. Airlines' own reporting tied 65% of the domestic delays that lasted at least three hours in 2022 at least partly to the carrier, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. Many travelers at the three-hour mark may therefore qualify both for the refund and for the care airlines promise when they cause a delay. They can't have both, though. The refund is paid only if the traveler declines rebooking and does not fly, and the choice comes down to one question: Does the traveler still need to get there?

The three-hour mark is a trigger for getting money back, not a price on a passenger's lost time. In 2024, the U.S. Department of Transportation asked for public comment, through an advance notice of proposed rulemaking, on whether passengers facing a lengthy delay should receive services or compensation. The refund threshold is the only firm number a traveler can rely on, and any standard for amenities or pay remains an open question.

For now, a delay's length matters only for when a traveler can leave with a refund, and that is the moment to plan around.

A refund helps a stranded traveler only if the airline pays it while the money can still cover a new flight or the trip home, and airlines face a firm deadline to do that. An airline must refund a ticket bought with a credit card within seven days, and one paid for any other way within 20 calendar days, according to InsideFlyer. Paying by card puts a traveler on the faster clock. That matters most to someone who has to buy a seat on another airline the same day, because that traveler pays for two tickets until the first one is refunded. A card holder carries that double cost for a week or a little more, while a traveler who paid another way may carry it much longer.

The traveler doesn't have to file anything to get the money. The airline owes the refund automatically to a passenger who walks away from a disrupted flight, so the deadline is the only way a traveler can check whether the airline has done its part. If the refund hasn't arrived once the deadline has passed, the airline is late, and the traveler should call or write to press for payment.

The airline has to pay without being asked, but the traveler has to notice when it doesn't.

A voucher offered at the counter after a canceled or badly delayed flight may look like a goodwill gesture, but it is the airline asking to keep a fare it already owes. Airlines may push credits, but eligible travelers can turn them down and demand cash, according to InsideFlyer. The U.S. Department of Transportation says that the refund is automatic and needs no request from the passenger, so accepting the credit is how a traveler would give the money up. The right to refuse a credit belongs only to travelers who qualify for a refund in the first place.

Some travelers will still want the credit, especially if they fly the same airline often. Airlines must honor such a credit for five years or more after issuing it, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. A long expiration date protects a traveler from losing the credit, but it does nothing for someone who needs the money for rent, a hotel or a ticket on another carrier. Miles need one more check. A traveler who paid for the ticket with miles gets miles back as the refund, but miles offered on a ticket bought with cash replace money the airline owes.

Before accepting anything, a traveler should ask one question: Is this my money coming back, or something offered in its place?

When an airline causes a delay, the big carriers commit to keeping the passenger's trip going, which is more help than a refund alone. InsideFlyer reports that these promises depend on whether the airline had control over what went wrong. That condition comes up often on long waits. Airlines listed at least one cause of their own for 62% of the domestic waits that ran three hours or longer in 2023, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. An airline cause in the mix doesn't settle how a delay is labeled, but on most long waits, the airline's share of the blame gives a passenger grounds to ask for help. A failure that looks like bad luck can also count against the carrier. In a proposal published in 2024, the department said it had told carriers that it considered the disruptions from Delta's IT outage controllable. In that case, regulators treated a computer systems failure as the airline's problem rather than as an act of nature. At the gate, a passenger's first question should be what caused the delay.

The rebooking promise is narrower up close than it first appears. Airlines generally commit to rebooking a passenger for free, according to InsideFlyer, but a seat on another carrier is a different matter. Among the 10 largest airlines in 2024, five promised to move a passenger onto a partner carrier when they caused a long wait, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. A passenger on any of the rest had no such promise and might have to wait for that carrier's own next flight.

Meals carry a condition of their own at some airlines. Carriers generally commit to meals or meal vouchers when they are at fault, according to InsideFlyer, yet that help can depend on the passenger speaking up. Southwest Airlines said in its customer service plan that meals and hotel rooms were available "upon request," as reported by the U.S. Department of Transportation. On an airline with a plan worded that way, a traveler who knows the cause and asks is the one who collects.

Congress has ordered regulators to act on delays the airline causes, but passengers can't yet claim anything new under that order. Under the FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024, the department must direct carriers to set policies for paying back travelers' meal and lodging costs after delays the airline directly causes, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. The department's proposal on these rights is an advance notice seeking public comment, not a final rule, and airlines have no new obligations under it. Until a final rule arrives, what a passenger gets at the gate depends on the commitments in that airline's own plan.

At a controllable delay, a traveler gains more by knowing the cause and asking than by citing the law.

Starting with flights on Jan. 1, 2027, federal regulators will settle part of a delay's blame before any plane runs late. InsideFlyer reports that airlines can no longer code 10 causes of delay as their own fault on those flights, under a rule that takes effect Feb. 1, 2027. The U.S. Department of Transportation says that airline customer service plans commit to care only for disruptions within the airline's control, so a delay from any of those 10 causes will come with no airline promise of rebooking, meals or a hotel room. Travelers $TRV flying through 2026 keep the current treatment, while a trip flown in early 2027 falls under the new limits.

Airline promises have depended on a delay's reported cause once before, during an emergency. During a shutdown-related FAA order in November 2025, airline commitments did not apply to disruptions caused by the national aviation system, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. That notice was guidance and did not bind airlines. The 2027 limits come instead from a rule the department published on Sept. 3, 2026.

The causes moving out of the airline's column include problems on the airline's own planes and in its own cabins, not only storms and other acts of nature. InsideFlyer reports that from 2027, hacking attacks, urgent fixes, government system outages and the ejection of a disruptive flyer are among 10 triggers that will no longer be blamed on carriers. To a passenger stuck at the gate, a rushed repair or a removed troublemaker looks like the airline's problem to solve. Yet a traveler delayed by either will have no airline promise of a meal or a room. Nor can that traveler know at the gate whether the airline will provide care, because a repair delay may or may not count as an urgent fix.

Regulators have already excused delays from one round of required repairs, well before 2027. On December 10, 2025, federal officials said they would use enforcement discretion over delays from maintenance required by an emergency airworthiness directive from the Federal Aviation Administration, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation. That notice covered only delays from that single directive. It also concerned the amenities airlines commit to, not refunds. Even before the 2027 limits take effect, a passenger delayed by repairs under that directive could not count on regulators to hold the airline to its promise of meals and a hotel.

When an agent blames a hack, a forced repair, a government system failure or a removed passenger, the traveler should stop waiting for a hotel voucher and weigh the refund instead.

A new federal rule about departures gives a delayed passenger nothing to claim. The FAA's line-up-and-wait rule went into force in late November 2026, and although it may add to delays, it creates no new right to cash, according to InsideFlyer. The FAA wrote the rule for its own workforce. The notice is addressed to the Air Traffic Organization's service units and facilities, according to the FAA, and its requirements bind those offices, so it is a rule in force rather than a proposal. Nothing in it addresses passengers or compensation. A traveler should count it as a possible cause of a delay, not as a remedy for one.

The rule sets how the FAA supervises its controllers. It has no bearing on the people waiting at the gate. The agency turned advice into an order, and the wording of its guidance changed from "should" to "must," according to the FAA. Facilities now have to meet the requirement instead of treating it as a recommendation. A delay tied to the rule starts with an FAA requirement for its own facilities, though the airline still decides how to code the cause.

A passenger still has a claim when the hold-up begins in the control tower. The FAA notice gives passengers nothing, but the refund rule does not ask whose staffing caused the wait. A traveler who declines a trip after the delay passes the thresholds is still owed a refund, whatever the cause. Once the wait runs that long and the trip is no longer worth taking, the traveler should turn it down and claim the money.

The notice also carries an end date, though that date does not settle how long the requirement itself will apply. The FAA notice is set to be canceled on June 9, 2027, according to the FAA, and it does not spell out what follows.

Whatever the FAA decides next, a traveler's leverage over this kind of delay is the refund right.

A traveler who hears that an international treaty covers flight delays may expect a set payment for the hours spent waiting, but the Montreal Convention offers no such payment. Passengers flying abroad can seek compensation for proven losses from delays, though the treaty caps the amounts, according to InsideFlyer. What a passenger can claim is the money a delay cost them, not the time or patience it took. The treaty applies to international flights, so a delayed domestic trip gets nothing from it. A passenger who walks away from a disrupted flight can take back the fare under U.S. refund rules. A passenger who keeps going abroad and pays for a hotel, meals or a new connection along the way can try to recover those extra costs under the treaty, as long as they can show what they spent.

Because payment depends on proof, a passenger delayed on an international trip should keep a receipt for every cost the delay causes, from the airport meal to the extra night in a hotel.

A traveler's own insurer or credit card issuer may pay for a hotel room and meals during a delay when the airline won't, according to InsideFlyer. That makes such coverage something to try after the airline has said no, not the first place to ask. It is a benefit that comes with a policy or a card, not a right the airline owes, so what a traveler collects depends on the plan's own terms. Weather and FAA disruptions are the delays where this coverage can matter most. An airline commits to care only for disruptions within its control, so a passenger stranded by a storm or an air traffic problem may have no airline promise of a meal or a room.

An insurer or card issuer may also cover more than a room and food, including the costs of an interrupted trip. That matters when one late departure throws off every later leg of a journey. Each of these benefits is possible rather than certain, so only a traveler who has checked their own policy or card terms knows which ones they can count on. A passenger who learns the terms at the gate, after the airline has refused, may find out too late that the plan excludes the very delay they are sitting through.

After the cash refund, the airline's promises and the treaty, the last source of help is one travelers have to confirm for themselves by reading their policy or card terms before they fly.
