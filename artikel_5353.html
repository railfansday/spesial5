‘Dillon Brooks? On Kalshi? Hell nah’: LeBron James sues the sports betting app whose AI version of him said it

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

In an ad, LeBron James sits in an NBA locker room in a No. 23 jersey with “Bron” printed across the back, scrolling through bets on his phone. He stops on a photo of Phoenix Suns forward Dillon Brooks, one of his best-known on-court rivals. “Dillon Brooks? On Kalshi? Hell nah,” he says, in James’ voice.

Except it isn’t James. It’s an AI-generated version of him, made by a two-person startup to sell a sports betting app called Bron. And James, who has an exclusive endorsement deal with Kalshi-rival Polymarket, is now suing.

King James has been at the center of many notable brand deals—spanning massive contracts with Nike, Sprite, and of course, Polymarket. But the NBA’s oldest active player, and his company LBJ Trademarks LLC, is accusing the app’s creator PlayVision Inc. of exploiting his likeness in an AI-generated ad—and using an alleged affiliation with the star to promote itself.

The suit, filed on Nov. 17 in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York against the app’s parent company and its cofounders Anish Gupta and Marc Zoghby alleges trademark infringement, false endorsement and false advertising. According to the complaint, the founders are PlayVision’s only employees, and it includes  an argument that the company deliberately chose the name Bron and used an AI version of James in its marketing to capitalize on his global recognition.

“The word ‘Bron’ has no dictionary definition and no special meaning in the context of sports analytics, wagering, or artificial intelligence,” the filing read. “Rather, in the context of anything sports-related, it refers to only one man—LeBron James.”

The lawsuit describes the campaign as an effort to create the false impression that James was affiliated with the app, which offers sports betting research tools—including bet tracking and odds comparisons. The complaint is seeking an injunction preventing the defendants from using James’ name and likeness, repayment of profits generated through the alleged infringement, and compensatory and punitive damages.

The complaint accuses the appmakers of violating James’ right of publicity under a 1903 New York law, stemming from a woman whose portrait appeared without her consent on 25,000 posters of Franklin Mills flour—and led to New York State requiring written consent to use a person’s likeness in ads. It also leans on a far newer law: New York’s first-in-the-nation rule signed by Gov. Kathy Hochul in Dec. 2025 that requires ads to disclose when they use an AI-generated “synthetic performer.”

The Bron ad carried no such disclosure, the complaint says.

“The integrity of NBA competition—and the public’s confidence that players are not betting on the games they play—is of paramount importance to the league, its players and its fans,” the complaint stated.

Gupta posted the ad to his X account on Nov. 2 with the caption “Where is Bron?”—and the AI figure, shown mostly from behind, copies James’ build and tattoos. It speaks in what the suit says is a synthesized version of his voice. Another scene shows a rider in a Bron cap on a New York City subway car stopped at 14 Street.

The complaint says James’ betting deals, first with DraftKings and now with Polymarket, have focused on football, not basketball, because the NBA bars players from betting on NBA games. By showing him scrolling NBA player props and naming Kalshi, the suit argues, the ad “falsely communicates that Mr. James wagers, or considers wagering, on NBA games and NBA player performance.”

“The Ad catapulted the Bron App from obscurity to controversy, with the Ad gaining a million views within a day—exactly as Defendants intended,” the complaint read. “But Mr. James has no affiliation with Defendants or their product. Defendants’ intentional use of artificial intelligence to steal Mr. James’s name and likeness was instead an unlawful effort to trade on Mr. James’s identity to enrich Defendants.”

The suit alleges the founders built the ad to provoke James. “Defendants calculated that the value of that attention would exceed the cost of any legal consequences, and they treated the violation of Mr. James’s rights as a marketing expense,” it reads.

PlayVision, a California-based company founded in 2025 by Gupta and Zoghby, initially developed AI software designed to analyze game and practice footage for sports teams and fans. Describing itself as “AI moneyball for sports,” the company later launched Bron as an app that marketed itself as a sports analytics and betting research platform—but by spring 2026, it pivoted to consumer betting. The Bron app pulls recommended wagers from more than 150 sportsbooks and prediction markets. It is free to download: Bron Pro costs $29.99 a month or $249.99 a year.

PlayVision and the Bron app did not immediately respond to a request for comment from Fortune.

And according to the complaint, Gupta responded to a cease and desist letter from James’ team on Nov. 5 by suggesting the app would adopt a new name. However, the company had not announced a replacement name when James filed the suit. On Nov. 16, he wrote on X: “We found Bron. Apparently, the lawyers found it too. Find out our new name Wednesday. 10 AM PT.”

At 10:02 AM PT on Wednesday, Gupta posted another video on X showing they had renamed the app to Evie.

The filing, however, still said that’s too little, too late.

“They continue to use the Bron name to this day, prolonging the controversy for as long as it continues to generate attention for the Bron App and violating Mr. James’s rights to serve their marketing goals,” the filing read. “And even a belated name change now would not remedy the exploitation of Mr. James’s brand and goodwill that allowed Defendants to garner these millions of views and attention in the first place.”

The suit also identifies fellow NBA star and former James’ teammate Luka Doncic as another player whose likeness appeared in the app’s campaign. Doncic separately sent the company a cease-and-desist letter, according to the filing.

But that hasn’t intruded on the app’s stride. In response to the suits sent their way, Gupta posted on X—this time, an AI image of the two cofounders sitting in an empty courtroom with the caption: “Taking this to another court. We ain’t done yet.”
