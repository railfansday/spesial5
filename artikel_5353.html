Revolut’s Italian Business Faces Court Oversight: 265 Warnings, Zero Suspicious Reports

A Milan court has put Revolut’s Italian branch under judicial administration for one year, in a ruling dated November 12 that came to light on Monday. The Revolut investigation found that the bank’s anti-money laundering checks let suspected criminals move money through it for five years.

The court found that at least 24 clients moved or hid about €41.9 million through Revolut accounts between 2021 and 2026. All of them are under investigation or already convicted, and some have suspected mafia ties. Two court-appointed administrators will now work alongside the bank’s managers on customer checks and transaction monitoring.

We lined up the figures from the ruling. Other banks filed 265 suspicious-transaction reports on those same 24 people, about 11 each. Revolut filed none.

Revolut is not under criminal investigation, and it says its services in Italy, where it has more than 5 million customers, will keep running normally. The case also reaches into crypto. It began with a 2024 police inspection of Revolut Digital Assets Europe, the unit that serves the app’s crypto users in Europe.

The order targets Revolut Bank UAB, the Lithuanian-licensed bank that runs the Italian branch. Milan prosecutor Paolo Storari asked for it after the Revolut investigation by the Guardia di Finanza, Italy’s financial police.

Judicial administration is a preventive measure, not a penalty. Italian courts have used it on fashion and logistics companies to fix weak controls without taking over the business. In the 39-page ruling, the court said Revolut’s failures were “embedded in a stable management model, repeatedly applied over time.” Prosecutors called the bank a “financial reservoir” for serious criminals.

Revolut said it takes its “regulatory obligations and anti-money laundering controls extremely seriously” and will cooperate fully.

The ruling’s sharpest number is the gap in suspicious-transaction reports, the alerts banks must send to Italy’s financial intelligence unit (UIF) when money looks dirty. Rival banks flagged the same 24 people 265 times. Revolut, which held their accounts too, sent nothing. That gap sits at the center of the Revolut investigation.

The suspects are linked to money laundering, tax fraud, extortion, and drug cases, some with suspected mafia ties. Prosecutors say there is no evidence that Revolut knowingly took part in their schemes. The case is about controls that didn’t catch them.

The Revolut investigation started with crypto. In 2024, Milan’s financial police inspected Revolut Digital Assets Europe, formerly RT Digital Securities Cyprus. That is the company that runs crypto trading for the app’s users across Europe, under a MiCA licence from Cyprus.

The police report, finished in December 2024, alleged more than 1 million breaches of customer due diligence and record-keeping rules and 60 failures to report suspicious transactions. It went to Italy’s Economy Ministry, which can fine the company. The ministry hasn’t opened sanction proceedings yet, and Revolut’s window to object runs into 2027.

Two more reviews are under way. The UIF has finished its own inspection of the Italian branch and is due to report soon. Bank of Italy inspectors are also checking how the bank handles transparency and customer-protection rules.

The Milan order adds to a run of trouble in Europe. Lithuania’s central bank fined Revolut Bank €3.5 million in April 2025 over gaps in how it monitored transactions, its largest fine at the time. In April 2026, Italy’s competition authority fined Revolut over €11 million for unfair practices, including blocking payment accounts without warning. Revolut said it would appeal.

The timing matters beyond Italy. Revolut has conditional approval for a US national bank charter. Cetin Duransoy, head of its US unit, said on Monday that “a massive hiccup somewhere” can have an impact in other places.

For Revolut’s customers in Italy, nothing changes today. Accounts, cards and crypto trading keep working, and the administrators don’t touch customer money. The risk is to the bank’s growth: stricter checks can mean more questions at sign-up and more frozen transfers, the same practice Italy’s watchdog fined it for in April.
