This Climber Just Broke Dean Potter’s Half Dome Speed Record By Free Soloing ‘Snake Dike’

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Michael Reese on the summit of Half Dome after breaking the trailhead-to-summit speed record (Photo: Courtesy of Michael Reese)

On October 28, 32-year-old American climber Michael Reese set a new fastest known time on Yosemite’s Half Dome, breaking an 11-year record set by the late Dean Potter. Reese climbed the 2,000-foot batholith from the Mist Trailhead to its summit in 1 hour 12 minutes 58 seconds, following Potter’s original line up Grizzly Gully and free soloing Snake Dike (5.7 R; 800ft). He broke Potter’s previous record from May 3, 2015, by about six minutes.

Potter, who famously competed with Hans Florine to set the speed record on the Nose (5.9 C2; 3,000ft), saw the Grizzly Gully to Snake Dike line as a step toward blending trail running and climbing; he was aiming for a sub-two-hour time from trailhead to trailhead. He died 13 days after setting the record in a wingsuit accident at Taft Point.

Reese grew up in Boulder and came to climbing from running and triathlon. In September, he set a new speed record on the First Flatiron, at 7 minutes 51 seconds. After interviewing him last week in Boulder for a deep dive on the culture of free solo speed climbing, Reese told me he was headed for Yosemite the following day. I wished him luck. Having climbed both the First Flatiron and Snake Dike, I knew the latter would be a different game. The grippy, coarse sandstone of the Flatirons is a far cry from the slick, polished granite of Yosemite.

A few days after his Half Dome lap, I caught up with Reese to talk about his extensive training, the day itself, his safety margins on route, why he’s hoarded the same shoes as Potter since 2021, and why he thinks Potter’s record has stood for over a decade.

The following has been lightly edited for concision and clarity.

Michael Reese: Since I got into climbing in 2014, Dean Potter has been one of my idols, probably the one who I looked up to the most. The reason for that is he’s a hybrid athlete, you know? I come from a background in triathlon. I really like to mix cardio and strength. So Dean Potter was such an amazing individual because he had a professional-level aerobic and running fitness in addition to being one of the best free soloists, one of the best speed climbers, and one of the best BASE jumpers and highliners in the world. So I said, “Wow, how amazing is this individual to mix disciplines into territory no one’s ever seen?”

In 2021, one of Dean Potter’s good friends told me about this as a potential record to try. He became one of my main climbing partners. Of course, you know, this is 2021. Dean Potter had passed away years before. I went in 2021. I got totally destroyed because the approach was way too technical. I got lost, turned around in Grizzly Gully. It’s just such technical terrain. I went back on occasion, and started to learn the very complex approach of Grizzly Gully of alternating slabs, mazes, trees.

Bit by bit, I learned every year. I first went for a speed attempt in 2023. I went again in 2024, but I was really far off. I did 1 hour 37 [minutes] both times because it was really hard to figure out where to go. I wasn’t in as good of shape as I am in now, so I needed more fitness; I needed more practice.

Finally I came back this year, being in a lot better shape, spent two weeks practicing the route, then gave it a go.

Reese: Sixteen times before going for the record. Eight times free solo. Eight times with ropes and practicing the moves.  I practiced the crux 10 times in a row each lap, going back and forth across memorizing holds.  I didn’t really tick anything.  I jumped up and down until I purposefully would slip [while being clipped in]. I’d purposefully slip and find, “Okay, it took this amount of mistakes to actually cause a fall.”

Reese: Yes. The [complexity] of the Grizzly Gully approach, which is probably a low fifth-class approach with a lot of slabs. It has everything. It has running, it has steep, fourth-class dirt. That’s why I consider it the premier of technical mountain running, mixed running, and climbing, because you’re constantly switching gears.

Reese: We always did it a combination of simul-climbing and pitching it out. We would basically simul-climb to the crux section or sections. And then on those crux sections, I would be leading, and I would stop and [built a] traditional anchor and belay them up. That way they could give me a good belay for practicing the crux section.

Reese:  There was a lot of things that almost made it where I wouldn’t have been able to get the record. For example, I had gotten very sick just from being out in the cold. I had a cough. I had rolled my ankle two days before. So I was trying to nurse all these things, and I was just right on the fence of being like, “Am I healthy enough to go through this?”

Reese:  I’d scouted it out two days previously. Then I had a nice rest day where I just went for a picnic. I tried to ice my feet and I taped up my ankle.

My girlfriend started hiking a few hours before me to get in position because I wanted her to get some footage at the base. I hung out in Curry Village and rested. I did my typical routine where I drink a tablespoon of matcha powder to wake myself up and get amped up. I drank a ton of electrolytes because I knew that dehydration could be an issue. It was a very sunny day.

I went to the parking lot, I got on my shoes, and I put on gaiters to try to prevent a rock from going in[to my shoes in] the very dirty terrain. I had on just my watch, shoes, and shorts. No shirt because it was getting warmer. So then I did my typical warm up. I jogged for 15 minutes. I went up Grizzly Gully for a little bit to warm up my steep hiking muscles. I did some 30-second strides to get my heart rate elevated.

I  sipped some water, but tried not to overdo it, did some more strides, and then I meditated for a few minutes just to get in a good head space and say, “This is what I’ve been training for. This is my opportunity and it’s time to live in the moment.”

Reese: Not particularly. People looked at me like I was kind of crazy. Why am I sprinting back and forth at the trailhead? Why do I have no shirt when it’s 40 degrees out?

Reese:  Yeah. I connected my watch to GPS and began the effort from the Mist Trailhead on the pavement there. I had to swerve around some tourists for a minute and then I turned into Grizzly Gully. I didn’t see anyone [else] until I got to the base.

Reese:  Around noon. It was a pretty chill there, about 40 degrees. I wouldn’t mind it to warm up a little. And also because I needed to be very careful about other parties on route. Most places I could pass a party, but in a few places, like those first few pitches in the crux section, it could have been devastating to have a party on the way there.

Reese: I figured starting later would improve my chances of other parties at least being out of the crux section and a little bit further up, and, and that did luckily work out like that.

Reese:  Yeah, that’s a good question. A little bit of both. There’s a lot of places where the beta would vary so much I can’t rehearse everything. On the other hand, as you know, there’s a couple sections that the dike splits into two sections, and I do have each section rehearsed. So for the most part, I have options, and it would be okay besides that crux section. That would be pretty catastrophic.

Reese:  That’s a really good question. I do always prioritize safety. So if I came across a party and they were both in the anchor, or for example, if there was maybe just a just a follower that was securely on a tight top rope and it wouldn’t add any risk to them … and I was on record pace, I would be pretty keen to find a way to make it work, and maybe ask them just not to move, and I could tiptoe around. If there was a case where there was like a leader on the crux who was not looking solid, and I thought that going through would add risk to them or me … I would maybe bail on the attempt. Safety does come first. So yes, I would bail.

Reese:   I was going really hard, but I was saving just enough gas that I knew I wouldn’t, like, completely buckle on the crux. Then I hit the climb and felt really well. I instantly knew it was gonna be a good day on the rock. I was just kind of detecting every minuscule dish, kind of nailing the beta. And there were the people filming me at the crux section, cheering. I’d rehearsed it so much, it felt like a treat just to get across the crux and nail everything and feel really solid there. I still didn’t know if I would get it because I was gonna have to climb it faster than I ever had for the record still.

But it just flowed so well. So I did Snake Dike in 10 [minutes] 55 [seconds]. There was a special moment where I knew I would get the record because I knew I needed to finish them in, you know, 16 minutes or something. I knew I had done them in about 12 or 13 before. [That day] I did them in about 11 minutes. That Death March—that was the crux. As you know, that steep stuff after the Snake Dike, I was starting to kind of buckle, and I had a headache from dehydration. My knees and arms were starting to kind of buckle from lactic acid. But I knew I’d get the record, but I also just wanted to do the best I could and make a time that would last. So I pushed to the summit, sprinted across to the true summit, past the cables to the highest end on the north end. There was a party hanging out there.

I ran and tagged the summit, breathing hard. They were like, “Geez, dude,” like, “What did, what did you do?” I couldn’t even… My response was just dry heaving ’cause I’d gone so hard.

Reese:   The Five Ten Tennies. I bought a big stockpile of them before they went out in 2021 because I knew they were some of the best shoes and may not be made again. It’s the same ones that Dean Potter used on his attempt.

Reese:    I think the length of time honestly was kind of borderline. I think anything longer, it would be important to have something. Anything shorter, you don’t really need it. You do have a good point there. This actually was kind of a borderline scenario . Everyone’s different. For me, this one was right on the fence of bringing anything or not. And it depends on the day. If it’s a hot summer day, maybe I would change and bring something. Under the same conditions, I’m happy with not having anything.

Reese:    I think it’s because of how unique and special the line is. The magic is that most peaks, the fastest way is gonna be running around to the summit. On El Cap, the impressive nose record, you know, an hour 50… you can run around El Cap faster than that. You could run around the side and get up there in under two hours.

On Half Dome, it’s interesting, because from the Mist Trailhead to the summit, you summit up the cable. Most people go up the cables in the Subdome. So that requires running around everything, whereas Snake Dike allows a very direct line to the top.

One reason I think it’s lasted so long is Dean Potter was such an amazing athlete with his combined running and climbing. He had this high level of running combined with being able to solo Snake Dike, obviously a very easy free solo for him. So there are people that may be able to run a little bit faster, but they can’t solo Snake Dike. There are people that can solo Snake Dike, but they have nowhere near the fitness.

There have been some amazing athletes like Nick Elson from Canada.  He was able to tie Dean’s time by taking the shortcut through the forest and running around, splitting Liberty Cap and Broad Nick, shortcut through the forest to cut the switchbacks, and then up the cables.  Now everyone, including myself, starts at the Mist Trailhead. Which is an extra 60 seconds. [Editor’s note: Elson’s trailhead-to-summit time was 1 hour 20 minutes 57 seconds. He currently holds the round-trip speed record on Half Dome, with 2 hours 3 minutes 52 seconds].

When people said, “Oh, is Dean Potter’s vision really the best to go up Grizzly Gully and Snake Dike?” I think what I did proves that Dean Potter had the vision because Nick Elson, being one of the best trail runners… He’s extremely fit, one of the best athletes. I wouldn’t be able to beat his time by eight minutes to the summit off of pure fitness. So the eight-minute difference shows that Dean Potter’s line, the Grizzly Gully-Snake Dike, is just faster than the shortcut through the forest and going up cables.

Reese:    The honest answer is I don’t know. Maybe someone will get really motivated and beat it sooner rather than later. Maybe it’ll be another really long stretch. I tried to put enough of a difference where it’s gonna be a little bit hard.

Records are made to be broken. I’m not gonna be surprised if it’s broken one day. I don’t know when that’ll be. The thing is, it’ll probably have to be beaten by Grizzly Gully-Snake Dike, because it’s already been shown that some of the best trail runners can’t go under 1 hour 20 minutes by any other means running around. I think and hope it’ll have to be done by someone comfortable soloing snakes, like a runner-climber hybrid. Which makes Half Dome so special. The fastest way to the top is by climbing. It’s gotta be done by a climber.
