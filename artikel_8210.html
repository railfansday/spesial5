The Best Climbing Skins for Skis of 2027

What to know: This gear guide covers the five best climbing skins for skis, tested last season by our experts. The best climbing skins overall are the Pomoca Tour Pro for their excellent glide and reliable tail clip. The most durable climbing skins are the Black Diamond Glidelite Tour for their longevity and lightweight construction.

Unless you’re one of the lucky few who go catskiing or have a helicopter at your disposal (give us a ring, if so), backcountry skiing almost always involves a fair amount of climbing. And while climbing skins—the adhesive-backed strips of fiber that glide up but not down (like fish scales), then get removed for the descent—might not feel like the most exciting piece of gear in our closet, they have the power to make or break our day.

Last winter, our testers took to the hills (worldwide) and put eight different pairs to the test to bring you our favorite climbing skins for 2027. We indulged in deep powder days in the Tetons, dropped pillows in BC, explored wide-open glaciers in the Swiss Alps, and traversed the rugged mountains of Kyrgyzstan, all the while evaluating each pair of skins for grip, glide, durability and packability.

Pros and Cons + Excellent glide + Reliable tail clip – Plush needs break-in period

If we had to choose one skin to accompany us on all of our backcountry adventures, the Pomoca Tour Pro is an easy choice. After big missions in Grand Teton National Park, short dawn patrols on Teton Pass, and quick skins out the gates at Jackson Hole, testers said the Tour Pro blends excellent grip, glide, and packability, checking all the boxes for a trusty pair.

The Tour Pro features Pomoca’s Power Hook metal tail clip, an update last season to their plastic tail clip that was frequently an early fail point on the skins. Testers who’ve been using Pomoca skins for years all commented on the small but crucial update—which “essentially added seasons to the life of these skins,” reported one tester in the Tetons.

Underneath, Pomoca’s dual fiber plush technology is designed to walk the line between grip and glide, placing grippier fiber blends at the edges of the skin (more nylon), and fiber blends with better glide (more mohair) in the center. The result is solid edgehold on firm, refrozen skintracks, while maintaining the energy-saving glide skiers have come to love from Pomoca. “A great choice for everyday ski touring,” said one tester after a season in the Tetons. “They’re no skimo skin, but they easily handled everything I could throw at them.”

While the plush layer proved to glide well on long days in the mountains, testers noticed they took a tour or two to break in. Still, give ’em a few days and these skins will be your companion for years to come.

Pros and Cons + Excellent glide + Super packable – Finicky tail clip – Hard to peel apart at first

Skins don’t have to be boring. Atomic’s MultiFit Bent Chetler skins feature tip-to-tail artwork by celebrated artist and professional skier Chris Benchetler, making a solid case for sprucing up skin design. But they’re more than just pretty: After rigorous testing in the Tetons, Alps, Kyrgyzstan, and Alaska, these skins, which feature a 70/30 mohair/nylon blend, boasted the best glide.

“After a few tours, they were super fast,” said one tester after three weeks of ski guiding a mixed bag of conditions in Kyrgyzstan, ranging from deep powder to alpine variable. “And they packed up neatly so I could stash them in a pocket.”

A few drawbacks: One tester said the tail clips felt larger than necessary and easily slid off his skis if he wasn’t careful. And while the glue was incredibly reliable, even on sub-zero days, it took some muscle to peel them apart for the first 10 or so tours before getting progressively easier. Despite the sticky nature of Atomic’s Hot Melt glue, they never left residue on the skin.

Pros and Cons + Excellent grip + Solid glide + No breakdown in stickiness over multiple laps – Glue needs a break-in period

There’s hardly a better testing ground for skins than a spring hut trip in British Columbia, and one of our testers put G3’s new Alpinist Universal skins through the wringer while ascending wide-open glaciers, heavy isothermal snow, and bulletproof re-frozen crud. “An awesome mid-point between pretty packable, extremely durable, and super reliable,” reported the tester after he returned from Blanket Glacier Chalet in the Monashee Mountains.

G3 completely overhauled their skin collection this season with a redesign of their ChannelBond glue adhesive, which you’ll find in the new Alpinist Universal, Alpinist Grip, Alpinist Glide, and Alpinist Speed. A vertical channel pattern of adhesive on each skin provided a super solid hold around the edges of the ski, which is where we typically see snow sneak in on long tours.

The glue was ultra sticky at first, but after four or five tours it got significantly easier to peel them apart. The updated glue also maintained performance during multi-lap tours, even in deep snow. It’s easy for skin glue to start to fail when you repeatedly pull them on and off but testers found the Alpinist Universal to be just as solid on lap five as lap one.

Pros and Cons + Highly packable + Durable design – Glue takes a few tours to break in

Our favorite skins for longevity, Black Diamond’s new Glidelite Tour skins replace the Glidelite Mix (our longstanding favorite for durability), with a lightened up version of the reliable construction skiers have come to know and love.

“The glue had excellent hold on skis, even during multi-lap days where the snow really starts to cake up,” said one tester after a week of skiing on Teton Pass. Testers noted that the Glidelite Tour felt lower profile than the previous Glidelite Mix skins, making it easier to fit them in a pack. “These skins folded up really nicely, so I could stuff them in a jacket pocket for quick laps where I didn’t need to do a full transition,” said one Jackson, Wyoming-based tester.

A solid metal tail clip provided a secure hold, while the wide reinforced metal tip loop fit a variety of skis, from fat powder sticks to narrower spring skis. While we still found the glue needed a tour or two to break in, it was nothing like previous iterations of the Glidelite Mix, which a few testers had found to need five to 10 days in the backcountry for the glue to mellow out.

Pros and Cons + Super packable + Reliable glue in a variety of conditions – Plush is less durable – Middle of the road glide

The Kohla Freeride Air Skins were a rock-solid midwinter companion this season. They were packable, lightweight, and easy to peel apart right out of the box. We were impressed with how well the glue held up on a seven-day hut trip in the Selkirks, where we’d frequently complete at least ten transitions while lapping short pillow runs.

Packability is thanks to a thin construction, which does result in a small hit in durability. “I usually wouldn’t throw skins in my jacket for laps since they are just so bulky, but I find myself shoving these in my jacket pockets since they lie so flat,” said a tester after a week of nonstop skiing in British Columbia.

The rubbery tail clip was our favorite of all the ones we tested this season, stretchy yet secure, and easy to slide between sizes for adjustments on the fly. The glide is solid, but nothing to write home about—it felt pretty middle of the road compared to the other skins we tested. We’ve had one pair for two seasons now, and so far the glue has held up, though we see the plush deteriorating a bit after 100 days on them—so you might look elsewhere if you tend to beat the crap out of your gear. You can get these skins from Kohla, but brands like Head, Faction, Fischer, and others also brand Kohla skins with their logo, pre-cut to specific skis.

When choosing skins, the first thing we look at is plush material. Mohair (a fiber made from angora goat hair) provides glide, while nylon provides grip. Skins heavier in nylon will feature more grip than mohair-heavy skins. Most of our favorite skins worked out to be in the middle ground range, with a pretty standard 65/35 blend of mohair and nylon respectively. If you’re newer to skinning and looking for extra grip, the Black Diamond Ascension Skins will provide extra security, albeit at the cost of weight and bulk.

We also look for packability, since there’s somehow never enough room in our packs as it is, and skins that fold up nicely (like the Kohla Freeride Air Skins) make it easy to stuff them in a jacket pocket for quick laps.

Some skins come pre-cut to your skis if you buy them as a package, which can be nice if you’re not into cutting them yourself. Buying skins to match your skis isn’t essential (most skins come with a skin-cutting tool), but will guarantee a precise fit and often a tail clip that lines up well.

Tail clips vary in shape and look but work mostly the same. Most backcountry skis already have a flat spot or notch for a tail clip to hold skins in place, but if you have rounded rockered tails it’s worth checking on how they integrate before buying.

Last winter, we put ski climbing skins to the test all over the world, seeking out the full gamut of backcountry conditions. Our testers put eight pairs of climbing skins to the test in Jackson, Wyoming, Victor, Idaho, the Swiss Alps, Washington’s Cascade Mountains, Kyrgyzstan, Alaska, the Monashees and Selkirks of B.C. and Mammoth Lakes, California, evaluating skins for grip, glide, packability and ease of setup. We sorted through which skins felt like trusty companions lap after lap, and which skins felt more finicky. While testers enjoyed plenty of backyard missions, we believe truly putting skins to the test involves taking them along on multi-day trips and missions, gliding along windswept ridges, refrozen chunder, through deep powder and across questionably frozen lakes—all the while taking note of how they did day after day.
