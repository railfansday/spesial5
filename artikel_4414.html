BYD Ti7 banned from the EU for copying the Land Rover Defender’s design

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

A Dutch court has barred BYD from selling or building its Ti7 SUV anywhere in the European Union after ruling that it infringes Jaguar Land Rover’s registered design for the Land Rover Defender. Breaking the injunction costs BYD €100,000 per day, up to €10 million.

It’s a sharp turnaround from 10 days ago, when the same court in The Hague handed BYD an early win in the Defender copycat fight. BYD’s Hong Kong shares fell as much as 4% on Monday before closing 1.9% lower.

The preliminary relief judge at the District Court of The Hague issued the ruling on Friday, November 20, in summary proceedings JLR brought against 11 BYD companies.

JLR put six registered EU designs in front of the court. The judge only needed one of them: design 005248580-0004, registered in April 2018 and covering the current Defender 110, known internally as the L663.

According to the ruling, the elements that dominate that design are the rounded “shoulders,” the rounded corners, the signature panel on the C-pillar, the rounded wheel-arch cutouts, the continuous window line, and the overall silhouette. The court found every one of them on the Ti7.

BYD argued that the headlights, grille, bumpers, hood and side profile are all different. Not enough, said the judge, who ruled that those differences, on their own or combined, don’t give an informed user a different overall impression. The court also found the Ti7 sits closer to the Defender than to older boxy SUVs like the Ford Flex, Jeep Wrangler and Mercedes G-Class.

BYD doesn’t sell the Ti7 in the EU. It told the court it has “no concrete plans” to launch it there and that the SUV can only be ordered in the UK.

The judge didn’t buy that as a defense. By BYD’s own account, it developed the seven-seat layout for the UK and Europe, a promotional video called the Ti7 the first seven-seat SUV “in Europe,” and BYD wouldn’t commit to keeping it out of the EU. That was enough for the court to find a threat of infringement.

So BYD’s EU entities can no longer import, produce, offer, sell, store or supply the Ti7. The order kicks in one week after the ruling is formally served, and it even covers BYD’s Hungarian unit, since the judge considered it conceivable the Ti7 could come out of the new Szeged factory.

The UK is a different story. The court said it has no jurisdiction over BYD UK and threw out JLR’s UK design claims, so British deliveries are still on for January 2027. That’s the one European market where BYD actually opened orders, pricing the Ti7 at £47,995, about £24,000 below the £71,840 plug-in Defender 110.

BYD said it “strongly disagrees” with the judgment and will appeal, adding that the Ti7 “is the result of BYD’s own independent design work and infringes none of JLR’s rights.” JLR told AFP it protects its intellectual property and takes legal action when necessary.

BYD has four weeks to appeal, but the injunction stays in force while it does. JLR, for its part, has six months to file a full case on the merits, or BYD can get the injunction lifted.

There’s more coming. JLR also has a separate case in Rotterdam over copyright and “slavish imitation,” with a hearing scheduled for Wednesday.

This whole thing started in August, when JLR had bailiffs raid BYD’s Dutch offices in Schiedam and Hoofddorp. The court later ordered JLR to destroy everything it seized, saying it didn’t need BYD’s internal files to make its design case. And it didn’t. JLR won on the design registration alone.

The Ti7 looks like a Defender. We said so when BYD first brought it to the UK, and so did pretty much everyone else. Chinese automakers have been building Defender lookalikes for years, but most of them stayed in China.

BYD brought one to the Defender’s home turf at a £24,000 discount, so a fight with JLR was always coming.

Nobody owns “boxy.” JLR even failed to trademark the classic Defender’s shape in the UK, a fight it lost in its legal battle with the Ineos Grenadier. But it registered the new Defender’s design in 2018, and that specific combination of shoulders, C-pillar panel and wheel arches is exactly what the court protected. That’s how design rights are supposed to work.

The actual business damage is small for now. The Ti7 sells roughly 30,000 units a month, almost all in China, and the UK launch goes ahead. The stock dip was about headlines more than lost Ti7 sales.
