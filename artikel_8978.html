singapore’s artscience museum shows design beyond the human realm

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

discover how design goes beyond humans to include animals and ecosystems as active collaborators in this expanded exhibition

When the Design Museum, London developed More than Human: Making With the Living World, it began with a deceptively simple proposition: what if design were not understood as solely by and for humans, but as something shaped in collaboration with animals, plants, and ecosystems?

Living environments are already shaped by countless organisms and relationships, each participating in the ecosystems of which humans are also a part. The exhibition asks how design might acknowledge these relationships, shifting away from an understanding of nature as a resource toward ways of craft that recognize other living beings as participants and collaborators. Ultimately, it invites us to develop more reciprocal and responsible relationships with the living world.

Following its presentation in Zurich, the exhibition now makes it way to Singapore. From November 28, 2026, to May 16, 2027, visitors can experience More than Human: Making With the Living World at ArtScience Museum.

Building on the original London presentation, the Singapore edition has been significantly expanded and co-curated by ArtScience Museum and the Design Museum, London, with support from the DesignSingapore Council.

Twenty-four works and installations by regional artists, including commissions, adaptations, and international loans, join the existing exhibition. This brings the total to more than 140 artworks, objects, and installations contributed by close to 55 artists, designers, architects, researchers, and communities, notably featuring the works of eight local Singaporean designers.

The additions bring perspectives from across Southeast Asia into the exhibition, exploring how design can be understood as part of living, interconnected systems rather than as something separate from the living world.

The exhibition includes basketry by Ye’kuana people from the Amazon region, whose practices reflect relationships between material gathering, cultural knowledge, and the forest.

There is also Aki Inomata’s How to carve a sculpture. Inomata placed wooden blocks in beaver enclosures and collected the forms left by their gnawing. Because beavers tend to chew around hard knots, the wood itself helps shape what emerges. She presents the beaver-gnawed originals alongside two versions enlarged to three times their size: one hand-carved by a sculptor, the other produced using computer-controlled milling (CNC). By placing all three side by side, Inomata asks who, or what, creates a sculpture: the beaver, the wood, the sculptor, the machine, the artist, or the relationships among them?

Taking a more hands-on but equally reflective approach, MOTH’s The More-than-human Rights mural explores Rights of Nature frameworks, exploring a world where rivers and ecosystems are recognized as living entities with intrinsic rights. Masks available to visitors reveal texts hidden in the mural.

Also worth highlighting is Zheng Mahler’s Lantau Trilogy, presented in full in this format for the first time, which focuses on the non-human inhabitants of Hong Kong’s Lantau Island. It unfolds in three acts, each devoted to a different inhabitant: bats, water buffaloes, and fungi.

To help visitors immerse themselves in these ideas, Lekker Architects designed an exhibition space that encourages shifting perspectives. Drawing abstract inspiration from navigating a hedge maze, the formation of archipelagos, and wandering through woodlands, the spatial design invites visitors to encounter the living world from different viewpoints and reconsider their place within it.

More than Human: Making With the Living World will be on view at ArtScience Museum until May 2027.
