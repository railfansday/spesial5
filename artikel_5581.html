The affordable housing we save is as important as the affordable housing we build

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

New York City’s housing plan targets 350,000 new homes and 100,000 preserved units in 5 years. A 2026 ANHD report finds 63,700 subsidized units in distress, and estimates replacement could require $10.2 billion to $26.8 billion in subsidies.   AI Summary

New York City needs approximately 700,000 new homes over the next decade. That’s the conclusion of the city’s recently released Fair Housing Growth Strategy, which establishes an ambitious framework for easing the affordability crisis in the City of New York.

The strategy sets five-year targets of 350,000 new homes and 100,000 preserved units. But we have to ask: Are we placing enough value on the housing we already have? Because, from an investment perspective, preservation may offer advantages that deserve considerably more attention.

The financial case is simple: Protecting an existing asset can require less capital than replacing it. New York City’s affordable housing inventory challenges provide an interesting case.

According to a 2026 report from the Association for Neighborhood & Housing Development, more than 63,700 subsidized affordable apartments are experiencing financial distress. The report estimates that replacing those units through new construction could require between $10.2 billion and $26.8 billion in public subsidies.

Those figures illustrate the potential financial consequences of allowing existing affordable housing to become unsustainable. Of course, not every distressed property will be lost, and not every building can be preserved economically. But the broader implication is clear. Every viable affordable property that can be preserved represents an important opportunity to protect existing housing capacity without incurring the full cost of replacement.

As we know, conventional real estate financing frequently relies on expectations of rental growth and asset appreciation. Affordable housing operates under a more stressed dynamic. On one hand, income restrictions limit revenue. On the other, maintenance, insurance, utilities and capital improvements continue to drive expenses.

These disparate realities can create financing gaps that conventional lenders and investors are unwilling or unable to absorb independently. And this is where public-private capital structures can make a substantial difference. By combining public subsidies, subordinate financing, private investment, tax incentives and long-term debt, preservation transactions can accommodate different financial objectives within a single investment.

Public capital can help reduce financing costs or absorb risks that would otherwise prevent a transaction from proceeding. Private capital can contribute investment capacity, underwriting discipline and long-term financing. Together, these sources can make preservation feasible while maintaining affordability commitments.

New York already employs variations of this approach to preserve its own large portfolio of aging housing. Facing aging buildings, constrained revenues and rising operating costs across its public housing portfolio, New York City has increasingly turned to public-private capital structures to support preservation and long-term affordability.

For example, the recently completed Reid Park Rock, a 1,696-unit, 87-building affordable housing development in East New York, redeveloped by Fairstead, BRB, LDCENY and NYHCA, cost approximately $375,000 per unit to acquire, restructure and completely modernize for the next generation. Financing partners included Vistria’s impact capital, Merchants Bank and a subordinate subsidy financing from the City of New York. The combination of public financing, expanded HUD rental subsidies and low-cost private capital made this possible.

By comparison, the cost of acquiring and developing this housing at scale today would far exceed these costs. Recently completed Innovative Urban Village, also in East New York, Brooklyn, reported a total of $270 million for 385 units, for an average of $700,000 per unit.

Similarly, Alafia Phase I, a 576-unit affordable property in East New York, recently delivered at $387 million or $672,000 per unit. Both projects required significant public subsidy, including low-income housing tax credits and subordinate low-cost subsidy loans from the state, but both came in at a staggering 80% higher per-unit development cost compared to the recently completed preservation of the Reid Park Rock partnership.

A successful preservation investment must provide sufficient resources for rehabilitation, support sustainable operations, establish appropriate reserves and maintain affordability over the intended investment horizon. That requires sophisticated capital structuring, but it also requires recognizing that an affordable housing asset’s value extends beyond immediate financial returns and ultimately serves as community infrastructure.

Preservation offers another important financial advantage: it can safeguard public and private capital that has already been deployed. Affordable housing developments frequently benefit from substantial public investment during their initial construction or rehabilitation. Those investments are intended to produce housing benefits over decades.

However, when a property becomes financially distressed or approaches the expiration of its affordability commitments, that original investment may no longer deliver its intended long-term value. Strategic reinvestment can facilitate the asset’s adjustment to the current regulatory and operational landscape, but also extend the property’s useful life, improve its financial performance and protect the affordability that the original capital helped create.

While some may view this as spending additional money on aging buildings, I see it much more as an exercise in investment stewardship. Especially because the alternative can be considerably more expensive.

New York unquestionably needs more housing construction. Preservation alone cannot create the additional inventory required to close the city’s overall supply gap. But new construction alone cannot guarantee that the city’s existing affordable housing opportunities will remain available. The two strategies must advance together.

The city’s 350,000-unit production target and 100,000-unit preservation target recognize that relationship. The challenge now is to ensure that preservation receives the same financial attention, investment expertise and execution capacity necessary to fulfill its role in this endeavor. Especially as the ANHD describes preserving existing subsidized housing as “the most practical and cost-effective strategy to maintain affordability in New York City.”

Ultimately, the success of New York’s affordable housing ambitions should be judged not only by the number of affordable homes created, but also by the number sustained.
