What it's really like to kill a fellow athlete - and the terrible grief 'you carry for the rest of your life': The cursed band of haunted sportsmen reveal the nightmares that never leave them

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Published: 21:00 EST, 19 November 2026 | Updated: 22:15 EST, 19 November 2026

Towards the end of the Australian summer of 2014-15, as the country prepared to host the Cricket World Cup, I took a flight from Sydney to Hobart, Tasmania, to watch a Sheffield Shield game between Tasmania and New South Wales at the Bellerive Oval, a beautiful ground perched on the banks of the Derwent River.

Australian sport was still in shock. A few months earlier, Australia's Test opener, Phillip Hughes, had died after he was struck on the side of his neck by a short ball from a young bowler called Sean Abbott in a game between New South Wales and South Australia at the Sydney Cricket Ground.

Hughes was treated at the ground for 40 minutes and then taken by ambulance to St Vincent's Hospital in nearby Darlinghurst in a critical condition. 'Feeling for Phil Hughes after being hit,' former Australia Test cricketer Dean Jones wrote on Twitter. 'He is not in a good way! Stay strong Sean Abbott. Not your fault, young man.'

The impact of the ball had compressed a vein in Hughes' neck and when it split, it caused a catastrophic bleed on the brain. Hughes had emergency surgery and was placed in an induced coma. He never regained consciousness and passed away two days later.

Hughes was a popular, talented, gutsy cricketer with three Test centuries already under his belt. A small guy with the heart of a lion. He was 25 years old. Cricket mourned a terrible tragedy – some called it the sport's saddest day and the agony of Hughes' family was hard to bear. But there was something else. There was an awareness that two tragedies were playing out in front of us.

Hughes was lost but the man who had bowled the delivery that felled him had been left behind, desolate and distraught. Many thought Sean Abbott, who was 22, would never play cricket again. That he would not be able to find the strength. But that was why I had flown to Tasmania. Because Abbott had found the strength.

Phil Hughes was the golden boy of Australian cricket in 2014, with three Test centuries already to his name, when he was hit by a ball bowled by Sean Abbott and died

Abbott visiting the Sydney hospital where Hughes was in a coma that he would never wake from, having suffered a catastrophic bleed on the brain

That February afternoon at the Bellerive Oval, I sat among a smattering of spectators who watched Abbott walk to the top of his mark. His team-mates shouted encouragement to him. They were willing him on with everything they had and Abbott bowled his heart out.

'Beautifully bowled, Abbo,' one of the NSW fielders shouted after one ball to former Test opener Ed Cowan. 'That's a beauty, Seanie,' another yelled after the fifth ball of his first over, when Cowan jerked his head out of the way of a short delivery so fast he fell over. Only one wicket fell that afternoon. Abbott took it.

It was one of the bravest sporting performances I have ever witnessed. Because even though cricket had voiced its support for Abbott, the sport could not protect him from his thoughts and from the knowledge he had become one of a cursed band of sportsmen who have unwittingly caused the death of an opponent during a contest.

Sometimes, tragedy in sport is not just the tragedy of the player who dies; it is the tragedy of the opponent who must live the nightmare of what he has done over and over again in his mind. To those that it has befallen, they say it is a grief that never leaves them.

That grief, that sense of a double tragedy, was brought sharply back into focus earlier this month when Tenerife striker Jesus de Miguel died in hospital the day after a sickening collision with FC Andorra goalkeeper Jesus Owono. The accident happened in the final minutes of a game in Spain's Segunda Division, when Owono hurtled out of his goal and collided with De Miguel in the centre circle. He was shown a red card for the foul.

There was an outpouring of grief for De Miguel. I was among those who observed a minute's silence for him before the Barcelona-Aston Villa Champions League tie at the Nou Camp the next evening. Tributes continue to be paid to him. Owono, meanwhile, has become a pariah. It is said that the 25-year-old rarely leaves his house.

Owono does not appear to have had the same sympathy extended to him that Abbott benefited from. His challenge on De Miguel, a striker who had had a peripatetic career in Spain's lower leagues, was reckless and cavalier. For him, the struggle with guilt and responsibility and public judgment is only just beginning.

FC Andorra will play their first home game since the tragedy against Leganes on Friday night but when they run out at the Encamp Stadium, a neat little ground beside a babbling river, the Valira d'Orient, and in the shadow of the mountains that make Andorra such a popular ski resort, Owono will not be there.

Earlier this month Tenerife striker Jesus de Miguel died in hospital the day after a sickening collision with FC Andorra goalkeeper Jesus Owono

Owono has become a pariah. It is said that the 25-year-old rarely leaves his house

The goalkeeper, who has 47 caps for Equatorial Guinea, was given the maximum sentence of a 12-match ban by the Spanish Football Federation.

Tenerife manager Alvaro Cervera suggested Owono's ban 'might feel light' to all those mourning the striker, who was 30 years old, while FC Andorra owner Gerard Pique, the former Barcelona and Spain centre back, said the maximum sanction 'puts him (Owono) in a situation he does not deserve, because he is not to blame for all of this having happened'.

No one knows whether Owono will feel able to return to football when his ban ends. Footage circulated of him in an on-field argument moments before the clash with De Miguel, and it was used to suggest that he was in an unstable state of mind.

Some have pressed for him to be charged with manslaughter. His Instagram account was deactivated after it was deluged with abuse, including messages which branded him 'a murderer'. It has been reported that his brother, who is also a footballer, has been the victim of abuse because of his association with Owono.

Owono has only communicated through a statement released by his club. 'These are the most difficult days of my life,' he wrote. 'I struggle to find the words, and I find it very hard to ​process what has happened. I feel enormous pain at the loss of Jesus. I think of him, his family, and all the people who loved him. I know there will be many questions to address, and I will face whatever comes with the utmost respect.'

I spoke to the great British fighter Barry McGuigan about Owono this week. McGuigan has suffered his share of tragedies. His brother, Dermot, took his own life. He lost his daughter, Danika, to cancer when she was just 33, in 2019. And in 1982, three years before he won the world featherweight title at Loftus Road, he nearly quit boxing altogether.

In the 12th fight of his professional career, McGuigan fought a Nigerian boxer called Young Ali at the World Sporting Club in Mayfair. He still remembers the clink of cutlery coming from the diners who were sitting ringside that night. That sound still takes him back.

McGuigan still remembers the sequence of punches that put Young Ali down in the sixth round of the fight. He saw his opponent's eyes roll back in his head and a feeling of dread washed over him. Young Ali, just 21, fell into a coma from which he never emerged and died five months later.

I spoke to the great British fighter Barry McGuigan (right) about Owono this week. McGuigan has suffered his share of tragedies

'There was the horror of that and we sat and we waited and we prayed that he would be better and he never got better,' McGuigan told me. 'I didn't know whether I wanted to continue boxing. I thought: "If I can do this to somebody…"'

He shuddered when he talked about Owono. 'Other tragedies happen and other terrible things happen,' he said. 'Life is a struggle. Something like that, you never leave it behind you.

'It is something invisible and it depends how emotionally vulnerable he is. He may never be the same again but I hope he is able to overcome it.'

There are others out there in sport, still trying to overcome it. Think of Matt Petgrave, the Sheffield Steelers ice-hockey player who was arrested on suspicion of manslaughter after his skate fatally cut the neck of Nottingham Panthers' Adam Johnson in an Elite Ice Hockey League match at Sheffield Arena in October 2023.

Think of Jochen Mass, the Formula One driver, who spent years blaming himself for the death of Gilles Villeneuve during qualifying for the Belgian Grand Prix at Zolder in 1982, because he slowed and moved to the right in front of Villeneuve. The Ferrari ran into the back of Mass and flipped in grotesque cartwheels across the track.

Think of Matthias Behr, the German fencer who killed his opponent, Vladimir Smirnov, at the 1982 World Fencing Championships in Rome when Behr's foil blade broke during a match, penetrated the Ukrainian fencer's mask and pierced his eye.

Think of Gary Cully, the Irish fighter, who learned this week that his last opponent, Lee Reeves, had died after spending three months in a coma following their bout in Dublin in August.

'What a character and a gent,' Cully wrote in tribute to Reeves. 'It's never been nothing but love between us champ, can't really believe I'm writing this but RIP brother.'

Nottingham Panthers' Adam Johnson died in October 2023 when opponent Matt Petgrave's skate cut his neck during a match

Gary Cully (left), the Irish fighter, learned this week that his last opponent, Lee Reeves (right), had died after spending three months in a coma following their bout in Dublin in August

Dr Victor Thompson, a prominent UK clinical sports psychologist, has studied the effect on competitors of being involved in the death of a rival.

'The biggest thing a player will have had to absorb normally is bad performance,' Dr Thompson says. 'Now he has to absorb the fact that he was involved in somebody dying. He will be trying to work out was it his fault? Is he to blame? And all of that, because the emotions are so high, gets associated with playing the sport in question.

'So when Sean Abbott thought about going out there again, when he thought about putting his pads on, or putting his kit on and being around team-mates or the smell of the cricket bat – it'll all be associated to that very traumatic day.'

University of North Carolina academic John M Silva III argued that aggression in sport is defined in two categories: hostile aggression and instrumental aggression. Hostile is when the main aim is to cause harm or injury to your opponent. Instrumental is aggression for a purpose, perhaps ball recovery or to sow doubt in an opponent’s mind.

The interpretation of a player's actions at a time of high stress, frustration or adversarial combat is often central to the reaction to a sporting tragedy. Whether forgiveness is extended to or opprobrium visited on the competitor who has caused the death of another.

That interpretation was used against Petgrave, who was notorious for having a bad disciplinary record on the ice. Some likened the action which fatally injured Johnson to a kung-fu kick and drew a link between it and some of his past behaviour. He was mercilessly trolled on social media. Team-mates, and many ice hockey fans, leapt to his defence and said any suggestion his action was deliberate was fanciful. They said his unusual action in lifting his left skate high was a direct result of being knocked off balance by a collision.

Petgrave could not return to ice hockey immediately even if he had wanted to. It was a full 18 months before the Crown Prosecution Service and South Yorkshire Police decided there was no realistic prospect of conviction for manslaughter and that no criminal charges should be brought against the 34-year-old Canadian.

In the intervening period, Petgrave's family set up a crowdfunding page, pointing out that he was unable to work or to return home until the CPS had decided whether to press charges. The page raised nearly £20,000 from well-wishers.

The interpretation of a player's actions at a time of high stress, frustration or adversarial combat is often central to the reaction to a sporting tragedy - as it was for Petgrave

Petgrave did receive widespread support from inside his sport, with a standing ovation at his Sheffield side's next home match, while he was beginning 18 months under investigation

'This has been the most painful and overwhelming period of our lives,' a message from Petgrave's family said at the bottom of the page. 'Watching someone I love go through this has been incredibly difficult.

'Despite everything, we are doing our best to stay strong, and the support and kindness from so many people have been a source of strength. We continue to recognise the heartbreaking nature of this situation, and our thoughts remain with everyone affected. We hold on to hope that this will soon come to an end.'

Petgrave now plies his trade in the minor league ECHL for the Indy Fuel, a team based in Fishers, Indiana. He is an ever-present this season, playing opponents like the Wheeling Nailers, the Greenville Swamp Rabbits, the Kalamazoo Wings and the Bloomington Bison.

'We understand the responsibility that comes with the great support we receive from this community,' Sean Hallett, CEO of Hallett Sports, which owns the Fuel, said when they hired Petgrave. 'We are confident he will be a positive addition to our team and our city.'

I wrote to the Fuel, asking if there was any chance of speaking to Petgrave. They did not respond. The reticence is understandable and, in many ways, laudable. Players involved in this kind of tragedy are preoccupied with respect for the victims' families and the sensitivities that come with such terrible grief. There is nothing they can say or do to make it better.

Sometimes, it is the other way round. Sometimes, it is the family of a lost loved one who find a way to ease the pain of a sportsman eaten up by shame for inadvertently causing the death of a competitor.

Mass, who died last year, blamed himself for the sickening crash that killed the most exciting, charismatic, talented, electrifying driver in Formula One. As Villeneuve's Ferrari cartwheeled across the track, breaking into pieces, he was flung clear of the wreckage still strapped to his seat, catapulting across the track until he came to rest in the catch-fencing by the side of the circuit. He died of catastrophic injuries later that evening, aged 32.

Mass was absolved of responsibility. It was a tragic accident. Villeneuve was running hot after a disagreement with his team-mate Didier Pironi and was pushing even his own boundaries. There were any number of extenuating circumstances but Mass still blamed himself. He retired from the sport the same season.

As Gilles Villeneuve's Ferrari cartwheeled across the track, breaking into pieces, he was flung clear of the wreckage still strapped to his seat, catapulting across the track

Despite being absolved of responsibility, Jochen Mass (pictured in 2019) blamed himself for the accident and retired from the sport the same year

It was years later when he felt a tap on the shoulder as he was sitting on a plane. He looked up to see Villeneuve's son, Jacques, the 1997 F1 world champion, standing over him.

'I felt so guilty about what had happened and then suddenly, there was Jacques,' Mass recalled. 'He said: "Jochen, our family – we never blamed you. It was a racing incident – just a wrong decision by the two of you."'

All the shame that Mass felt ebbed away. He found his own peace just as Behr found redemption by sheltering the grandchildren of Smirnov and his widow, Emma, at his home in Germany to protect them from the Russian invasion of Ukraine.

Behr, tortured by guilt about the accident that killed Smirnov, had written to Emma 30 years ago but the letter was lost. When he did not receive a reply, he was convinced that Smirnov's widow blamed him for killing her husband.

They finally met when a television documentary crew brought them together and Emma reassured Behr that she had never held him responsible. Behr has suffered from depression and suicidal thoughts because of flashbacks to the fatal accident.

'They are always there,' Behr said later. 'Even if I go into a liquor store and see vodka with the Smirnoff label on it, the thoughts of what happened immediately come back to me. But now that I am playing a part in the lives of Vladimir's grandchildren, it has come full circle for me. It was a gift for me. I can finally give something back.'

Maybe Abbott has been trying to give something back all these years, too. Perhaps the fine career he has had is his tribute to Hughes. He has represented Australia in one-day and Twenty20 cricket and is the all-time leading wicket-taker in the Big Bash League. He has represented Surrey in English cricket and was part of Australia's World Cup winning squad in 2023.

Many had worried that Abbott would not play again after Hughes' death. Not your fault, young man. Easy to say, harder to grasp amid the grief and the disbelief. But there was an outpouring of support for him, including from Hughes' family.

Perhaps the fine career 2023 World Cup winner Sean Abbott (bottom right) has had is his tribute to Hughes

That afternoon at the Bellerive Oval, I asked him about the day's play as he came off. He said he was happy with how it had gone. Afterwards, I sat in the stand with Abbott's team-mate Nathan Lyon, the Australia spinner, who spoke movingly about Hughes and about Abbott. At the end of the interview I thanked him for making the time to speak to me in such difficult circumstances. 'Too easy, mate,' he said.

Except it wasn't too easy. Inside, Lyon and the rest of Australian cricket were still in agony. Later that day, Lyon complained to Cricket Australia that he had not been expecting to be asked about Hughes. He was upset about my questions. I had made a misjudgment. Things were still too raw.

Lyon was comfortable talking about Abbott, though. 'The way Sean has carried himself through the whole incident, well, credit goes to him,' Lyon said. 'He is a very impressive bloke. I've got a lot of time for him. He will do anything to help anyone out. He always puts mates first. He always has your back. He is a typical good mate. I'm proud to call him a mate.

'I think (Abbott not playing again) went through the minds of a few of us. But natural instinct will come over and you do what you do best and if that's play cricket, you go out and play cricket. It's about being around your mates and the mates who are there with him now, they're the guys who are going to get him through it.'

Abbott has never spoken about the tragedy. He gave written evidence in January 2016 to the inquest into Hughes' death.

'I saw him start to sway and I ran to the other end of the pitch and I held the right side of his head with my left hand,' Abbott wrote. 'I remained on the field until Phillip was placed on the medicab and then returned to the changing room. Once in the changing room I felt confused and upset, I had a headache, people kept coming up to me but I cannot recall what they said.'

Much of the inquest was dominated by suggestions that Hughes had been subjected to a barrage of short balls and sledging. The inquest heard that Abbott's bowling partner, Doug Bollinger, had said to Hughes 'I'm going to kill you' at one stage during the game. Bollinger denied that accusation. Abbott was exonerated of any responsibility for the tragedy that unfolded.

Only rarely are there glimpses of the impact that Hughes' death has had on him. At a tribute to Hughes on the tenth anniversary of his death, Abbott broke down and wept at the end of the minute's silence at the SCG. One of his team-mates put an arm around his shoulders to try to comfort him.

Abbott at Hughes' funeral in his hometown of Macksville, Australia, in 2014

'The difficulty with boxing is that you are in this sport to hurt your opponent,' says McGuigan. 'Nobody believes - or wants to believe - that it is going to do lasting damage'

McGuigan says the pain never quite goes away. 'I had a love-hate relationship with boxing,' he says. 'The difficulty with boxing is that you are in this sport to hurt your opponent. Nobody believes – or wants to believe – that it is going to do lasting damage.

'After Young Ali died, I had to make a very serious decision about whether to carry on or not. I refocused and tried to make it a goal to win the world title and think about him when I achieved it. And the night I won the world title, I told Harry Carpenter through my tears that I was dedicating the fight to Young Ali.

'What happened in Tenerife looked like a terrible accident. Jesus de Miguel did not see him coming and he had no chance to brace for the impact. The outcome is the same as the outcome I had to deal with.

'I hope the goalkeeper will be able to come to terms with it. Sometimes, you can't. Sometimes, you are never quite the same, emotionally or mentally. It is the burden you carry for the rest of your life.'
