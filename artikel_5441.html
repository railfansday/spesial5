Who should referee a national MLS, brokers or a neutral body?

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

The piece supports national listing standardization but opposes a broker-controlled system. It argues the operator should be neutral, like payment networks, to protect competition and limit reliance on private channels.  AI Summary

When Open Real Estate was announced, the question of a national listing system moved back to the center of our industry. I am early in my read on this particular initiative. But I have spent a long time on the underlying question, enough that I have written a white paper of more than 70 pages on why a broker-controlled national MLS is the wrong design and what a right one would look like. For the leaders reading this, I want to lay out the core of that thinking, because this is a decision our industry may actually make in the next few years, and we should make it with our eyes open.

First, credit where it is due. A coalition of large brokerages banded together and put their names to a public position. That takes coordination and conviction, and it is plainly a response to a real and growing trend, the migration of listings into private and exclusive channels that never touch the open market. The concern is legitimate. When inventory moves into walled gardens, buyers see less, agents compete on access instead of expertise, and the consumer is quietly disadvantaged. Leaders who are worried about that are worried about the right thing.

I see two problems, one practical and one structural, and the structural one is the one that should concern every leader who does not run one of the three or four largest firms in the country.

The practical problem is straightforward. The founders have said that Open Real Estate is not a national MLS and is not meant to replace local MLSs. Whatever we call it, it is built to function as shared national listing infrastructure, and that infrastructure includes a participant view option, where a listing can be seen only by agents at participating brokerages and cannot be publicly marketed by anyone. In other words, the model does not stop private listings. It gives them a larger, better-organized home. The exact behavior this is meant to counter can continue, and we have built a bigger shared system that still has walls inside it.

Consider the architecture we have now. Hundreds of local MLSs operate across the country. We complain about that fragmentation endlessly, and it does create real friction for agents and consumers. But the fragmentation also delivers a benefit almost no one names. Each MLS acts as its own silo, its own firewall. Because the data lives in hundreds of independent repositories, no single company controls the national market. There is a series called Silo that captures the idea well. Each silo runs itself, and no one can command the whole system. Our MLS structure has handed us that same protection almost by accident. The walls are inconvenient, and the walls are also the reason no one firm owns the country’s data.

Now collapse all of it into one national system, whatever label it carries, and ask the only question that matters. Who runs it? If the answer is that brokers run it, then the brokers with the deepest pockets will control the nation’s listing infrastructure. The independents, the single-office shops, the regional firms, they will have little to no voice. Whoever sits atop a broker-controlled national system writes the rules for everyone downstream, including their direct competitors. That is not a free market with shared plumbing. That is a tollgate owned by a few, and everyone else pays to pass through.

Powerfact: Whoever controls a national MLS controls the terms of competition for every agent in the country. That cannot be a competitor.

So, let me be clear that I am not against unification. A national MLS done correctly would be a real asset. It would let an agent see listings nationwide rather than stitching together access market by market. It would mean one playbook, the same rules, forms, and fields in every state, so practicing across state lines or relocating would not require relearning the mechanics each time. That consistency would benefitagents, brokers, and consumers. The debate should not be unify or do not unify. The fight is already here, and it should be about who holds the technology in the middle.

And we already have a proven answer inside our own economy. Visa and MasterCard.

The first bank card came from Bank of America. When other banks saw it working, each began building its own card and its own processing, and the result was chaos, because every bank set different rules and the consumer experience depended on which card you carried. The resolution was not to let the largest bank operate everyone else’s cards. It was Visa, a separate entity sitting in the middle. Each bank still runs its own business and competes on its own terms. The technology and the standards that make the system uniform for the consumer are handled by a neutral party that no single bank owns.

That is the template for a national MLS. Not control by the largest brokerages, but a neutral, broker-independent organization that operates the technology and maintains uniform rules, while every brokerage, large and small, continues to run its own business and compete on skill and service. Keep each firm a silo where it should be a silo. Make the shared rails in the middle belong to no one in particular. That is the only structure that stays fair to the smallest independent and the largest franchise at the same time.

When the next national platform is announced, and there will be more of them, do not evaluate it on the feature list. Evaluate it on governance. Ask who owns it, who sits on the board, how the rules get changed, and what recourse a small brokerage has if the controlling firms decide to change the terms. If the governance concentrates power in a handful of competitors, the convenience on the surface is not worth the dependency underneath. If the governance looks like Visa, neutral, independent, and accountable to all participants rather than to the biggest ones, then we may finally have something worth building.

I appreciate the effort behind Open Real Estate, and I share the concern that prompted it. But good intentions do not fix bad architecture. A national listing system run by brokers would trade the problem of private listings for the larger problem of a privatized commons, owned by the few and rented to the many. We can do better, and we have a model sitting in our wallets that shows us how. Build the referee first. Then build the league.
