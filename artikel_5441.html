‘The bottleneck used to be dumpling-folding talent’: Din Tai Fung CEO on America’s top-grossing restaurant chain turning craft into corporate training

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

The restaurant chain with the highest per-store sales in America says the thing that used to limit its growth was a labor shortage of a very specific kind. Anyone who’s had the soup dumplings at the memorable chain can attest to seeing the lab-coated technicians behind the glass windows working their magic.

“The bottleneck used to be dumpling-folding talent,” Albert Yang, CEO of Din Tai Fung North America, told Fortune by email. “But through continuous improvements to our training programs, we’ve largely overcome that challenge.”

Dumpling-folding talent is a phrase with a lot of history behind it. For most of the time Chinese food has been sold in America, the people folding it were family: the owner, the spouse, the kids and, as Lucas Sin, chef at the New York chain Junzi Kitchen, put it in a 2020 Fortune roundtable, “a cousin of a cousin of a cousin from Fujian.”

At Din Tai Fung, folding is now a corporate job track: a 12-week training program, and a dumpling training manager in every U.S. restaurant, Yang told the Los Angeles Times in 2023.

That swap, from family labor to systems, is reshaping Chinese food in America. Din Tai Fung’s U.S. restaurants averaged $27.4 million in sales in 2024, nearly double the next-closest chain, according to Technomic data reported by Restaurant Business. Panda Express, co-run by an engineer who built her own point-of-sale system in the 1980s, now tops $6 billion a year.

The old Chinese-American restaurant, as the people who ran it describe it, depended on cheap labor at the start and a ready buyer at the end.

Foreign Policy reported in 2019 the waves of migrants from rural Fujian, who had staffed takeout kitchens in the 1980s and 1990s had died down as living conditions in China improved. Wages for takeout cooks had climbed from about $1,500 a month in the 1980s to close to $4,000. The family side ran out too. A 2019 New York Times analysis of Yelp data found Chinese restaurants fell from 7.3% to 6.5% of all restaurants across the 20 largest U.S. metro areas over five years, about 1,200 fewer places, as owners retired and their college-educated kids took other jobs.

“There was no succession plan, because success was seen as not having to do it anymore,” Jason Wang, cofounder of Xi’an Famous Foods, said in the Fortune roundtable.

The pressure hasn’t eased. In 2025, chain restaurant sales grew at their slowest pace outside the pandemic since the Great Recession, and Restaurant Business listed an immigration crackdown among the reasons, along with weak consumer confidence, weight-loss drugs and bad weather, in its Technomic Top 500 coverage.

Yang tells the demand side through his own childhood. Many Americans, he said, first met Chinese food at cheap fast-food and fast-casual spots opened by immigrants who couldn’t afford beautiful dining rooms.

“They were focused on surviving, supporting their families, and building a future for the next generation,” he said.

But now, the Chinese food offered by his family’s company is downright aspirational.

“I remember growing up, my mom would pack me Chinese food for lunch, and other kids would sometimes make fun of me for it,” he said. “Now, I hear from parents that their kids actually think it’s cool when someone brings leftover Din Tai Fung to school for lunch.”

Din Tai Fung was inducted into the Asian Hall of Fame this month as a “Global Family Legacy” honoree; Albert and Aaron Yang accepted on behalf of the family at the Nov. 5 ceremony in Los Angeles.

The Chinese food businesses that have scaled in America are the ones that replaced family labor with process early.

Panda Express is a top example. Andrew Cherng’s family opened Panda Inn in Pasadena in 1973, and business was so slow at first his mother would sprinkle salt on the threshold to chase off evil spirits, he said at Nation’s Restaurant News’ CREATE conference in July. His wife, Peggy Cherng, is an engineer by training. When point-of-sale systems barely existed in the mid-1980s, she built one.

“We had to develop it ourselves,” she said. Panda now has more than 2,600 U.S. locations, more than $6 billion in annual sales, and its own Panda University for employees—ranking it 11th among U.S. chains.

People in the business saw this coming years ago.

“Automation and central kitchens will take over,” Wong of the culinary federation told Foreign Policy in 2019. Zhao predicted takeouts would be consolidated under new brands that keep prices low through automation.

The newest arrivals come with systems already built. Chinese chains including HeyTea, Chagee, Luckin Coffee, Wallace, and Haidilao, some with thousands of stores at home, have pushed into U.S. cities to escape a price war in a saturated home market, the Times reported in December 2025.

“China’s food service industry is suffering from severe oversupply,” Bob Qing, founder of the restaurant investor Tomato Capital, told the paper. Limited-service Asian and noodle chains grew U.S. sales 7.8% in 2025, faster than any other segment, according to Technomic data reported by Restaurant Business.

Din Tai Fung is the cleanest example, because its whole product is the thing that’s hardest to systematize. Each Din Tai Fung restaurant turns out about 10,000 dumplings a day with roughly 30 chefs on rotation, each dumpling held to 18 folds and 21 grams, the LA Times reported in 2023. Kan Kuang-Ming, the chain’s head chef in Taiwan, told the paper: “You have to fail about 1,400 times before you can make one that is beautiful.”

In Taiwan, new chefs learn by shadowing a senior chef. In the U.S., they go through a training program instead.

“Our current training program is 12 weeks, though it still takes six months and up to a year to get up to speed,” Albert Yang told the Times then. Entry-level hourly jobs started at $20 an hour, he said, and managers at $100,000.

The family business started the old way. Founder Bing-Yi Yang opened a cooking oil shop in Taipei in 1958, according to Restaurant Business, and later hired a Shanghai-born chef to make soup dumplings, the LA Times reported. When his son Frank opened the first U.S. location in Arcadia, Calif., in 2000, the grandsons worked the register.

“We were running credit cards when we could barely even see over the counter,” Aaron Yang told the paper.

Albert Yang now says the system is far enough along to speed up. Din Tai Fung opened three U.S. restaurants this year, in Irvine and Brea, Calif., and Scottsdale, Ariz. Yang credited years of spending on the company’s people, leadership development, training programs, operational systems and production capabilities.

Asked about a ceiling, he told Fortune: “I don’t believe in setting an arbitrary limit.”

The constraint he names now is the company’s own standards.

“Today, our bottleneck isn’t necessarily a physical or operational constraint,” he said. “It’s a self-imposed one driven by the quality standards we’re unwilling to compromise.” The other is real estate: A Din Tai Fung typically needs 10,000 to 20,000 square feet, which he called “incredibly challenging” to find in a prime spot in Manhattan or Brooklyn.

But for New Yorkers who had to wait many years before the first location opened in the Broadway district, in the former Mars 2112 space, the wait is already ending for location number two: The next one is set for Downtown Brooklyn, where Din Tai Fung has signed for 20,000 square feet in The Brook, a 52-story tower at 567 Fulton St., scheduled to open in 2027, according to the Brooklyn Paper.

Nick Lichtenberg is business editor and was formerly Fortune's executive editor of global news.
