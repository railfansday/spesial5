Myanmar committed genocide against Rohingya, top UN court rules

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

The International Court of Justice has upheld large parts of a genocide case against Myanmar linked to the 2017 conflict with its Rohingya minority. It called for it to pay reparations and let refugees return safely.

The International Court of Justice (ICJ) on Thursday found Myanmar in breach of several of its obligations under international treaties to prevent and not to commit genocide.

The ICJ's president, Judge Yuji Iwasawa said that the panel of 15 judges had decided by nine votes to six that Myanmar had committed genocide against the Rohingya in Rakhine state.

The judges also found the country guilty of a series of other more specific violations by majority rulings.

"On the basis of all the evidence taken together, the Court is of the view that the only reasonable inference that can explain Myanmar's actions is an intention to destroy a substantial part of... the Rohingya population in northern Rakhine State," ICJ President Yuji Iwasawa said.

Iwasawa said that Myanmar should make efforts to pay reparations to victims or their associates where possible and appropriate.

He also called for it to facilitate the safe return of refugees, and to take measures to investigate and, if necessary, prosecute individual wrongdoing by its nationals and security forces.

The ICJ said in its consideration, the Rohingya constituted a distinct ethnic group with their own defining language and culture, as well as self-identifying as such. This means they qualified as a protected group for the purposes of international laws against genocide.

Myanmar's security forces "killed thousands of Rohingya civilians, perpetrated acts of sexual violence, as well as extreme violence against children," and also attacked Rohingya as they tried to flee the fighting, the ICJ's Iwasawa said.

"The court is fully convinced that the 2017 clearance operations caused serious bodily and mental harm to a significant number of the protected group," Judge Iwasawa said.

Iwasawa also said that some civilian participation in the clearance operations could not necessarily be considered state-ordered or state-sanctioned behavior. But the court found that the government and security forces also made no meaningful efforts to prevent non-Rohingya civilian atrocities and fanned the flames of ethnic and religious tension.

At one point, reading the details of the findings, Iwasawa noted "Myanmar's failure, genuinely to investigate the alleged atrocities, and its refusal to facilitate access to evidence and witnesses, or provide information requested."

He said this meant it was possible the court did not have reliable knowledge of information available only from within Myanmar to which it was not given access.

There was no immediate response from the government in Myanmar or the military, which is regarded as the real underlying power.

Rohingya activist Yasmin Ullah — a Canadian citizen since 2016 but who was born in Rakhine state and who was a member of Gambia's delegation that filed the ICJ case — called the verdict more important for the world than for her community.

"The Rohingya never needed a court to tell us what happened to us," she said. "We needed the world to hear it, and today it has."

Wai Wai Nu, a Rohingya activist who directs the Women's Peace Network NGO, said the world's highest court's finding followed "decades" when "the Burmese military tried to erase our identity, deny our existence and dismiss our suffering."

And the co-chair of the Arakan Rohingya National Council, Nay San Lwin, said that for Rohingya people, "justice means the restoration of our citizenship, recognition of our identity and our safe and dignified return home."

Myanmar's shadow national unity government, which includes remnants of former President Aung San Suu Kyi's National League for Democracy Party, said it "respects the authority" of the ICJ.

It said that since the beginning of the "revolution against military dictatorship" in 2021, it had affirmed its "commitment to the rule of law, human rights, justice and states' obligations to prevent and punish genocide."

Myanmar was under nominally civilian rule at the time of the atrocities, but the extent to which Suu Kyi's government held sway over the military and its actions is debatable.

UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres welcomed Thursday's verdict, calling it a "powerful recognition of the Rohingya victims of genocide" and noting it was the first verdict of its kind against a state.

"Myanmar must act in accordance with its obligation to comply with the Court's decision," Guterres said in a statement, adding that this includes "the safe, voluntary and dignified return [of Rohingya] to their place of residence nad reunification of families."

Gambia's Justice Minister Dawda Jallow, who attended the hearing, spoke to reporters afterward at the Peace Palace in The Hague.

"The victory belongs to the Rohingya," Jallow said. "They survived. They testified. And today, they will believe."

UK Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband issued a statement saying the former colonial power was "taking immediate action today in light of this judgment."

Miliband said Britain would expel Myanmar's defense attache in London and was calling for a special UN Security Council session in response. He called on Myanmar to "comply fully with the judgment," and on the international community to "play its part in supporting implementation, accountability, and justice for the Rohingya."

UN human rights chief Volker Türk said the ruling "clearly concludes the State of Myanmar stands responsible for one of the most egregious crimes known to humankind."

The case centers around the military crackdown in Rakhine state on the country's western coastline against the predominantly Muslim Rohingya minority in 2017.

Violence had been brewing for several years beforehand, but the most intense phase of the military crackdown in the north of region was triggered by a coordinated attack by a Muslim militia group against several police and military installations and personnel in August 2017.

A UN fact finding mission later concluded that some 10,000 people likely died in the military clearance operations that followed. Around 700,000 people fled to Bangladesh, where most still reside in the world's largest refugee camp.

Gambia filed the complaint at The Hague in 2019, alleging a deliberate campaign of arson, rape and murder designed to wipe out the Rohingya minority in whole or in part.

Myanmar's influential military, now back in full de facto control of the country albeit amid a long-running civil war, has maintained that it was engaged in a legitimate counterinsurgency.

Aung San Suu Kyi, who was the civilian president at the time, took a slightly more nuanced stance, but nevertheless traveled to The Hague to contest the charges, calling them "incomplete and incorrect."

The case is likely to draw close attention from those pursuing ICJ prosecutions against Israel and Benjamin Netanyahu for the war in Gaza and against Russia and Vladimir Putin for the invasion of Ukraine.
