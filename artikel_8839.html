Reading the City from Above: Drone Imagery and the Material Layers of Urban Environments

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

"Urban mining. Scoping resources for circular construction," by Areti Markopoulou and Oana Taut, introduces MatterSite, a research project developed as part of the Master in Robotics and Advanced Construction at the Institute for Advanced Architecture of Catalonia (IAAC) in Barcelona. The project explores how drone photography, robotics, and computer vision systems can be combined to identify and classify building materials and components in existing structures, particularly those approaching demolition. By investigating how certain elements could retain their construction value rather than becoming mixed with waste and losing their potential for recovery, the research proposes an approach based on the prior digitization of buildings. Through automated image processing, it aims to locate recoverable materials and components, record their positions, quantify their presence, and assess their condition to inform subsequent decisions about selective dismantling and reuse. By connecting available materials with project requirements, this survey becomes more than a documentation tool: it can also help plan a building's transformation and the management of its material resources.

When Material Surfaces and Environmental Performance Come Into Play

Connecting the material composition of cities with their environmental performance makes it possible to move beyond considering materials as isolated elements and instead examine how accumulated construction decisions influence temperature, solar absorption, water infiltration, and vegetation availability. By combining aerial imagery with thermal sensors and spatial analysis tools, researchers can investigate temperature differences across rooftops and pavements, the distribution of impermeable surfaces, the presence and continuity of green roofs, how different urban areas respond to solar radiation, and the relationships between vegetation, materials, and urban microclimates. These observations provide an architectural and territorial perspective on concepts such as urban heat islands, albedo, thermal performance, and urban permeability.

For example, the study "Combating the Heat Island Effect with Drone-Based Thermal Visualization," by Keenan Gibbons, proposes using high-resolution thermal imagery to examine a five-block urban corridor along Huron Street in Ann Arbor, Michigan. The research explores how different materials and exposure conditions influence surface temperatures. Using a drone equipped with thermal and RGB cameras, maps were generated to distinguish temperature variations across paved surfaces, rooftops, vegetation, and shaded areas. While vegetated surfaces and tree canopies recorded temperatures close to the ambient air temperature, sun-exposed concrete sidewalks reached approximately 11°C (20°F) above air temperature. Shaded concrete surfaces were considerably cooler than those exposed to direct sunlight, while some dark, bituminous roofing materials reached surface temperatures of approximately 82°C (180°F).

As Gibbons explains, heat is not distributed uniformly across the urban environment; rather, it is influenced by what lies beneath our feet and above our heads. The research highlights the significant impact of material properties on thermal conditions. Dark, dense surfaces, particularly asphalt and black roofing materials, absorb solar radiation and store heat, which is subsequently released back into the surrounding environment. In contrast, lighter-colored surfaces, vegetation, and tree canopies can reduce heat absorption and limit direct solar exposure, resulting in lower surface temperatures. Nature-based solutions also benefit from evapotranspiration, a process through which water evaporates from surfaces and is released by plants into the atmosphere, contributing to ambient cooling.

Repeated drone surveys conducted before and after construction, and across different years, can help monitor how nature-based solutions and alternative material finishes perform under changing environmental conditions. By comparing thermal data over time, researchers can assess whether these interventions maintain their cooling potential and how their performance responds to seasonal variations, material aging, and changes in vegetation.

Tracking Material Transformations Over Time

When examining materials from an aerial perspective, periodic surveys also make it possible to track how built surfaces change, new materials are introduced, and existing infrastructure is transformed. Just as cities are constantly evolving, their material composition is continuously changing. These transformations can be observed in the replacement of existing buildings with new construction, the conversion of industrial areas to other uses, the addition of green roofs, rainwater harvesting systems, and solar panels across different urban environments, as well as the ongoing evolution of public spaces.

The study "Dune Belt Restoration Effectiveness Assessed by UAV Topographic Surveys (Northern Adriatic Coast, Italy)," by Regine Anne Faelga, Luigi Cantelli, Sonia Silvestri, and Beatrice Maria Sole Giambastiani, uses drone-based monitoring to evaluate a dune restoration project in the protected natural area at the mouth of the Bevano River, along the northern Adriatic coast near Ravenna, Italy. Coastal dunes are important ecosystems that provide protection against flooding, support groundwater storage, help prevent saltwater intrusion, provide habitats for different species, and offer recreational opportunities. However, their dynamic nature also makes these landforms particularly vulnerable to environmental changes and human activity.

The study evaluates the impact of fences installed to support dune development by measuring sand accumulation and changes in vegetation cover over a five-year period. The researchers used a systematic data-processing workflow based on Structure-from-Motion (SfM) photogrammetry and Geomorphic Change Detection (GCD), comparing drone surveys conducted in 2016 and 2021. By analyzing elevation data from both surveys, the results showed that the fences effectively promoted dune recovery and growth. Significant sand deposition was recorded along the dune toe and front, covering a total area of 3,799 m², with a volume of 1,109 m³ and an average deposition depth of 0.29 m.

Advances in drone technology and Structure-from-Motion photogrammetry have made monitoring geomorphological changes and estimating sediment budgets increasingly practical, both in research and in real-world applications. Beyond documenting changes in the built environment, these methods demonstrate how repeated aerial surveys can help track the movement and accumulation of natural materials, offering valuable insights into the evolution and resilience of urban and coastal landscapes.

The value of drone imagery extends beyond documenting the physical appearance of cities. When combined with photogrammetry, artificial intelligence, geographic information systems (GIS), and other digital tools, aerial images can become a source of "material intelligence." By transforming aerial photographs into georeferenced maps, 3D models, and point clouds, these technologies facilitate the identification of changes in building surfaces, the estimation of material volumes, and the monitoring of resource flows across urban and natural environments. In this way, the focus shifts from simply observing the built environment to generating data that can inform material inventories, selective demolition, and circular construction strategies.
