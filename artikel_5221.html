TL;DR: Canaan’s results put hardware sales, treasury assets and a newly announced share-buyback program under scrutiny. Operating performance and accounting gains tell different stories.

Canaan’s quarter calls for a closer look at which activities generate cash, which change reported earnings and which consume accumulated capital. A share repurchase, an equipment sale and a change in Bitcoin’s valuation can each affect shareholders through a different route.

For investors, the practical task is to follow three different activities: selling mining equipment, operating miners and managing accumulated digital assets. Each contributes to the company’s economics in a different way.

In its November 10 earnings release, Canaan reported $4.8 million in products revenue, versus $118.6 million a year earlier. Total revenue fell from $150.5 million to $14.5 million. The company attributed the equipment decline to lower computing power sold and weaker selling prices.

That combination matters. Selling fewer units affects scale, while lower prices can squeeze the amount available to cover development and overhead. A recovery in demand would therefore need to be assessed alongside margins, rather than judged solely by the number of machines shipped.

Canaan reported $8 million in net income alongside a $42.9 million operating loss. Its financial tables separately identify gains from cryptocurrency revaluation and financial derivatives. Those items help explain why the bottom line and the operating result point in different directions.

A rising value for assets already held can improve reported earnings without creating a matching amount of new cash. Equally, selling part of a reserve can generate cash while reducing future exposure to the asset. Neither observation makes the accounting result invalid; they explain why it is incomplete as a measure of trading health.

The contrast between revenue, backlog and losses at Bitdeer is another example of why mining-related companies need more than a headline earnings comparison.

Canaan’s September operating update, issued October 15, said it had monetized 3,952 ETH and 54.5 BTC for approximately $13.9 million. It also described repurchases funded through its existing program. Those sales were previously disclosed activity, not a new November transaction.

The latest release adds an initial $20 million repurchase program under a $100 million board authorization. Authorization gives management permission to act; it should not be confused with completed purchases of that amount.

Buying back shares and retaining Bitcoin involve competing uses of capital. A repurchase may benefit remaining holders if the price and funding choices are attractive, but it also uses resources that could support equipment development, liquidity or infrastructure. The decision needs to be judged on those terms, rather than treated as automatically bullish for either asset.

The company’s second-quarter report had already described reducing inventory, matching production to demand and preserving financial flexibility. It reported $31.9 million in revenue for that earlier period, showing that the latest result follows an existing slowdown.

NewsBTC’s account of MARA selling Bitcoin while developing AI infrastructure provides a related capital-allocation comparison, though the companies’ operating models differ.

The next useful checkpoints are cash generated by operations, actual repurchases and disclosed project commitments. A larger infrastructure ambition or an improved crypto valuation can change the investment story, but neither by itself demonstrates that demand for Canaan’s core products has recovered.
