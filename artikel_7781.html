Since lunch boxes designed for the office or coolers for the beach may not stand up to the rigors of the jobsite, evaluating construction worker lunch boxes based on the following key elements will ensure you get the right product for your needs.

Lunch boxes aren’t always treated with the greatest care. Those that have a tough outer shell will be more resistant to damage from the knocks and bumps that are part of life on a busy construction site. Hard-sided lunch boxes can help prevent food from getting smashed, and some are even strong enough to sit on while you eat. Though they are more durable, their construction can make them a bit heavier, bulkier, and less portable.

On the other hand, soft-sided and collapsible coolers are quite portable. They’re almost always lighter than hard models, are a snap to store, and are frequently less expensive. Though soft-sided coolers don’t provide as much protection for their contents as hard options, a soft lunch box can be a perfectly viable option if your lunch will wait in a vehicle rather than be toted directly to the site.

Capacity requirements largely depend on how big a meal you typically pack. A larger capacity may be more versatile—it can be used for a family weekend outing, for example. Conversely, the best small coolers will be sized just right for single-meal use as well as more portable and easier to store.

Capacity descriptions vary. Often, they are given as a number of standard beverage cans, making capacity fairly easy to visualize. Quart capacity is another common measurement. Those given in inches can make it difficult to picture the true size, so it may be smart to fold some cardboard into roughly the measurements given to avoid ordering a lunch box that’s too small or large.

Multiple compartments (as often found in bento boxes) are convenient for those who take various items for lunch and wish to keep them separated. One of our top picks even includes a pill organizer.

A well-insulated construction lunch box can keep food cool or warm for some time. However, while some manufacturers indicate how long they expect the contents to maintain temperature, their estimates may be optimistic, and there isn’t any recognized standard for the amount of insulation a lunch box or cooler provides. Shoppers should use their best judgment: Look at the thickness of the insulation, how rugged the fabric and seams are, and how well the insulated area closes.

How a construction worker lunch box is carried and how easily it opens and closes is also worth considering, so examine the soundness of handles and clasps. Those likely to pack ice in the lunch box should also check how leakproof it is, particularly with soft cool boxes.

All aspects of jobsite safety are important—even regarding your lunch box. Certain products previously used to produce plastics are now deemed unsafe for use with food. The organic compound bisphenol A (known as BPA) is one of the main culprits, though PVC and phthalates (which make plastics more flexible) should also be avoided. Many manufacturers will state whether their plastic containers are free of these materials. Since food hygiene is a crucial part of food safety, remember also to consider how easy it will be to thoroughly wash all areas of the container.

As with the best coolers, keeping lunch cold in a lunch box is best accomplished by using an insulated ice bag or box with ice blocks or bags of ice inside.

Hot soups and stews will maintain the desired temperature inside an insulated container or vacuum bottle. Plug-in warmers like the Travelisimo Electric Heated Lunch Box can also heat meals so long as a wall outlet or DC port is available.

The body can absorb aluminum through contact with food. However, many sources say it passes through naturally and does not present health problems. Some aluminum lunch boxes are specifically marked as food safe.

The best way to clean most lunch boxes is with a sponge and warm, soapy water, though the manufacturer may have its own suggestions, and these should take priority.

Health experts often suggest you replace your lunch box every year since even the best options can accumulate small amounts of mold and bacteria in hard-to-clean crevices. If damaged, a lunch box should be replaced immediately to prevent any risk of contamination.

Generally speaking, no, lunch boxes are not microwavable. If you need a microwavable container, consider packing something like these PrepNaturals glass meal-prep containers inside your lunch box.
