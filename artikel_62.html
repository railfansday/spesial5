Solomon Islands ends controversial security pact with China for Australian treaty

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Australia and the Solomon Islands have signed a new treaty on security and economics

The Solomon Islands has signed a "historic" treaty with Australia, after reportedly telling China it was ending a controversial security pact signed in 2022.

At a press conference in Canberra on Wednesday, Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese and Solomon Islands counterpart Matthew Wale said the new treaty would ensure that Australia was the Pacific nation's "security partner of choice".

In 2022 the Solomon Islands, then under the leadership of now-Opposition Leader Manasseh Sogavare, signed a security and policing pact with Beijing.

There were fears the pact gave China a crucial military and strategic foothold in the Pacific amid a battle for influence in the region with the US.

On Tuesday, Australian media outlets reported that Honiara had told Beijing it would not renew the pact, understood to be expiring in April 2027.

Wale told reporters on Wednesday there were "ongoing discussions" with China but he was "unable to talk about" the pact, because of a non-disclosure agreement.

He said the treaty with Australia provided a reset after several years in which the Solomon Islands had been a point of "uncertainty and doubt in the region".

Albanese said the treaty would strengthen the two countries' security partnership.

"Backing our Pacific neighbours is an investment in Australia's own security and our own prosperity."

"We'll do that in a way that completely respects Solomon Islands sovereignty and its right to make its own decisions."

On Tuesday China's foreign ministry spokesman, Guo Jiukan, did not confirm or deny that the pact had been terminated.

"There is no element of geopolitical rivalry or bloc confrontation in cooperation between China and Pacific island countries, including the Solomon Islands," Guo said.

The pact was never publicly released, but leaked excerpts revealed it gave China extensive involvement in security and policing including deploying its own officers to the Solomon Islands, and allowed it to establish a quasi military base.

It was a "massive coup" for Beijing, said Lowy Institute Research Fellow, Dr Connor Graham, but its unprecedented scale sparked alarm in the Pacific and the West.

Wednesday's treaty had delivered "an enormous win for Canberra", and "closes the loop" on a series of security deals Canberra signed with Pacific nations in the wake of the Solomons-China pact.

"That [China pact] was the thing that put fear in the hearts of diplomats in Washington and Canberra," he told the BBC.

William Yang, the International Crisis Group's Senior Analyst for North East Asia, said the end of China's "flagship" Pacific deal and the signing of the new treaty also reaffirmed the Pacific's "longstanding consensus-based decision-making model, especially in the security space".

Under the new treaty, the Solomon Islands commits to prior consultation with Australia on critical infrastructure and to ensure any security cooperations are with Pacific Islands Forum members. China is a dialogue partner but not a member of the forum.

Honiara also commits to its sensitive infrastructure remaining "free from militarisation, any form of foreign interference or unauthorised access", and to not permit its territory to be used for any foreign military or security base.

The Solomons will receive support for its free education system and sports programs, improved visa arrangements, and support for the expansion of affordable and renewable energy and infrastructure development.

Wale visited Australia to sign the treaty, accompanied by two dozen Solomons MPs. He and Albanese said the size of the delegation demonstrated that the treaty had strong support from the country's parliament, after years of political instability - largely driven by disagreement over the country's relationship with China.
