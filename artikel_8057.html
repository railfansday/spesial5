How I Sent ‘The Prophet’ and ‘Free Rider’ on El Cap in 18.5 Hours

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Left to right: Alex Honnold, Connor Herson, and Tommy Caldwell take a celebratory selfie at the top of 'The Prophet' on El Cap. (Photo: Courtesy of Alex Honnold)

My alarm goes off, but I’m already wide awake; I never sleep well before an early start. I’ve got half an hour to pull myself together before Alex gets here and we start hiking. As I choke down breakfast, I can’t help but wonder if I’m in over my head. Despite Alex and Tommy’s best reassurances, The Prophet scares me.

Established by Leo Houlding and Jason Pickles over a 10-year saga, The Prophet (5.13d/5.14a R; 1,800ft) has developed a reputation as one of El Cap’s scariest and most dangerous free routes. Indeed, it’s broken a few bones over the years, and to this day, Leo and Jason’s ground up attempt remains one of the boldest and most heroic chapters in El Cap’s history. Growing up, I always told myself that I’d never climb The Prophet. Maybe I’d rap into the A1 Beauty (the splitter crux pitch, rated 5.13d, though Leo, Sonnie Trotter, and Alex have all talked of upgrading it to 5.14a1) to try it, but the rest of the route was too dangerous for me—I don’t do dangerous stuff.

I wasn’t particularly surprised when Alex Honnold and Tommy Caldwell started trying The Prophet this season, but I watched their progress as a friend and spectator, not a potential suitor for the route. My own project on Leaning Tower was taking all my time this season.

On November 11, while eating breakfast and preparing for a day on my project, I ran into Alex and congratulated him on his send. He was hiking up that afternoon to belay Tommy, who was four days into his send push and trying the A1 Beauty. Alex offered to jumar and belay me on a ground-up push the next day, promising that the route was safer with his beta. I joked that I’d take him up if I couldn’t find a partner the next day for my project.

I had a very promising session on my project, but that afternoon, I was still partnerless for the following day, so I called up Alex to ask if his offer was still valid. In between Tommy’s redpoint goes on the A1, Alex called me back to tell me what gear I’d need. We planned to start climbing around 4:00 a.m. the following morning.

Packing up that evening, I felt wholly unprepared. I’d been sport climbing all summer and hadn’t done more than about six pitches in a day since spring. The only valley granite I’d touched this season was my overhanging, physical project on Leaning Tower, which was almost certainly a different style from the slabby, polished, and technical El Cap granite ahead of me. I’d need to re-adapt efficiently and quickly, working with a lower stamina threshold than I was used to.

Hiking up the following morning on November 12, Alex started breaking down the route for me. He told me how he and Tommy had cleaned all the pitches, how they ticked and recorded every gear placement. As I started leading the first few pitches, I realized just how helpful this was going to be: I took only the exact rack I’d need for each pitch, and I didn’t have to stress at all about beta or gear. I took Alex at his word about the relative safety of each section (a risky decision, I know…) and tried to turn my brain off to the fullest extent possible.

I settled into a sort of flow state; with the darkness obscuring any large runouts and Alex reassuring me of each gear placement ahead of me, I climbed calmly and intentionally over the lower pitches of the route. I couldn’t fathom Leo and Jason coming up here to find loose rock, grass-filled cracks, and no obvious route. It was certainly a far cry from my own experience on the freshly cleaned and ticked route. I remained relatively calm even over pitches four through six, the first few 5.13 pitches.

As we climbed higher and dawn broke, we saw Tommy above us; he’d not quite sent the night before, but he’d decided to spend another night on the wall and watch my attempt in the morning. With Tommy’s encouragement, I climbed the Guillotine Pitch (pitch eight, 5.13b), then the Devil’s Reacharound (pitch nine, 5.13a). Before long, I joined him at his portaledge at the base of the A1 Beauty just as the morning sun hit. What a perfect splitter it looked like, too—everything I could ever want in a pitch, at the very top of El Cap.

The A1 Beauty sees direct sun from first light until sunset, and it bakes. It was already quite warm, and I figured it would only get warmer as the day progressed. Without much waiting, I racked up and started what I thought would be a beta sussing mission. A few moves in, I realized this attempt could be more than just that; with Tommy hanging on a static rope above me, telling me exactly what to do, I found myself through the low crux (~V8), then through the next section. As I got higher and higher, I tried to push down the feeling that I might just pull it off on this first attempt and instead focus on each move, each foothold, each gear placement. It felt perfect. Before I knew it, I was at the next anchor, absolutely thrilled.

As excited as I was about the A1, suddenly the route became much more intense. I was only two pitches below the summit. Two pitches from flashing the entire route in a day. Two pitches from an El Cap flash. The following pitch was easy enough, but the exit pitch intimidated me: a 13b boulder problem right off the belay, involving an insecure standup on poor footholds.

I climbed up to the boulder and back down to the belay a few times, feeling increasingly nervous. Eventually, I went for it: I placed the foot, took a deep breath, and stood. The foot held, and I excitedly reached for the next hold, which guards easier climbing to the top. I briefly lost focus, allowing a small voice to say, I’m doing it! It’s happening! The next thing I knew, my foot had slipped and I was hanging on the rope. My flash go was botched.

At that moment, I lost all perspective of the incredible morning of climbing I’d had; I was devastated. Half an hour later, topping out The Prophet after successfully climbing the pitch, I felt the most hollow I’ve felt upon topping out El Cap. Alex and Tommy joined me on top. As they packed their haulbag and portaledge, I couldn’t help but regret my missed opportunity.

Within a few minutes, however, I regained some perspective. It had been an incredibly memorable morning of climbing, and I’d still freed The Prophet ground-up in about seven hours. Only that morning, doing this would have been a dream come true, and I smiled a bit. I checked the time: 11:30 a.m. “Wow! I can drive home and meet my family in time for dinner!” I laughed and joked to Tommy and Alex.

Alex turned and gave me a wry smile. “You know, you could do a Double right now. Compared to The Prophet, Free Rider will probably feel pretty easy.”

Tommy pitched in: “Yeah, I’d jug Free Rider this afternoon if you want!”

I stopped sorting my gear and stared. Great. I’m getting peer pressured by my heroes.

Among the rules I live by is that if Alex or Tommy offers something, I don’t turn them down. Especially a support mission on El Cap. As tired and emotionally drained as I was, I reminded myself just how much I’ve wanted to do the El Cap Double my entire life. I remembered topping out the free Nose-in-a-day last year, when I called every climber in the Valley to see if I could find a partner for a Double that day, to no avail. This sort of opportunity doesn’t come around particularly often. Even as I waffled about whether to go for it, the decision had been made. In a few hours’ time, I knew I’d be starting up Freeblast.

The climbing on Free Rider is significantly easier than on The Prophet, and I’d done most of the pitches when I freed the Salathé in 2022. I knew there would be little suspense about whether I’d be physically capable of sending it. However, I also knew I wasn’t in big walling shape, and besides, this would be far and away the most pitches I’ve climbed in a day. I remember watching the video of Tommy freeing the El Cap double back in 2005, where he nearly lost it at the Enduro Corner. I wondered if I was in for a similar bout.

The first few pitches on Free Rider went remarkably smoothly. Alex, still keen on supporting me, had jugged our day pack up the Heart Lines, which made Freeblast an absolute breeze. My feet were hurting, but I could feel the ibuprofen I’d pounded at the base starting to kick in. I settled into a flow, and pitch after pitch flew past. I felt remarkably fresh. With Tommy’s encouragement and psych, I was optimistic.

Around the Monster Offwidth (pitch 20 of 30 on Free Rider), darkness fell. I could feel the fatigue building; our pace slowed, but we kept moving. I was making a conscious effort to stay positive and cheerful, to act like I had endless energy in the hopes that I could fake that energy into existence.

When we reached the crux, I opted for the Teflon Corner instead of the Boulder Problem. I’d never tried the Boulder before, and I didn’t want my first attempt to be 40-odd pitches into the day. The Teflon went down on my first try, and before long, we were at the Enduro Corner. Tommy suggested that I skip the hanging belay at the end of the Enduro Corner and link it into the Round Table Traverse. I rolled my eyes, thinking that I’d be lucky to scrape my way up the Corner on its own, but to my surprise, I felt remarkably fresh. I reached the hanging belay and kept climbing, thankful to have cleared that last hurdle.

At 10:45 p.m., a little more than eight hours after starting Free Rider and 18.5 hours after starting up The Prophet, Tommy and I topped out to strong, cold winds. We didn’t stick around long to celebrate; already shivering, we quickly packed and started running to stay warm on the hike down. As we got lower and warmed up, however, we slowed down and soaked in the unforgettable day we’d just had: around 45 pitches of climbing for a free El Cap Double!

Alex and Tommy both have families. They both had good reason to rush home ahead of the incoming storm instead of supporting some kid who wanted to try their project. And to be honest, I wouldn’t have blamed them if they didn’t want me to mooch off their hard work prepping The Prophet. There’s absolutely no way I could have done The Prophet ground up and in a day without the labor Tommy and Alex spent to find the route, clean loose rock, and find all the gear beta. After four days on the wall and away from his family, I would have completely understood if Tommy wanted to get home to his kids rather than go up El Cap a second time that day—especially with Tioga Pass imminently closing, which meant supporting me on Free Rider would lengthen his drive home by about an hour.

Nevertheless, their support that day exemplifies the exact values I love to see in climbing. This is the sport I want climbing to be: one where the giants, the biggest legends of the game, are generous enough to go out of their way to help younger up-and-comers. A sport where the younger generation is not seen as a fresh wave of competition but is instead embraced and mentored.

Not flashing The Prophet is certainly going to sting for a while. I still replay that moment on the exit pitch daily, and I’m sure I’ll always feel some regret about it. Still, the more I reflect on that day, the more Alex and Tommy’s support stands out to me. Every outing leaves us with a lesson or slightly shifted worldview. In 10 or 20 years, I’m confident that looking back on the El Cap Double will remind me that there is a world larger than my own climbing; that, like Tommy and Alex, I can make another climber’s entire season by simply offering them a few hours of my time and a bit of encouragement. As I grow and find my place in the climbing world, I can only hope to be as influential a mentor to future generations.

1It’s hard to guess after only one attempt, but I wouldn’t argue with 5.14a for that pitch! I also could see upper-end 5.13d, though.
