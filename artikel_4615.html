The Psychology of a Hippopotamus's Passing

Updated November 23, 2026 | Reviewed by Ekua Hagan

Last week, the death of Fiona, a hippopotamus residing at the Cincinnati Zoo, resulted in a massive outpouring of grief. As a Cincinnati resident, I was caught off guard by the magnitude of concern expressed not only locally but also around the world. This episode led me to ponder the psychology underlying why Fiona's death had such a profound impact.

As a member of the Cincinnati Zoo myself, I felt very sad to learn of Fiona's passing. In Cincinnati, this hippo had become a community icon. Fiona was born prematurely nearly 10 years ago, weighing about half as much as a typical newborn hippo (Cincinnati Zoo, 2026). Over the weeks that followed, the public watched zookeepers keep Fiona alive through round-the-clock monitoring in a makeshift ICU, feeding her by hand and celebrating each milestone she reached on her path to survival. When Fiona died, buildings throughout Cincinnati were illuminated in purple to commemorate her passing, and news outlets around the world covered her death and its impact.

Below, I outline several psychological processes that help to explain how this zoo animal touched so many people, both during her rise to celebrity and following her tragic and sudden death.

Anthropomorphism involves attributing human qualities to nonhuman entities, and people often anthropomorphize animals with whom they feel a close connection (McConnell et al., 2025). When people see a baby hippopotamus being bottle-fed by caregivers holding her in their laps, it evokes associations with parents caring for their own babies. Even her name, Fiona, was inspired by Princess Fiona, a character from the movie Shrek, because her fluted ears reminded zoo patrons of the animated princess (ironically, we're dealing with two layers of anthropomorphism here!).

As people anthropomorphize nonhuman entities, they often attribute humanlike emotional capacities to them, which can increase their moral concern and desire to care for those entities (Waytz et al., 2010). Thus, seeing similarities between Fiona being bottle-fed by zookeepers and human babies likely increased people's concern for Fiona and, consequently, intensified their grief following her death.

Moreover, the frequency with which people encountered Fiona throughout her life (e.g., numerous news updates about her progress, t-shirts and ice cream flavors featuring Fiona) may have increased their positive feelings toward her through the classic mere exposure effect (i.e., we tend to like things that become familiar to us through repeated exposure; Zajonc, 1968).

One of the striking things about Fiona's introduction to the world was that she was an underdog. Fiona was premature, underweight, and needed round-the-clock care from zookeepers just to survive. In other words, Fiona faced extraordinary odds from the moment she was born.

People are often drawn to underdogs and root for their success. Research shows that when individuals or groups are not expected to succeed and appear to face substantial disadvantages, people are more likely to support them in contexts ranging from Olympic competitions to geopolitical conflicts (Vandello et al., 2007). Thus, Fiona's helplessness at birth and the challenges she faced may have encouraged people to take her into their hearts and root for her survival, making her unexpected passing all the more crushing.

Research on parasocial relationships shows that people can form strong emotional attachments to individuals, such as celebrities, with whom they have no direct, reciprocal relationship. Indeed, people often feel meaningful psychological connections with professional athletes, movie stars, and even fictional characters, such as those in the Harry Potter series. Research also shows that such symbolic social connections can contribute to people's sense of belonging and well-being (Gabriel & Young, 2011).

Thus, even though many people grieving Fiona's passing never visited the Cincinnati Zoo, and those who did typically only "interacted with her" from the other side of a large glass window, people experienced a genuine connection with Fiona. Consequently, they can experience meaningful grief following her unexpected death.

Moreover, when communities share emotionally significant moments, they can experience collective effervescence, powerful feelings of social connection and shared significance that can make an experience feel special or even sacred (Gabriel et al., 2020). Thus, the collective mourning of Fiona may have made her loss especially poignant while simultaneously bringing people together through their shared grief.

Fiona died on November 19, 2026, from complications after swallowing a child's knit hat that, through a freak accident, landed in her enclosure. Her death led to a surge of anger from internet commenters calling for "justice" (Contrera et al., 2026). Why couldn't people simply accept Fiona's death as a very sad and tragic accident?

One important contributor to these feelings of anger may be counterfactual thinking, or people's tendency to imagine alternatives to events that take place. When trying to make sense of the world, people often engage in "if only" thinking to imagine how things might have turned out differently (e.g., "Would I have made my flight on time if there hadn't been an accident on the expressway?"). The unusual circumstances of a child's hat ending up in Fiona's enclosure make it especially easy to imagine alternative outcomes in which Fiona never swallowed the hat and would still be swimming in her zoo enclosure today.

Indeed, people often experience particularly strong negative emotions when it is easy to mentally "undo" a heartbreaking outcome. Under such circumstances, people may find it difficult to accept an outcome as simply a "bad break" or a matter of chance (Sherman & McConnell, 1996).

It's not surprising that Fiona's death had such a profound impact on people, both here in Cincinnati and around the world. Yet, animals die in zoos every day with far less (and often no) public attention.

Fiona's story illustrates how several psychological processes can converge to make an animal especially meaningful in people's lives. Anthropomorphism encourages us to extend moral concern to animals (McConnell et al., 2025), underdog narratives give us someone to root for (Vandello et al., 2007), parasocial relationships allow us to feel connected to individuals we do not personally know (Gabriel & Young, 2011), and counterfactual thinking can make an unexpected loss feel especially tragic when it seems so easily avoidable (Sherman & McConnell, 1996).
