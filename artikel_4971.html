Is the AI Bubble Finally Popping? Nvidia Crashes 48% as Wall Street Experts Sound the Alarm

Wall Street's artificial intelligence boom is facing its biggest test yet.

Nvidia plunged 14% on Friday, the Nasdaq Composite lost 5.3%, and the broader S&P 500 entered correction territory as investors questioned whether the trillions of dollars being poured into AI infrastructure will ever produce the expected returns.

The Nasdaq closed at 22,617.73, approximately 18% below its October 6 record closing high of 27,599.79. Its 10% weekly decline was the worst since 2020, while the index has now erased its gains for 2026.

Nvidia, the company that powered much of the AI rally, finished Friday at $125.53, down 47.5% from its October closing record of $239.24.

The chipmaker lost nearly 23% during the week as the fallout from former CEO Jensen Huang's departure and reports of an SEC accounting investigation added to concerns about the AI business model.

Other AI-linked companies have suffered even steeper losses. CoreWeave, which rents computing capacity to AI developers, closed at $39.51, almost 80% below its 2025 peak, while Oracle has lost roughly 70% from its highs and finished the week at $101.19.

AMD shed 20.5% over the week, closing at $424.39, while Alphabet lost 10.3% to $265.29. The iShares Semiconductor ETF, SOXX, dropped 17.4% during the same period, showing that investors are selling across the companies supplying the AI industry, not merely its most speculative businesses.

The selloff accelerated just as Anthropic completed its historic $81 billion IPO. The Claude developer priced its shares at $83 and closed its first trading session 17% higher, but its stock fell another 9% on Friday as enthusiasm for the offering failed to restore confidence in the wider sector.

With so many stocks in the red, it’s logical that many AI fanboys are starting to get nervous.

Traders on Polymarket have become increasingly pessimistic. Contracts assessing whether the AI industry will experience a defined downturn showed approximately 54% odds by December 31, 2026, and 65% by June 30, 2027, according to Friday's market snapshot.

The market has attracted approximately $4.1 million in trading volume, but its definition of a bubble bursting is more demanding than a falling stock market. At least three specific events must occur within a single 90-day period, including Nvidia closing 50% below its all-time high, SOXX falling 40%, major AI suppliers experiencing similar collapses, or severe financial problems at AI developers.

SOXX has already fallen approximately 43% from its split-adjusted record closing price, satisfying one of the specified price thresholds on paper. Nvidia is approaching another, although crossing either threshold alone would not settle the contract.

The damage has also spread into cryptocurrencies, undermining expectations that 2026 would bring a sustained recovery. Bitcoin finished the week near $58,895, down 9.8% over the period and more than 50% below its 2025 peak.

The total cryptocurrency market capitalization fell to approximately $2.01 trillion, down from more than $4 trillion at its peak. As Decrypt reported Friday, the Crypto Fear and Greed Index dropped to 9, signaling extreme fear and its weakest reading since February.

This was supposed to be the year of the usual pre-halving moon. Instead, for the first time in the history of Bitcoin, the coin is on its way to have two consecutive red years.

Bitcoin has increasingly behaved like other speculative assets sensitive to liquidity conditions, particularly when investors expect borrowing costs to remain elevated.

The pressure is also visible in investment flows. U.S. spot Bitcoin ETFs recorded $582.2 million in net withdrawals on Thursday, while forced liquidations added to the selling as Bitcoin broke below the psychologically important $60,000 level.

Owen Lamont, a portfolio manager at Acadian Asset Management and former University of Chicago finance professor, believes the warning signs have become difficult to ignore. After arguing in January that AI stocks were not yet in a bubble, he told Fortune this week that he now assigns a 70% probability to the U.S. stock market already being in one.

Lamont tracks four warning signs: excessive valuations, widespread belief in a speculative boom, new stock issuance, and investors pouring money into the market. His latest assessment suggests all four are now present, although his issuance figures are heavily influenced by SpaceX's massive IPO earlier this year.

Bank of America's November fund manager survey offers another indication of deteriorating confidence. Investors reduced their technology exposure by the most since 2006, and 52% expect at least one major AI hyperscaler to announce a capital spending cut within six months, Fortune reported.

The European Central Bank has also raised concerns about how AI development is being financed. Its November review estimates that capital expenditure by major American technology companies could reach $5.5 trillion cumulatively by 2030, with growing reliance on debt as infrastructure spending outpaces internally generated cash.

The similarities to the dot-com era are difficult to dismiss: investors are assigning enormous valuations to companies based on projected demand, businesses are racing to build infrastructure ahead of proven profitability, and financial markets are increasingly dependent on a small group of technology companies.

During the original dot-com collapse, the Nasdaq lost approximately 78% between March 2000 and October 2002. The present decline is considerably smaller, but history also offers a reminder that a technology can succeed commercially while the companies financing its expansion lose enormous amounts of market value.

There are important differences, however. Many of today's largest AI beneficiaries, including Nvidia, Alphabet, and Microsoft, operate profitable businesses with substantial existing revenues, unlike numerous internet startups whose valuations depended almost entirely on future growth.

Nvidia's latest quarterly results illustrate the contradiction. The company beat earnings estimates, reported record revenue and net income, and issued stronger-than-expected revenue guidance, yet its shares continued collapsing.

Richard Saperstein, chief investment officer at Treasury Partners, described the downturn as an "AI repricing" in comments to Reuters, citing slowing data center construction and tighter funding conditions. Lawrence Gillum, chief fixed income strategist at LPL Financial, offered a less alarming interpretation, saying the decline was "not a bubble bursting" and arguing that the AI investment cycle still has room to run.

Higher interest rates complicate both arguments. Investors are increasingly expecting the Federal Reserve to raise rates again in December, increasing financing costs for data centers while reducing the appeal of stocks whose valuations depend on profits years into the future.

The balance of evidence points to more than a routine bout of nerves, but less than a confirmed systemic bust. Stocks have repriced faster than the underlying technology has changed.

What happens next depends on whether actual demand for AI can justify the data-center spending spree—and whether the companies financing it can withstand tighter credit. The real test isn't simply whether Nvidia can keep selling chips. It's whether its customers can turn all that computing power into profits.

Right now much of the AI hype is just… hype. Markets are moving more by the perceived potential of AI in the future and not so much by what AI is actually doing right now.

Crypto investors have seen this before with ICOs, the TPS wars, the metaverse, the NFT fever and more. For many of them, the distinction may offer little comfort.

Bitcoin's slump shows how quickly markets can stop distinguishing between promising technologies and overpriced bets when confidence disappears. The dot-com crash didn't kill the internet; it killed the assumption that every internet company would make its investors rich.

AI may be the next world-changing technology to prove that being right about the future is not the same as making money from it.
