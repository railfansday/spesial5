A will (technically a "last will and testament") is a legal document that specifies how your assets should be distributed after your death. It must go through probate — a court-supervised process that validates the will, pays debts and taxes, and distributes assets to beneficiaries. A living trust (specifically a revocable living trust) is a legal entity you create during your lifetime that holds your assets. When you die, the successor trustee distributes those assets according to the trust terms — without court involvement.

You need both: A trust does not name guardians for minor children (only a will can do that), and a trust only controls assets that have been formally transferred into it. A pour-over will acts as a safety net — it directs any assets not in the trust at your death to pour into the trust, where they are distributed according to the trust terms. The pour-over will still goes through probate, but it ensures nothing falls through the cracks.

The term "living trust" almost always refers to a revocable living trust — one you can change, amend, or dissolve at any time during your lifetime. You remain the trustee, manage the assets as you always have, and pay taxes on trust income using your personal Social Security number. For income tax purposes, a revocable trust is invisible — the IRS treats it as if it does not exist.

The federal estate tax exemption is $13.61 million per person ($27.22 million for married couples) in 2026, but this historically high exemption is scheduled to sunset after 2025, potentially dropping to approximately $7 million per person. FIRE households who have built $3 million to $10 million in net worth through decades of saving and investment growth should monitor this sunset closely. If the exemption drops, irrevocable trusts become relevant at much lower wealth levels.

For most FIRE households with $1 million to $5 million in net worth, a revocable living trust provides the right balance of control, flexibility, and probate avoidance. Irrevocable structures become compelling only when estate tax exposure is real or asset protection needs are elevated.

Estate planning is governed primarily by state law, and the value of a living trust versus a will varies significantly depending on where you live.

Nine states are community property states: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin. In these states, most assets acquired during marriage are owned 50/50 by both spouses regardless of whose name is on the title. At death, only the deceased spouse's half passes through their estate plan.

The remaining 41 states follow common law (also called "equitable distribution"). Ownership depends on whose name is on the title. This distinction affects how assets pass at death and whether a trust or will provides better spousal protection.

The most common estate planning mistake with trusts is failing to fund them. Creating a trust document costs $1,500 to $5,000, but if you never transfer your bank accounts, brokerage accounts, and real estate into the trust, it provides zero probate avoidance. After signing your trust, immediately re-title assets into the trust name. Your attorney should provide a funding checklist. An unfunded trust sitting in a drawer is an expensive piece of paper — not an estate plan.
