Lucy Sante Talks About What Collage Enables That Writing Can’t

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Executive Editor, ARTnews & Art in America

Lucy Sante started making collages long before she established herself as a writer of incisive theories of photography (see Walker Evans, from 1999, Evidence, from 1992, and 2009’s Folk Photography: The American Real-Photo Postcard 1905–1930); of urban studies (see Low Life: Lures and Snares of Old New York, her 1991 chronicle of the city’s underworlds); and of personal history (2024’s I Heard Her Call My Name: A Memoir of Transition). As a teenage émigré in the 1960s, she would clip and assemble images from magazines, a practice she has continued on and off ever since—drawing lasting inspiration from the metaphysical juxtapositions of René Magritte, a fellow Belgian. As a clerk at the Strand, a storied used bookshop in New York, she began working with a wider range of materials, from cast-off paperbacks and technical manuals to medical textbooks and other arcane publications. All the while, she developed a mysterious and poetic style of collage that mixed imagery from different eras and suggested the outlines of stories, however elusive those might be. Over the decades, Sante devoted herself most fervently to writing, with a focus on photography: In 2023, she retired as a professor of writing and the history of photography at Bard College. Her newest book, My Heart & I Agree (2026), is an essay collection that covers subjects including Surrealist photography, cowboy iconography, and Soul Train. There, her keen eye toward camera culture remains evident. She refocused some of her energies on collage in recent years, and her visual work featured this past spring in solo exhibitions at Picture Theory in Chelsea and at the American Academy of Arts and Letters in upper Manhattan, where an early collage from the ’70s joined works made since 2020. From her home in upstate New York, Sante spoke with A.i.A. about learning to look through magazines, moving her way into fine-art collage, and finding poetry in images she gives new life.

What do you remember about your early collages that made you want to keep making them?

I had two sources of materials for my earliest collages: a stack of Arizona Highways magazines from the 1950s and early ’60s, and my parents’ subscription to Life. I remember a collage that I made featuring Jimi Hendrix and a mushroom cloud: That was hip. I was imitating collages I saw—not artistic collages, but collages in popular media. In the early Rolling Stone, on the letters page, almost every week they’d have a collage by this graphic designer named Wilfried Sätty, whose house I stayed in years later, after his death in 1982. He had a particular style: outer space plus old engravings.

I’m colorblind, and that was a big problem for me. I drew, but had to work in black and white. I wasn’t that bad—just not as good as I wanted to be. In high school, I started making band flyers, which I continued when I was living on the Lower East Side in the 1970s and ’80s. I made several for the Del-Byzanteens, a band I also wrote lyrics for. Many people in the band became celebrated in one way or another: Jim Jarmusch as a filmmaker, Jamie Nares as a painter, and Phil Kline as a composer.

How did your work at the Strand Bookstore find its way into your collages?

We started a collective publication called Stranded that was originally going to be like a workers’ gripe sheet. But instead, we published art and poetry and stuff like that. I had collage-like things in all four issues. Within a week of my starting, Fred Bass [fabled owner of the Strand] named me the paperback department—I was the entire department all by myself. Bookmen in that period did not take paperbacks seriously and just put them out in the 25-cent carts. That meant I had to deal with romance books, and when I put those out on Thursdays, they were mobbed by old ladies, and it was a nightmare. But it also meant that whenever they bought a library, they would throw me everything that wasn’t a hardcover book, including magazines, maps, postcards, and all kinds of stuff that I took home and still have. I made collages recently with material I acquired back then at the Strand.

What was the Strand like as a downtown institution then compared to now?

Nobody was very literary on the Lower East Side in the ’70s. People were starting bands and making movies. There wasn’t even painting at the time. We had to wait for Basquiat. In the ’70s, we had some bad poets, a few bad essayists. Nobody [in the neighborhood] wrote fiction. But at that point, the sociology was nothing to compare to now, because you could waltz into New York City hitchhiking from Denver or somewhere and find yourself a cheap apartment and get a job at the Strand. Your salary would pay your rent, and you had money left over to go clubbing. That’s long gone now.

What were you looking for? What made something conducive to collage?

I liked texture and was really interested in contrasting eras. I liked faded color. I was taking inspiration from a lot of different sources. Of the flyers I made for the Del-Byzanteens, one of them [drew from] big posters that a syndicate put out to try to promote a science-fiction comic strip. I X-Acto–knifed out the thought balloons and filled them in with other material. That was because I had a friend who’d just come over from Paris with a Situationist International book, and I got inspired by that.

You mentioned beginning with an interest in popular-media collage. What art world collagists interested you later on?

I was inspired by Max Ernst and Robert Rauschenberg. I found a book by Jess, Robert Duncan’s partner and a great collage artist. I discovered Bruce Conner and, of course, the Surrealists and Dadaists—Berlin Dada, especially, with John Heartfield and Hannah Höch. And the Russians, like El Lissitzky and Alexander Rodchenko. There’s a whole world of collage out there.

How would you describe your decision-making process?

There are collages that I don’t quite know how to resolve. They might need something else, but I don’t know what. Often they’re just made on the spot.

One work that especially caught my eye in your Arts and Letters show was De La (2023). How did you arrive upon the composition for that?

That’s my favorite work of mine. That’s one I’m keeping. It just came together in a particular way, and it zinged. And I can take you through exactly how I made it, because I do remember. The materials go back to 1981, which was the tail end of people selling shit on the street on the Lower East Side. There had been this kind of ad hoc 24/7 flea market around Astor Place. One night, I was walking down St. Marks Place, and there was a guy selling damaged posters. I bought a shopping bag’s worth for 10 bucks; they were all falling to pieces. The yellow and white in De La is from a poster for an early movie by the Italian director Elio Petri, and the lettering comes from miscellaneous papers from a local auction up here [in upstate New York]. It had all kinds of crap, including a poster for a touring Laurel and Hardy anthology film in Spanish. The tire at the top comes from a French car magazine from the ’30s, and the chrysanthemum comes from a seed-company ad in Life. The glove is from a 1950s Punch, a humor magazine that had wonderful advertising. And then the star of the show is the woman with the fingerprints on her back, which I’d been saving up for the longest time. It comes from a French soft-porn magazine from the ’30s. It was magic how they all came together.

What do letters and different bits of text do for you in collage work?

There’s lettering in, I’d say, 95 percent of my collages, and that’s partly because I kind of think of them as movie posters. One of my original inspirations was the posters I saw when I first started going to the movies as a child, for films like Ben Hur (1959) and Hawaii (1966). They had enormous, sculpted letters for the title, and then there would be little figures swirling around them illustrating the scope of the story. In a way, everything I do refers back to those.

Are there writerly aspects to the collage-making process for you? They can’t help but suggest some sort of narrative for the viewer, but I don’t know about the maker, necessarily…

It’s ambiguity central. I started life, so to speak, as a poet. I later realized I was a prose writer, but poetry is at the heart of everything for me. Among other things, that means making a structure from a sort of lean-to of ambiguities—one ambiguity resting on another, all suggesting a story and all suggesting a meaning but never actually giving it to you. If I make a collage and it seems to have a legible message, I have failed.

What makes you turn to collage instead of writing when a creative impulse strikes?

It’s something I feel in my hands—like, “By the pricking of my thumbs, something wicked this way comes.” It’s the feeling that I need to make something. Being a writer does not involve the body the way that making visual art does, and I have that craving. I wanted to be an artist before I ever thought of being a writer.

Because I have so much to do right now, I’m not running down to the basement to make more collages. But in the meantime, I’ve bought things on eBay. I bought a bunch of 19th-century chromolithographs I’m eager to use, and somebody sold me these incredibly dirty comics—I don’t mean dirty like sexy, but dirty like they were lying in a crawl space for 75 years. I like dirt. I like fading. I like things that are crinkled, things that are ripped. I prefer things that are damaged, because they carry their histories. They establish a custodial chain and have a lot of dead people’s fingerprints on them. If something looks like it comes out of the box, even if it was published in 1922, I’m far less interested than if it’s fucked up.
