Can We Build Human-Like AI Before We Understand the Human Condition?

The technology industry is racing to build artificial intelligence that can reason, communicate and increasingly behave in ways that seem human.

But there is an obvious paradox in that ambition: science still does not fully understand the extraordinary mind we are trying to reproduce.

In 2016, __an editorial in the academic journal *History of the Human Sciences *__presented the problem in stark terms: despite incredible advances in fields such as molecular biology, evolutionary psychology, artificial intelligence and cognitive neuroscience, one central category continued to resist definition: “the human.”

The importance of that problem had been expressed more directly decades earlier by psychologist B. F. Skinner, who wrote in his 1974 book About Behaviorism that that “the major problems facing the world today can be solved only if we improve our understanding of human behavior.”

That question has become even more relevant as AI systems have grown more capable. What exactly are the characteristics that distinguish human intelligence? How did consciousness emerge? And how did our remarkable cognitive abilities develop alongside insecurity, aggression, cooperation, creativity and psychological conflict?

These questions form part of the much older scientific and intellectual problem of understanding the human condition.

Nearly four centuries ago, philosopher and scientist René Descartes, while probing the nature of the thinking mind, described the intellectual predicament in striking terms:

“So serious are the doubts into which I have been thrown…that I can neither put them out of my mind nor see any way of resolving them. It feels as if I have fallen unexpectedly into a deep whirlpool…”.

Almost four centuries later, consciousness remains one of the most difficult pieces of the puzzle.

Philosopher David Chalmers, co-director of New York University’s Center for Mind, Brain and Consciousness, famously distinguished the relatively tractable problem of explaining cognitive functions with what he termed the “hard problem” of consciousness: explaining why information processing is accompanied by subjective experience.

But consciousness is not only a question of subjective experience. It also gave humans an extraordinary capacity to understand the relationship between events, anticipate outcomes and increasingly manage behaviour on the basis of that understanding.

There is also an evolutionary puzzle. If the ability to understand cause and effect and consciously adjust behaviour provides such an advantage, why did anything approaching full human consciousness emerge so rarely in evolutionary history?

These questions are approached from different directions by philosophy, neuroscience and evolutionary biology. Together they illustrate how much remains unresolved about the mind that AI increasingly seeks to emulate.

They also raise another question about the human condition: what psychological consequences followed when humans acquired this extraordinary capacity for conscious self-management?

Australian biologist Jeremy Griffith approaches the problem from an evolutionary direction. His explanation of the human condition asks what happened when a conscious, understanding-dependent intellect emerged within an organism already equipped with genetically acquired instincts.

Griffith places the emergence of consciousness, and this resulting conflict with instincts, at roughly two million years ago, linking it to the major development of the brain’s association cortex during human evolution.

Natural selection can provide a species with inherited instinctive orientations, while a conscious mind develops understanding by experimenting with cause and effect. Griffith proposes that as our distant hominin ancestors became sufficiently conscious to manage life through understanding, their experiments inevitably began departing from established instinctive orientations. The instincts inevitably resisted those departures, and that orientating ‘pressure’ amounted, in effect, to criticism of an emerging intellect that could not yet explain why its experiments were necessary.

Griffith argues that the insecurity that resulted from this seemingly unwarranted criticism produced defensive reactions of anger, egocentricity and alienation. His hypothesis therefore interprets these troubled characteristics primarily as psychological responses to the emergence of consciousness rather than simply manifestations of selfish or aggressive instinct-based instincts.

Stephen Hawking once observed that while physics and mathematics may tell us how the universe began, they are “not much use in predicting human behavior because there are far too many equations to solve.” It is precisely this seemingly impenetrable complexity that Griffith’s hypothesis accounts for, presenting a framework in which the full spectrum of human behaviour, including its many apparently contradictory aspects, can be made sense of. Its proponents argue that the more fully Griffith’s explanation is understood, the more persuasive it becomes, a view reflected in the growing number of favourable assessments it has received from scientists and academics who have examined his work.

This also gives the hypothesis a further implication. If the insecurity arose because the conscious intellect could not explain why it had to depart from instinct, Griffith argues that gaining that understanding would resolve the original conflict. On his account, anger, egocentricity and alienation are therefore not immutable features of human nature, but defensive responses that can become redundant once their underlying cause is understood.

To advance research, discussion and public understanding of the human condition Griffith established the international charity Fix The World.

Neuroscientist Stanislas Dehaene, who’s based at the Collège de France, has approached consciousness from a very different direction. His global neuronal workspace model proposes that conscious access occurs when information becomes globally available across a distributed network of brain regions, allowing it to be shared with otherwise specialised cognitive systems.

Chalmers, Dehaene and Griffith are therefore asking different questions and proposing fundamentally different explanations. Chalmers focuses on why subjective experience exists, Griffith on the evolutionary and psychological consequences of consciousness emerging in humans, and Dehaene on the neural mechanisms associated with conscious access.

Together they illustrate a larger point: understanding consciousness remains a substantial scientific and philosophical frontier.

For AI developers, the immediate challenge is not necessarily to solve the human condition. Today’s systems can reproduce remarkable aspects of human language and reasoning without requiring science to possess a complete theory of human nature.

But the closer the ambition moves toward genuinely human-like intelligence, the more difficult the unanswered questions become to ignore.

What distinguishes intelligence from consciousness? How did human consciousness arise? Why are emotion, insecurity, morality, cooperation and conflict so deeply entangled with human cognition? And which of these characteristics, if any, would accompany a genuinely conscious artificial intelligence?

What there is instead is a continuing endeavour across neuroscience, evolutionary biology, psychology, philosophy and now artificial intelligence to understand the mind that makes us human.
