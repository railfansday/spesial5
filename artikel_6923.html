5 Fall Trends Designers Think Will Still Look Good Next Spring

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Above: A hallway view of Eric Egan’s Milan apartment.

If any season has the power to inspire a complete aesthetic overhaul, it’s fall. Nature’s changing palette inspires us to follow suit, as each year our mood boards make new space for jewel tones, cozy textures, and a bottomless candle budget. Autumnal trends are so alluring, it almost feels like a risky time to make long-term design decisions. But with the right consideration, that seasonal inspiration can translate into choices that look good year-round.

This fall in particular, designers are urging clients to trust their guts—making sure they see themselves in the spaces they create, rather than blindly following the latest fads. “For me, a trend has to be authentic or it’s just noise,” says designer Chad Dorsey, who’s eager to ditch the spare minimalism of years past for looks with more personality. ELLE Decor A-Lister Kerry Joyce seconds the motion: “By prioritizing personalized storytelling, a space becomes evergreen, evolving as smaller objects are swapped out over time.”

But an expert nudge in the right direction never hurts. This fall, top designers are gravitating toward creative color-drenching, cleverly concealed storage, tactile upholstery, and rooms built around art. Read on for their takes on the fall 2026 trends worth investing in, plus tips on building (as Dorsey puts it) “spaces designed to be lived in, not looked at.”

Fabric and upholstery are trending ultra plush and textured for fall, but when thoughtfully integrated, they feel more timeless than seasonal. “Design is shifting toward how a space feels, not just how it looks,” says Joyce, who’s seeing an increased popularity in fabrics like mohair, velvet, cashmere, and alpaca hair. “This focus on texture does more than elevate a room's aesthetic,” he explains. “It creates a sensory experience that comforts the soul.”

Start your bedroom redesign this fall and your space will strike the perfect balance between fresh and lived-in by spring. Between client requests and a personal home build, Amber Lewis has bedrooms on the brain right now. One trend takes the lead in her eyes: “I love matching the walls to the bed upholstery and bedding so the whole room feels wrapped in one warm color family. It’s calm and peaceful, and that enveloping feeling works in any season.”

Luckily, this cozy trend doesn’t require a complete room overhaul: “The easiest place to start is the bed itself. Reupholster your headboard in a pattern you love, or drape a vintage suzani across the foot of the bed,” Lewis says. “One move like that changes the whole mood of the room without starting over.” To bring in a little dimension and pop, she recommends adding a singular statement, like a lone striped pillow.

If your primary suite needs a more holistic update, Lewis recommends creating a small sitting area. Sourcing vintage chairs and small sofas for primary suites this fall has given Lewis a new perspective on where bedroom design is headed: “True sanctuaries that feel layered, personal, and built for actually living in.”

Color drenching isn’t going anywhere for fall 2026—or long after, according to Dorsey. When choosing a shade for a room, he finds that any color works harder when it’s layered and intentional. Color capping—a term coined by Benjamin Moore referring to painting a room in multiple tones from one color family—can help keep a space from feeling flat or overwhelmed beneath a statement hue.

According to Dorsey, the staying power of color drenching goes hand in hand with the shift away from spareness and toward “collected” spaces: “Neither is really a ‘look;’ they’re about living with things you love, which is why they’ll carry well into spring.”

Designer Regan Baker says that cleverly hidden space solutions are also on the rise. “It could be a TV that slides behind a commissioned art panel, a Murphy bed tucked into a wall of millwork or that serves as a desk when it's stored, or a peg wall that’s a place to hang a jacket one minute and a standing desk the next,” she says. These solutions are tailored to her clients’ spaces (rather than tied to a season or palette), which gives them staying power.

This concept is an easy way to turn the corner you’ve never known how to style into your favorite part of the room. “In an existing home, look at the awkward spots, like an alcove or the wall around a fireplace, and treat built-ins there as a design moment instead of an afterthought,” Baker advises.

If there’s one built-to-last fall trend most designers can agree on, it’s added eccentricity. “Homes are steering away from the sterile, overnight look of a showroom delivery,” Joyce says. “The defining philosophy now focuses on layering modern staples with antiques, secondhand finds, travel mementos, and new pieces that carry a sense of patina.”

Dorsey describes this style as “collected rather than prescribed,” where a high-low mix of vintage and custom elements gel together. And while it may feel loftier than a trending, sold-out piece, Baker says that most clients start with an artistic anchor, then build around it: “We’re designing more homes around a piece of art than around a color trend. That might be wallpaper by local designers, a commissioned piece, or even a palette pulled from a lichen-covered rock found on the property.”

One key takeaway? Curate like a collector. Your reward may be a space that never goes out of style.
