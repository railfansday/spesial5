Second Amendment Roundup, I: The Common Use Test and the Federal "Assault Weapon" Ban

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

The federal law did not prohibit the basic AR-15 rifle design.

Connecticut argues before the Supreme Court, in its Grant v. Higgins brief, that before 2004, "few civilians owned AR-15s." That was supposedly "because federal law prohibited 'manufacture, transfer, or possess[ion]' of the weapons between 1994 and 2004" under Pub. L. No. 103-322, 108 Stat. 1796, 1996-97 (1994). That was the federal "assault weapon ban" that expired in 2004.  The brief continues, "Under the common-use-only approach, if Congress had extended that legislation in 2004, AR-15s would never have entered into common use—and thus could be banned today."

That argument exhibits a profoundly mistaken view about the what the federal law actually did.

First, while the law made it "unlawful for a person to manufacture, transfer, or possess a semiautomatic assault weapon," that provision did "not apply to the possession or transfer of any semiautomatic assault weapon otherwise lawfully possessed under Federal law on the date of the enactment of this subsection."  18 U.S.C. § 922(v).  So it did not ban possession or transfer of a single firearm that was already lawfully possessed. Moreover, the federal law did not ban future manufacture of the subject firearms as long as certain aspects were eliminated.  The firearms were defined, first, in a list of names, including "Colt AR-15" and "copies or duplicates" thereof.  Second, they included certain features, including:

a semiautomatic rifle that has an ability to accept a detachable magazine and has at least 2 of –

(ii) a pistol grip that protrudes conspicuously beneath the action of the weapon;

(iv) a flash suppressor or threaded barrel designed to accommodate a flash suppressor; and

(v) a grenade launcher….  18 U.S.C. § 921(a)(30).

Under the above, Colt could not engrave simply "AR-15" on its rifles, but could use other names.  A manufacturer could not make a "copy or duplicate" of an AR-15, but as discussed below, a rifle would be a "copy or duplicate" of an AR-15, inter alia, only if it had at least two of the five listed features.

But guess what: the sine qua non of an AR-15 is the conspicuously protruding pistol grip.  None of the other features listed above are inherent in the AR-15 design.  Many AR-15s never had a folding or telescoping stock.  How many people cared whether it had a bayonet mount?  A flash suppressor (or threading "designed" to accommodate one) could not be mounted, but a muzzle brake or compensator could.  Congress must have included grenade launcher to sound dramatic, as that was already restricted under the National Firearms Act.

And so the federal law did exactly nothing to "ban" the basic AR-15 design.  Manufacturers kept making and consumers kept buying semiautomatic rifles with protruding pistol grips sans bayonet mounts.

Manufacturers submitted samples of rifles to the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF) to verify compliance.  I have reproduced some samples of ATF approval letters in my Firearms Law Deskbook § 10:6 (available in book form or on Westlaw).  As an example, ATF examined a Bushmaster XM-15E2, which was "based on the AR15 design and incorporates a pistol grip that extends conspicuously beneath the action of the weapon." However, it had none of the other listed features. It did have a muzzle brake, but it was not a flash suppressor. The rifle was found not to be an assault weapon "because it does not have at least two of the features specified in the statute. Additionally, the firearm is not a copy or duplicate of the Colt AR15 rifle." Edward M. Owen, Jr., Chief, ATF Firearms Technology Branch, Feb. 16, 1996.

Instead of a two-feature test like the expired federal law, Connecticut bans rifles based on a single feature, including: "Any grip of the weapon, including a pistol grip, a thumbhole stock, or any other stock, the use of which would allow an individual to grip the weapon, resulting in any finger on the trigger hand in addition to the trigger finger being directly below any portion of the action of the weapon when firing…." This is a more capacious ban that affects a much broader collection of firearms than the expired federal law.

Connecticut is wrong to argue that had Congress extended its law in 2004, "AR-15s would never have entered into common use—and thus could be banned today." AR-15s were already in common use in 1994.  The fact is that the federal law did nothing to dampen the ever-increasing demand for and use by the American people of the AR-15 basic design.  And it goes without saying that semiautomatic rifles with detachable magazines had already been around for a century when the federal law was in place.
