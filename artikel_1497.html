Cancer cells show highly heterogeneous and plastic states in glioblastoma, an incurable brain tumour1,2,3,4,5. However, how these malignant states arise and whether they follow defined cellular trajectories across tumours is poorly understood. Here we generated a deep single-cell and spatial multi-omic atlas of human glioblastoma that pairs transcriptomic, epigenomic and genomic profiling of 12 tumours across many regions. We identify that glioblastoma heterogeneity is driven by spatially patterned transitions of cancer cells from developmental-like states towards those defined by a glial injury response and hypoxia. This cellular trajectory regionalizes tumours into distinct tissue microenvironments and manifests in a molecularly conserved manner across tumours as well as genetically distinct tumour subclones. Consistently, using a new deep-learning framework to map cancer cell states jointly with clones in situ, we show that tumour subclones are finely spatially intermixed through glioblastoma tissue microenvironments. We validate this cellular trajectory by using somatic mutation-based hierarchies in vivo and an experimental patient-derived organoid model ex vivo. Finally, we show that this cancer cell trajectory is intimately linked to myeloid heterogeneity and unfolds across regionalized myeloid signalling environments. Our findings define a stereotyped trajectory of cancer cells in glioblastoma and unify glioblastoma tumour heterogeneity into a tractable cellular and tissue framework.

The extensive tumour heterogeneity of glioblastoma (GB), an incurable adult brain cancer, presents a major obstacle to treatment. Nearly two decades of molecular profiling has found that malignant cells exist across a spectrum of cellular states within each tumour, resembling developmental neural progenitors as well as mesenchymal-like and injury response states1,2,3,4,5. These states are suggested to vary in abundance across tumours and stratify GBs into subtypes1,2. Furthermore, patient-derived xenografts have shown that malignant cells can transition across cellular states, indicating their plasticity2,6. Despite these extensive efforts, we still do not understand how cancer cells diversify into distinct states and give rise to GB heterogeneity. In particular, whether malignant cells follow stereotyped or variable cellular trajectories across GB tumours is unknown. A cancer cell trajectory that is conserved and tractable across GBs would present a highly attractive therapeutic target.

Single-cell, bulk and spatial genomics have revealed stereotypical aspects of GBs. Several malignant cell states can be detected within each tumour2,5,7 and their single-cell transcriptional profiles can be mapped onto an axis between developmental-like (dev-like) and injury response-associated gene expression programs4,8. Genetic heterogeneity1,2 and tumour microenvironment (TME) signals9 also influence malignant cell state identity. Furthermore, these states show a recurrent spatial organization across tumours5,10. Yet, these observations have not been linked together to resolve malignant cellular trajectories in GB, in part due to the lack of multimodal profiling efforts that can integrate insights from various cell and tissue atlassing technologies. Here we address this challenge by means of deep single-cell and spatial multi-omic profiling of human GBs and identify that a stereotyped spatiotemporal trajectory of cancer cells gives rise to GB heterogeneity.

To comprehensively profile GB heterogeneity, we generated a single-cell and spatial multi-omic atlas of 12 isocitrate dehydrogenase wild-type (IDH-wt) primary GB tumours (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1), combining three main approaches. First, we profiled each tumour across many sites, ranging from 4 sites (roughly 1.25 cm3 each) for most cases to 15 sites effectively covering one whole tumour (Extended Data Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1), spanning 57 tumour sites in total. Second, we generated paired single-nuclei and spatial omic data from each sampling site, profiling consecutive tissue sections with single-nuclei joint transcriptome sequencing and chromatin accessibility sequencing (‘multiome’ snRNA + ATAC-seq) and Visium spatial transcriptomics (ST) (Fig. 1a). Select sites were assayed with orthogonal Xenium ST and immunohistochemistry, as well as whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of tumour microbiopsies (Supplementary Fig. 1). Third, we profiled large numbers of nuclei (more than 1 million) and spatial tissue sections (more than 0.3 million spatial transcriptomes across 97 Visium sections) (Supplementary Table 2). The resulting ‘GBM-space’ dataset, accessible on a user-friendly interactive webportal11 (www.gbmspace.org/), enables comprehensive multimodal characterization of GB and presents a unique holistic tumour atlas for cancer research.

To examine GB heterogeneity, we first annotated malignant and TME cell states in our multiome data. After standard data processing workflows, we filtered nuclei on the basis of quality of both RNA and ATAC profiles, resulting in 1,025,329 nuclei with more than 85,000 nuclei per tumour on average (Methods, Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 1b,c). We then annotated transcriptomic cell states in snRNA-seq data and examined ATAC data in a companion study to map GB gene regulatory networks12.

We integrated snRNA-seq data using scVI13 to account for batch variations, then distinguished malignant clusters on the basis of inferred copy number (CN) gain of chromosome (chr.) 7 and loss of chr. 10, as well as enrichment of known GB expression programs2 (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). Subclustering yielded 28 malignant clusters, including 10 highly common clusters present across 50% of the tumours, as well as 71 TME clusters (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 4).

We annotated malignant clusters by relating them to GB cell state gene programs described in ref. 2 and developing human brain single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data14 as well as gene-set enrichment analysis (Methods and Supplementary Fig. 5a,b). As a result, we hierarchically grouped malignant clusters into four main classes and nine subclasses of GB cell states that were each observed across several tumours (Fig. 1c,d, Supplementary Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 4).

Two main classes of GB cell states resembled distinct developmental progenitors: neural progenitor-like and oligodendrocyte progenitor-like (NPC-OPC-like) and neuronal progenitor-like and neuronal-like (NPC-neuronal-like) cells. These two dev-like states matched those previously described in ref. 2 and expressed respective developmental markers of oligodendrocyte (PDGFRA, OLIG1) and neuronal lineages (MYT1L, STMN2) (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Note 1).

The third main GB cell state showed a transcriptomic spectrum from astrocyte progenitor (AC-prog)-like to gliosis-like (that is, glial injury response) and hypoxic states, broadly expressing core astrocyte markers (SLC1A3, GFAP) (Fig. 1c). Whereas AC-prog-like cells showed Neftel ‘AC-like’ signatures and resembled developmental glioblasts, AC-gliosis and gliosis-like cells expressed genes related to wound healing and injury response (VIM, SERPINE1) and angiogenesis (VEGFA) and the remaining cells were defined by hypoxia (HILPDA) (Fig. 1c). The gliosis-hypoxic cells were initially described as mesenchymal-like states1,2 defined by Neftel ‘MES1-2’ programs. Recently, they were reinterpreted as more similar to reactive astrocytes responding to injury4 and glial wound healing states4,8. Here we adopted the latter nomenclature that better reflected malignant cellular trajectories in our atlas (sections below). The final main malignant state represented proliferative cells. Although dominated by G2M and S phase gene expression, we resolved that these cells represent actively cycling analogues of main GB cell states (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 6a and Supplementary Note 1).

GB cell states showed substantial inter-tumour and intra-tumour heterogeneity. Most tumours showed a dominant GB cell state among the main classes (for example, NPC-OPC-like for AT5 and 9) (Fig. 1d, left). However, despite their stratification, each tumour contained substantial fractions of both dev-like and AC-gliosis-hypoxia cells. Two tumours (AT4 and 10) showed near equal proportions of both classes, whereas in others, the minority states represented 5–21% of all malignant cells (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table 4). Individual sampling sites also contained a mixture of GB states broadly reflecting the parent tumour (Supplementary Fig. 8b,c). Yet, we could often identify at least one site per tumour with a unique cell state composition. In a striking example, tumour AT10 showed distinct dominant GB cell states at each site (Fig. 1d, right). Consistent with other studies5,7,15, these observations show that single biopsies can be confounded to stratify GB tumour phenotypes.

We also captured all main cell lineages in the GB TME (Supplementary Figs. 4b and 7). Although OPCs, mature oligodendrocytes and myeloid cells were the most abundant, we also annotated fine-grained neurons, astrocytes, lymphocytes and vasculature (Supplementary Note 1). Taken together, our single-nuclei transcriptomic atlas presents a deep, fine-grained cellular census of malignant and TME states in GB.

Next, we examined the spatial organization of GB cell states. To resolve fine GB states in Visium ST data, we mapped malignant and TME clusters from each patient’s snRNA-seq profile to their matched Visium data using cell2location16 (Fig. 2a). Whereas integrating paired snRNA-seq and ST accounted for inter-tumour heterogeneity, it also enabled us to link GB spatial organization to malignant cell trajectories, subclonal organization and TME signalling that we subsequently inferred from snRNA-seq.

GB cell states showed striking segregation across all tumours. In particular, gliosis and hypoxia cell states segregated from dev-like states (Extended Data Fig. 2), consistent with previous surveys10,15. GB states also showed regional heterogeneity across tumour sampling sites (Extended Data Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 8c). We also observed concordant spatial mapping results between cell2location and alternative computational methods, especially for highly abundant GB cell states (Methods and Supplementary Fig. 10a–c).

We then asked whether GBs have a conserved spatial architecture. To determine whether malignant and TME cells assemble into stereotyped tissue microenvironments (TMs), we first identified spatially colocalized cell states (that is, microenvironments) per tumour by means of non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) of cell2location results16 (Fig. 2a and Methods). This resulted in 192 factors across our cohort (Supplementary Table 7 and Supplementary Fig. 11a), which we then clustered by their cell state similarity to identify 16 TMs that recurrently appear across tumours (Fig. 2b).

We annotated the recurrent GB TMs on the basis of their cellular composition and anatomical location (Fig. 2b,c and Supplementary Note 2). ‘Tumour’ TMs represented malignant, immune and vasculature states in the tumour core, whereas ‘infiltrated’ TMs spanned non-malignant neurons and glia in the surrounding infiltrated regions (Fig. 2b,c). Most tumour TMs were defined by their distinct GB cell state composition and appeared across many tumours (Extended Data Fig. 3). Core malignant TMs such as Dev-like (OPC), Dev-like (AC), Proliferative, Gliosis transition, Gliosis and Hypoxic 1 were detected in more than 50% of tumours, whereas others such as Dev-like (Neuron) and Hypoxic 2 were rare. Crucially, 8 out of 12 tumours (for example, AT3, AT10) showed nearly the full complement of core TMs (Extended Data Fig. 3a), suggesting these features are fundamental to GB organization. Finally, we recapitulated these TMs with cell2location alternatives on our GB atlas and on public GB Visium ST datasets10,15 (Methods and Supplementary Fig. 12).

Previous ST studies have mapped hypoxic ‘mesenchymal’-like states at the necrotic core and dev-like states at the infiltrating edge of tumours10,15. Correlating GB TMs with tumour anatomy showed Dev-like TMs occurred in the cellular tumour compartment bordering the infiltrating tumour and leading edge. By contrast, Gliosis and Hypoxia TMs were localized further from the infiltrating regions, forming layered zones around perinecrotic and necrotic areas (Fig. 2c, Extended Data Fig. 3a and Supplementary Note 2).

To quantify their precise spatial arrangement, we calculated the pairwise spatial proximities of GB TMs across all tumours (n = 97 Visium sections) into a network graph (Methods). This showed that GB TMs are ordered along a main anatomical axis between the infiltrating tumour edge and the necrotic tumour core (Fig. 2d). Dev-like (OPC) was found closest to infiltrated white matter. Dev-like (AC) spatially overlapped with the Dev-like (OPC), yet extended deeper into the tumour towards successive layers of Gliosis transition, Gliosis and Hypoxia (Fig. 2d). Dev-like (Neuron) was similarly positioned near the infiltrated regions when present (Fig. 2c). In tumour AT15 that we profiled in whole, these gradual transitions from dev-like TMs to gliosis and hypoxia were observed across each sampling site (Supplementary Fig. 13a,b).

We validated these observations with orthogonal 315-plex Xenium ST data (n = 17 sections from 8 tumours) annotated at the single-cell level by means of snRNA-seq integration (Methods). This validated malignant cell state zonation, in which dev-like states bordered infiltrated regions marked by neurons, contrasting with the gliosis and hypoxia states situated further around necrosis (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 13c). Profiling centimetre-scale tumour sections revealed this pattern at each sampling site (Fig. 2e), indicating stereotyped spatial organization throughout each tumour. Xenium also enabled us to validate the accuracy of cell2location predictions on GB data (Methods and Supplementary Fig. 14).

GB TMs also reflected TME organization. ‘Vasculature’ captured pericytes, VLMCs and endothelial cells, anatomically correlated with microvascular proliferation and hyperplastic blood vessels, and was found in close proximity to Dev-like (AC) and (OPC), Gliosis transition and Proliferative malignant TMs (Fig. 2b,d and Extended Data Fig. 3a). Two ‘Immune’ TMs captured distinct myeloid cell compartments (last section). Among infiltrated regions, ‘White matter’ was found in close proximity to tumour leading edge (Fig. 2c,d), consistent with being a main GB invasion route17,18,19.

Taken together, we reveal stereotyped stepwise spatial transitions of malignant cells from dev-like to gliosis and hypoxia states in GB.

We proposed that the stereotyped spatial transitions of GB states relates to their cellular trajectories. Specifically, we proposed that as GB tumours expand at their leading edge into normal brain tissue, the dev-like malignant cells progressively transition towards gliosis and hypoxia states. To test this, we first examined putative malignant trajectories related to gliosis and hypoxia.

In the snRNA-seq data, we observed an expression gradient of astrocyte, injury response and hypoxia-related genes through AC-gliosis-hypoxia states (Fig. 3a). The AC-prog-like states showed the highest levels of developmental and homeostatic astrocyte gene expression (EGFR, SLC1A3, AQP4, ALDH1L1). These were downregulated in AC-gliosis-like cells and replaced by a transcriptomic program resembling astrogliosis, including genes from the integrin–N-cadherin pathway (ITGAV, ITGB1, CDH2) associated with glial scar formation20. This program expanded in gliosis-like cells to genes associated with JAK–STAT signalling (JAK2, STAT3, SMARC4), wound healing (ANXA2, ANXA5, YAP1, F1, AKAP12), cytokine production (IFI16, IL6R, IL1R1) and coagulation (SERPINE1), as well as angiogenesis and hypoxia (VEGFA)21,22,23. This trajectory terminated in hypoxia (HILPDA, BNIP3L, VEGFA) and stress-response gene expression (JUN, FOS, HSPA1B) (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 4a). Transcriptome-wide pseudotime analysis produced consistent results (Methods and Supplementary Fig. 15).

Whereas they were previously thought to resemble mesenchymal cells2, we observed negligible and non-specific expression of key epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition regulators (for example, SNAI1/2, TWIST1/2, ZEB1/2) in gliosis-hypoxia states24,25 (Extended Data Fig. 4a). Hence, our nomenclature places these states in the more specific biological context of glial injury response and hypoxia, putatively stemming from developmental astrocyte-like malignant cells.

The molecular trajectory was correspondingly patterned in space, manifesting as concentric segregation of AC-gliosis-hypoxia states in ST data (Fig. 3b,c and Extended Data Fig. 4c). AC-prog-like cells resided in the non-necrotic cellular tumour in the Dev-like (AC) microenvironment. AC-gliosis-like and gliosis-like cells formed Gliosis transition and Gliosis microenvironments, respectively, that spatially intermixed to varying degrees. In tumours such as AT10, AC-gliosis-like was located at the margin of the cellular tumour and gradually transitioned into gliosis-like within the perinecrotic zone. Hypoxic cells were located either within or adjacent to the tumour necrotic core. The marker genes of these states showed consistent zonated expression with ST and immunohistochemistry (Fig. 3b and Extended Data Fig. 4b).

Taken together, we identify putative GB cellular transitions from developmental astrocyte-like to gliosis then hypoxia states, unfolding across an anatomical axis from tumour expansion towards hypoxia and necrosis (Fig. 3d).

To explore GB cellular transitions, we then asked whether dev-like and gliosis-hypoxic states could emerge within the same tumour subclones (that is, genetically related malignant sublineages). Initially, we performed copy number aberration (CNA) inference to our multiome data, jointly calling clones from tumour snRNA-seq and snATAC-seq profiles (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Note 3). Distinguished by chr. 7 and chr. 10 CNs, most tumour subclones contained both dev-like and gliosis-hypoxic cells (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 16b,c), with the minority states making up an average of 15% (ranging from 3% to 49%) of each clone. Whereas subclones tended to show similar GB state compositions, three tumours contained individual subclones with divergent states. For example, tumour AT10 clone 0 was marked by chr. 17p loss and chr. 17q gain and was preferentially enriched for NPC-neuronal-like states, whereas AT10 clone 1 was enriched for AC-gliosis-hypoxia (Fig. 4b).

To identify CNAs and subclones at the DNA level, we performed WGS on 100–200 × 100–200-μm sized tissue regions of interest (ROIs) by means of laser capture microdissection (LCM)26 (Fig. 4c). We profiled several ROIs across two sampling sites of two tumours (AT3, AT10), generating 61 high-quality WGS profiles (Supplementary Fig. 17). Notably, one ROI from AT10 showed a CN loss in chr. 17p and gain chr. 17q, validating our multiome CN calls. We then defined tumour subclones in multiome data on the basis of the CN events validated by means of LCM-WGS27 (Extended Data Fig. 5a). In AT10, this validated chr. 7 gain and chr. 10 loss in malignant cells and distinguished two subclones based on chr. 17 alterations (Fig. 4d). Both subclones contained a mixture of dev-like and gliosis-hypoxia cells, with clone L0 enriched for NPC-neuronal-like cells and marked by chr. 17 CNs (Extended Data Fig. 5b,c), mirroring our multiome findings. Hence, paired multi-omic evidence demonstrates shared clonal origins of dev-like and gliosis-hypoxia cell states.

Next, we examined spatial organization of GB subclones and whether each subclone spans dev-like to gliosis-hypoxia TMs. In the multiome data, we detected many subclones within each centimetre-scale tumour sampling site (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 18a). Different sampling sites showed largely consistent clonal composition, with exceptions including AT10, in which the chr. 17-altered clone 0 is predominantly located in one site (Fig. 4b). These data indicate coarse spatial intermixing of GB clones, as shown in refs. 5,7.

To investigate finer-scale clonal organization, we examined our LCM-WGS data (roughly 100 × 100 µm resolution). Considering GB clonal intermixing and that our LCM cuts span many cells, we used PyClone-VI28 to infer clonal population structure of LCM ROIs on the basis of single nucleotide variant (SNV) allele frequencies (Fig. 4c). Focusing on AT3, our most comprehensively profiled tumour, we identified 14 SNV clusters (that is, putative subclones) across 35 ROIs (Supplementary Fig. 18c). Individual SNV clusters were present across several LCM ROIs and individual ROIs contained several SNV clusters, indicating clonal intermixing at fine spatial resolution (Fig. 4e).

Clustering ROIs on the basis of their subclonal composition revealed broad spatial clonal domains spanning several millimetres (Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 5g). Broad spatial gradients of intermixed subclones demarcated these domains (Supplementary Note 4). Different tumour sampling sites showed more extensive clonal differences than suggested by multiome CNA inference (Extended Data Fig. 5g), probably owing to the better sensitivity of SNV-based clone calling. Inferring the AT3 phylogeny (Methods), we observed a high degree of spatial intermixture between ancestral (that is, 12), mid-timed (that is, 9) and late (that is, 4) SNV clusters (Fig. 4d,e and Supplementary Figs. 18c and 19).

Individual clones and clonal domains spanned diverse GB cell states and TMs resolved on the adjacent Visium sections (Supplementary Fig. 18c). As our LCM-WGS dataset was limited to two tumours, we then surveyed clonal organization across our Visium dataset. To resolve GB subclones intermixed within Visium spots, we developed the SpaceTree computational framework that leverages scRNA-seq to jointly deconvolve tumour subclones and cell states in ST data (Fig. 4f). SpaceTree integrates reference scRNA-seq and query ST datasets by means of a multi-task graph neural network architecture coupled with label propagation, enabling joint spatial mapping of clones and cell states annotated in scRNA-seq (Supplementary Fig. 20a and Supplementary Computational Note).

We benchmarked SpaceTree on ST data simulated from our GB snRNA-seq profiles, finding that it offers spatial clone deconvolution accuracy near alternative tools at notably reduced computational cost (Supplementary Computational Note). We then applied SpaceTree to integrate our GB snRNA-seq and Visium ST data per tumour site, leveraging our cell annotation and multiome clone calls. As expected, SpaceTree distinguished tumour versus normal and/or infiltrated regions on the basis of the spatial mapping of malignant versus diploid and/or TME clones, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 5h,j and Supplementary Note 4).

SpaceTree revealed widespread spatial intermixing of subclones across all tumours (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 20b), quantified as high clonal entropy in Visium spots dominated by malignant cells and TMs (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Note 4). SpaceTree also resolved broad spatial subclonal domains that overlapped with diverse malignant cell states (Extended Data Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 20c,e). For example, AT15 subclones 0, 3 and 4 each overlapped with both dev-like and gliosis-hypoxia cell states (Fig. 4g). Yet, we also observed regional clonal enrichment, such as clone 3 contributing more heavily to gliosis-hypoxia (Fig. 4g) and the AT10 clone 0 marked by chr. 17 CNs enriched in tissue areas with NPC-neuronal-like cells (Extended Data Fig. 5i). Finally, we observed concordant spatial clonal mapping between SpaceTree and LCM-WGS (Extended Data Fig. 5g and Supplementary Note 4).

In summary, we present a new framework for GB subclonal spatial architecture (Fig. 4i), in which individual subclones finely spatially intermix and organize into broad clonal domains cutting across GB TMs.

Given their shared clonal origins in single-cell and spatial data, we proposed that dev-like cells continuously transition into gliosis and hypoxia states during the rapid expansion of GBs, which double in size every 50 days (ref. 29), and that our ‘snapshot’ snRNA-seq profiling can capture the different stages of such transitions. To infer cellular trajectories from snRNA-seq, we used cell2fate, a probabilistic RNA velocity model30 (Extended Data Fig. 6a).

We applied cell2fate to the snRNA-seq profiles of individual tumour subclones, avoiding confounding genetic differences across clones and tumours, and reconstructed robust trajectories from 23 out of 58 subclones (Extended Data Fig. 6b,c and Supplementary Note 5). Most of these subclones (18 out of 23, more than 78%) showed temporal transitions from dev-like to AC-gliosis-hypoxia and the rest were dominated by an individual state and often showed low transition scores (less than 0.2; 6 out of 7, 86%) (Fig. 5a and Extended Data Figs. 6b–e and 7a).

We observed this trajectory across six out of the nine tumours (for example, AT4, AT10, AT15), in which both NPC-neuronal-like (AT15_0) or OPC-NPC-like (AT6_0) states led to AC-gliosis-hypoxia (Fig. 5a). GBs of all presumed subtypes showed this transition, including the NPC-OPC-like enriched ‘proneural’ (for example, AT5), the NPC-neuronal-like enriched ‘neural’ (that is, AT15) and the AC-gliosis-hypoxia enriched ‘classical-mesenchymal’ (for example, AT6) tumours (Fig. 5a). Within each tumour, many subclones converged on this trajectory (for example, chr. 17 CN+/− clones in AT10). Hence, this trajectory dominates GBs across tumour subtypes and genetically distinct subclones.

Next, we examined whether this cellular trajectory is molecularly conserved across GB, leveraging temporal gene expression modules (that is, RNA velocity modules) identified by cell2fate30 (Supplementary Note 5). We identified 12 meta-modules representing recurrent temporal transcriptional programs across subclones and tumours (Extended Data Fig. 8a). Dev-like modules occurred early in the trajectory, were enriched in dev-like states and shared their marker genes (for example, MBP, MYT1L). By contrast, AC-gliosis-hypoxia modules occurred late and were enriched in AC-gliosis-hypoxia states with respective marker gene expression (for example, GFAP, SERPINE1, HILPDA). Consistently, mapped in Visium ST data, these modules showed spatial transitions akin to those from dev-like to gliosis-hypoxia cell states (Extended Data Fig. 8b). Furthermore, survival analysis identified worse outcomes for patients enriched for late gliosis-hypoxia modules (Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Fig. 22), consistent with poor ‘mesenchymal-like’ GB prognosis1,31. Finally, these modules also nominated new candidate regulators and effectors of GB cellular trajectories (Supplementary Note 5). Hence, this trajectory is spatially patterned and molecularly conserved across GBs.

To orthogonally validate the GB trajectory, we initially used somatic mutations in our most extensive LCM-WGS dataset (tumour AT3) to reconstruct GB clonal phylogenies. This delivered a SNV-based hierarchy of clones (Fig. 5b and Methods), more finely resolving clonal architecture than CNA inference, which we then correlated with GB cell states mapped in adjacent Visium ST sections. We focused on the most ancestral and the most frequently observed late SNV clusters, 0 and 4, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 18c). The abundance of the ancestral SNV cluster 0 was positively correlated with NPC-OPC-like cell states and negatively correlated with AC-gliosis-hypoxia, whereas the late SNV cluster 4 showed the opposite pattern (Fig. 5b). These data are consistent with a GB trajectory along which AC-gliosis-hypoxia cell states are derived from NPC-OPC-like cells.

To experimentally validate the GB trajectory, we engrafted patient-derived GB cell lines into patient-derived tumour organoids and traced their population-level cellular trajectories over time in situ (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Note 6). First, we derived GB organoids (GBOs) from a primary IDH-wt tumour32. Using 5,101-plex Xenium ST (Methods), we observed that GBOs recapitulate the spatial transitions of GB cell states, in which NPC-OPC-like states were located most peripherally, AC-progenitor-like states were positioned relatively deeper and Gliosis-like states formed an inner ring around the hypoxic core (Fig. 5e and Extended Data Fig. 9d). Second, to trace the cellular trajectories arising from dev-like GB cells, we characterized two dev-like patient lines33, E25 and E30, labelled them with green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporters and engrafted them into GBOs (Methods). At pre-engraftment (day 0, D0), scRNA-seq showed that E25 overwhelmingly consisted of dev-like states (89.2% OPC/NPC-OPC-like), whereas E30 was a mix of dev-like (25.7%) and AC-gliosis-like cells (48.1%) (Fig. 5d).

We then separately engrafted E25 and E30 GFP+ cells into 3-week old GBOs and used 5,101-plex Xenium ST to profile their spatiotemporal trajectories until D28 (Fig. 5c, Extended Data Fig. 9 and Supplementary Note 6). After GBO engraftment, the dev-like E25 line gave rise to AC-gliosis-hypoxia cells (Fig. 5d,e). At D7 and D14, engrafted cells showed expected spatial patterns, in which OPC-NPC-like and AC-prog-like GFP+ cells were located most peripherally, the Gliosis-like GFP+ cells were positioned deeper and the hypoxic GFP+ cells were at the GBO core (Fig. 5e, right panel, and Supplementary Fig. 24a). Consistently, in the E30 engrafts, dev-like cells reduced and AC-gliosis-hypoxia states accumulated over time (Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 9e), manifesting fine-grained cellular transitions akin to patient tumours (Supplementary Note 6).

Taken together, we identify and validate a spatially patterned and molecularly conserved malignant cell trajectory across GBs. Combined with our fine-grained mapping of AC-gliosis-hypoxia trajectories (Fig. 3), these data indicate stepwise transitions of malignant cells from dev-like towards gliosis and hypoxia states throughout tumour expansion (Fig. 5f).

Finally, we investigated whether the GB TME is specialized to accommodate the GB malignant cell trajectory. We focused on myeloid cells, given their prevalence (Supplementary Table 3) and that macrophage-derived signals can alter GB malignant cell states9. We annotated myeloid subclusters in our snRNA-seq atlas by expression of canonical markers, MSigDB gene sets and glioma myeloid programs34.

We classified 11 myeloid subtypes, most being tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs), into 3 main classes (Extended Data Fig. 10a and Supplementary Note 7): (1) resident and pro-inflammatory myeloid cells (P2RY12, CX3CR1) spanning microglia and anti-tumour TAMs, (2) infiltrating and anti-inflammatory myeloid cells (CD163, STAB1, CD14) including monocytes and TAMs showing immunosuppressive and protumour signatures and (3) stress-response and angiogenic TAMs expressing hypoxia and angiogenesis (VEGFA, VCAN) and ‘lipid-laden’ macrophage (GPNMB, PLIN2) markers.

To precisely relate these myeloid cell states to GB states, we examined their distribution across GB TMs and observed spatially distinct myeloid compartments (Extended Data Fig. 10a,b and Supplementary Note 7). Resident and pro-inflammatory populations were enriched in the ‘Immune (resident)’ TM overlapping with dev-like GB cells (for example, OPC-like). Infiltrating and anti-inflammatory populations formed the distinct ‘Immune (infiltrated)’ TM in closer proximity to AC-like and Gliosis-transition states. Stress-response and angiogenic TAMs were highly enriched in the ‘Gliosis’ and ‘Hypoxia’ TMs.

These observations indicate distinct myeloid microenvironments for dev-like and gliosis-hypoxia GB cell states. To determine whether these malignant states are exposed to distinct myeloid signals, we performed cell–cell communication (CCC) analysis in snRNA-seq data among spatially colocalized TME and malignant states (Extended Data Fig. 10c and Supplementary Note 7). This analysis identified distinct myeloid signalling environments across the GB cellular trajectory (Extended Data Fig. 10d,e). In dev-like TMs, myeloid ligands linked to protumour and anti-tumour functions, such as TRAIL (TNFSF10), progranulin (GRN) and galectin 9 (LGALS9), putatively signalled to dev-like states. In gliosis-hypoxia TMs, distinct myeloid ligands linked to wound healing, hypoxia and angiogenesis, such VEGFA, versican (VCAN) and annexin 1 (ANXA1) putatively targeted gliosis and hypoxic cancer cells (Supplementary Note 7).

Taken together, the myeloid TME is regionalized across the GB malignant cell trajectory and exposes malignant cells to distinct signals (Supplementary Fig. 28).

GB heterogeneity and plasticity have undermined efforts to pinpoint the cellular trajectories of cancer cells and develop targeted therapies. Here we applied a holistic multimodal mapping approach to jointly characterize the genetic, cellular and tissue architecture of GB tumours. We describe remarkable intra-tumour heterogeneity and spatial organization of GBs, extending previous surveys. Yet, we distinctly link GB heterogeneity and tissue architecture to a spatiotemporally patterned cancer cell trajectory from dev-like towards glial injury response and hypoxia states. This trajectory is molecularly conserved across presumed GB tumour subtypes and subclonal lineages within each tumour, and is recapitulated in GBOs ex vivo. Furthermore, it is coupled to regionalized myeloid TME signalling. Hence, this trajectory represents a fundamental organizational principle of GB tumours unifying their heterogeneity.

Our multimodal atlas facilitated the biological reinterpretation of GB cancer cell states. Notably, we found that the previously reported mesenchymal cell state1,2 was in fact a malignant cell gliosis and hypoxia response. Wound healing-like states and hypoxia are present across many cancers including pancreatic35 and colon36 cancer, suggesting dev-like cancer cells transitioning into such states may be conserved37. Beyond hypoxia, whether other cell and tissue cues push dev-like cancer states into gliosis remains to be identified.

The spatiotemporal trajectory of cancer cells challenges the stratification of GB tumours into distinct subtypes. The regional heterogeneity within each tumour suggests that GB subtypes, commonly classified from single biopsies, rather represent region-specific cancer cell states and TMs. The temporal nature of the GB trajectory may pose another challenge. Tumours enriched for dev-like or gliosis-hypoxia states may represent early or advanced stages of GB, respectively, possibly accounting for the worse survival outcomes of mesenchymal GBs1.

Genetic alterations can promote distinct cancer cell states across GBs2. Whereas the GB trajectory is shared across the tumour genetic hierarchy, our results also raise the possibility that GB cell states can be modulated within genetically distinct subclones. Exemplified here by the association of chr. 17 CN events with neuronal-like cancer cell states, previously documented in medulloblastomas38,39, functional studies are needed to determine whether genetic alterations can expand intra-tumour heterogeneity.

We identify multi-scale spatial architecture of GB cancer cell subclones. Whereas GB subclones parcellate tumours into broad spatial domains, we observe their intimate spatial intermixture within each domain. Contrasting with the spatial segregation of clones in breast40 and prostate41 cancer, this pattern may reflect the lack of physical barriers in the brain, such as ductal structures in the breast40, that can restrict clonal outgrowth, as well as the migratory nature of GB cancer cells.

In a companion study, we leveraged the joint transcriptomic and epigenetic data generated here to define and perturb gene regulatory networks controlling GB plasticity12. Beyond identifying master regulators of GB states, this orthogonal approach suggested hierarchical transitions across GB cancer states and recapitulated the GB trajectory identified here.

Our study has several limitations. First, although we trace population-level cellular trajectories in organoids, we do not provide single cancer cell-resolution lineage tracing42. Second, whereas we examine the dominant cellular trajectories of cancer cells in primary GB, we do not interrogate their plasticity potential. Specifically, previous studies and ours2,6 do not resolve whether GB cancer cells can revert from gliosis and hypoxia into dev-like states. Yet, our companion study predicts limited cell state transitions of gliosis-hypoxia cells on the basis of gene regulatory networks12, raising the possibility of limited cancer cell plasticity in GB beyond the trajectory illustrated here.

The significant intratumoural heterogeneity of GB underscores the challenges to therapeutic intervention. Our findings propose targeting the spatiotemporal trajectory of cancer cells and destabilizing the myeloid TME associated with this trajectory, two conserved features of GB tumours identified here, as potential avenues for exploration.

Histological annotations were defined according to the IVY Glioblastoma Atlas Project reference; an existing taxonomy system with established inter-neuropathologist agreement and clinical relevance45. Histological annotations included the following:

Leading edge (LE): the outermost boundary of the tumour where the laminar architecture of the cortical layers is frequently evident.

Infiltrating tumour (IT): the intermediate zone between the leading edge and cellular tumour, frequently marked by perineuronal satellitosis.

Cellular tumour (CT): the main part of the tumour core.

Necrosis: dead or dying tissue, marked by the presence of karyorrhectic or cellular debris and the absence of crisp cytological architecture.

Pseudopalisading cells around necrosis (PAN): the narrow boundary of cells arranged like pseudopalisades along the perimeter of necrosis in the tumour core.

Perinecrotic zone (PNZ): a boundary of tumour cells in the tumour core along the edge of necrosis that lacks a clear demarcation of pseudopalisading cells around necrosis.

Microvascular proliferation (MVP): two or more blood vessels sharing a common vessel wall of endothelial and smooth muscle cells typically in the tumour core, and arranged in the shape of a glomerulus or garland of many interconnected blood vessels.

Hyperplastic blood vessels (HPV): blood vessels with thickened walls found anywhere in the tumour.

This involved removing nuclei with total gene counts fewer than 500, total counts UMI < 1,000 and nuclei with more than 10% of reads mapping to mitochondrial content. We then applied Scrublet50 (v.0.2.3) on each library individually and filtered our data on the basis of a two-step method adapted from previously described median absolute deviation thresholding. Individual cells were first filtered on the basis of methods described in ref. 51 (false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05) and then clusters were subsequently filtered using similar methods51,52 to ensure the removal of probable subthreshold doublets (FDR < 0.1). Following doublet detection and removal, any remaining cell with a total UMI count more than 75,000 was removed from the dataset. Finally, we calculated cell cycling scores using Scanpy based on the gene list described in ref. 53. ATAC data were processed according to the ArchR54 workflow (minimum fragments more than 1,000 and more than 4 transcriptional start sites). These barcodes were then filtered by the list of barcodes passing RNA quality control filters, effectively removing most low-quality ATAC barcodes. Finally, the RNA data were subset by barcodes passing ATAC filters to ensure symmetry between modalities for downstream applications.

snRNA-seq libraries were concatenated into a single dataset and log-normalized for highly variable gene (HVG) selection. HVGs were identified with Scanpy using tumour ID as a batch key and dispersion-based feature selection with following non-default parameters: (min_mean=0.0075, max_mean=4, min_disp=0.1). The resulting list of 10,701 HVGs was then used for batch integration with scVI13 (v.0.19.0), for which we used each 10X reaction as the batch and included tumour ID, tumour site, 10X reaction date and cell-cycle phase as model covariates. Model hyperparameters included 50 latent space dimensions and 2 hidden layers, with 1,024 nodes per layer. After training the model, leiden clustering was performed using the nearest neighbours graph constructed from the resulting scVI embeddings. Leiden clusters corresponding to putative and broadly defined TME populations were then annotated (for example, oligodendrocytes, myeloid cells, neurons, lymphocytes) on the basis of marker gene expression.

The detection of cell-specific CNAs was performed on each tumour individually using inferCNVpy (https://github.com/icbi-lab/infercnvpy), a Python implementation of the inferCNV55 method, with parameters consistent with previous GB studies56 and a window size of 250 genes. InferCNV also requires a set of non-malignant reference cells, for which we used the least ambiguous TME populations identified on the basis of marker gene expression: macrophages, oligodendrocytes, neurons and lymphocytes.

Following integration and coarse-grained clustering, the first step in atlas annotation was accurately delineating between malignant and non-malignant cells. This followed an approach adapted from previous GB studies2, in which two separate CNA metrics were used to score cells: CNA signal and CNA correlation. We calculated the absolute CNA signal by averaging absolute CNA scores originating from the diagnostically relevant chr. 7 gain and chr. 10 loss events. CNA correlation was computed by calculating the correlation between the CN profile of a given cell and mean CN profile of cells identified as non-malignant through marker gene expression. Malignant cell signal was defined as a CNA signal more than 0.02 and a CNA correlation more than 0.3. As static malignant cell thresholds may not identify all malignant cells and are dependent on the coverage in expression-based CN inference methods, we then annotated clusters as putatively malignant on the basis of the percentage of malignant cells. Most malignant clusters showed a malignant percentage greater than 20% but malignant clusters with a percentage 3% or more were flagged as putatively malignant.

We then reintegrated and subclustered each main TME population and all malignant cells, individually. This was followed by a subsequent CNA evaluation to ensure there were no misannotated subclusters. TME cell types were then identified manually using lists of well-established human brain marker genes (Supplementary Fig. 7a). TME subclusters with a malignant cell percentage 3% or more were also flagged. Malignant cells subclusters were annotated using composite of four different approaches: (1) we calculated a series of gene module scores based on the workflow outlined in ref. 2 in conjunction with the score_genes function in Scanpy, which is analogous to their methodology. (2) We mapped our GB data onto a developing brain atlas14 using scPoli57 with default parameters to identify developing brain analogues to malignant cell state identities. (3) Markers specific to each cluster were identified using scanpy.tl.rank_genes_groups based on Wilcoxon rank-sum. (4) Gene-set enrichment analysis was performed on differential gene expression results using decoupler58 with MSigDB signatures59,60.

To assess GB cell state phenotypes of proliferative subclusters, we derived gene expression signatures of GB cell states by means of consensus NMF61. We applied consensus NMF independently to each tumour using tumour-specific raw count matrices comprising 10,000 genes. Candidate factorization ranks from K = 10 to 25 were evaluated, with 200 iterations performed per rank. Following factorization, replicate runs were aggregated and donor-specific ranks were selected on the basis of factor stability and reconstruction error. Consensus programs were then derived at the selected rank, and normalized program usage together with the top contributing genes for each factor were extracted for downstream analysis. To perform cross-tumour program analysis, each tumour-specific factor was clustered into metaprograms similar to refs. 15,62. Each factor was represented by its top 200 genes, which were filtered on the basis of two criteria: (1) if several factors within a tumour showed 40% overlap (80 out of 200), only one factor was retained, and (2) factors were retained if they had 20% similarity (40 out of 200) with at least one factor from another tumour. We then clustered these factors into metaprograms using average-linkage agglomerative clustering based on shared gene membership. Genes were ranked by intra-cluster overlap and those with at least 50% overlap with all factors in a metaprogram were retained for biological interpretation and program annotation.

Visium spatial RNA-seq data were mapped to the official Cell Ranger GRCh38 using 10X Genomics Space Ranger Software Suite (v.2.0 for standard Visium, v.2.1 for Visium CytAssist). To spatially map malignant and TME cell states in Visium data, we used the cell2location model16 to deconvolve the Visium spot transcriptomes to cell state abundances on the basis of paired snRNA-seq reference signatures. We generated unique reference signatures for each tumour to better reflect malignant cell gene expression heterogeneity. This was done using the negative binomial regression model packaged with cell2location and included the following changes to default training parameters: max_epochs=400, batch_size=10000 and lr=0.002. The cell2location model was run on each individual tumour (that is, batching many Visium sections) with the following modifications to default parameters: model parameters were adjusted to N_cells_per_location=30 and detection_alpha=200, and training parameter adjustments included max_epochs=6000 and a batch_size set to roughly 25% of the total number of spots profiled for a given tumour.

To identify TMs, we applied the scikit-learn NMF model packaged with cell2location to our cell state abundance results16. This allowed us to decompose matrices of inferred cell state abundances into components representing patterns of cell state colocalization, analogous to TMs. To select the ideal number of NMF factors, we ran NMF per tumour across high factor numbers (k = 10, 12, 14, 16, 20, 25, 30, 40, 50) with 10 restarts. For factor selection, we analysed factor stability by computing column-wise correlation coefficients between restart pairs. We then calculated stability scores on the basis of average maximum column-wise correlation between factors for each pair of restart runs. To ensure factor selection was optimal, yet high enough to identify rarer features, we prioritized the largest factor number (k = 16) that retained high stability (score more than 0.8) across most tumours (Supplementary Fig. 11a), The model was then trained using 16 factors for each individual tumour. Of the resulting matrices, we normalized the matrices composed of factor loadings per cell state and performed a cross-tumour annotation of associated factors. Factors were grouped into meta-factors, described as microenvironments, using agglomerative hierarchical clustering with cosine distance. These grouped factors were annotated according to the most frequently observed cell states in each factor, in addition to the correlation between cell state and factor abundances.

To define a measure of spatial intermixing we calculated Shannon entropy on the basis of cell2location abundance frequencies for each Visium spot. To ensure spots were highly representative of a given cell state, only spots in the 98th percentile of abundance for each cell state were considered.

To quantify the relative spatial organization of cell states, TM factors and cell2fate modules, we calculated the minimum pairwise spot distance between all component pairs for each of these features independently using k-dimensional trees. As the inferred abundance of these features are continuous variables, we imposed thresholds on the basis of feature abundance to quantify spots associated with high feature abundance. This included two filters: abundance in a spot must be greater or equal to the median abundance of a feature, and, to remove abundances with poor signal, the abundance must also be greater than a base threshold. This allowed us to focus on comparing regions of high density while also accounting for biologically relevant differences in abundance. The base threshold varied depending on the application and was based on the distribution of abundances (cell2location = 4, microenvironment factor = 4 and cell2fate modules = 2). As some spatial features have inherently different spatial distributions—for example, the compactness and density of vasculature compared with malignant microenvironments—we summarized these distances by calculating the bottom 25th percentile of nearest neighbour distances.

To compare cell2location with alternative methods, we also deconvolved GB Visium data with robust cell type decomposition (RCTD) (spacexr package v.2.2.1). To make a head-to-head comparison, we used the same tumour-specific snRNA-seq reference signatures used for cell2location described above (that is, calculated using negative binomial regression). We then ran RCTD with precomputed references (by providing it as cell_type_profiles parameter in create.RCTD function) using the run.RCTD function. We used default parameters except doublet_mode that was set to ‘full’.

We observed that broad spatial trends were largely conserved across both methods (Supplementary Fig. 10). For example, dev-like and AC-gliosis-hypoxia cell states were spatially segregated across both methods, including spatial stratification within the latter class (Supplementary Fig. 10a). We found low to moderate positive correlations when we assessed all cell states (median r ranging from 0.33 to 0.65, Supplementary Fig. 10b,c). Yet, we observed considerably higher correlation coefficients for high-abundance cell states (that is, defined as with median abundance more than 90th percentile per Visium section) (median r ranging from 0.61 to 0.85, Supplementary Fig. 10c). These high-abundance states spanned all main malignant subclasses across the dev-like to AC-gliosis-hypoxia, such as OPC-like 1, AC-progenitor-like 2, AC-gliosis-like 1/2 and gliosis-like clusters across tumours AT10 and AT15 (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Overall, whereas cell2location and RCTD results slightly diverge across low-abundance cell states, the spatial transitions of main GB cell states are highly correlated.

We also examined whether the GB TMs are recapitulated in RCTD analysis (Supplementary Fig. 12). Applying NMF to RCTD predictions of each tumour, we could readily match the resulting TMs to those derived from cell2location on the basis of their cell state composition (that is, done by Hungarian matching at the level of each tumour). The RCTD and cell2location TMs matched one another on the basis of their spatial patterns (Supplementary Fig. 12a) and cell state compositions (Supplementary Fig. 12b–d), with most RCTD TMs showing a clear match to one cell2location TM (Supplementary Fig. 12b). The cosine similarities of overall cell state compositions of the RCTD and cell2location TMs showed modest to high median cosine similarity, ranging from 0.48 to 0.86 with a global median of 0.63 (Supplementary Fig. 12c). We observed that gliosis-hypoxia TMs showed high similarity (more than 0.7) whereas the dev-like TMs showed lower similarity, most probably due to the presence of several intermixed malignant cell states in the latter. Yet, when we inspected the most frequently observed cell type across each TM, we observed strong consistency across RCTD and cell2location results, in which both dev-like and AC-gliosis-hypoxia-related TMs were enriched for similar cell states (Supplementary Fig. 12d). Of note, the main malignant cell states across the cancer cell trajectory identified in our paper showed consistent TM distributions across both analysis approaches.

To benchmark the accuracy of cell2location predictions, we pseudobulked Xenium data to emulate Visium spots and deconvolved using cell2location and a tumour-matched snRNA-seq reference signature. We then computed the correlation between cell2location estimates and the single-cell-resolution cell state assignments in Xenium data. First, we generated 55-µm2 pseudospots in Xenium section AT6-BRA-3-FO-4-S52-ii (tumour AT6) with a custom 460-plex gene panel (Supplementary Table 15) to simulate Visium spot diameter, then assigned cells (segmented by means of default on-instrument multimodal cell segmentation) to spots by centroid coordinates, and discarded empty pseudospots. Second, to generate ground-truth single-cell state assignments, we annotated Xenium data at single-cell level by Tangram (v.1.04, ‘Xenium panel design and data processing’ section below). We used snRNA-seq data from the same tumour as a reference, in which malignant cells were annotated at subclass level (n = 9 malignant states) and the TME were annotated at coarse or fine-grained level (n = 9 and 56 TME states, respectively). Third, we generated pseudobulked gene expression count matrices as input to cell2location deconvolution with the same tumour-specific snRNA-seq reference signature used for Tangram, with default max_epochs=30000, detection_alpha=20 and following authors’ instructions we adjusted N_cells_per_location=21 from randomly sampling ground-truth cell numbers (that is, number of single cells identified in Xenium per pseudospot) across 30 pseudospots. Last, we calculated cell state proportions in the cell2location analysis as the mean posterior cell state abundance estimate divided by the total pseudospot mean posterior cell abundance estimate, and benchmarked by Pearson correlation coefficient against Tangram cell state proportions per spot.

We observed the highest correlation (r = 0.84) when using the snRNA-seq reference annotated at the level of 9 malignant subclasses and main TME cell types (n = 18 cell states total) (Supplementary Fig. 14b). Highly abundant cell states showed better correlation, such as the AC-progenitor-like, AC-gliosis-like and hypoxic cells that dominate this section. Repeating this analysis with the more granular TME annotation (n = 65 cell states total), we observed more modest overall correlation (r = 0.65) (Supplementary Fig. 14c). Abundant malignant states consistently showed high correlation (r = 0.68-0.90 for AC-gliosis-hypoxia states), whereas fine-grained TME states such as myeloid subtypes showed a range (for example, stress-response TAMs at r = 0.70, but anti-inflammatory TAMs at r = 0.31). We speculate that the fine-grained states with low correlations represent those that are better resolved with transcriptome-wide Visium than with the targeted Xenium approach.

To analyse the Visium data from ref. 15, we downloaded the corresponding datasets from the Sequence Read Archive and Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE237183) and processed them using Space Ranger v.2.0.0. We then used cell2location to deconvolve cell state abundances using a snRNA-seq reference generated from our whole single-nuclei dataset. Otherwise, we calculated the snRNA-seq reference signatures and ran cell2location as described above. To fit the data of ref. 15 into graphical processing unit memory, we randomly split their data into three batches ensuring that each sample is distributed uniformly between batches.

The resulting spatial cell state trends were broadly consistent with ref. 15 that annotated Visium spots as their dominant NMF-derived metaprograms (Supplementary Fig. 10d). For example, spots annotated as mesenchymal-Hyp metaprograms were enriched for cell states such as stress-response TAMs and hypoxic cells, and contained gliosis-like and AC-gliosis-like cell states in the peripheral parts of their tissue regions. Similarly, regions associated with OPC-like (for example, sample GSM7596597) and AC-like (for example, GSM7596592) metaprograms were regionally consistent with our OPC-like 4 and OPC-NPC-like 2, or AC-progenitor-like 1 and 4 cell states, respectively. In addition, spots annotated as ‘Vasc.’ and ‘Mac.’ were largely aligned with regions enriched for our monocyte and vascular cell states. To demonstrate this quantitatively, we computed the log fold change between cell2location abundances between spots assigned to a given metaprogram and all other spots. This showed overall good biological concordance (Supplementary Fig. 10e). For example, TME such as macrophages, or vascular cells showed strong enrichment in Mac. and Vasc. spots, respectively. Regarding malignant cells, mesenchymal-hyp spots showed high stress-response TAMs, hypoxic and gliosis-like enrichment across the cohort in ref. 53. Similarly, OPC-like 2 and 4 were strongly enriched in OPC and ‘Prolif. Metab.’ (metabolomic) spots.

We also examined whether the GB TMs are recapitulated in data from ref. 15 (Supplementary Fig. 12e–g). Using the cell2location integration of our snRNA-seq reference pooled across all tumours and the Greenwald Visium data15 (Supplementary Fig. 10d), we applied the same cell2location-associated NMF method used to define TMs. As cell2location was applied to all tumours from the cohort in ref. 15 inclusively, we omitted meta-factor identification and instead compared resulting factors directly to the loadings from our TMs. Owing to the appearance of low-signal factors at the equivalent k = 16, we selected k = 11 on the basis of whether factors had high global stability (stability more than 0.8; Methods and Supplementary Fig. 11a) and the absence of low-signal factors. These factors were then annotated on the basis of cell state enrichment and showed a strong resemblance to our TMs (Supplementary Fig. 12e). Specifically, the 11 TMs we identified in the data from ref. 15 recapitulated the cell-type compositions of main malignant TMs mapped in our study including Dev-like (OPC), Dev-like (Neuron) and/or Neuron-like, Gliosis and Hypoxia TMs, as well as those defined by TME such as Vasculature, White and Grey matter (Supplementary Fig. 12e). Furthermore, these TMs showed concordant spatial zonation. Gliosis and Hypoxia TMs spatially segregated within perinecrotic regions, that are also consistently enriched in the mesenchymal-Hyp metaprogram of ref. 15 (Supplementary Fig. 12f). The Dev-like (OPC) TM was spatially distinct from gliosis-hypoxia, overlapping OPC metaprogram from ref. 15 (Supplementary Fig. 12f). On the basis of state-normalized loadings per factor, we observed modest to high cosine similarity of our TMs to their top matching TMs in the data of ref. 15 (range 0.46–0.92 with global median of 0.69) (Supplementary Fig. 12g), including high similarity of core malignant TMs such as Dev-like (OPC), Dev-like (AC), Gliosis and Hypoxia. These similarities largely recapitulate the stepwise spatial transitions of malignant cell states observed in our study. The differences between the TMs in our work and in ref. 15 included the lack of pial layer TM, rare TMs such as Hypoxic 2 and distinct TAM versus resident Immune niches in the latter. These differences can be explained by anatomical differences between the samples across the two studies as well as the lack of tumour-specific snRNA-seq references for Greenwald Visium data15 precluding further granularity.

We designed three Xenium panels for different applications:

To validate spatial GB architecture across many tumours (n = 17 sections from 8 tumours and 18 tumour sites in total; Fig. 2e, Extended Data Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 14), we supplemented the standard 266-gene Xenium v.1 human brain panel with an extra 49 probes (Supplementary Table 8). The extra custom probes were largely selected by applying a probe selection method, Spapros63, to our snRNA-seq data. Spapros was run without selecting a feature number cutoff (n = none) and default parameters. Malignant and TME cells were run inclusively to ensure useful cell state delineation; however, the atlas was downsampled for each tumour and cell state to reduce the computational burden. We then selected high-quality markers on the basis of the spatial expression according to our Visium data. For each cell state associated with a given marker, we calculated Pearson correlations between the marker voxel expression and cell state abundance inferred through cell2location. Markers with the highest median correlation and strongest inter-tumoural concordance were selected. Markers that were already present in the human brain panel were omitted. This resulted in 44 Spapros-derived cell state markers that were further supplemented by 17 known GB markers, which were expressed in our Visium data. The panel was used on 18 blocks from 7 different patients, on 19 sections in total.

To validate the spatial architecture of fine-grained GB cancer cell states and benchmark cell2location deconvolution on Xenium data (n = 1 section; Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 14), we designed a fully custom Xenium v.1 panel of 460 genes (Supplementary Table 15). Gene selection was guided by cell-type biomarker discovery and functional cell–cell interaction analyses, primarily derived from our snRNA-seq atlas data. We also applied Spapros to refine the panel by selecting genes that jointly optimize transcriptional variation across the targeted fine-grained cell-type subsets.

To quantify GB cell states in GBOs, we supplemented the Xenium Prime 5K Human Pan Tissue & Pathways Panel (1000724) (n = 40 sections from 9 GBOs; Fig. 5) with 100 custom genes (Supplementary Table 16). This add-on panel comprises genes marking the different GB malignant cell states and TME, as well as genes associated with proliferation, developmental neuronal-migration, astrocyte differentiation, ECM remodelling and mesenchymal invasion and stromal-immune programs. Probes for eGFP were also added to track GFP-labelled cells.

For Xenium experiments applying panel 1, default on-instrument nucleus segmentation and expansion was performed on Xenium datasets (Xenium Onboard Analyser versions xenium-1.5.0.3 and 1.6.0.8). Nuclei were segmented from DAPI images, followed by the definition of cell boundary as either a 15 µm expansion or the presence of another cell boundary. For Xenium experiments applying panels 2 and 3, default on-instrument segmentation was performed with multi-tissue staining (Xenium Onboard Analyser version xenium-3.1.0.4 for panel 2 and version xenium-4.0.1.4 for panel 3). After nuclei segmentation with DAPI, cell boundaries were segmented (ATP1A1, E-Cadherin and CD45), followed by defining the cell interior (18S ribosomal RNA (RNA), alphaSMA and Vimentin). In absence of boundary or interior staining, segmented nuclei are expanded by 5 µm or until encountering another cell boundary.

Single-cell Xenium gene expression data were preprocessed using standard Scanpy workflow49. For Xenium v.1 datasets, cell-by-gene matrices were filtered to exclude cells with low numbers of transcripts (total gene count fewer than 10) and genes that expressed in fewer than 10 cells across the dataset. Gene counts were normalized and log-transformed before downstream analysis. Tangram64 (version 1.0.4) was used to transfer coarse cell state labels from our snRNA-seq atlas onto Xenium sections using default parameters. Patient-matched snRNA-seq profiles were used as reference for each Xenium dataset. Intersecting and non-zero expressed genes from both modalities were used as training sets (315 genes). Tangram was trained with default learning rate (0.1) for 500 epochs, in ‘clusters’ mode with a uniform density prior and most probable cell-type labels were transferred to Xenium cells from output mapping. See the section ‘GBO spatiotemporal lineage tracing data analysis’ for the processing and quality control of Xenium Prime datasets.

scFates65 was run independently for each donor. For every donor, cells annotated as AC-progenitor-like, AC-gliosis-like, Gliosis-like or Hypoxic were subset from the raw count matrix; clusters with fewer than 10 cells in that donor were dropped, and donors retaining fewer than two clusters were excluded. Counts were normalized to 10,000 per cell, log-transformed and the top 2,000 HVGs were selected (seurat_v3 flavour), followed by principal components analysis (up to 50 components). To suppress the cell-cycle signal, we computed one-way ANOVA F statistics of each principal component against cluster identity and against cell-cycle phase, and retained only principal components with a cluster F greater than cell-cycle F. Donors with fewer than two such cluster-dominant principal components were excluded. A principal tree was then fitted on this cluster-dominant principal component subspace with scFates.tl.tree (method ppt, σ = 0.1, λ = 10, 50 fitting steps, seed 42), using min(30, max(5, n_cells/100)) nodes to adapt to donor size. The tip with the highest soft-assignment-weighted fraction of AC-progenitor-like cells was set as the root, and pseudotime was computed with scFates.tl.pseudotime. Per-cluster mean pseudotime was then summarized across donors.

We leveraged multiome data to jointly infer subclones from multiome data. For snRNA-seq, we used the inferCNV results described above. For snATAC-seq data, we detected CNs directly on the Cell Ranger ARC ATAC output using epiAneufinder66 with a 1-MB window size and blacklisted regions of hg38 provided by the authors. Cells with total fragment counts of fewer than 20,000 were filtered to ensure sufficient chromosomal coverage.

Clones were called by applying leiden clustering on a neighbourhood graph generated from genome-wide CN calls for each modality. We observed that scATAC-seq-based CNAs showed greater variability than RNA-based CNAs, probably due to the higher sensitivity of ATAC-seq in detecting open chromatin regions, resulting in a larger number of events. To be more conservative and reduce false positives, we matched clones from scATAC-seq to scRNA-seq clones. To reduce clone redundancy, we assessed the similarity between scATAC-seq clones using correlation distance. Clones with pairwise distances below a predefined threshold were merged to eliminate highly similar clones.

To mitigate differences in resolution between modalities, we applied interpolation to the RNA-based copy number variant (CNV) profiles, aligning them with the larger genomic windows of scATAC-seq data. For matching clones across modalities, we computed two metrics: cell overlap and cosine similarity between cluster centroids. Cell overlap was defined as the proportion of cells shared between scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq clones, whereas cosine similarity was calculated on the basis of the mean CNV profile for each clone. These metrics were averaged to create a composite similarity score.

Next, we retained the top 20% of clone matches based on this composite similarity score. For each RNA clone, we greedily selected the most similar scATAC-seq clones, assigning cells shared between matched RNA and ATAC clones to a new joint clone identifier. Finally, we applied label spreading to propagate clone labels to classified RNA cells on the basis of their CNV profiles. Each cell was assigned a probability of belonging to a clone, allowing flexibility in downstream analyses by using different confidence thresholds. For most analyses, we applied a confidence threshold of 0.9 to ensure high-confidence clone assignments. Overall, the methodological concordance with LCM-based WGS orthogonally validated the efficacy of counts-based methods at identifying core clone populations. Furthermore, although there may be minor clones with small artefactual signals introduced by inferential nature of this method, we reasoned that redundant granularity would allow us to conservatively control for genetic heterogeneity when applied to downstream trajectory analyses.

Following sequencing, all low-input WGS data were mapped to the hg38 reference genome using BWA-MEM67. All mutations were called against matched normal blood samples using the Wellcome Sanger Institute variant calling pipeline.

CN variants were called using ASCAT68 and Battenberg69. Battenberg CN results were used for subsequent analyses. For each patient, LCM libraries with sufficient DNA concentrations (10 μg ml−1) and CN diversity were used as the basis for alleleIntegrator27. CNAs were called in the snRNA-seq data using alleleIntegrator. The standard workflow was applied using Battenberg CN segments alongside the standard alleleIntegrator workflow. Clones were identified by first subsetting cells with support for a chr. 7 gain, which is diagnostically relevant to GB, followed by support for putative lineage-specific CNAs (maxPostProb > 0.7).

CaVEMan70 was used to call substitutions. Variants were filtered to account for artefacts consistent with other low-input WGS studies26,44, which involved applying a filter designed to remove false positive variants such as erroneously processed cruciform DNA (https://github.com/MathijsSanders/SangerLCMFiltering). Subsequently, variants were retained if they met a minimum median alignment score of 140 or more, fewer than half reads were clipped (contaminated log-phase multiplier  =0), were supported by at least four variant reads and had a total sequencing depth of 10× or greater. The minimum thresholds for mapping quality and base quality were set to 30 and 25, respectively. Finally, we used a beta-binomial model adapted from the Shearwater variant caller in ref. 71 designed to remove both germline variants and consistently observed low-quality variants. Site-specific error rates were calculated for each variant and only retained if the FDR-corrected P value was less than 0.001. Sample phylogenies were generated using Seqouia72 with default parameters and no application of the binomial mixture model. To ensure Sequoia compatibility with our tumour data, substitutions in CN variable regions were omitted.

Following filtering, PyClone-VI28 was used to cluster variants outside patient-associated CNAs into putative subclones for each patient, separately. Each patient was run with the following non-default parameters: number of grid points 150, number of restarts 100 and number of maximum expected clusters 30. Relationships between inferred clone populations, including phylogenetic analysis, was performed using PairTree73,74 with, apart from default parameters, a burn-in of 0.4 and the RPROP method for fitting subclones to trees. LCM samples were grouped into clusters on the basis of clone population frequencies with agglomerative clustering using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and complete linkage.

Phenotypic comparisons were performed using adjacent Visium sections. Visium H&E sections were manually annotated with adjacent LCM ROIs through alignment with overlaying adjacent sections. For any spot overlapping with a given LCM ROI, cell state abundances from cell2location were summed to generate ROI-specific cell state distributions. To quantify subclonal diversity in our LCM-WGS data, we used Rao’s Q with a distance matrix corresponding to the cumulative mutational differences between PairTree-defined phylogenetic lineages.

To assess the spatial distribution of multiome-inferred clones (that is, called from joint RNA and ATAC) in Visium data, we developed the SpaceTree model for joint clone and cell state deconvolution (Supplementary Computational Methods). This method follows a multi-step process, beginning with graph construction and followed by the use of a multi-task graph neural network to predict labels for each Visium spot. We used cell2location cell state mapping results to further evaluate the performance of SpaceTree, showing a high degree of concordance (Supplementary Computational Methods, section 2.5.3). Further orthogonal validation of this method was performed through comparison with LCM-derived analyses, including spatial clone cluster concordance (Extended Data Fig. 5g). SpaceTree was run on each Visium section, individually. Cluster concordance evaluation involved summarizing SpaceTree voxel probabilities that were histologically consistent with LCM ROIs into equivalent ROIs. These were hierarchically clustered using the same methodology as LCM samples (agglomerative clustering with Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and complete linkage). Tumour purity was calculated according to SpaceTree clone mapping results and represents the sum probability of all malignant clones in a given Visium spot. Shannon entropy was calculated on clone probabilities per spot and defined more generally as clonal entropy.

To compare SpaceTree results on GB data to alternative methods, we also predicted clonal abundances in Visium ST data by means of cell2location. To do so, we prepared tumour-specific snRNA-seq reference signatures as described above except that here we separately derived (1) concatenated clone and cell-type annotations (that is, joint cell state × clone) and (2) malignant subclone and TME cell state annotations (that is, malignant clone + TME). We used obtained references to run cell2location on Visium ST data combining samples from each tumour together with following parameters: detection_alpha=20, N_cells_per_location=30 and max_epochs=50000. Predicted clone abundances were compared to SpaceTree predictions; for option A, clone abundances were calculated as marginal sums of cell2location predictions.

Cell fate trajectories were inferred using a Bayesian generative model, cell2fate30 from snRNA-seq data with default parameters. This method infers temporal expression modules resulting from the factorization of RNA velocity data and then applies associated models of gene activation to infer cell-specific time along a trajectory. A key advantage of the Bayesian approach is that this method also returns posterior uncertainty estimates for every parameter, allowing for the evaluation of the confidence of cell state transitions. As input, we generated spliced and unspliced counts using STARsolo75 v.2.7.10a. Cell2fate analysis was run for each tumour clone separately using 10X reaction as a batch covariate, with 20 minimum shared genes and 3,000 HVGs in total. Individual cell2fate runs were evaluated first on the basis of maximum cell-specific time (maximum(Tc) > 20), and then flagged as high-quality if average cell state transition scores exceeded 0.25. To quantify cell state transition scores, posterior distributions of cell-specific times were compared between two cell states, with the score equalling to the percentage of cells in state B that have a greater inferred time than the 90th percentile of cells in state A (Extended Data Fig. 6b,e). For applications involving inter-clonal comparison across tumours, inferred times were minimum–maximum normalized. Before streamgraph visualization of cell states (Fig. 5a), cell counts binned across inferred time were smoothed using a Gaussian kernel to reduce the influence of sparsely populated bins (see Extended Data Fig. 7 for raw cell count histograms). Proliferative states were also merged into their corresponding cell class according to proliferative state subclustering results (Supplementary Fig. 6).

RNA velocity modules were subsequently hierarchically clustered using Jaccard distance of the top 200 genes in each module for the purposes of inter-clone module annotation. The ‘meta’-modules were then manually annotated according to (1) the identity of shared top genes, (2) the results of over-representation analysis of MSigDB signatures for this gene list and (3) enrichment of a given meta-module across malignant cell state annotations. Meta-module markers were compiled by taking the top 50 genes associated with each module, and sorting them by the degree of overlap across all modules in a given meta-module.

Using the standard cell2location workflow and the methods outlined in ref. 30, we also spatially mapped RNA velocity modules from cell2fate for a subset of tumours. Sections were selected if the multiome clone associated with the cell2fate run had a frequency of at least 30% in the associated site of sampling.

Count matrices from CPTAC76 and TCGA77,78 bulk RNA-seq samples were downloaded from the Genomic Data Commons. Samples were aggregated and log-normalized using Scanpy49. Relative expression scores were calculated for each meta-module on the basis of commonly observed top genes (n = 200) and samples were annotated as ‘enriched’ for a meta-module if the expression scores were greater than the 75th percentile. Kaplan–Meier analysis followed by the log rank test were performed using the Python package lifelines79 between meta-modules with early and late inferred times.

FASTQ files were processed using the Parse Biosciences Pipeline (v.1.5.0, Parse Biosciences). Following this, we applied quality control thresholds similar to our snRNA-seq dataset: total UMI > 1,000, gene counts more than 500 and expression less than 99th percentile. Whereas doublet detection was applied with Scrublet, scores remained low (median 0.04) and only 0.05% of the dataset flagged.

We applied a stringent transcript count filter to the cell-by-gene matrices (total counts less than 150). Next, we aimed at identifying GFP+ engrafted cells through the expression of eGFP. Owing to possible off-target probe binding activity, eGFP expression was low-count inflated. The distribution of log-transformed eGFP expression was biomodal, so we modelled cell-area-normalized eGFP counts using a two-component Gaussian mixture model to separate background and reporter-positive cells (Supplementary Fig. 23a). To ensure stringency, we estimated expression thresholds by calculating the minimum values among the eGFP-high component (posterior probability more than 0.9). This resulted in two primary thresholds: eGFP expression more than 32 counts and eGFP counts and/or area greater than 12%.

To harmonize our pre-engraftment Parse-seq data, Xenium GBO data and our cell atlas, we integrated all three modalities with scVI13, using sample, study, and cell-cycle phase as covariates (see section ‘snRNA-seq integration’ for hyperparameters). The model was trained without HVG selection due to the limited size of the Xenium panel. Next, we trained a scANVI model, initialized from this scVI model, to perform label transfer between our atlas and the other modalities. Efficacy of label transfer was then evaluated by means of marker gene inspection (Extended Data Fig. 9c).

To analyse spatial organization of malignant cell states along a periphery-core axis, we first generated a concave hull using Shapely with increased vertices (ratio 0.05) providing reference coordinates to the outer periphery of each organoid. We then calculated the Euclidean distance of each malignant cell to these coordinates. Downstream analysis of distance was minimum–maximum normalized within each organoid tissue section to facilitate cross-sample comparison (Fig. 5e).

We performed two sets of CCC analysis to predict TME-derived ligands targeting receptors expressed on malignant cell states across different dev-like versus gliosis-hypoxia microenvironments, focusing on myeloid signalling.

In step 1, we identified TME cell states spatially colocalized with dev-like versus gliosis-hypoxia malignant cell states from Visium ST data (Fig. 2). These included the entire GB TME including myeloid, vasculature and T cells, and was summarized across all tumours (Supplementary Table 11). We then selected malignant cell states present within dev-like versus gliosis-hypoxia microenvironments per each individual tumour (Supplementary Table 12).

In step 2, we performed CCC analysis using the LIANA+ framework (version 1.2.0)80, which integrates several computational methods and previous knowledge resources into a consensus-based approach to identify ligand–receptor interactions. We used the ‘consensus’ approach, combining five different methods implemented in LIANA+: CellPhoneDB, Connectome, log2FC, NATMI, SingleCellSignalR and CellChat. Consensus scores across these methods were calculated using the robust rank aggregation method81, with an ‘expr_prop’ (filtering criteria based on the proportion of cells per cell type) value of 0.05 and ‘n_perms’ (number of permutations) set to 1,000. Here TME cell states were set as the sources (ligands) and the malignant cell states as the targets (receptors). An expression threshold of 0.05 was applied to filter out ligands and receptors below this threshold. We applied the LIANA analysis separately to the dev-like and gliosis-hypoxia microenvironments.

In step 3, the resulting ligand–receptor pairs were analysed using the Tensor-cell2cell method82 (version 0.7.4), which used the LIANA+ results as input to identify shared CCC events across tumours as factors. This method automatically determined the number of factors using elbow analysis and used robust tensor factorization to ensure consistent results across donors. The main parameters and their values used in this analysis were as follows: ‘score_key = magnitude_rank’ (the LIANA+ score used for factor analysis), ‘tf_optimization = robust’ (the robustness setting for tensor factorization), ‘upper_rank = 15’ (the upper limit for the number of factors) and ‘tf_svd = numpy_svd’ (the singular value decomposition method used). We then identified factors shared across tumours that involve myeloid cells, specifically factors 1 and 2 from the dev-like analysis and factor 1 from the gliosis-hypoxia analysis (Supplementary Fig. 26).

In step 4, we sorted cell interactions associated with each factor on the basis of their loadings and selected interactions in the top 50th percentile. We then focused on ligands from the myeloid populations that are predicted to target receptors on their respective malignant cell states. We filtered the ligand–receptor pair list to ensure at least one relevant cell state showed mean gene expression 0.5 or greater and had a total expression fraction 0.1 or more for each associated gene pair. This resulted in the identification of 80 unique interactions in the dev-like microenvironments (involving 37 ligands), 35 unique interactions in the gliosis analysis (involving 18 ligands) and a total of 63 shared interactions shared across both microenvironments (involving 28 ligands).
