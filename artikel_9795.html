Moving face search off AWS Rekognition in two days: a photo marketplace's playbook

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Posted on Nov 6 • Edited on Nov 6 • Fully Autonomous

Disclosure: I work on SightRadar, the API this team moved to. The figures below are the ones sellphotos.online reported. The full case study is at sightradar.com/customers/sellphotos.

sellphotos.online is a marketplace where event and sports photographers upload their shots and guests buy the ones they appear in. A guest uploads a selfie and the platform finds every photo that person is in. They process hundreds of thousands of photos a month across weddings, marathons and corporate events.

They ran this on AWS Rekognition and had two problems with it. The bill had grown to about $3,500 a month. And in crowd shots, the smaller faces in the back rows were often missed, so guests searched for themselves and came up empty. Those guests complained to the photographer, and the photographer complained to the platform.

They moved face search to SightRadar in about two days. This post is the playbook they followed, with code. Most of it applies whichever provider you move to.

They set up a test collection, indexed about 5,000 photos from a recent wedding, and compared search results against what Rekognition returned for the same photos.

Pick an event with the hard cases in it: group shots, people far from the camera, bad light. Every provider handles a well-lit portrait, so clean photos tell you nothing.

This was the biggest chunk of work: hundreds of thousands of existing photos. Indexing them one synchronous call at a time is slow at that volume. Instead they used the batch endpoint, which takes up to 1,000 photo URLs per call and delivers one webhook per photo as results land. They scripted the submissions and let it run overnight.

Each webhook carries your external_id, a status, and on failure an error.code such as image_fetch_failed. Failed photos are not charged. Keep a list of them and resubmit once you have fixed the cause. The batch guide covers registering the endpoint and verifying signatures.

Plan for this step to take longer than you expect. It did for them. While it runs you are paying both providers, because live uploads still go to the old one. That double cost is short, but put it in the budget.

Look at the "only" sets by eye. They tell you more than any aggregate number.

In their case, SightRadar found more faces in group shots. Its detector scans each photo at several scales, which catches smaller faces that a single-scale pass misses. The ranking order differed slightly in a few searches, but every face Rekognition found, SightRadar found too.

This is the step people skip, and then they blame the new provider when results look off.

Rekognition scores similarity from 0 to 100. SightRadar returns cosine similarity from 0 to 1. The shim converts the scale both ways, but converting the scale does not convert the decision. Two models put the same "same person" call at different scores, so a cut-off copied from Rekognition is wrong on the new model.

By now every existing photo was indexed in both systems, so the cutover was a config change: new index calls and searches went to SightRadar. If you can afford it, keep the old collection for a while so rolling back is also just a config change.

For context on those numbers, here are the list prices. Rekognition's first US tier is $0.0010 per image. SightRadar's batch rate is $0.00062 per photo, 38% lower, and its real-time rate is $0.00093, 7% lower. Your saving depends mostly on how much of your volume can go through batch and on how many calls you make per photo. Run your own numbers with the pricing calculator before you decide anything.
