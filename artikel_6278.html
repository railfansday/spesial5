What happens when gamers swap their chairs for standing desks?

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Standing gave gamers an early boost in a soccer video game, but half ended their standing sessions early because of fatigue or leg discomfort.

Standing at a desk feels like the healthy choice, the thing you do instead of slumping in a chair all afternoon. Put a gamer at one for three hours and the picture splits into two halves.

Early on, players standing at a desk reported a better mood and took more shots in a soccer video game. At 15 minutes, they also handled distractions better on a test than they had before play.

By the one-hour mark, players were more fatigued and had more trouble ignoring distractions than when they sat. Five of the ten ended their standing sessions early because of fatigue or leg discomfort.

Takashi Matsui, an associate professor in the Institute of Health and Sport Sciences at the University of Tsukuba in Japan, ran that test and a second one.

In the second, a chair tilted slightly forward gave players some of the same early lift, and none of them quit.

After three hours of reclined play, accuracy on the arrow test hadn’t declined significantly from the start.

The chairs and desk came from a furniture maker, and so did part of the funding. That’s part of the story, and it’s told below.

Matsui’s team recruited 25 men between 18 and 35 who played video games regularly but never competed.

Ten of them took part in the first experiment and played the soccer game eFootball 2022 for up to three hours on two separate days.

One day they stood at a height-adjustable desk, and the other they sat in a gaming chair, in random order.

Every so often the players stopped for tests, run in the free psychology software PsychoPy. The one that moved was a simple arrows task: pick the direction of a central arrow while ignoring the arrows beside it.

Fifteen minutes into standing play, the players handled that distraction faster than before they started.

Standing players also reported more energy and more pleasure through the first hour. In the game, between 15 and 60 minutes, they took more shots, put more of them on target, and shot more accurately.

Their hearts beat faster the whole time, their pupils were wider, and their saliva carried more cortisol and testosterone for the first half hour.

By the one-hour mark the arrows test had flipped. Standing players were now slower to shake off the distraction than they were seated, and they reported more fatigue.

Five of the ten stopped standing before the three hours were up, on average after about 103 minutes.

Standing sessions ran about 142 minutes on average against the full 180 seated.

Enjoyment was lower at the end of standing play than seated play, and the players who quit standing conceded more goals in their last match than players who finished.

The authors suggested that the effort of standing may have contributed to the later fatigue and difficulty ignoring distractions.

For the second experiment, the team seated 15 different men in the same chair, set two ways: tilted 10 degrees forward, or reclined 23 degrees.

Each man played for three hours in both positions on separate days.

No one quit either seated session. Leaning forward, players reported more energy through the first two hours and, in the first half hour, took more shots, more shots on target, and more passes than when reclined.

At 60 minutes they also handled the arrows distraction faster.

Then the forward lean ran out. By three hours, those players were less accurate on the arrows test than at the start, while accuracy in the reclined position showed no significant decline from the start.

Reclining had its own signature in the game, too, with more interceptions.

Both the desk and the gaming chair were made by Okamura, a Japanese office furniture company.

Okamura helped fund the study through a contract research grant to Matsui, and four of its employees are co-authors.

The researchers noted that the funders had no role in the design, the analysis, or the decision to publish.

Players came from the university and from gaming communities in Tokyo’s Akihabara district.

Each was paid 5,000 yen for about five hours in the lab, which was kept at 73°F (23°C). They drank water in small amounts and skipped caffeine and alcohol beforehand.

Every arrows test was taken sitting in one standard position, whatever posture the player had been gaming in.

Two other distraction tests, one with color words and one with shapes appearing on the wrong side, showed no posture effect at all.

The authors list the limits plainly. Twenty-five people is a small group for the number of things measured, the study was not registered in advance, and the players were young men playing one soccer game.

Whether any of this holds for women, older players, shooters, or an office job is untested.

The physiology is a sketch, not a mechanism. The team measured heart rate, hormones, blood sugar, and pupils, but not brain or muscle activity, so why standing or leaning helped early and hurt later isn’t settled.

The practical question is one the team didn’t test. If leaning forward helps early and reclining helps late, switching between them during a long session might capture both.

Whether it does, and how often a player should switch, is unknown, and the authors name self-chosen posture changes during real play as the next thing to measure.

The full study was published in the journal Computers in Human Behavior Reports.
