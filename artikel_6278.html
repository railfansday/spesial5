What makes a hug truly memorable has a lot to do with empathy and how you handle relationships

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

A survey of 15,049 adults shows most people remember hugs for comfort and support. Empathy, attachment patterns, and hugging habits also play a role.

We tend to think of a hug as a universal source of warmth, yet nearly three in ten adults in a large survey could not recall one that had left a positive mark.

When a hug did stick in someone’s memory, it was much more likely to be for comfort, support, or understanding than for romance.

Whether someone could remember a hug at all came down to a few key traits. The top three were how much empathy they felt, how they handled closeness, and whether they usually hugged their friends hello.

Anna Lena Dueren of the University of Bristol‘s School of Psychological Science led the analysis of 15,049 adults in the U.K. Their answers came from the Touch Test, a national survey on touch that BBC Radio 4 promoted in early 2020.

The study is a snapshot of who remembers what. It can show which traits go together with a hug memory, not what causes one.

In an interview with Earth.com, Dueren put the main finding simply. “Most participants remembered hugs because they conveyed comfort, understanding, or support,” she said.

To her knowledge, she added, it’s the first large study to ask people why a hug stayed with them.

The question was plain: is there a hug you remember because of its positive emotional effect on you? Of the 15,046 people who answered, 10,457 said yes, or 69.5 percent.

People who said yes then picked the single best reason from a short list. Among the 9,703 who chose a listed reason, about a third picked comfort, 29 percent said the hug conveyed understanding, and 20 percent said support.

Comfort, understanding, and support together cover more than four in five of the hugs people kept. Just 147 people, or 1.5 percent of the group, said the hug was memorable because it was physically arousing.

Earlier studies had linked daily hugs to lower stress and better mood, and these answers fit.

Men and women were about equally likely to remember a hug at all. The reasons they gave, though, differed in a small but consistent way.

Among men, 3.9 percent chose arousal as the reason, compared with 0.8 percent of women. Men were also more likely to pick friendliness, 12.3 percent, in comparison to 4.8 percent of women.

Women chose comfort more often, at 34.6 percent to men’s 25.3 percent, and understanding more often too. The team suggested that the nurturing side of a hug may count for more among women.

Dueren’s team noted that only 2,197 men gave a reason, against 7,506 women, so they advise reading the gender differences with caution.

Dueren’s team, which includes researchers at UCL, the University of Sussex, and the University of Greenwich, then checked which traits went with keeping an emotional memory of a hug.

The strongest link was a habit: people who always greet friends and family with a hug were more likely to recall one.

Disliking the feel of being hugged went the other way, and so did finding it hard to know how firmly to hug someone. Asked by Earth.com what someone who dislikes hugging might take from the work, Dueren pointed to the size of the gap.

“In our study, people who disliked the feeling of being hugged were slightly less likely to recall a hug that had a positive emotional effect on them than those who did not dislike it,” she said. “The findings highlight that hugging experiences are not equally remembered by everyone.”

Empathy mattered too, whether measured as reading other people’s feelings or as feeling them alongside someone. Higher scores went with higher odds of remembering a hug.

So did attachment style, how a person relates to those closest to them. Insecure attachment comes in two forms.

People who feel distress at separation, the anxious form, were more likely to hold a hug in memory. People who prize independence in relationships, the avoidant form, were less likely to.

All of these effects were small, and together the traits explained only 10 to 14 percent of the variation in who remembered a hug.

Dueren’s team expected that people who are less satisfied with their bodies would be more likely to remember a hug for the comfort or support it gave. That expectation failed.

A person’s satisfaction with their own body did not influence whether they remembered a hug, or why. However, there was a measure that did.

The team built a five-item scale of how much a person’s sense of their body runs through touch. One item asked whether they choose clothes partly for how the fabric feels on the skin.

People who scored higher on it were more likely to remember a hug, and to remember it for understanding or arousal rather than comfort. The scale is new, and the team called those results preliminary.

Being tucked in with a hug and a kiss as a child had no bearing on whether a person remembered a hug. Neither did how often that person gets hugged day to day.

The people who answered chose to take part and were not paid, so they may care about touch more than average. Three-quarters were women, and all lived in the U.K., where attitudes to touch differ from those elsewhere.

The survey didn’t ask who gave the remembered hug or where it happened, so it can’t say whether comfort from a partner is remembered differently from comfort given by a friend.

Answers were collected between January and March 2020, and fewer than 1 percent arrived after the U.K.’s first lockdown began. So this is a record of hugging before the pandemic changed it.

Dueren told Earth.com that she wants to pursue why the same hug feels so different to different people. “The same type of touch can feel pleasant for one person and uncomfortable to another,” she said. “I would like to better understand factors that contribute to these differences in experience.”

Empathetic people may store up hugs, or memorable hugs may make people more empathetic, and this survey can’t tell which. Settling that would mean following the same people over years, and that study has not yet been run.

The full study was published in the journal PLOS One.
