<b><strong>Why Sleep Consistency May Be the Missing Piece When It Comes to Heart Health</strong></b>

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

It’s not just about getting 7 to 9 hours anymore.

You probably already know that getting seven to nine hours of sleep each night is one of the best things you can do for your health and wellbeing. But what happens if you’re consistently hitting that goal while going to bed at different times? Think 10 p.m. on Monday, midnight on Wednesday, and well past 1 a.m. on Saturday because it’s the weekend, after all.

As it turns out, your heart might not be too happy about that. New research suggests that sleep consistency—which includes both when you go to bed and wake up, and how much sleep you get from night to night—could play an important role in cardiovascular health. And, while getting the recommended amount of shut-eye still matters, researchers are finding that how much your sleep fluctuates may deserve some attention, too.

Before you start stressing over every late night or weekend sleep-in, though, there's some important context to understand. Here's what the research actually found, why your body seems to care so much about routine, and how consistent your sleep schedule really needs to be.

Meet the experts: Nguyen Minh Tuan Viet, MD, PhD, is a neuroscientist and postdoctoral research fellow at Boston Children's Hospital and Harvard Medical School and lead researcher of the new study. Nour Makarem, PhD, is a cardiovascular epidemiologist in the department of epidemiology at Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health. Marie-Pierre St-Onge, PhD, is a professor of nutritional medicine and director of the Center of Excellence for Sleep & Circadian Research at Columbia University Irving Medical Center.

For the new study published in Circulation: Cardiovascular Quality and Outcomes, researchers analyzed more than one million nights of sleep data from 10,362 adults participating in the National Institutes of Health's All of Us Research Program, an initiative that aims to collect data from over one million Americans and create the country’s largest biomedical database.

Unlike previous studies that only looked at people's sleep habits for about a week, this one used about six months of Fitbit data to see how participants' bedtimes, wake-up times, and total sleep duration changed from night to night. They then compared those patterns with participants’ medical records and tracked their health for nearly four years.

The findings were pretty eye-opening: For every additional 29 minutes of variation in how long someone slept from night to night, their relative risk of serious heart problems, including heart attacks, heart failure, heart muscle disease, and certain strokes, was 24 percent higher. To be clear, that doesn't mean going to bed 29 minutes later than usual on a given night increases your heart disease risk by 24 percent. The researchers were looking at a statistical measure of how much someone's total sleep duration varied over time, rather than the consequences of one late bedtime. As of now, all this information does it point to a potential link between sleep schedule variation and heart disease.

Researchers also found that greater night-to-night fluctuations in bedtime and wake-up times were associated with a 23 percent and 20 percent higher relative risk of cardiovascular problems, respectively. This means that both the consistency of your sleep schedule and the consistency of the duration of that sleep may play a role in heart health.

There are a few things worth keeping in mind, though. For starters, this study found a connection between inconsistent sleep and heart problems, but it couldn't prove that irregular sleep is what causes those problems. In fact, Viet points out that the relationship could work in the opposite direction too (i.e. early illness might actually make someone’s sleep more irregular). Plus, most participants were women and white, so we can't necessarily assume the findings apply to everyone. Still, the results add to growing evidence that sleep consistency deserves a place in the heart-health conversation.

Your body has its own internal clock, known as your circadian rhythm, which determines when you're tired, when you're wide awake, and even how bodily functions like blood pressure and heart rate change throughout the day. And, it works at its best when it has a reasonably predictable schedule to follow. When you're constantly changing when you sleep and wake, you may disrupt that rhythm, a phenomenon known as circadian misalignment, explains Nour Makarem, PhD, a cardiovascular epidemiologist in the department of epidemiology at Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health.

That disruption can impact some pretty important cardiovascular processes, like how your body regulates blood pressure. Under normal circumstances, blood pressure usually drops by about 10 to 20 percent while you're sleeping to give your cardiovascular system a period of rest, but research by Makarem suggests that irregular sleep patterns may be associated with higher blood pressure and an increased risk of hypertension. “This is problematic because higher nocturnal blood pressure and having a non-dipping profile (blood pressure that does not dip or dip enough during sleep) are associated with higher risk for heart disease and stroke, the leading causes of death in women,” she says.

Your sleep schedule may also interact with other daily rhythms, including when you eat and exercise. These behaviors help regulate biological clocks throughout your body, including those involved in metabolism, Makarem says. When those clocks fall out of sync, processes like blood sugar regulation may be affected, too. In fact, research has linked irregular sleep patterns with poorer glucose regulation and a higher risk of metabolic issues.

Research is still emerging, but scientists are noticing some important links between sleep consistency and heart health. A 2025 study published in the Journal of Epidemiology & Community Health followed more than 72,000 adults and found that people with irregular sleep patterns had a higher risk of major cardiovascular events, including heart attacks and strokes. Another study published in Sleep in 2024 analyzed sleep patterns in more than 60,000 adults and found that sleep regularity was a stronger predictor of mortality risk than sleep duration in that population.

So, does this mean having a consistent bed and rising time matters more than getting eight hours? Not necessarily. "The evidence for sleep regularity is rather nascent relative to that for sleep duration," says Marie-Pierre St-Onge, PhD, a professor of nutritional medicine and director of the Center of Excellence for Sleep & Circadian Research at Columbia University Irving Medical Center. She explains that while several studies have made the connection between irregular sleep and poorer health outcomes, more research needs to be done to determine whether making someone's sleep schedule more consistent can actually lower their cardiovascular risk. And, we do have plenty of evidence that getting enough sleep has positive affects on just about every system in the body, from brain function to muscle recovery.

Overall, there are still things researchers are learning about sleep consistency and heart health, but it’s safe to start thinking of it as another piece of the puzzle, alongside getting enough hours and making sure those hours are restorative.

Here's the million-dollar question: Does protecting your heart mean going to bed at exactly 10:30 p.m. every single night, no exceptions? Thankfully, no.

Ultimately, you’ve got some wiggle room. "In general, we would recommend that individuals try not to deviate by 60 min up or down from their average bedtime or sleep duration," says St-Onge, but the smaller the gap, the better.

Think of that as a target, not a hard-and-fast rule. An occasional late night or weekend sleep-in isn’t a reason to panic. What matters more is establishing a reasonably consistent pattern over a period of time rather than expecting yourself to follow a perfect schedule every single night.

If you've had a particularly short night, St-Onge recommends trying to go to bed a little earlier and waking up a little later when your schedule allows, rather than trying to make up for all that lost sleep by sleeping in for hours. Most importantly, don't sweat it. "If one night is off, it's not all doom and gloom; you get right back on schedule the following night," says St-Onge.

Here are a few small changes experts recommend to improve your sleep routine without completely overhauling your lifestyle.

Before committing to being in bed every night by 9 p.m., consider what will actually make sense for your schedule. Earlier isn't automatically better. What's important is getting adequate sleep on a schedule that works with your body's natural rhythm and everyday responsibilities, explains St-Onge.

If your current bedtime is midnight but you'd ideally like to be asleep by 11, there's no need to make that change all at once. Makarem recommends gradually shifting your bedtime earlier by about 10 minutes each day until you reach your target. The idea is to give your body time to adjust rather than suddenly expecting yourself to fall asleep an hour earlier than usual.

It's tempting to stay up late on Friday and Saturday nights and make up for it by sleeping well into the morning, but even those brief changes can leave you feeling out of sync by the time Monday rolls around. If possible, St-Onge recommends keeping your weekend bedtimes within about an hour of your usual routine.

If you wear an Apple Watch or Fitbit to bed, you know how easy it is to get caught up in the numbers. One morning you're celebrating a great sleep score, and the next you're wondering what went wrong because your tracker says you barely got any deep sleep.

While these devices can be helpful for spotting patterns, the last thing you want is to start losing sleep over your sleep. Makarem recommends using trackers to keep tabs on your habits or remind you when it's time for bed, but only if they're actually helping. If checking your sleep stats is making you anxious, it's fine to ditch the data and simply pay attention to when you're going to bed and waking up.

And remember, you don't have to get your sleep schedule exactly right every single night to do something good for your heart. Small changes that you can actually stick with are worth more than chasing a perfect score. As Makarem puts it, "Progress is better than perfect."
