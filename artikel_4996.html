Vitamin C May Lower Your Risk of Cancer from Certain Processed Foods

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Here’s what experts say about the “promising” new study results.

While diving into a plate of bacon or noshing on some salami is always tasty, there’s been growing concern that compounds found in cured meats may raise the risk of cancer. Specifically, once they hit your stomach, nitrates and nitrites found in these foods can spark a chain reaction called nitrosation that allows carcinogens to develop. This isn’t just a meat-eating issue either: fruits and vegetables that have grown in areas affected by pollution can also lead to this chain reaction.

Now, new research published in the Journal of Theoretical Biology suggests a possible solution: Take a carefully timed vitamin C supplement. It can cut this chain reaction and, in theory, lower the odds that carcinogens will develop after eating certain foods linked to this reaction, according to the findings.

Researchers stress that this doesn’t mean you should pop a vitamin C every time you have bacon. But the findings raise a lot of questions about why this may work and how to actually put it into practice in a safe way. Here’s what the researcher behind the study and dietitians want you to keep in mind.

Meet the experts: Gordon R. McNicol, PhD, lead study author and postdoctoral scholar in the Department of Applied Mathematics at the University of Waterloo; Jessica Cording, MS, RD, registered dietitian and author of The Little Book of Game-Changers; and Keri Gans, MS, RD, registered dietitian and author of The Small Change Diet.

For the study, researchers developed a mathematical model that simulates how nitrates and nitrites travel through the body, and where they undergo chemical changes. The model looked at a range of areas, including the salivary glands, stomach, small intestine, and plasma (i.e. the liquid part of your blood). Researchers discovered that having vitamin C alongside foods that contain nitrates and nitrites may halt chain reactions that happen in the body, including nitrosation, which makes substances that are linked to cancer development.

The researchers also looked at whether taking vitamin C after each meal could help, and found that it may reduce the formation of nitrosation products linked with cancer risk. The boost was most meaningful when people had foods that contained nitrates and nitrites, like salami, bacon, and other processed meats.

The researchers also theorized that this may explain why certain types of produce that happen to contain both vitamin C and nitrates (like spinach) don’t seem to increase cancer risk, while others do.

It’s important to stress this: The study was based on a mathematical model and was not a clinical trial or data analysis. Because of that, it's hard to draw too many conclusions right now.

Still, this link isn’t out of nowhere. “Under acidic conditions in the stomach, nitrites can undergo chemical reactions that lead to the formation of potentially harmful N-nitroso compounds,” says Gordon R. McNicol, PhD, lead study author and postdoctoral scholar in the Department of Applied Mathematics at the University of Waterloo. “Vitamin C can interfere with these reactions, reducing the formation of these compounds.”

Why vitamin C? “Vitamin C is an antioxidant,” points out Jessica Cording, MS, RD, registered dietitian and author of The Little Book of Game-Changers. Meaning, it helps to neutralize unstable molecules in the body—and may do the same for nitrosation.

But the effectiveness of vitamin C depends on when it’s in the stomach compared to when these reactions happen, McNicol adds. “Our simulations suggest that taking vitamin C shortly after meals could be particularly effective, as this allows vitamin C to overlap more closely with periods when nitrosation would otherwise be relatively high,” he says.

Experts stress that it’s important to keep the study in perspective. “While the findings are promising, more research is needed to determine whether taking vitamin C after meals actually reduces cancer risk,” says Keri Gans, MS, RD, registered dietitian and author of The Small Change Diet.

Cording agrees, stressing that while the findings are “promising,” they’re still just based on a mathematical model at this point. “That said, getting enough vitamin C and other antioxidants, alongside nutrients like fiber and other beneficial plant compounds, has been associated with reduced risk of disease and with overall immune system function,” Cording says. “There are lots of great reasons to include vitamin-C rich fruits and vegetables throughout the day.”

There are other things to consider, like practicality and safety. “Our research does not establish that taking vitamin C supplements after every meal is necessary or advisable,” McNicol says. “Although our simulations suggest that dividing a fixed daily supplement across meals may be more effective than taking the same amount at once, further experimental and clinical studies are needed to determine whether these strategies are effective and safe in people.”

High doses of vitamin C from supplements can also lead to GI discomfort, including nausea and diarrhea, Gans points out.

For now, this is more of an interesting finding vs. an actual dietary recommendation. “Until we have more research, there's no reason to recommend intentionally adding extra vitamin C, whether from food or supplements, after every meal specifically to reduce cancer risk,” Gans says.
