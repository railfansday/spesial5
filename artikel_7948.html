Two Ginger Brothers Became the First Documented Cases of Marfan Syndrome in Cats

Cats come in all shapes and sizes. Some have legs that seem a little too long for their bodies, others have ears big enough to pick up satellite signals, and a few look like they haven't quite grown into themselves yet.

But when Gary and Shaggy were kittens, their disproportionately long limbs turned out to be more than an unusual-looking but perfectly charming physical trait. The two brothers would eventually become the first domestic cats confirmed to have Marfan syndrome through genetic testing; not exactly what any cat owner would expect when bringing home a pair of kittens.

As the cats grew, veterinarians identified abnormalities affecting their eyes and enlargement of the aorta, the major blood vessel that carries blood away from the heart. Researchers at Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine and the Baker Institute for Animal Health worked with veterinary and genetic specialists to investigate what was going on.

Their long limbs were one clue. The eye abnormalities and enlarged aortas added two more. Slowly, the pieces of the puzzle began to fit together, pointing researchers toward Marfan syndrome, a rare inherited disorder far better known in human medicine than in veterinary practice. Genetic testing confirmed the connection, uncovering what made these two brothers so unusual.

Marfan syndrome affects connective tissue, the body's natural support system. This tissue helps give structure and strength to the bones, ligaments, skin, eyes, and blood vessels. When something goes wrong with this system, the effects can show up in several different ways.

In humans, the condition often causes unusually long limbs and fingers, a tall, slender build, eye abnormalities, and potentially serious cardiovascular complications.

Abraham Lincoln was famously tall and lanky, which has led to speculation that he may have had Marfan syndrome. No one can say for sure, though. Turns out, even Honest Abe left a few medical mysteries behind!

For Olympic volleyball player Flo Hyman, long limbs gave her an edge on the court. However, her life was tragically cut short, dying at age 31 from cardiac complications from Marfan syndrome.

One of the genes involved in Marfan syndrome is called FBN1. It contains the instructions for producing fibrillin-1, a protein that helps maintain the structure and function of connective tissues throughout the body.

When that gene doesn't work as it should, the effects can extend well beyond a person's or animal's appearance. As the gorgeous ginger duo, Gary and Shaggy, began showing signs similar to those seen in humans with Marfan syndrome, their veterinary team started to wonder if cats could also suffer from the same condition.

Genetic sequencing revealed that Gary and Shaggy both carried two altered copies of FBN1. In other words, they had inherited the mutation from both parents.

In humans, Marfan syndrome typically develops when a person inherits one disease-causing copy of the gene. Having two altered copies is exceptionally rare and can be associated with more severe disease.

So the brothers presented researchers with an interesting puzzle.

Their particular genetic variant didn't completely prevent the gene from functioning. Instead, it partially disrupted how the gene's instructions were processed, allowing some normal fibrillin-1 function to remain, which may explain why Gary and Shaggy survived into adulthood despite carrying two copies of the variant.

Their genetic instructions weren't simply switched on or off; it was something in between, which may help explain why the brothers developed the way they did.

Genes rarely behave like a tidy set of instructions. The effects of a genetic change depend on the specific variant involved and how it affects the resulting protein. Sometimes, a seemingly insignificant variation can have catastrophic consequences, altering the way the body functions.

The case of Gary and Shaggy is undeniably rare, and Marfan syndrome is unlikely to find its way onto the list of common feline diseases. However, in diagnosing and documenting the condition, veterinarians have something they didn't have before: a confirmed case of feline Marfan syndrome to help recognize, investigate, and compare with future cases.

These findings may help veterinary teams identify similar cases and better understand how particular FBN1 variants affect cats. They also demonstrate the value of bringing clinicians, genetic researchers, and pet parents together when a case doesn't fit neatly into an existing diagnosis.

Gary and Shaggy's shared genetic variant offered researchers a valuable opportunity to investigate the relationship between their physical features and the underlying condition.

Their owner, Nicole Desmond, also contributed to this process, and Cornell has highlighted the case as an example of how pet parents can collaborate with veterinary and genetic experts to advance knowledge that may eventually benefit other animals.

Even more importantly, Nicole loved the unique feline brothers with her whole heart. Vets did not expect either cat to survive for long, but they defied the odds. Gary was 6 years old when he passed away in July 2024, and in November 2025, at the incredible age of 7, Shaggy joined his brother across the rainbow bridge.

And that's one of the interesting things about veterinary medicine. Not every discovery starts with a large clinical trial or a disease affecting thousands of patients. Sometimes, it begins with two unusual kittens and a veterinary team willing to keep asking questions.
