When I thought of Goodwill, I pictured used clothing. Then I toured its Miami-Dade facility and learned so much more.

Goodwill Industries of South Florida helps people with disabilities and other employment barriers gain greater independence through job training, employment, and placement services. According to president and CEO David Landsberg, it is Florida’s largest employer of people with disabilities and the 18th-largest employer in South Florida.

Its businesses include manufacturing military and other apparel, providing custodial services to federal workplaces, and operating a commercial laundry. They also provide career training in fields such as HVAC and solar installation and place graduates in community positions.

I was particularly moved by the employees making American flags for veterans’ burials. These flags will cover caskets for our veterans. I watched a blind man sew with remarkable precision and speed.

I saw enthusiasm and pride throughout the tour, especially during a Spirit of Goodwill Band rehearsal. The musicians, who work at Goodwill and have a variety of disabilities, were superb. They are given time during the workweek to practice, and they perform at community functions, including the organization’s fundraising gala.

Many of us carry disability insurance in case illness or injury prevents us from working. But what about someone born with a disability, or who developed one in childhood, who has never had the opportunity to work?

The World Health Organization’s 2022 guidelines recognize meaningful work as a source of confidence, accomplishment, and inclusion. Work gives our days structure, with tasks to complete and opportunities to grow. A conversation over lunch or someone asking how our weekend went may seem trivial until those interactions are missing, as many of us discovered during the pandemic.

As a parent who has spent decades advocating for my daughter and worrying about her future, I know how deeply reassuring it is to see an adult child take on work responsibilities and form relationships outside the family.

The Americans with Disabilities Act protects equal opportunity among qualified people with disabilities. Employers with 15 or more employees should not discriminate and should provide reasonable accommodations if the applicant can meet the job's qualifications. Yet, too many are unemployed.

According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, in 2025, the unemployment rate among people with disabilities ages 16 and older was 8.3 percent, compared with 4.1 percent among those without disabilities. Among people ages 16 to 64 with a disability, just 41.8 percent were working or looking for work in 2025.

Eileen Grubba, a disability activist, award-winning actress, writer, and producer, shared with me her valuable perspective: “Those with disabilities often have the most to offer. They have survived the unthinkable. We rarely consider what they handle every single day, and what they’ve learned because of it. We need to open our minds and turn to those who know best how to adapt, adjust, and overcome, simply because they’ve been overcoming all their lives."

Goodwill’s founder, Edgar J. Helms, expressed the organization’s guiding idea: “Not charity, but a chance.”

I left the tour thinking how little I knew about workplace accommodations and how heartwarming it is to see others succeed.

You might contact your local Goodwill or another employment organization and ask whether you can visit. A lot can be achieved through training, time, and connection.
