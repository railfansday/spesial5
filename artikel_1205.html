Intra-stellar: a star inside a giant star

Jason Nordhaus is at the Center for Computational Relativity and Gravitation and the National Technical Institute for the Deaf, Rochester Institute of Technology, Rochester, New York 14623, USA.

Towards the end of their lives, stars experience a drastic change in their structure as they swell and enter what is known as their giant phase. Because most stars are thought to host orbiting companions (such as planets, other stars or black holes), interesting interactions can happen as the giant host expands. The most striking of these occur when a companion star is engulfed by a giant: astronomers refer to this as the common-envelope phase1. Despite theoretical expectations that common envelopes should be widespread in stellar evolution, they have been extraordinarily difficult to observe. Writing in Nature, however, Karambelkar et al.2 report the first direct detection of a star inside a giant star.

To give a sense of the scale of an expanding star in its giant phase, if the object were the Sun, its surface would grow outwards to, and possibly beyond, Earth’s orbit — reaching hundreds of times its previous size. This kind of expansion can lead to an orbiting companion being swallowed. In these common-envelope phases, the companion and the core of the giant star orbit each other inside the giant’s envelope (its outer layer of gas and plasma)1.

As it orbits inside this envelope, the companion star experiences a drag that causes it to migrate rapidly towards the giant’s core in a spiral pattern (Fig. 1a). During this ‘inspiral’, energy and angular momentum are transferred from the orbit to the envelope. The common-envelope phase can then progress in one of two ways3: either the envelope is blown off, leaving a tight binary system in a short-period orbit, or the companion is destroyed inside the envelope, leaving a giant star with a substantially altered evolution.

Figure 1 | Envelope evolution. When the surface of a giant star expands, it can engulf a companion star such that its core and the companion have a common gas envelope. a, The companion’s orbit will decay and spiral inwards, potentially resulting in the destruction of the star when it nears the giant’s core (as shown), or in the eruption of the envelope (not shown). Karambelkar et al.2 have now identified the spiralling-in and destruction of a 0.7-solar-mass companion star inside a 7-solar-mass giant star in the Andromeda galaxy, during the common-envelope phase (the companion star is not shown to scale; it would be much smaller than is shown). b, Most of the giant star is vigorously convective, and the authors’ observations confirm theoretical predictions5,7–9 about the effects of convection on the common-envelope phase.

The shrinking of the orbit, which is accompanied by a reduction in the period from years to hours or days during the common-envelope phase, is thought to be the main mechanism by which short-period binaries are produced in the Universe. This makes understanding the physics of common envelopes essential for many subfields of astrophysics, covering phenomena such as the mergers of black holes and neutron stars — accompanied by the production of gravitational waves — and the orbits of planets in long-lived habitable zones around white dwarf stars4.

Common envelopes are hard to observe because the timescales involved are short (months to years) and the rates at which they form are low (just a few stars exist in this phase at any given time in a galaxy such as the Milky Way)5. But Karambelkar et al. now report the first direct detection of a common envelope.

They made the observation in WTP19aalzlk — a luminous red giant in the Andromeda galaxy that has a mass seven times that of the Sun — over a period of eight years. In 2018, the star started to exhibit a gradual increase in luminosity that continued for more than seven years, culminating in an eruption that ejected around 10% of the giant’s envelope. Previous long-term monitoring had detected no intrinsic variability, and extensive spectroscopic observations before and after the eruption suggest that the system originated from the orbital decay of a 0.7-solar-mass star inside the envelope of the giant. The eruption and partial ejection of the envelope was probably caused by energy released when gravitational forces tore apart the star as it approached the core of the giant6.

Following this initial discovery, astronomers are learning much about the physics of common envelopes. The motions of gas inside giant stars are turbulent, like they would be in a pot of boiling water (Fig. 1b). Vigorous convective zones extend from the surface deep into the star’s interior; for a typical giant, all but 0.0001% of the volume is turbulent.

Convection is interesting in that it distributes and transports energy outwards in stars. If the orbit of an engulfed star decays slowly compared with the rate at which energy is carried to the surface through convection, then the stellar surface is expected to be quasi-static and the extra energy released as the orbit decays should emerge as light5. (In non-convective common envelopes, by contrast, the stellar surface expands immediately and the light output peaks within a few months5, effectively masking what is occurring in the interior.) The effects of convection on common envelopes — including the emergence of a radiatively efficient phase, a substantial lengthening of light curves (meaning that graphs of stellar brightness seem to be stretched out temporally) and an increase in detection times — were theoretically predicted5,7–9, and are now confirmed by Karambelkar and colleagues’ observations.

The existence of a long-lived radiatively efficient phase also provides a fresh way to probe the interiors of giants. Because stars are highly opaque, it is not possible to directly examine the structure beneath the photosphere (the star’s ‘surface’). But in a common envelope, the energy released through frictional drag as the orbit decays depends on local properties, such as the density of the gas. Convected gas carries information about those properties to the surface, which means that details of the orbital-decay process are encoded in the light emitted from the system.

Karambelkar and co-workers’ discovery has come at a remarkable time. Upcoming wide-field surveys will take deep snapshots of the sky at repeated intervals, generating unprecedented amounts of time-series data. Facilities such as the Vera Rubin Observatory in Chile and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope should therefore routinely discover common envelopes in hundreds of nearby galaxies. This crucial and elusive phase in the evolution of stars will be mapped and unveiled, not because stars are placid, but precisely because they are turbulent.
