Paleontologists discover “one-in-a-million” fossil with 450-million-year-old soft tissue

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

A fossil dating back more than 450 million years has revealed something scientists almost never find: delicate soft tissues from an animal that lived hundreds of millions of years before dinosaurs appeared. The extraordinarily rare discovery is giving researchers a new understanding of how some of Earth's earliest marine animals lived, fed, and evolved.

The fossil belongs to an ancient crinoid, a marine animal related to modern starfish. Crinoids often resemble underwater flowers, with branching arms extending from a central body. More than 450 million years ago, these unusual creatures were among the animals thriving in Earth's earliest coral reefs, long before plants and animals had become widespread on land.

Although scientists have discovered millions of crinoid fossils, nearly all preserve only hard skeletal structures. Delicate body parts usually decay long before fossilization can occur, leaving major gaps in our understanding of how these ancient creatures functioned.

Now, paleontologists at the University of Oklahoma have identified an exceptionally rare specimen that preserves soft tissue structures, providing an extraordinary opportunity to investigate life in Earth's ancient oceans.

"After an animal dies, soft tissues like skin, eyes, or internal organs are the first things to decay," said Dr. Lena Cole, OU paleontologist and assistant curator of invertebrate paleontology at the Sam Noble Oklahoma Museum of Natural History. "Most fossils are only made up of hard parts like bones, teeth, or shells. Soft tissues are only preserved when the environment acts almost like a natural refrigerator or vacuum‑sealer -- conditions that are incredibly rare."

A 450 Million-Year-Old Fossil With Exceptionally Rare Soft Tissue

The remarkable specimen belongs to Dendrocrinus simcoensis, an extinct species of crinoid. Its preserved tube feet represent only the second known example of fossilized soft tissue in crinoids and the oldest such discovery ever documented.

Tube feet are small, flexible structures found in echinoderms, a group of marine animals that includes starfish, sea urchins, and crinoids. Because these structures are soft and easily destroyed after death, finding evidence of them in fossils is extraordinarily unusual.

"Preservation like this is truly one in a million," Cole added. "Crinoid fossils number in the millions, and this is only the second time soft tissues have ever been found."

The age of the specimen makes the discovery even more remarkable. These delicate structures have been preserved in the fossil record for nearly half a billion years, offering direct evidence of anatomy that would otherwise have disappeared.

"It's incredible these soft tissues have survived more than 450 million years," said Dr. David Wright, another OU paleontologist and assistant curator of invertebrate paleontology at the Sam Noble Oklahoma Museum of Natural History, who co‑authored the study with Cole. "For reference, these soft tissues are more than 200 million years older than the oldest dinosaur."

Ancient Feeding Structures Reveal Evolutionary Secrets

The fossil's preserved tube feet are especially valuable because they provide clues about how crinoids obtained food and interacted with their surroundings.

Unlike the teeth and jaws that many familiar animals use to eat, crinoids rely on specialized structures to capture food particles from seawater. Their tube feet play an important role in feeding and in how these animals interact with ocean currents.

The size, arrangement, and shape of these structures can differ between species, reflecting adaptations to particular habitats and feeding methods. By studying those differences, paleontologists can reconstruct aspects of an extinct animal's lifestyle, even when no living examples of that species exist.

"Since crinoid tube feet are used for feeding, you can think of them in a similar way to how we think about teeth in mammals," Wright explained. "Differences in their structure tell us about what kinds of environments a species lived in and how it fed."

Researchers also compared the ancient fossil with crinoids living in today's oceans. Those comparisons revealed notable anatomical differences, suggesting that the feeding structures of these animals have undergone substantial evolutionary changes over hundreds of millions of years.

"Comparisons with living crinoids show that the anatomy of this ancient species was very different," Cole added. "This gives us new insight into how crinoids evolved and how their feeding strategies changed over hundreds of millions of years."

A Rare Window Into Earth's Earliest Ocean Ecosystems

Beyond revealing details about one ancient species, the discovery could help scientists piece together how marine ecosystems developed during the early Paleozoic Era.

The Paleozoic was a vast chapter of Earth's history that began about 541 million years ago. During its early stages, marine environments supported an expanding variety of complex animals, while life on land was still relatively limited.

Crinoids were among the creatures that became established in early reef communities. Their fossils provide important evidence of how marine animals occupied different ecological roles and adapted to changing environmental conditions.

However, studying only living species cannot reveal the full range of forms and adaptations that existed in the distant past. Some extinct animals possessed anatomical features that have no close equivalent among their modern relatives.

"Fossilized remains of long‑extinct species can show features well outside the range of variation we see in living species," Wright said.

"By comparing ecological ways of life for extinct and modern species, we can understand how patterns of adaptive evolution have changed through time and what factors shaped the modern biosphere."

The biosphere includes all living organisms on Earth and the environments they inhabit. Understanding how it developed requires scientists to examine not only which species existed, but also how they interacted with their surroundings and one another.

Because soft tissues are so rarely preserved in ancient echinoderms, discoveries like this one provide evidence that conventional fossils cannot offer. They can help researchers identify previously unknown feeding strategies, understand ancient ecosystems, and trace evolutionary changes that eventually shaped modern marine life.

An Extraordinary Discovery Hidden in a Museum Collection

Perhaps one of the most surprising aspects of the discovery is where the fossil was found.

Although major paleontological breakthroughs are often associated with expeditions to remote locations, valuable scientific evidence can also remain unnoticed for decades in museums.

Fossil collections contain enormous numbers of specimens, many of which have never been examined by specialists with the expertise needed to recognize their most important features. As scientific knowledge and research methods improve, these collections can reveal discoveries that earlier generations overlooked.

"New fossil discoveries ultimately come from fieldwork, but museum collections play a significant role in this kind of integrative research," Wright said. "We don't always know the full significance of the specimens we collect. New technologies, ideas or expertise often find surprising ways to utilize existing specimens to make new discoveries."

The Dendrocrinus simcoensis fossil had been housed for years at Montréal's Musée de paléontologie et de l'évolution, a small museum that operates entirely with support from community donations.

Its remarkable scientific value became apparent when Cole and Wright, both specialists in crinoids, visited the museum and examined the specimen more closely.

Their investigation revealed that the fossil preserved an extraordinarily rare record of ancient soft tissue, transforming a specimen already held in a museum collection into an important source of new evolutionary evidence.

"This discovery highlights the importance of museum collections and the community support that keeps them alive," Cole said. "Without the dedication of many people caring for these collections, this research would never have been possible."

More Discoveries May Be Waiting in Museum Drawers

The finding also demonstrates why preserving and maintaining fossil collections is essential for future research.

At the Sam Noble Oklahoma Museum of Natural History, Cole and Wright oversee invertebrate paleontology collections containing more than one million specimens. Additional fossils are added every year, creating new opportunities to investigate ancient organisms and identify previously unrecognized species.

With collections of this size, no individual researcher could possibly examine every specimen in detail. A fossil that appears ordinary today might become the focus of a major discovery when studied by someone with a different specialty or new scientific tools.

Making collections available to researchers around the world increases the chances that these overlooked treasures will eventually receive the attention they deserve.

"This is why we work to make our collections accessible to researchers around the world," Wright said. "There are simply too many fossils to study over one person's career. There's more than a lifetime's worth of discoveries waiting to be found."

The extraordinary crinoid fossil is a reminder that some of the most revealing clues about Earth's distant past may already have been collected. They are simply waiting for someone to recognize what makes them special.
