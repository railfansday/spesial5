Which One Is the Real You?

At work, you are known to be a serious, efficient, and decisive manager. When you are with your old high school friend, you become the comedian and social connector, leading karaoke dances, and cracking jokes. On a first date, understandably, you are a bit nervous, carefully choosing your words and may be a little too agreeable.

We tend to assume that somewhere beneath these different personalities, there is a single true self. The others are performances, adaptations, or masks that we put on.

What if all these versions of ourselves are real?

No one can be exactly the same person in every social situation. In fact, failing to consider the situation and the people around us may be selfish or even destructive, argues Japanese novelist Keiichiro Hirano in his book What Is the Self? From Individuals to Dividuals (my translation of the Japanese title).

His idea is simple on the surface: There is no “one” self. We are an aggregate of many selves” – Hirano calls this “dividuals” – a play on the word individual, suggesting that we are, after all, divisible. This is not a reference to dissociative identity disorder, but to the different selves that emerge through our relationships.

As a neurologist, I have become increasingly skeptical of the idea that there is a single, fixed self, hidden somewhere deep inside our brains. Our thoughts, emotions, and behaviors emerge from dynamic interactions among multiple neural systems, constantly responding to our internal states and the world around us.

From this perspective, it seems unlikely that our sense of self would remain entirely unchanged across different situations and relationships. The part of us that wants to help a friend move and the part that wants to spend Saturday on the couch are both real.

Hirano describes three forms of dividuals that emerge through our relationships. With strangers, we may express a generic social dividual—a polite, socially appropriate version of ourselves. Within groups, such as workplaces, communities, or circles of friends, we develop tribal dividuals shaped by shared identities and expectations. In closer relationships, more personalized dividuals emerge, allowing us to reveal intimate aspects of ourselves. None is necessarily better or worse than the others. Each serves a different purpose.

One of the most important ideas in dividualism is that our different selves are not necessarily masks concealing a hidden, authentic self. Rather, they are constantly changing, shaped and reshaped through our interactions with others. Our memories, values, and experiences may provide some continuity, but no single dividual needs to represent our entire identity.

Hirano takes the reciprocal nature of relationships one step further. Our feelings toward another person may depend not only on who that person is, but also on who we become in their presence. We may enjoy spending time with a friend because we like the person we become around them—perhaps more relaxed, generous, or playful. Conversely, suppose my friend David disapproves of my new girlfriend, Mary. I may begin to resent David or feel guilty about disappointing him. Over time, I may avoid David not only because of his disapproval but also because I dislike the resentful, uncomfortable version of myself that emerges in our friendship.

When we have a crush, we desperately want to have that person like us. We may try to be funny, smart, charming, and may try to do something nice. But as we all know too well, relationships have this elusive “chemistry” aspect to them, and dividualism may help explain why that is. In relationships, not only there is another person at the end, but there is also our dividual – our own with the other person. Perhaps part of what we call "chemistry" is not simply liking another person but liking who we become together.

And when a relationship ends, we may mourn not only the loss of that person but also the version of ourselves that existed with them. When a parent dies, we may lose the only person with whom we still felt like a child. When a long career ends, we may miss not only the work but also the version of ourselves that existed among our colleagues. Perhaps this is part of what we mean when we say, "I feel as though I've lost a part of myself."

Over the course of our lives, some dividuals fade as relationships change or end, while others grow and flourish. Some lie dormant for years, only to reappear unexpectedly. Some reveal qualities we admire, while others expose parts of ourselves we would rather not see.

Perhaps the question is not, "Which one is the real me?" but "Which versions of myself do I want to nurture?"

We do not need to love every version of ourselves, nor excuse every action. But perhaps recognizing that no single version defines us will allow us to approach our many selves with greater curiosity and compassion.
