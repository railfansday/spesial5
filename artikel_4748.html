Unintentional Intentions: When a Photo Tour Yields a Different Mindset

Since one of my goals is to keep learning, I am trying something new this autumn. And sometimes that lets me smash my thinking, change my views, and maybe my behavior. I had (partial) success recently when I went on a photography workshop/tour in Vietnam.

I’ve worked in Hanoi, Vietnam, for decades, so I know it well. I’ve seen remarkable economic change—some people describe it as going from “from bicycles to Bentleys.” And, I’ve been there long enough to know people who have married, had children, lost spouses, and had grandchildren.

The photo tour appealed to me for two reasons. First, I could see and learn about other intriguing parts of the country, from the south (Ho Chi Minh City) to the central area (the arts community of Hoi An, the ancient capital of Hue), and the world-class caves of Phong Nha. And someone else would organize it all.

My second reason for going was to learn how to “see differently, like photographers.” I went as a writer, not a photographer, but joined people who are (nearly) professionals. They showed up with their 20-plus pounds of fabulous gear, and I showed up with my iPhone camera. No question I would learn from them.

The learning about techniques part was straightforward, mainly because of the photography instructor.

She was phenomenally skilled and refreshingly nonjudgmental. She worked with each of us wherever we were in our photo journeys. She helped us on equipment, technique, and how to shape and create a photo. She taught me about the iPhone (and admitted that she has transitioned from a big camera to the iPhone as her main piece of gear!). I learned about new (to me) photo approaches, like ICM. At the first showing of our photos, everyone was going on about ICM. I’ve never been shy about asking the “dumb question,” so I admitted I had no idea what they were talking about.

“Intentional camera movement,” four people said at once.

My translation: blurry photos that can look like multiple exposures in a single frame, but done with intention instead of as a mistake. The person who used ICM took gorgeous images that danced with color.

By the end of the tour, I decided I was doing U-ICM—un-intentional camera movement—and got lucky with some of the photos that turned out to be interesting.

So I did learn about photography and how others looked at a scene and shaped it. But the biggest lesson, now that I look back, was my mindset change.

Taking a photo with intention forced me to slow down, observe closely (foreground, background in addition to the main area of focus), and, as the instructor would say, “think about what feeling or idea you want to convey.”

I have a long way to go, but my goal is to do this every day for at least 5 to 10 minutes: slow down, choose a scene, and think about how to frame it. Then shoot it.
