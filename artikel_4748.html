I must achieve internal consistency. —Edmund Husserl, turning an Erlebnis (Type I experience) into an Erfahrung (Type II experience)

The gods decreed that I shall not be a translator, and I am grateful for that. A direct translation from one language to another is a goal we can strive for, but rarely attain fully. The words of any particular language are rich in unique shades of meaning, allusions, emotional undertones, and cross-references to synonyms, which live lives of their own.

Homer called his hero Odysseus πολύτροπος (polytropos), literally “a man of many turns.” But being “of many turns” does not call forth useful associations in English, and so translators have struggled with—and argued with each other about—what to make of polytropos. Some render the idea as “endlessly cunning” (Mitchell, 2014), while others prefer “complicated” (Wilson, 2018). Neither hits the mark. One might as well try shrewd, crafty, clever, or inventive. “Endlessly cunning” is marred by the addition of the adverb, and “complicated” makes it sound like Odysseus needs therapy. “Complex” would have been a better choice. I prefer the phrase “a man of many devices” (Murray, 1919), which recognizes Odysseus’s rich and variable toolbox of survival skills, including those involving deception.

Different languages emphasize and elaborate different concepts, whatever is important in their natural and cultural context. We can marvel at some of these differences. Since Franz Boas (1911) suggested the Greenland Inuit have four different words for snow, others have grown the list, while still others have declared Boas’s observation a myth, one they find vexingly persistent. Other inter-language differences are less controversial, and some of these refer to psychological states. How many types of love are there? The Hebrew tradition counts four. What kinds of longing are there? Is the Portuguese saudade equivalent to the German Sehnsucht? On Seinfeld, Kramer asks George if he has ever yearned. “Yearned?” George replies. “I’ve craved!”

Psychology can be understood as the science of behavior and experience. Both terms carry complexity, and the latter more so than the former. What, we ask, is an experience? It does not take a Ludwig Wittgenstein to see that this concept embraces many variants that may not even share a single common feature. To say that an experience is whatever one experiences is to kick the can down the road; it is a tautology.

When I asked A.I. Claude what an experience is, I received this response: “Experience (noun) has two main senses, and the difference matters.” Claude goes on to explain that one of these senses refers to a process or event, with a first-person involvement. A first kiss delivers this kind of experience. Another sense refers to accumulated knowledge or skill; the sort of thing you put on your résumé. The first sense is what is sometimes called lived experience, whereas the second sense goes further by demanding that something has been learned, something that contributes to the person’s σωφροσύνη (sophrosyne), or practical wisdom. Anyone can have a store of Type I (lived) experiences, but this does not make them “experienced.” The participle must be earned. To have lived experience may simply mean that one hasn’t died yet. To have Type II (empirical) experience means to have made active investments in life that can be cashed out to help reach one’s goals and enhance one’s well-being.

Explaining the conceptual difference between Type I and Type II experience in English requires space, patience, and some circumlocution. The German language offers a clean distinction between Erlebnis, ɛɐˈleːpnɪs, and Erfahrung, ɛɐ̯ˈfaːʁʊŋ. The difference between the two was elaborated by the philosophers Wilhelm Dilthey (1883) and Edmund Husserl (1900-1901), whose work is interesting to consider in light of today’s debates over epistemology (Krueger, 2026). Expressing a popular view, Aziz and Anjum (2025) suggest that the prevailing scientific ethos in psychology is limited and systematically biased by excluding, misrepresenting, and disadvantaging many human populations. What is silenced, according to this view, are the lived experiences existing in these populations. These lived experiences, it is further argued, must be given their due; they must be heard and respected. And so, the argument goes on, these lived experiences must be acknowledged as an alternative epistemology, a way of knowing things, a way of having “knowledges.” Authors arguing the line pursued by Aziz and Anjum differ in how far they wish to go. Some are willing to settle for a lived-experience epistemology being accepted as equivalent to the scientific theory-and-data epistemology. Others wish to see psychological science be remade in light of lived experiences. These writers view the traditional scientific epistemology as oppressive, colonialist, and exploitative.

Dilthey and Husserl would pause here. To them, Erlebnis (Experience Type I) and Erfahrung (Experience Type II) are equally essential to the human condition; they cannot be pitted against each other. What contemporary critics of conventional empirical science seem to miss is that Erlebnis cannot absorb Erfahrung. Being effortful, empirical, and hard-won, Erfahrung is the grounds for reasoned beliefs that can be put to the test, that can be modified or overturned by continued activity. Erfahrung remains a work in progress. An Erlebnis, by contrast, is registered, perhaps committed to memory, and then gradually lost. A tourist has Erlebnisse; a traveler gathers Erfahrungen. It is the difference between perception and cognition.

Science is Erfahrung tamed, structured, and refined into a systematic method. Well-designed studies are efficient ways of learning Nature’s secrets. We can have an Erlebnis when walking into the woods, but only by planting a garden, by experimenting with different seeds, and by pruning excess growth, can we come to have the kind of Erfahrung that allows us to explain things and to teach what we have learned. The rejection of science from the lived-experience perspective is a short-sighted endeavor. The most it can hope to achieve is a narrative. Listening to these narratives can be an Erlebnis, but what can it teach us?

Once the difference between the two types of experience is understood, many familiar narratives appear in a new light. An ancient myth tells us that Adam and Eve had lovely Erlebnisse in the Garden of Eden. In the Land of Nod, they were forced to make Erfahrungen. With that, they became self-aware.
