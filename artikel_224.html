Kalshi makes “difficult decision” to halt Utah trading after losing court fights

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Kalshi’s pause in Utah draws cheers as critics bash ads and boo insider trading.

Kalshi made the “difficult decision” to pause operations in Utah after losing a court fight to block the state’s ongoing enforcement actions under Utah gambling laws, Kalshi spokesperson Jacki McGavick said Monday.

It seems Kalshi’s legal troubles in Utah have become too much for the prediction market platform to gamble on continued operations there. In Utah, several lawsuits are still playing out, and Kalshi also lost a bid to persuade a federal appeals court to block those cases until the appeal is resolved.

Kalshi also wasn’t saved by an emergency motion from the Commodity Futures Trading Commission—which argues that the CFTC has exclusive jurisdiction over prediction market trading. At this time, the federal district court has not granted that motion to block Utah’s enforcement actions, either.

“This is an unfortunate outcome for the more than 177,000 Utahns who use Kalshi,” McGavick said. “We will continue to fight this in court, and we look forward to returning to Utah once our legal position is vindicated.”

Utah’s attorney general, Derek Brown, had filed both civil and criminal charges against Kalshi, as he defended the state’s 130-year ban on gambling.

In a press release discussing the initial lawsuit, he accused Kalshi of running “an online casino” that violates the state’s constitution. “Not only do our state laws ban gambling but Utah is one of only two states where gambling is also prohibited under the state constitution,” Brown said. “For 130 years, Utah has been clear: gambling has no place here, and we will enforce our laws.”

Late Monday, a major Utah-area news outlet, KSL.com, reported that Brown was prepared to jail Kalshi’s co-founders, who had been summoned to be booked on 14 charges on December 5. He defended those plans, saying, “There is a question I have seen from people asking, ‘Well, do we modify this booking process if someone is a very wealthy and well-known tech billionaire?’ and the answer is ‘No, the process is the process; we don’t modify it based on who you are.’”

However, on Tuesday, Utah decided to drop the criminal charges against Kalshi, McGavick confirmed to Ars. In a statement on X, Kalshi general counsel Yaakov Roth said, “We are pleased that Utah has chosen to dismiss criminal charges against Kalshi, while proceeding with civil litigation. Good faith jurisdictional disagreements should never be criminalized. We stand ready to work with State AGs on reasonable interim measures until courts resolve the dispute over who regulates prediction markets.”

In a press release, Brown explained that the charges were dropped specifically due to the pause.

“Kalshi said it would geofence Utah, which blocks access to its site in the state,” the press release said. “The company also committed to monitor for Utah-based IP addresses trying to reach its site. The Office then reevaluated its position and moved to dismiss the criminal case against the company and the individual defendants.”

Brown threatened that the charges would be raised again if Kalshi tried to renew operations.

“Utah does not allow gambling, and prediction markets are gambling,” Brown said. Kalshi has pulled out of Utah, and if it returns, so will the charges. Every company in this space should take note. If they promote, support, or enable gambling, we will hold them accountable.”

Discussion of the Kalshi pause in a Utah sub-Reddit found some Redditors skeptical of the state’s cases as potentially overreaching.

However, many top commenters seemed pleased with the enforcement efforts, suggesting that Utah may have bipartisan support among some residents. One commenter cheered on the attorney general’s efforts as “the one item in the Utah GOP’s agenda I can get behind,” while another agreed that “prediction markets are incredibly toxic and are manipulated against ordinary people.”

Many complained that Kalshi ads flooded their social media feeds. Some suggested the state should focus on banning advertising of prediction markets, while potentially still allowing adults to trade on event contracts if they want to throw their “money away.”

Fears of insider trading were also raised. One commenter laid out why prediction markets seem suspiciously designed to promote insider trading and other market manipulation:

“I’m pretty libertarian and hate banning things. But gambling and prediction markets are a scourge. Even the term ‘prediction market’ is a misnomer. Because ultimately when people with big dollars get involved it creates a financial incentive for them to make fucked up things happen in the real world. Think about it, decades ago we understood that sports betting was a problem because it corrupted sports—something that ultimately is pretty meaningless. Then we got all these people betting on the stock market with puts and it created huge incentives to cause havoc with markets. Now if people are betting big money on politics and wars and everything else, it just means people with influence can make money by making improbable/unexpected things happen.”

One commenter joked, “I wonder how Kashi would have reacted if the judge in charge of their case had ‘traded’ $500,000 on the event of them losing the case. Not a problem at all, right?”

On the same day Kalshi announced its temporary pause in Utah, a judge rejected a request from New York Attorney General Letitia James to immediately shut down Kalshi in that state, Courthouse News reported.

New York Supreme Court Justice Melissa Crane said she was “uncomfortable” granting the temporary restraining order so early in the case because “there does seem to be an indication that there would be market disruption that I’m concerned about.” Kalshi attorney Grant Mainland described it a little differently, claiming that a sudden halt would cause a “market emergency.”

“I think this is the ultimate relief in the case, which is improper for me to do without full briefing,” Crane said. Instead of granting James’ request, she recommended that Kalshi take steps to address the state’s top concerns by considering “safeguards for users between 18 and 20,” who are under the state’s legal gambling age, Courthouse News reported.

According to Kalshi’s estimates, New York’s Kalshi user base (1.5 million) is much larger than Utah’s, so the market disruption from the Utah shutdown may not have carried the same dire stakes for Kalshi or its broader user base.

The CFTC has sought to intervene in that case, too, and it seems clear that Kalshi expects the questions of who can regulate prediction markets and what trading qualifies as a “bet” to reach the Supreme Court. Kalshi’s spokesperson did not respond to Ars’ questions on how the Utah pause broadly impacted users or the platform.

So far, “three federal appeals courts have ruled that states can regulate prediction markets, while a fourth found they cannot,” Courthouse News reported. But despite the current balance tipping toward allowing states to regulate prediction markets, Kalshi appears confident that the Supreme Court will resolve the disparate court rulings in its favor.

In a lawsuit filed by a disgruntled Kalshi user, Kalshi argued that it would waste lower courts’ time to continue litigating these cases and that no case should be resolved until the Supreme Court weighs in.

In that case, Kalshi user Scott Bright alleged that he lost more than $300,000 on sports bets that were technically illegal in Washington.

According to Kalshi, all of Bright’s claims rest “on the premise that state gambling laws reach KalshiEX’s federally regulated exchange—the question now before the Supreme Court. Plaintiff puts the cart before the horse; Defendants ask only that the Court let that threshold question be resolved first.”

The court has yet to address Kalshi’s request to stay the case, but Bright has argued that Kalshi is trying to “sideline a case governed by binding Ninth Circuit precedent so that they can wait for a Supreme Court decision that may never come.”

Kalshi did not respond to Ars’ request for a statement on Bright’s lawsuit. In their court filing, they argued that “a stay will let the Supreme Court address an issue that could dispose of every claim, will not meaningfully harm Plaintiff, and will spare Defendants and the Court litigation under a framework that may soon be displaced.”
