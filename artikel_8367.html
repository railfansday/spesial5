Day 36: Solitude Before the End

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

I woke up to my alarm at 4:30, tired but excited for the adventure of the morning.

In the same way I had the crazy idea of walking all night to Cruz del Ferro, my Argentinian brother had one of his own: meditating in the forest at sunrise. It took a bit of investigation, but we decided on going to the forest near Portomarín.

As I waited in the common room of the albergue, I was curled up with my headlamp on in the darkness. Another pilgrim joined me, offered me an instant coffee, and proceeded to give me fantastic advice about Santiago and the route after. This was his eleventh Camino, and he said,

“I always say it’s going to be the last one, but I keep coming back.”

My brother woke up, we stepped into the cloudy darkness, and walked toward the river in the valley. The long, long bank on either side was covered in knee-high plants that I could feel were leaving stickers in my pants.

I felt his hesitancy as he changed his mind on the route and we turned around to walk on the road.

What if we did it in the forest by Santiago after we finished?

I pulled out my map, double-checked that it could be a possibility, and brought it up.

Mother Nature told us to wait, giving the idea more time to simmer over the next few days.

It was a blessing in disguise because a few minutes after we made that decision, my Australian friend called me and invited us to breakfast.

All three of us sat together in her modern and elegant albergue while the employee took such good care of us.

Once we had our fill, we all hit the trail together.

As we walked and talked, the lush green grass and trees came in and out of view. The clouds were so dense at some points that we couldn’t see twenty feet in front of us.

While we walked, the craving for solitude crept up again. I just didn’t know how to say it.

We stopped at the first cafe open on a Sunday, two hours into the walk, and drank hot coffee outside while my brother and I attempted to lighten our packs with all the food we were carrying.

When I told him privately that I didn’t know how to ask the Australian friend for space, he encouraged me in Spanish not to “ask” but just “say”.

My brother and I started together, easily reading the other person without words. Between speaking in Spanish, English, or walking in silence, it was the perfect balance.

But he remembered I wanted to walk alone, gave me a hug, and stopped so I could go ahead. When I walked away, he taught me the old, traditional Camino phrase:

While I walked alone, I thought about the past few hours. My Argentinian brother really was my teacher, not only in Spanish, but also in how to be a more compassionate, more grounded human being. We all joked around that he looked like Jesus, but he might be one of the most “Christ-like” people I’ve ever met.

I felt so amazing during my solitude. I met and walked briefly with new people and ones I’d known before. Each time, after a bit of chatting, I let them walk ahead and came back to myself.

When I arrived in the town of Palas de Rei, the first albergue I went to was full, with the person right in front of me in line. I sat on a bench outside and decided to pivot.

The next cheapest option was Albergue Restaurante Castro. Only €5 more, I got real sheets, a memory foam pillow, and a curtain for my bunk. This felt luxurious compared to what I was used to. And with that, I fell right asleep.

When I awoke around 4, I grabbed the food I’d carried all day—half baguette, tuna, tomato, and mayo—and went to the church at the top of the town.

There, I ate my dinner overlooking the uneventful town. This was the first dinner I’d had alone in almost two weeks. The brothers weren’t with me, and it felt so strange. It felt like the universe saying,

“You wanted to be alone. Do you remember this?”

A few people nearby were speaking English, so I chimed in. The people from North Carolina looked shocked and amazed when I told them I had been walking since France. They were an example of the many people around me who were doing the last 100 km. Right before they left, one of them walked over to me and handed me a ceramic scallop shell she made.

“You’re the first person I’ve met who did the whole thing and I want you to have the first one.”

I strolled around town, trying to see if there was anything interesting to find on a Sunday evening. I ran into my Australian friend, then my Italian brother. My brother and I bought some gelato, sat in the plaza, and talked about the next week.

As he walked back to his albergue, I saw the sadness in his eyes behind a fake smile.

It was an early night, but I knew it’d charge my battery up for the intensity of the end.
