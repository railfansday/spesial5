The speaker of the House holds the gavel, but the job covers much more

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

What the speaker of the House does goes well beyond holding the gavel — 10 duties, from shaping floor votes to standing second in line for the presidency

The speaker of the House is best known as the person holding the gavel on the House floor. That image covers only a small share of the job. The speaker helps decide which bills reach a vote, shapes who sits on key committees and leads the majority party. The speaker also stands second in line for the presidency, behind only the vice president.

Any account of what the speaker of the House does starts with a split at the heart of the role. The speaker is an officer of the whole chamber, chosen by a vote of the full House. The speaker is also a party leader, the top-ranking member of whichever party holds the majority. Those two jobs sit side by side. One involves running the institution for every member. The other involves advancing a party's agenda and holding its majority together.

The powers attached to the office are real, but they are not unlimited. The speaker shares control of the floor with the majority leader and the Rules Committee. Rank-and-file members have tools of their own. A discharge petition can push a bill to a vote over leadership objections. A motion to vacate the chair can remove a sitting speaker.

The office's formal outline comes from a single clause in the Constitution. Much of its practical weight comes from House rules, precedent and party custom built up over more than two centuries. Sam Rayburn held the post longer than anyone else, a little over 17 years spread across several stretches between 1940 and 1961.

The 10 slides that follow break the job into its main parts. They cover how the Constitution creates the post, how members elect a speaker and how the speaker runs floor debate. They also explain the influence over scheduling and committees, the party leadership role and the place in presidential succession. The remaining slides cover administrative and ceremonial duties, along with the process for removing a speaker.

The speaker of the House exists because Article I, Section 2 of the Constitution says the House chooses its own speaker and other officers. That short provision is the legal footing for the office.

The power to choose belongs to the House alone. The clause gives the president and the Senate no role in the selection.

That arrangement makes the speaker an officer of the chamber itself. The person in the post owes the job to House members, not to another branch of government.

The same provision reaches beyond the speaker. It also lets the House pick the other officers who help run the institution, not only the person who presides over it.

Nothing in the text says the speaker must be a sitting member of the House. On paper, the chamber could elect someone from outside its ranks. It has never done so. Every speaker so far has been a member.

That unbroken pattern began with Frederick Muhlenberg, who became the first speaker in 1789.

The full House of Representatives elects its speaker at the opening of every new Congress. To win, a candidate needs a majority of the members who vote for a named person. Winning the most votes is not enough on its own.

That threshold depends on who casts a vote for someone. A member who answers "present" or is absent does not count toward the total. With all 435 seats filled and every member naming a candidate, the winning mark is 218 votes. Each "present" vote or absence lowers that mark. The number needed to win can then fall below half of the full chamber.

The first stage takes place before the floor vote. The majority party picks its nominee first, usually in a closed-door vote of its own conference. That private step is meant to line up the party behind one candidate before the whole chamber weighs in. The minority party usually nominates its own candidate as well.

The system relies on party unity. A nominee who loses even a few colleagues on the floor can fall short of a majority. The smaller the majority party's edge, the fewer defections it takes to stall the vote.

Kevin McCarthy's election in January 2023 showed what that looks like in practice. He won the speakership only on the 15th ballot. Every failed round sent the House back to vote again, because no candidate had yet cleared the majority mark.

Because the election takes place at the start of each Congress, the post is never held indefinitely. A sitting speaker who wants to stay in the job has to win the vote again when the next Congress convenes.

The process splits the choice between a party and the full chamber. The majority party chooses the nominee in private. The formal decision still belongs to every member who votes on the House floor.

The speaker of the House is the chamber's presiding officer. The speaker opens each session, recognizes members who want to speak and rules on points of order raised during floor proceedings.

Recognition is the starting point of the floor job. A member who wants to address the House does not simply begin talking. The member first needs recognition from the chair. Granting or withholding it is one of the speaker's presiding powers.

A point of order is an objection that the House is breaking one of its own rules. The presiding officer rules on each one, usually on the advice of the House parliamentarian. Those rulings become precedents that later presiding officers consult.

A ruling from the chair is not always final. Any member can appeal it to the full House, which then decides the question by majority vote. The majority often moves to table such an appeal. If that motion passes, the ruling stands.

The speaker does not have to sit in the chair for every hour of debate. Under House rules, the speaker can name another member to act as speaker pro tempore. That designation can last up to three legislative days without the House's approval. The handoff happens often during routine debate. The member holding the gavel on a given afternoon is frequently someone other than the speaker.

The speaker also steps aside when the House sits as the Committee of the Whole House on the state of the Union. That is the procedural form the House often uses to debate and amend bills. The speaker appoints a different member to chair it.

The speaker keeps the right to vote as a member of the House. In practice, the speaker usually does not cast a vote. House rules do not require the speaker to vote in ordinary proceedings.

There are two main exceptions to that habit. The speaker may vote on major questions. The speaker may also vote when a single ballot would break a tie.

The speaker of the House, working with the majority leader and the Rules Committee, largely decides which bills reach a vote on the House floor. That control over the agenda is one of the job's main sources of power. It is shared, though, not held by the speaker alone.

A bill that clears a House committee has no guaranteed path to the floor. Thousands of bills are introduced in each Congress, and most never receive a floor vote.

The majority leader manages the day-to-day floor schedule and announces the coming week's business. The speaker sets the broader priorities that the schedule follows. Decisions on major legislation are usually made by the two leaders together, in consultation with committee chairs and the majority party's members.

For most significant bills, the route to the floor runs through the Rules Committee. The committee writes a resolution, known as a special rule, that sets the terms of debate. The rule specifies how long members may debate a bill and which amendments, if any, may be offered. The full House must adopt that rule by majority vote before it takes up the bill itself.

A second route covers less contested measures. Under a procedure called suspension of the rules, a bill comes up with 40 minutes of debate and no floor amendments. Passage then requires a two-thirds vote of the members present and voting. Under House rules, the speaker has discretion over whether to recognize members to offer suspension motions.

The majority party also follows informal practices about what it brings forward. The best known is the Hastert rule, named for former Speaker Dennis Hastert. It describes a practice of bringing up only bills backed by a majority of the majority party. It is a custom rather than a written House rule, and speakers have set it aside on some votes.

Members who want to get around the leadership have one main formal tool: the discharge petition. A petition can be filed once a bill has sat in committee for 30 legislative days. If 218 members sign it, the bill can be forced to the floor over the leadership's objections.

That threshold equals a majority of the full 435-member House. When the minority party holds fewer than 218 seats, its members cannot complete a petition on their own. They need signatures from at least some members of the majority party.

The speaker of the House shapes several House committees by choosing who sits on them. In practice, the speaker names the majority-party members of the Rules Committee, along with the House members of conference committees and some select committees.

The Rules Committee writes the resolutions, known as rules, that govern how a bill is handled on the floor. A rule sets how long debate runs and which amendments members may offer. A rule that blocks amendments can keep a bill's text intact all the way to the final vote.

The speaker once ran that panel directly. Until the 1910 revolt against Speaker Joseph Cannon, the speaker chaired the Rules Committee.

Today the speaker does not hold the chairmanship. The speaker still picks the panel's majority-party members, however. Through those picks, the speaker keeps a direct tie to the committee that sets the terms of floor debate.

Conference committees are temporary panels of House and Senate negotiators. They resolve differences between the two chambers' versions of the same bill. The speaker chooses the House side of those teams. Those appointees represent the chamber while a final text is worked out. The compromise they reach then returns to both chambers for a vote.

Select committees are created for a set purpose, often an investigation, instead of serving as permanent standing panels. The speaker names the members of some of these committees. Those choices shape who conducts an investigation and how it proceeds.

Speaker Nancy Pelosi used that authority in 2021. She appointed the members of the House select committee on the Jan. 6 attack. Pelosi had become the first woman to serve as speaker in 2007.

These appointment powers have limits. Choosing committee members does not give the speaker sole control of the House. The majority leader and the wider majority party also influence how the Rules Committee's work reaches the floor.

The speaker of the House is the highest-ranking member of the majority party in the chamber. The speaker is also that party's public face, the person most closely identified with what the House majority wants to do.

That party post sits inside the same office that runs the chamber. The speaker presides over the House on behalf of all its members. The same person also leads one side of it.

The party side of the job centers on holding the majority together. That task grows harder when the margin is narrow. A slim majority leaves little room for members of the speaker's own party to break ranks on a vote.

The arithmetic of a close House shows why. If the minority party votes as a bloc against a measure, a small group of defectors from the majority can be enough to defeat it. That gives a handful of members leverage over the speaker. They can ask for changes to a bill as the price of their support.

The party role also explains why the speaker draws so much public attention. As the majority's most visible figure, the speaker stands in for the party in the House. No rank-and-file member carries that role. When the majority's agenda moves forward or stalls, the speaker is usually the person asked to account for it.

The political weight of the office has deep roots. In the early 1800s, Henry Clay built the speakership into a post that drove policy. Under Clay, the office became a seat of political leadership as well as the chair of the chamber. The modern speakership still carries both parts: chairing the House and pushing the majority's agenda.

The speaker of the House is second in line to the presidency. Under the Presidential Succession Act of 1947, only the vice president stands ahead of the speaker.

The 1947 law also sets the order behind the speaker. The president pro tempore of the Senate comes next. Members of the Cabinet follow after that.

The result places two officers of Congress ahead of every Cabinet member. The speaker and the Senate president pro tempore both outrank the executive branch's department heads in the line of succession.

The arrangement ties the presidency to a post the House fills by its own vote. The officer second in line is the person the House has chosen to run its chamber.

The speaker of the House swears in members at the start of each new Congress and signs enrolled bills before they go to the president. Both duties are formal steps that the chamber's business depends on.

The oath itself comes from the Constitution. Article VI requires members of Congress to be bound by an oath or affirmation to support the Constitution. The oath is the step that turns a member-elect into a sworn member of the House.

On opening day, the newly elected speaker first takes the oath from the dean of the House, the longest continuously serving member. The speaker then administers the oath to the other members together. They raise their right hands and take it as a group, returning members alongside first-term ones.

The House also elects its own officers at the start of each Congress. They include the Clerk, the Sergeant at Arms, the Chief Administrative Officer and the Chaplain. The full chamber chooses them, and the speaker does not appoint them alone. Once in place, they answer to the House leadership. Between them, the Clerk, the Sergeant at Arms and the Chief Administrative Officer keep the chamber's records, security and day-to-day operations running.

Signing enrolled bills happens at the other end of the legislative process. An enrolled bill is the final official copy of a measure that both chambers have passed in identical form. It is printed on parchment by the chamber where the bill began.

The speaker signs that copy. The Senate's presiding officer signs it as well. The signatures attest that the text is the version Congress approved. They do not change any of its contents. House rules also let the speaker designate another member to sign enrolled bills in the speaker's place.

Only after both signatures can the bill be presented to the president. Article I, Section 7 gives the president 10 days, Sundays excepted, to sign or veto it once it arrives. A delay in signing at the Capitol pushes back when that clock starts.

The speaker presides over joint sessions of Congress together with the Senate's presiding officer. The speaker also invites the president to deliver the State of the Union address.

A joint session brings the House and the Senate together for a single proceeding. Neither chamber's officer runs it alone. The speaker shares the rostrum with the Senate's presiding officer, so each chamber has its top officer at the front of the room.

That shared arrangement sets joint sessions apart from ordinary House business. On a normal legislative day, only House members take part, and the Senate conducts its own proceedings separately.

The State of the Union starts with the speaker's invitation. The speaker sends it to the president. The president then appears before both chambers to deliver the address.

The invitation runs from the legislative branch to the executive. The address takes place at the speaker's invitation rather than on the president's own initiative.

During the speech, the speaker sits behind the president. The vice president sits beside the speaker. The arrangement places the officers tied to the House and the Senate side by side for the length of the address.

The speaker comes from the party that holds the House majority. That party is not always the president's party. When the two differ, a leader of the opposing party sits behind the president for the full address.

A motion to vacate the chair is the procedure the House uses to remove a sitting speaker. The House first voted a speaker out on Oct. 3, 2023, when members removed Speaker Kevin McCarthy by a vote of 216 to 210.

If the motion passes, the speaker's chair becomes vacant. The House then has no elected speaker until it chooses a new one.

The McCarthy vote was decided by a margin of six votes. It came in the same Congress that had elected him to the post only months earlier. A switch by a handful of members would have changed the result.

The vacancy lasted 22 days. During that stretch, Patrick McHenry served as speaker pro tempore. He held the chair on a temporary basis and was not elected speaker. McHenry treated the role as limited mainly to overseeing the election of a successor. The House passed no legislation while the chair stayed empty. Floor business effectively waited on the outcome of the speaker race. The vacancy ended on Oct. 25, 2023, when the House elected Mike Johnson.

The rules that govern the motion are set by the House itself. They can change from one Congress to the next. In the 119th Congress, House rules require nine members of the majority party to back a motion to vacate before it is privileged.

A privileged motion takes precedence over ordinary business on the floor. Under the 119th Congress rule, that status depends on support from members of the speaker's own party. Members of the minority party cannot make the motion privileged by themselves. As a result, the most direct threat of removal comes from inside the majority conference rather than from the opposing party.

The motion gives the full House a check on the person it elected to run the chamber. The same body that chooses a speaker can also take the job away.
