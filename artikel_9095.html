In the Shadow of War, Frieze Abu Dhabi Continues With ‘Business as Usual’

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Editor’s Note: This story originally appeared in On Balance, the ARTnews newsletter about the art market and beyond. Sign up here to receive it every Wednesday.

Frieze Abu Dhabi makes its debut this week amid geopolitical uncertainty in the region, as the US and Israel’s war in Iran continues to cause concerns about traveling to the Gulf. Even still, the United Arab Emirates is expecting smooth sailing. “It’s business as usual in Abu Dhabi,” Dyala Nusseibeh, Frieze Abu Dhabi’s director, told ARTnews. “The collectors are spending and people are buying.” Related Articles As Art Week NYC Kicks Off, Galleries Have Millions of Dollars of Art to Sell--And No Shortage of Naras Jeff Koons Talks AI, a $100 M. Not everyone is so confident that Frieze Abu Dhabi will be a success, however. Beyond the ongoing conflicts, the fair also clashes with the marquee New York evening sales, where high-value, single-owner collections of Theodore and Ruth Baum, Herbert Lust, and the Blaquier-Arrieta family are being offered. “Frieze is a diabolical date—all of us can’t go because we’re all in New York,” Philip Hoffman, chairman and founder of the Fine Art Group, told ARTnews, noting that he thinks people are less likely to go to the fair when the long-awaited Guggenheim Abu Dhabi opens next month. “We’re all wondering what on earth Frieze [is doing] because they can’t get any VIPs ahead of the big sales. New York is where it’s all happening, why on earth would we all go to Frieze?”

Michael Bouhanna, a former Sotheby’s executive and founding director of the Miami-based Bouhanna Art Advisory, will also not be in attendance due to the conflicting schedule with the New York sales, with Art Basel Miami Beach following closely behind. “My clients are mostly in the US and Europe, so that week they are on the New York sales, where we are bidding,” he said. “The more the leading international galleries bring important works to Abu Dhabi, the more my clients will plan to be there. But the aim of Frieze Abu Dhabi is not to be another London or New York, and I wouldn’t judge it that way.” Instead, he said, Frieze Abu Dhabi’s program and the profile of the buyers attending point to a different ambition—putting the region’s own culture at the center, showing it in context with Western art, and giving local and regional collectors a prestigious platform on home turf.

As part of Frieze Abu Dhabi’s takeover of the long-running Abu Dhabi Art fair, Nusseibeh is streamlining the show floor, cutting down exhibitors from 142 last year to 89 this year, giving galleries more space. The new, blue-chip-leaning list includes international powerhouses such as Gagosian, Pace Gallery, Thaddaeus Ropac, and Victoria Miro; of that bunch, only Pace participated in the 2025 fair. They will exhibit alongside UAE galleries like Carbon 12, Lawrie Shabibi, and The Third Line; Saudi Arabia’s ATHR Gallery and Hafez Gallery; and Tehran-based Dastan.

“We have a full house of galleries,” Nusseibeh said, adding that there’s plenty to “attract people to come” to the fair, including a Frieze Masters section themed around trade across the Indian Ocean. She told ARTnews that the VIP team had confirmed attendance from several international museum groups, including the Aspen Art Museum, the Barbican, the British Museum, the Centre for Contemporary Arts Tashkent, the Gordon Parks Foundation, the Los Angeles County Museum of Art, Korea’s National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art, the Singapore Art Museum, and the Walker Art Center in Minneapolis.

Even still, the bulk of buying power will likely come from state funds and royal sources. “The largest purchases are still institutional because several museums are forming collections at the same time, and that is a good thing—it’s what makes galleries bring serious work,” Bouhanna said.

While Abu Dhabi might be in close proximity to conflict in the Middle East, politically, it’s still a green zone for many tourists and businesses—and Frieze plans to sail forward despite the chaos. “We continue to monitor everything in the region. We’re in constant dialogue with galleries and safety is always of foremost concern. But if you look at the current facts on the ground, it’s a robust art market and sales are happening,” Nusseibeh said.

Several galleries interviewed for this article told ARTnews they have found reassurance in the art fair’s partnership with the Department of Culture and Tourism – Abu Dhabi (DCT Abu Dhabi), an arm of the country’s government. Regional galleries, in particular, said they were unfazed by the possibilities of war. “It is, of course, a risk that we consider. Other than the financial risk, there is a risk to our security,” Rana Nasser Eddin, director of Beirut-based Sfeir-Semler Gallery, told ARTnews. “However, we do persevere. If we halted our work every time things were unstable, we would have never opened our gallery in Beirut. We would have never been able to do all the great artistic projects we see today.”

Kourosh Nouri, founding director of Dubai’s Carbon 12, put it more bluntly. “We are art professionals, not politicians. As long as the great galleries of Dubai and the region are present, the rest comes down to each gallery’s own strategy,” he said. Mohammed Hafiz, cofounder of Jeddah-based ATHR Gallery, told ARTnews that regional developments are something people consider when making any kind of travel or business decisions. “It would be unrealistic to pretend otherwise. We need to be careful about treating the Middle East as one place or assuming that events in one part of the region define the entire region.”

And at the fair, both galleries aim to highlight the strengths of their respective programs. Sfeir-Semler will be a group presentation of 12 artists, including works by Khalil Rabah, Bayan Kiwan, Samia Halaby, and Lawrence Abu Hamdan. Carbon 12’s booth will display new works by Sarah Almehairi, Bernhard Buhmann, Edgar Orlaineta and Amba Sayal-Bennett, while the gallery space in nearby Dubai will be a solo for British Iraqi painter Malik Thomas Jalil Kydd.

Dealer Dealer Thaddaeus Ropac participated in Abu Dhabi Art each year from its inaugural edition in 2009 through 2017. After a nine-year hiatus, he returns to the city as exhibitor now that the fair is under the umbrella of Frieze, where he will present a two-artist booth for Imran Qureshi and Yan Pei-Ming. “The regional specificity of Frieze Abu Dhabi is a draw for us,” he told ARTnews. “We participate in numerous fairs around the world and we have seen over the last few years that the majority have become more regionally specific, which is a good thing since we can focus on developing relationships particular to that region with people that we don’t see elsewhere, and there is potential to engage with a growing local audience for our artists.”

DCT Abu Dhabi’s 2025 investment outlook reports that one of the UAE’s key targets for 2031 is “strengthening the unified national tourism identity” through developing and diversifying specialized tourism products, building tourism capabilities, and encouraging the contribution of national cadres in the tourism sector, and increasing investments in all tourism sectors. The strategy aims to raise the tourism sector’s contribution to the GDP to $123 billion from $17 billion in 2025, with an annual increase of $7.4 billion.

One of Abu Dhabi’s largest investments is the development of Saadiyat Island, home to the Louvre Abu Dhabi, the Zayed National Museum, the Natural History Museum Abu Dhabi, and the Guggenheim Abu Dhabi, opening on December 11. The emirate’s sovereign wealth fund also infused Sotheby’s with $1 billion, for a minority stake, in 2024.

“I don’t think any city has invested more in cultural institutions over the last 10 years than Abu Dhabi or done more to bring the leading international players to the region,” said Bouhanna.

Beyond the Guggenheim Abu Dhabi’s opening, December will be active in the city, as celebrities and politicians, collectors and advisers descend for events ranging from the Formula 1 Abu Dhabi Grand Prix and the Abu Dhabi Finance Week conference to the Culture Summit Abu Dhabi and a Sotheby’s sale. ATHR Gallery’s Hafiz said he believes this confluence is the next step for the region in building a commercial market. “This is where the next chapter becomes particularly interesting. For many years, government and institutional investment have been major drivers of the regional art scene. The next stage is about developing a much broader private collecting base,” he said. Nouri, of Carbon 12, added that, at the moment, it’s private collectors who are “carrying galleries like ours.”
