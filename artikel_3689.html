Where Is Christa Pike Now? What to Know After She Survived, Left Hospital

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Christa Pike, the only woman on Tennessee’s death row, survived two injections of pentobarbital on September 30 during a failed execution attempt that left her gravely injured. More than a month later, the 50-year-old remains isolated in a prison medical ward, according to her legal team.

Pike spent 10 days in the hospital before returning to the Debra K. Johnson Rehabilitation Center, where she’s being held in the center’s infirmary, on October 9, according to court documents filed by her attorneys. She issued her first public statement since the botched execution on October 30, exactly one month after the state’s attempt to put her to death.

In the statement released through her legal team, Pike said she was “prepared to die” when she entered the execution chamber at the Riverbend Maximum Security Institution in Nashville on September 30.

“I had made my peace. I had told the people who loved me, supported me and stood by me through the years I was grateful for them. I had also told the people in the room and who were there to witness my execution that I was leaving them in loving kindness,” Pike said.

She described persistent pain since surviving the two lethal injections of pentobarbital.

“There hasn’t been a moment since that night when I haven’t been in pain. My body hurts. My arms hurt. My hands hurt. My throat hurts. I still struggle to speak. And the pain isn’t just physical. There is pain in my mind and in my soul,” Pike said.

Pike also addressed the family of Colleen Slemmer, the 19-year-old classmate she was convicted of killing in 1995.

“I had not contemplated life beyond that night, and I’m deeply hurt by the incompetence that has put so many people in this terrible position. I continue to think about my victim’s family and the terrible pain and loss they have endured,” she continued.

Pike thanked medical personnel for caring for her in the hospital, as well as her attorneys, and said she is “searching for meaning in what happened.”

“I don’t know why I survived that night. I don’t know what comes next. Some days, finding hope is harder than others. For now, I am trying to heal, one day at a time. And I am holding on to the love and kindness of so many people who have reminded me that I am not alone,” she wrote at the end of her statement.

Pike’s attorneys filed an emergency motion in federal court on Friday, October 30, calling on the court to enforce the terms 2024 settlement agreement that ended 28 years she previously spent in solitary confinement.

The motion argues prison officials are breaching the settlement “by holding Ms. Pike in indefinite isolation in the prison infirmary following the Tennessee Department of Correction’s failed execution that left her gravely injured.”

“She spent multiple days on a ventilator and over a week in the medical intensive care unit at a hospital following the State’s disastrous execution attempt,” attorney Angela L. Bergman wrote in the motion.

The filing states that prison officials are “denying her the opportunity to work, eat, and otherwise socialize with any other women at DJRC as set out in the Agreement,” and “refused Ms. Pike’s reasonable request to be housed with other women receiving temporary medical care.”

In a declaration dated October 29, Pike wrote that she’s only been allowed contact with limited staff, medical personnel, her spiritual advisor and legal team. She said she also has to use a shower with an insect infestation.

“I have a roach-infested shower that I had to clean on my own, which puts me at a high risk of infection and further injury,” Pike wrote.

“This renewed isolation, coming right after the execution attempt and after twenty-eight years of solitary confinement, is causing me serious psychological harm and trauma,” she concluded. “Each day I remain in isolation adds to that harm.”

Tennessee Department of Correction Communications Director Dorinda Carter declined a request for comment from Us via email on Monday, November 2.

“It is our policy not to comment on pending litigation,” Carter said.

The state has until November 4 to file a response.

Pike is experiencing “profound psychological trauma” more than one week after the botched execution, her attorneys told People on Thursday, October 8.

“Christa is dealing with the profound psychological trauma of having survived a failed execution, including the terror, pain and uncertainty of believing she was about to die,” the attorneys said. “We cannot yet know the full emotional and psychological toll of that experience or how long it will take her to begin to heal.”

Attorney Stephen Ferrell told BBC that Pike was still mentally “foggy” but was making progress physically and could move around her hospital room.

“What’s been described to me is that physically there’s quite a bit going on, that her arms are very injured and she’s not able to use them very much,” he said, noting that Pike could not go “very far.”

Pike’s mental state was “good and bad,” and Ferrell said she “can lose her train of thought really easily and forgets where she’s going.”

Physician Dr. Mark Fowler, who was assigned to pronounce Pike dead during the execution attempt, revealed he still wants her put to death in a new interview with Intelligencer.

Fowler, 69, said he was summoned from a waiting room after Pike received two doses of pentobarbital and gasped for air. “She’s not dead,” he recalled the warden saying.

“I thought, ‘Well, in human beings there’s a spectrum of responses to medications and we’ll give it a few more minutes,’” he continued.

When Pike didn’t die, Fowler worried she would have irreversible brain damage. He reentered the execution chamber and diagnosed her as comatose. The deputy commissioner then ordered the execution halted.

It was ultimately determined that the execution team did not properly place the intravenous lines, which caused the lethal doses of pentobarbital to enter subcutaneous tissue of Pike’s arms instead of circulating into her bloodstream.

Fowler said he wants the state to follow through on Pike’s execution, in part to provide “a sense of justice” for Slemmer’s mother.

“People will say, ‘Why don’t you just walk away from this?’ And the answer is, that’s exactly what the advocates against the death penalty want,” Fowler said. “They felt that if they could humiliate me I would step back from it — because I promise you, there ain’t anybody else in Tennessee going to sign up for it now — and the answer is, I cannot be pushed off a course that I find correct. I refuse to compromise.”

Fowler also offered a solution for avoiding failed executions in the future, saying he prefers the older three-drug cocktail that involves potassium chloride over pentobarbital.

Pike was sentenced to death in 1996 after a jury found her guilty of first-degree murder for beating, torturing and killing Slemmer, her classmate at the University of Tennessee, on the school’s campus in 1995. Pike was 18 when she committed the crime.

She killed Slemmer alongside her then-boyfriend, Tadaryl Shipp, who was sentenced to life in prison because he was a minor at the time. Shipp is eligible for parole but is currently in custody after his latest appeal was denied in October 2025.

Slemmer’s mother, May Martinez, traveled to Tennessee from Florida to witness Pike’s execution. “It was a mess,” Martinez told NBC News after the botched attempt.
