A clever new experiment with light solves a decades-old mystery about how some materials carry perfect electricity

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Researchers temporarily disabled a superconductor using light. They found that its flawless electrical flow actually fights against a strange rival state instead of growing out of it.

A superconductor is a special material that lets electricity flow through it without wasting any energy as heat. While copper-based materials are some of the best at doing this, physicists are still debating exactly how they make this perfect electrical flow happen.

One camp has long held that a strange state called the pseudogap is the seed from which superconductivity grows.

In Quebec, a team used a flash of light to switch superconductivity off in one of these materials for about a trillionth of a second. The pseudogap appeared in its place and, as superconductivity recovered, the pseudogap faded again.

The researchers read that as a sign that the two states compete, rather than one growing out of the other.

Copper-oxide superconductors, called cuprates, were discovered in 1986, and the discovery won a Nobel Prize the next year.

The crystal in this study loses all resistance below about –296°F (–182°C). That is cold by any everyday standard and warm for a superconductor.

In an ordinary metal, electrons fill a range of energies up to a top level, and current flows from that top. In a superconductor, the electrons pair up, and a gap opens around that level, with matching features on either side.

The pseudogap is different. It is a partial gap, lopsided, that shows up at temperatures too warm for superconductivity.

Fabio Boschini, the study’s senior author, leads a laboratory at INRS in Varennes, near Montreal, and holds a Canada Research Chair in the dynamics of quantum materials.

The first author is Dario Armanno, a Ph.D. student in his laboratory. Theorists at the Flatiron Institute’s Center for Computational Quantum Physics in New York built the model used to interpret the measurements.

The team cooled a crystal of a bismuth-based cuprate to about –442°F (–263°C) and hit it with a pulse of infrared light.

Light with enough energy breaks the electron pairs that carry a supercurrent. So the pulse switches superconductivity off for a moment without warming the crystal past the point where it normally fails.

A second flash, of ultraviolet light, then knocks electrons out of the crystal. An instrument at the Advanced Laser Light Source records their energy and direction, and from those the researchers can map which electron states exist at each instant.

Repeating the two flashes at different delays turns the maps into a film, with frames about 300 femtoseconds apart. A femtosecond is a millionth of a billionth of a second.

The team ran the experiment twice, once with a weak pulse and once with a pulse almost twice as strong.

After the weak pulse, the superconducting gap filled in partway but kept its balanced shape. After the strong pulse, the shape turned lopsided, with the states on one side of the gap suppressed.

That lopsided pattern is the pseudogap’s signature, and it lasted one to two trillionths of a second.

“It is a bit like uncovering missing frames from a film that scientists have been trying to piece together for decades,” Armanno said.

By about 3.5 trillionths of a second after the strong pulse, the balanced shape had returned. In an interview with Earth.com, Boschini said the pseudogap signature faded again as superconductivity recovered.

“What struck us was that the pseudogap’s electronic fingerprint emerged even when the electrons were cold enough for the material to normally be superconducting,” he said.

A strong pulse also warms the electrons. So the team compared moments after the weak and strong pulses when the electrons were at the same temperature. The lopsided pattern showed up only after the strong pulse.

Boschini said heating alone can’t explain the result, and that light lets researchers test the relationship between the two states in a way that simply warming the material can’t.

Next, the Flatiron theorists tried to reproduce the measured patterns. A model that simply added a superconducting state and a pseudogap together could not match the data.

Only a model in which the two states act on each other reproduced the lopsided pattern. “Competition does not prevent coexistence,” Boschini told Earth.com.

In the team’s reading, the pseudogap and short-lived superconducting pairing can share the crystal. The competition sets in only once superconductivity becomes long-range order across the crystal.

All of this was measured in one material, tuned to one chemical recipe. Exactly how the light pulse breaks pairs so quickly has not been confirmed in experiments, the team wrote.

Their theory describes how the two states interact without explaining where the pseudogap comes from.

The next question, Boschini said, is how general the behavior is across other quantum materials with competing states, which the same instrument can now study.

He said the study’s immediate contribution is new knowledge rather than a new technology, though over time it could help guide the search for better superconducting materials.

INRS lists more efficient power grids, medical technology and quantum computing among the long-term hopes for such materials.

“For power grids, the long-term hope is to carry electricity with less energy lost as heat,” he said.

Whether the rivalry seen in this one crystal holds in every cuprate is still open. The answer will come from pointing the same light pulses at the rest of the family.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Physics.
