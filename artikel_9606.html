I’ve now had a version of this conversation enough times that I could write the reply in advance. Someone forwards a headline about a chip that “computes with light instead of electrons” and is supposedly a hundred times faster than an Nvidia GPU, and the message attached is always some version of “wait, is this the quantum computer thing?” From the slightly more technical friends, it’s “isn’t this just fiber optics?” Neither is a dumb question. The best-known machines that compute with photons right now are quantum computers, with companies like PsiQuantum and Xanadu building their qubits out of light. And the photonics most people have actually touched is the fiber and optical transceivers that already shuttle data between servers in every large data center. So when a startup shows up talking about lasers, waveguides, and beating Nvidia, there are two ready-made boxes waiting for it.

It fits neither one. A photonic AI accelerator has no qubits, no superposition, no dilution refrigerator. It runs at room temperature, and the serious designs are built with the same CMOS-compatible manufacturing that makes ordinary chips. And it isn’t just moving data from one place to another; the light is doing the arithmetic. What it is instead is narrower and stranger than either guess, and explaining that difference is most of this post.

Strip a neural network down to what the hardware actually spends its time on, and you find one operation, repeated endlessly: multiplying a vector by a matrix. A GPU does that by pushing electrons through billions of transistors, one clock cycle at a time. A photonic processor does it by letting light do the multiplying. The input numbers are encoded into the intensity or phase of light, the model’s weights are encoded into the optical elements that light travels through, and the result arrives at a photodetector on the other side, where it gets converted back into a digital number. The multiplication happens as the light propagates. There is no clock grinding through it step by step, and the multiply itself costs almost no energy.

The companies chasing this take noticeably different roads to the same idea. Lightmatter, the MIT spinout that has been at it longest, published the result that made the field look like computing rather than a physics demo: a Nature paper in April 2025 describing a six-chip package with four 128-by-128 photonic tensor cores that ran ResNet, BERT and an Atari-playing reinforcement-learning agent without retraining, at close to full 32-bit accuracy, delivering 65.5 trillion 16-bit operations per second on 78 watts of electrical and 1.6 watts of optical power. Lumai, out of Oxford, skips the waveguides altogether and does it in free space: a lens fans an input vector across a membrane whose transparency encodes the matrix, which lets it multiply matrices as large as 2,048 by 2,048 in a single pass. It launched its first server family, Iris, in April 2026, with an evaluation unit that runs Llama 8B and 70B in a hybrid setup. Neurophos in Austin raised a $110 million Series A in January, led by Gates Frontier with Microsoft’s M12 participating, for an optical processing unit that packs more than a million metamaterial modulators onto one chip, elements it says are ten thousand times smaller than earlier photonic components. Q.ANT in Stuttgart is building on thin-film lithium niobate and testing with the Jülich and Leibniz supercomputing centers. And in China, the LightGen prototype from Shanghai Jiao Tong and Tsinghua crammed more than two million photonic “neurons” into about 136 square millimeters and used them to generate and edit images entirely in light.

A few things stack on top of each other here. The vocabulary is the first one. “Post-silicon,” “beyond Moore’s law,” “computing with photons”; every one of those phrases has been used to sell quantum computing to investors for a decade, and photonic AI companies borrow the same language because they’re making a cousin of the same pitch: the old physics is running out, here’s new physics.

The headlines are the second. Most of the “100x faster than Nvidia” stories are about narrow benchmarks, usually run against an A100, a GPU Nvidia launched in 2020, on tasks like image generation or classification where the prototype was designed to shine. LightGen’s own team describes it as proof that photonics can do real generative work, but only in tightly constrained domains. That nuance never survives the trip to the group chat.

The third is that “photonic AI chip” genuinely means two different products. One kind computes with light. The other kind just moves data with light, replacing copper links between GPUs with optical ones, and that second kind is where the money is landing first. Lightmatter’s own public roadmap now centers on its Passage optical interconnect, with near-packaged optics deployments this year and co-packaged optics in 2027. So when someone says a photonics company is “in production with hyperscalers,” it’s worth asking whether the light is doing math or just carrying it.

And the fourth is that the hype has already attracted the usual crowd. One Nasdaq micro-cap that started life as a pharmaceutical company, and whose filings earmarked it for cryptocurrency infrastructure, rebranded itself into photonic computing in September 2025. When a technology starts collecting those, people stop being able to tell the Nature papers from the ticker symbols.

The specific claim these companies are making is about inference, and more precisely about the compute-heavy part of inference. Running a large model splits roughly into two phases: prefill, where the model chews through your whole prompt at once and is limited by raw arithmetic, and decode, where it emits tokens one at a time and is limited by how fast it can pull weights out of memory. Lumai is explicit that it’s going after prefill. That’s where a big optical matrix multiply pays off, because of a quirk of the physics: the energy cost of converting numbers into and out of light grows with the width of the vector, while the useful work grows with the square of it. Make the matrix bigger, and the efficiency improves, which is the opposite of how digital chips behave and the reason everyone in this field is obsessed with packing more optical elements into less space.

The claimed gains are large. Lumai says its approach does the same inference math with about 90% less electricity than a GPU system. Neurophos claims up to a hundredfold improvement in performance and efficiency over current leading chips. Lightmatter’s 2025 system was estimated at roughly three times the speed and ten times the energy efficiency of an A100. All of those are company claims or early measurements, not independent production benchmarks, and they should be read that way.

What it isn’t built to do matters just as much. This is not a general-purpose replacement for a GPU. You can’t run arbitrary code on it. The non-linear parts of a neural network, the activation functions, normalization, the softmax inside attention, still get handed back to ordinary electronics. It doesn’t train frontier models. And because the light is an analog signal, it’s noisy: Lightelligence’s PACE platform averages around 7.6 bits of effective precision, and getting Lightmatter’s system to near-digital accuracy took careful calibration and a custom number format, not raw optics. Light also doesn’t solve the memory wall. If the bottleneck is moving weights out of memory, a faster multiplier doesn’t help, which is why decode-heavy workloads aren’t the target. These are limits by construction, not engineering shortfalls someone will patch next quarter.

The honest framing of where AI compute is stuck is the same one you’ll hear about every alternative chip right now: the industry has been solving an energy problem by throwing more energy at it. Bloomberg has reported that nearly half of planned US data center projects will be delayed or cancelled this year, with electricity and electrical equipment cited as a primary reason. Photonic computing’s bet is that the single most common operation in AI was never a great fit for shoving electrons through transistors, and that light can do that one operation for a sliver of the energy. That’s a much narrower and more falsifiable claim than “we’re replacing Nvidia,” and I think that’s exactly why it’s more likely to be true. It doesn’t require new physics. The lasers are the same ones already shipping by the million for optical networking, and Lumai says it needs no new materials at all. What it requires is getting precision, conversion overhead, and packaging under control at scale, which is a hard but very ordinary kind of hard.

If it works, the interesting consequence isn’t just cheaper inference. It’s that the compute-bound slice of AI inference, which today has one real hardware answer, buy more GPUs, gets a second answer built on different physics, at a moment when GPU supply and grid capacity are the two tightest constraints in the industry. The timelines are worth keeping honest, though. Lumai’s volume product is slated for 2028 and its follow-on for 2029, and Q.ANT is targeting series production in 2028. This is a claim about the 2028 data center, not next quarter’s.

The irony I keep coming back to is that the first place light is winning big in AI isn’t replacing GPUs at all. It’s helping them talk to each other faster. In the near term, photonics makes Nvidia’s racks better before it makes them obsolete.

I still don’t have a tidy one-liner for people who ask whether light-powered chips are about to kill the GPU. What I’ve settled on is something like: no, and they’re not quantum computers either, but the one piece of math that eats most of AI’s power bill might move into light, and that could matter more than either of those headlines.
