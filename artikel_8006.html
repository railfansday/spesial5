As a seasoned gardener, if you ask me to name an easy-to-grow flowering perennial that returns reliably year after year, salvia would be at the top of my list. In my own garden, my ‘Hot Lips’ shrubby salvia shrugs off summer drought, asks for very little maintenance, and blooms generously from late spring through to November. Their nectar-rich flower spikes are magnets for hummingbirds, bees, and butterflies, drenching borders in rich purples, electric blues, hot pinks, and velvety crimsons.

Yet, despite their tough and easygoing nature, winter is the one season where salvias do need a bit of care and attention. The genus Salvia is vast, encompassing over a thousand species of annuals, woody sub-shrubs, and herbaceous perennials from regions as diverse as cold alpine meadows, dry Mediterranean hillsides, and tropical cloud forests. Because their natural habitats differ dramatically, their winter cold tolerance does, too. What protects a hardy meadow sage in New England will not save a tender Mexican bush salvia during a sudden polar snap. So winter salvia care needs to be adapted accordingly.

Taking action now, in this narrow window between autumn's close and winter's first freeze, is critical. Winter damage can happen fast, and yet knowing how to winterize salvias is surprisingly easy. By understanding your variety's cold tolerance and taking action fast, you can help prime your salvia in winter. Here’s how to easily guide your garden favorites through to next spring, so you can enjoy their lush, vibrant blooms for years to come.

The precise winter protection required will depend on where you live and the type of salvia you are growing. Some are cold-hardy perennials, while others are tender, frost-sensitive tropicals. So successful winterizing begins with identifying growth habit and hardiness classification. Deciduous herbaceous salvias like meadow sage (S. nemorosa cultivars like Caradonna and May Night) and woodland sage (S. pratensis) are cold-hardy in USDA hardiness zones 3-8. These perennials die back after hard frosts, resting safely underground before surging back in spring. They need little winter protection beyond good drainage and light insulation.

Shrubby, semi-hardy salvias (S. greggii and S. microphylla) occupy a middle ground, thriving in zones 6-9. These semi-evergreen sub-shrubs develop woody stems that can be damaged by sudden cold snaps or damp winter soils. In colder microclimates within these zones, they require protective mulching and shelter from wet winds. Finally, tender subtropical and tropical salvias, including velvet-flowered Amistad and Mexican bush sage (S. leucantha), flourish only in frost-free zones 8-11. These are large, bushy salvias that are killed by hard winter freezes, so in cooler zones, they require extra winter protection.

Before you begin, test your soil moisture levels and check that water drains away readily after rain. The XLUX Long Probe Deep Soil Moisture Meter, available from Amazon is a great dual soil moisture and pH diagnostic probe, giving instant readings to help you identify waterlogged soil pockets before the ground freezes. Once your soil is identified, you’re ready to take action. Here we clarify when to cut your salvias back, how to protect the roots from cold and wet conditions, and how to ensure you see your jewel-toned flowering favorites again next year.

When salvias start looking ragged, the natural impulse is to reach for the bypass pruners and shear everything back to a tidy clump. This could be the most damaging mistake you make. Many perennial salvias (especially semi-woody and herbaceous varieties) have hollow, straw-like stems. Pruning these down in late fall leaves those open tubes exposed to the elements. Rain and melting snow funnel down the stems and collect at the base, causing crown rot and fungal decay in freeze-thaw cycles.

It’s best to leave these old stems, faded flower spikes, and withered foliage in place to keep driving rain away from the vulnerable center. This dried top growth also traps air, forming an insulating buffer that protects the dormant buds huddled around the crown. So resist all heavy pruning until early spring, when fresh shoots begin pushing through the soil. Even if you live in a warm-winter area, it’s still best to leave major pruning until spring. The only fall cuts you should make are removing broken or diseased stems. Use sharp bypass pruners like the Felco 2 Bypass Pruning Shears, available from Amazon, when removing broken salvia stems.

Whenever I mulch plants for winter, I think of tucking a blanket around a sleeping child. Mulching salvias as cold weather is coming offers a layer of winter protection. When you apply mulch before the first hard freeze, you protect the root crown from severe cold and freeze-heave. In regions with fluctuating winter weather, bare soil expands and contracts, pushing shallow crowns up and exposing roots. Fully hardy salvia don’t need much winter protection, but a light mulch won’t hurt. When it comes to the semi-hardy salvias, however, mulching is a must to help overwintering plants.

The type of mulch, and how you apply it, are critical to avoid trapping moisture around the plant. Avoid dense, heavy materials like wet grass clippings, uncomposted manure, or thick leaf mold, which pack down and suffocate the plant. Instead, apply 3-4 inches (8-10cm) of loose, breathable mulch such as shredded pine bark, clean wheat straw, dry bracken, or horticultural grit. Keep it 2-3 inches (5-8cm) from the stems to avoid trapping moisture and inviting crown rot. Buy Bell Nursery Mini Straw Bales from The Home Depot for a loose yet insulating blanket.

Cold isn’t necessarily fatal for salvia plants in winter. But too much water will take out your salvias faster than a freeze. Native to sun-drenched Mediterranean climes, salvias demand sharp drainage. When sluggish drainage keeps soil saturated, oxygen is pushed out of the roots, leaving the crown vulnerable to fungal diseases. You’ll want to take steps to avoid poorly drained soil and winter wetness for any type of salvia.

If your garden has heavy clay soil, take action now before winter sets in. Gently scrape away decomposing leaves, as wet leaf mats retain moisture over the crown. Work a handful of coarse horticultural grit, sand or perlite into the soil surface around the plant to speed up surface runoff. Back to the Roots Organic Perlite from Walmart is a great option for improving soil aeration and drainage around salvias. For container-grown plants, elevate the pots off cold, wet patio slabs. Use terracotta pot feet, pot toes, or bricks to ensure drainage holes remain clear of standing water.

If your salvia are tender varieties like Amistad or S. leucantha, treat them like the cold vulnerable plants that they are. Sub-tropical salvias can’t tolerate hard frosts or frozen soil. Leaving them unprotected in northern yards guarantees root death. Fortunately, tender salvias overwinter indoors with minimal fuss, provided you bring them into shelter before the first hard frost blackens foliage. If your tender salvias are in pots, trim the longest branches back by half, then move to an unheated garage, frost-free shed, cold frame, or cool porch, and keep at 35-45°F (2-7°C).

For tender salvias in beds, carefully dig up the root ball with a spade, keeping plenty of soil around the roots. Place into a plastic nursery container, backfill loosely with dry potting mix, and move it undercover. Use plant caddies such as the Bloem Plant Caddy from The Home Depot to wheel larger container-based salvias into the garage with zero strain or back pain. The goal is to keep the plant dormant. Water sparingly: just a cup or two a month keeps the root ball from drying out.

Even with meticulous winter care, an unseasonal polar vortex or prolonged freeze can challenge borderline-hardy salvias. Taking a few semi-ripe salvia cuttings provides a terrific insurance policy. Select healthy, non-flowering or semi-ripe shoots (or snip the flower tip off a vigorous stem). Take a 3-4-inch (8-10cm) cutting below a leaf node. Strip the leaves from the bottom half, leaving 2-3 pairs of small leaves at the top. Dip into a rooting hormone such as HydroDynamics Clonex Rooting Gel from Amazon, to seal cut stems and trigger root growth. Insert the cuttings into small pots filled with a 50/50 mix of sterile seed compost and perlite.

Water gently, then place a clear plastic dome or bag over the pot to maintain humidity. Set your baby cuttings on a bright, warm windowsill, but out of direct sunlight. They should root within 3-4 weeks. Keep them lightly watered through winter, and pinch the tips in late winter to encourage bushy growth. Just bear in mind that rooting will be slower now than it would be with cuttings taken in late summer or early fall. Grown on a sunny indoor windowsill or greenhouse bench through winter, your salvia babies will hopefully be ready to plant out next May.

By the time spring arrives, you’ll be eager for warmer days and fresh growth (and so will your salvias!). As sunny afternoons begin warming the soil, check your overwintered salvias for vibrant green shoots emerging. That is your signal that winter danger has passed. You can then prune away old standing stems just above the fresh growth nodes. If you have woody salvias, spring is the time to shape the plant. Cut it back by a third to clean up the shrub. Spring is also the time to pull back protective mulches and reintroduce sheltered container plants to outdoor sunshine. With their crowns preserved through the cold months, your salvias will soon be rewarding you with months of lush and lively color.
