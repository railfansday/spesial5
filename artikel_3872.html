Remembering the back alley brawls of The Warriors, a Rockstar city game that isn't an open world

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

The older I get, the more fascinated I somehow become by how videogames depict night, how particular technologies at particular times give rise to particular nightlives. I've already gushed about the Starlight Zone in Sonic the Hedgehog – the mild dissociation incubated by the soft, ceaseless glare of the construction yard, the eyeless brick at the level's nadir, the sleepless glitter of the skyline. Now, let me wind the clock forward, and backward, to Rockstar Toronto's The Warriors. One long night from the late '70s, sweaty and electric and menacing, fed through dank and sullen polygons from 2005.

Probably the best move Rockstar made with their adaptation of Walter Hill's film is to retain the element of phantasmagoria. Both the game and the movie open with a view of Coney Island's Wonder Wheel, its neon lamps forming a floating tracery of Morse code, cabins swinging almost imperceptibly against solid darkness. This is a kind of night in which light is more emblem than illumination, refusing to travel far, clinging to its source like a frightened ghost.

There's a lot of demand right now for a PC version of GTA 6. I'd love a proper PC port of The Warriors, first, and not just because I like coming up with fancy ways to talk about Ferris wheels. A PC port would be a timely reminder that Rockstar used to make city games that aren't open worlds, city games made of levels, and were pretty good at it. The classic thrill of GTA is to jump into a car and circle the metropolis, radio on and arm dangling, snatches of pedestrian dialogue bursting in your wake as you zip through downtown, the skyscrapers streaming by and evaporating, the world your oyster. In The Warriors, the city is No Man's Land and the night is a tightly compartmentalised maze. Your goal is to reach the other side of it on your own two feet, before a thousand rival miscreants kick your head in.

You lead a group of angry young guys of different ethnicities, wearing tribal colours and following a martial code - spiritual descendants of the stranded Greek soldiers of Xenephon's Anabais. Women barely feature in this relentlessly macho fable, and are mostly depicted as victims or seducers. Other genders are totally absent, while gay characters were considered for the film, but eventually cut. The game expands the storyline, filling in events before the titular Coney Island tearaways find themselves framed for a murder during an attempted city-wide gang uprising. But it otherwise adheres religiously to the movie's dingy and hyperbolic direction, hugging the visual language in ways that are slightly odd to return to, now that Rockstar have arguably outgrown their nerdy inferiority complex before the giants of cinema.

The game's New York is mysterious and sparsely populated: the only people abroad are other gangs and cops. The playable areas eschew affluent districts and pleasure zones; instead, you hop over a million chainlink fences in subway terminals and trashy alleyways. Most of the buildings are sealed and silent. The sky is a painted forest of chimneys, silos and stripped low cloud. You keep waiting for Batman to descend from the girders of a flyover; some of the enemy gangs certainly wouldn't look out of place in Rocksteady's Arkham. The murk isn't quite as pungent as you'd find in a PS1 flâneur like Urban Chaos, but it's respectably enveloping. I'd linger here, a plummy-accented tourist with a headful of references, cooing decorously over the stained and withdrawn shopfronts, were it not for the constant risk of getting stomped.

Back in the day, a few reviewers described The Warriors as having "deep" combat, offering a selection of directional jabs and kicks, grappling moves, terrain finishers and squad commands. I can't really see it. Certainly, The Warriors is no Sleeping Dogs. What the Warriors is is a believably chaotic brawler, with an enjoyable taste for friendly fire.

You spot a couple other Warriors surrounded by face-painted Baseball Furies, and launch into a crossbody tackle that bowls the whole group over. You straddle one of the clowns and are about to punch his teeth out when a friend lobs a trash can at the both of you. Now black-eyed and bleeding, you seize a dropped bat and swing randomly into the mob of warring outfits. Insults, taunts, chair legs and beer bottles fly back and forth. It feels like a fight. The riot spills out over each map, onto rooftops and into any businesses that have unwisely decided to stay open late. Looking back, I can see the first inklings of those spectacular drunken bar scuffles in Red Dead Redemption 2, though nobody's packing heat. The almost total absence of guns is, of course, the other thing that makes The Warriors novel, if you come to it from GTA.

I've yet to play Rockstar's latest GTA, which is now available on console worldwide. I've yet to decide how it fits into my evolving half-arsed anthropology of videogame nightlife. Still, I've spent a fair amount of time looking at screenshots and videos, and I'm finding the nights too bright. Too bright! All those shades seeping everywhere, all those rays being traced, all the spotlights and sequins, the way everything glimmers or glistens – it's too heady for me. Send me back to 2005's New York, where the darkness feels like it's part of the very mortar, and there's always a friend willing to "accidentally" hurl a bin at my head.
