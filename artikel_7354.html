AI Is transforming marketing. Can marketers prove it’s working?

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Marketing leaders have more tools, data and greater automation capabilities than ever before, but is their investment actually driving business growth?

Research cited in Incubeta's 2027 Marketing Outlook found that 92% of marketing leaders believe their measurement is precise, while 41.6% acknowledge that measurement limitations waste budget, and 70.4% believe they deploy their budgets effectively.

These findings suggest that confidence in marketing performance may be outpacing the ability to demonstrate its actual contribution to business outcomes.

The challenge becomes more urgent as artificial intelligence transforms how consumers discover products and how brands engage with them. Marketers are investing in AI-powered capabilities while the customer journey becomes increasingly difficult to track.

In 2027, marketers will face greater pressure to demonstrate return on investment.

Website traffic, search rankings and conversion data have long served as key indicators of how consumers interact with brands. While these metrics have never captured the full customer journey, they have provided marketers with a relatively consistent way to measure performance.

Consumers increasingly use conversational interfaces to research products and compare alternatives without visiting brand websites. This creates a significant measurement challenge. A brand may influence a purchasing decision through an AI-generated recommendation without receiving a website visit or another traditional engagement signal.

Answer engine optimization (AEO) encourages brands to evaluate how their products and information appear in AI-generated answers. However, visibility alone offers limited insight into business impact.

Marketers can track how frequently a brand appears in AI responses to help them better understand its presence in emerging discovery channels, but determining whether that exposure influences purchasing behavior is considerably more complicated. Marketers will need to reconsider how they evaluate the contribution of activities that generate influence without producing easily attributable clicks.

AI agents that can research products and potentially complete transactions on a consumer's behalf could further complicate attribution. Shoppers might describe their requirements to an AI assistant and receive a recommendation based on their preferences. Consumers may never interact directly with the brand until the transaction is completed.

Marketers will need to learn how brands earn consideration when algorithms increasingly influence which products are presented to customers. The consideration of a brand may depend increasingly on the quality of its product information and its relevance to specific consumer needs. Widespread adoption of agentic commerce is not guaranteed, but it illustrates why existing measurement approaches may become less effective as consumer journeys evolve.

AI is also transforming how marketing campaigns are developed and executed. Generative tools allow brands to produce and adapt creative assets at unprecedented speed. Organizations that manage campaigns across multiple markets can see considerable efficiency gains. However, producing more content does not necessarily improve marketing performance.

Incubeta's recent white paper showed that 77% of marketing leaders express confidence in their creative assets, even though many still assess effectiveness after media budgets have already been spent.

This creates a risk that AI will accelerate spending on creative executions whose actual contribution remains uncertain. Marketers shouldn't slow down creative automation but should pair it with testing built for the same pace. This will help marketers better understand which creative elements influence purchasing behavior.

Marketers can turn automation from a volume engine into a growth engine by connecting creative production directly to testing and measurement. Using that data, marketers can adapt their strategies and then share those insights back into the next round of production.

AI changes how brands have traditionally evaluated their marketing effectiveness. Advertising platforms provide detailed performance reporting but may stop measuring activities within their own environments. Now multiple platforms can claim credit for the same conversations, but investments that create demand over a longer period might receive insufficient recognition.

These limitations become especially consequential when marketing leaders use channel-level metrics to decide overall spending.

A more comprehensive approach combines marketing mix modeling (MMM) with incrementality testing and business performance data.

MMM can estimate the contribution of different marketing investments over time, while incrementality experiments help determine whether specific activities generated additional outcomes.

Combining these approaches allows marketers to evaluate spending decisions against broader commercial objectives, including profitability and customer value. The goal is to build a more reliable basis for deciding where to increase investment, where to decrease it and which activities contribute to sustainable growth.

AI's growing influence is also changing expectations for marketing leadership.

CMOs are being asked to evaluate emerging technologies while demonstrating financial accountability to CEOs and CFOs. This requires closer coordination between marketing and finance, particularly when assessing investments whose benefits may develop over time.

It also demands greater discipline around experimentation.

New capabilities should be evaluated against clearly defined business questions, with appropriate measures of success established before significant investment.

The organizations best positioned for 2027 will be those that can connect technological adoption with evidence of improved performance.

AI will continue to create opportunities for marketers to operate more efficiently and engage consumers in new ways. But greater technological sophistication will mean little if organizations cannot establish whether those investments are contributing to growth.

The defining challenge for marketing leaders will be closing the gap between what their technology can do and what they can prove it delivers.
