Now 40, Alex Honnold Must Juggle Climbing with Fatherhood and Fame. It Isn’t Always Easy.

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Alex Honnold, holding a bowl of Cheerios in one hand and a spoon of peanut butter in the other, stands at a kitchen island between his two giggling daughters and scowls at the clock. Here it is, barely 8 A.M., and he is already running late. Honnold quickly calculates the jam-packed agenda for what will be his family’s final summer day at their vacation cabin in South Lake Tahoe. Valuable seconds tick by.

After breakfast, Honnold must drop the girls off at preschool and then link up with a team of photographers and videographers at Pie Shop, a climbing crag on the other side of town. There, he will pose for a magazine cover shoot while clinging to rotund granite boulders, before speeding back to the cabin to have lunch with his aunt, who is visiting from Idaho. After that, Honnold will pack his luggage for an upcoming family trip to Italy. Finally, the Honnolds will host a few friends and neighbors for a goodbye barbecue.

The breakneck schedule is made more challenging by lingering jet lag from a recent trip to London, where Honnold delivered a motivational speech at an event hosted by his longtime sponsor the North Face.

“These days I feel like a ping-pong ball,” Honnold says. More seconds tick by.

Honnold will have to complete much of today’s agenda while answering the prying questions of a pesky journalist—me. Back in January, Honnold’s Netflix special Skyscraper Live thrust him into millions of living rooms across the planet, elevating him to a loftier stratosphere of fame. At Outside, we named Honnold an Outsider of the Year, in part, for bringing the grandeur of climbing to such a massive audience. Now, I have traveled to the Honnold family cabin to examine how a sudden spike in stardom is impacting outdoor recreation’s biggest celebrity.

How’s he coping? Well enough, I’d say, but boy does he seem exhausted. A coffee might help, but he still doesn’t touch the stuff. Honnold rubs his forehead. I notice more than a few streaks of gray dotting the sides of his close-cropped hair. Creases run across his cheeks, and deep crow’s-feet flare from both eyes. He yawns as he presents the bowl of cereal to June, who frowns. Sanni McCandless Honnold, his wife of six years, reaches over the sink to deliver Cheerios to Alice, who everyone calls Summer. There’s more giggling. McCandless Honnold presents each child with outfit options for the day. Red dress, yellow, or green? Definitely green.

Remembering his own wardrobe choices for the upcoming photo shoot, Honnold lumbers down the hallway toward the garage, which houses indoor climbing walls, free weights, and exercise equipment. He grabs several North Face shirts. Red or green? Definitely red. Back in the kitchen, a spoon clatters to the floor. The pitter-patter of approaching feet echoes from the hallway, and in a flash, the kiddos burst into the gym. Breakfast is over.

June, the elder of the two, grabs a gymnastics ring dangling from the ceiling and begins to twirl. Summer crouches on the padded floor and watches her sister.

“Yesterday was classic,” Honnold tells me later. “I woke up really early so I could do my gym workout before everyone got up. Somehow the babies heard me and woke up too and came in right when I was in the middle of everything.”

Honnold pauses before delivering a playful imitation of his wife. “Sanni is like, ‘You’re finally home from your London trip and all you want to do is climb?’’’

All you want to do is climb? may seem like an odd question for the world’s most famous rock climber. But these days, scaling rock is just one of the many tasks on Honnold’s daily agenda. In the wake of Skyscraper Live, Honnold’s life has become a dizzying array of paid speaking engagements, sponsor meet-and-greets, recording segments for his two podcasts Climbing Gold and Planet Visionaries, video shoots for his Outside TV show Out There with Alex Honnold, and other media hits. He graciously agrees to a high percentage of these interviews—yes, including Outside—which leads to an impossibly tight schedule.

He also has family commitments: dropping his daughters off at school, feeding them, and the usual weekend outings to playdates and playgrounds. In casual conversation, Honnold affectionately refers to his daughters as “the babies,” even though June is 4 and Summer is 2.

“The babies were harder this summer than last year,” Honnold says. He recounts a recent backpacking trip that sounds familiar to anyone who has attempted to adventure with small children. There was crying, smiles, ups, and downs. In total, the family covered just a few miles over the course of two days.

“You get those moments where it feels wholesome and fun and everything is cute,” Honnold says. “And other times when it feels like a tremendous amount of labor. I suspect that, for me, the best times are still ahead.”

So, back to rock climbing. Alas, it’s a somewhat touchy subject. On many days, climbing is an activity that Honnold squeezes in, pounds out, or cuts short. Daylong ascents of gleaming granite are often replaced by two-hour sweat sessions in his home gym. Honnold admits it is not what he predicted he’d be doing at age 40.

“This year I can count on one hand the number of times I’ve really been proud of something I’ve climbed on rock,” Honnold says. “The frustration of sucking is the essence of middle age.”

Honnold pauses before laughing. “Middle age is the ratio of how often you fall below your expectations, compared to how often you surpass them,” he adds. “Unfortunately, my standards for my climbing haven’t shrunk. I think it would be easier if they had.”

It’s well past go-time, and Honnold leads the charge toward his green Rivian SUV waiting out front. I snatch the middle seat in the back, flanked on each side by the girls in their car seats. Today, breakfast will be finished on the way to school. McCandless Honnold hands out fruit pouches. The girls squeal with glee.

There are hugs, kisses, and see-you-soons, and Honnold drives out onto the main road, toward preschool, Pie Shop, and what will be a serene afternoon in the Sierra Nevada. I process the whirlwind morning: the chaos, crunched schedule, spilled cereal, and Honnold’s occasionally futile pursuit of outdoor time. It’s all so much to take in, and yet, it feels so incredibly familiar. It’s the grind that millions of outdoors-loving moms and dads, myself included, live every day. We squeeze in that hike, that bike ride, that jog in between Zoom meetings and school pickups. We do so knowing that we’ll never quite be as fast or as strong as we were in our twenties, when our carefree lives afforded us the freedom to play outdoors all day, day after day, with zero consequences.

Honnold is one of us now, I conclude. Ever since Honnold burst into the mainstream consciousness with his death-defying free solo ascents, journalists—including yours truly—have sought to explain why he is so different from the average person. Now, at age 40, he is a Netflix star, a millionaire, and at the absolute zenith of his popularity. And yet, Alex Honnold is potentially more like us than at any point in his life.

Honnold has now been part of our lives for the better part of two decades. The iconic image of him tiptoeing across Thank God Ledge on the face of Half Dome graced the cover of National Geographic way back in September of 2011. The 60 Minutes profile of Honnold, with footage of him free soloing the Sentinel in Yosemite, beamed to TV audiences a month later. His memoir, Alone on the Wall, came out in 2015. And, of course, 2018’s Free Solo (and its Academy Award) made him outdoor recreation’s official crossover star of the 2020s.

Since Free Solo, the mainstream has sought to pinpoint the roots of Honnold’s fearlessness. Over the years, these explanations have ranged from the absurd (his amygdala is damaged!) to the offensive (he doesn’t have feelings!) to the highly speculative (he’s on the autism spectrum).

“That explanation has always felt off to me,” Honnold says when I present it to him. “I know I might be an unusual person, but I’ve taken a lot of tests, and I’m pretty sure I’m not on the spectrum.”

In a 2016 story for Nautilus, Honnold subjected himself to a bizarre psychological test, in which neuroscientists showed him photographs of gruesome or scary images and then placed him in an MRI machine to study his amygdala, the part of the brain that supposedly triggers a fear response. The scans seemed to confirm that Honnold’s brain responds less to fear than the average person. But a decade after the test, Honnold isn’t convinced that his ability to free solo is just the byproduct of some biological quirk.

“People always want simple explanations for anything that seems out of the ordinary. If someone sees an exceptional athletic performance, or something that seems different, they want to know that the person doing it is different,” Honnold says. “And, you know, the obvious explanation that it’s the product of lots of hard work and years of practice is just unsatisfactory. Because who the heck wants to dedicate their entire lives to doing that?”

“It’s just so much easier to say, ‘That guy is weird,’” he adds.

Honnold is unwavering in explaining the root of his superpower. For 25 or so years, climbing rock consumed every waking hour of his life. Unencumbered by preschool drop-offs or honey-do lists, he threw himself at big walls in Yosemite and abroad, climbing hundreds of days each year, year after year. His physical gifts helped him surpass his peers on the toughest ascents.

Was he ever scared? You bet—almost every day, he says. But, while clinging to the side of cliffs thousands of times over, Honnold found ways to silence his fear.

So, which explanation is more accurate: cerebral oddity or hard work? Longtime climber and film producer Cedar Wright, who has climbed with Honnold for two decades, says it’s closer to the latter—with an important caveat. Honnold chose to pursue free soloing, while most people—heck, most climbers—think it’s insane.

“He and myself and the small group of free soloists out there were of the mindset that soloing really matters, and is worth the risk,” Wright said. “A lot of really good climbers, like Tommy Caldwell or Adam Ondra, want nothing to do with it. But to us, the most badass thing you can do is climb without a rope. You kind of have to believe that if you want to keep going with it.”

This singular focus created, shall we call it, a relatability gap between Honnold and John Q. Public. Most stories about Honnold from his twenties and thirties mentioned that he ate and slept and filled Gatorade bottles with his own urine inside a 2002 Ford Econoline van. And in interviews from this era, Honnold comes across as, at best, robotic. At worst, an alien from another planet.

But the oddball lifestyle is ultimately what propelled him to National Geographic, the Academy Awards, and into our consciousness. Wright climbed with Honnold during the mid-aughts, when they were both dirtbags living in Yosemite. Even amongst his peers, Honnold stood out because of his utter love of climbing.

“There was a point when literally nobody on the planet was climbing more than Honnold, in terms of sheer volume and days in a row on rock,” Wright said. “The guy was on a whole different level of dedication and focus.”

Alas, those days are now long in the rearview. While Honnold’s motivation and passion and talent for climbing remain, the free time to climb every single day, day after day, has vanished, squashed by his many commitments. This tension is at the heart of Alex Honnold’s life in 2026.

The Rivian zips across South Lake Tahoe as Honnold points out landmarks. We pass a road that leads to Lover’s Leap, the local 600-foot wall where Honnold made some of his first free solo climbs in his early twenties. On the horizon sits Mount Tallac, where Honnold was saved by backcountry rescuers after he slipped and fell down a couloir when he was a teenager.

As kids, Honnold and his older sister, Stasia, spent summers at the vacation cabin in South Lake Tahoe. There were camping trips, baseball games, and afternoons spent at the nearby beach—usually with no parents in sight. The cabin also represents an eternal connection to Honnold’s father, Charles, who died of a sudden heart attack in July 2004, when Honnold was 19. Charles’ father built the cabin in a gated community a few hundred yards from the California/Nevada state line. Now, Honnold says, going to the cabin brings up memories of his dad.

“In some ways I feel his loss more now than when I was 19,” he says. “I realize that my kids never got to meet their grandfather. And he would have been fucking awesome with them. He’d probably be retired by now and would want to spend all summer here with the two of them.”

The Honnold family cabin is important for other reasons, too. On days when the Zoom calls are few and the indoor gym has grown stale, Honnold can bolt from the cabin to breathe fresh air, climb on rock, or push his body on a trail, while still making it back for dinnertime. Honnold’s handful of 2026 outings have included more than a few local hot spots: Space Invaders, Eagle Lake Buttress, and the Tahoe Triple Crown, a punishing 18-mile out-and-back up three high peaks.

“You have to do hundreds of climbs like these to feel like you’ve done something epic,” Honnold says. “That’s what it takes for me to feel normal these days. You can’t be a real climber if you don’t climb rock.”

Of course, in true Honnold fashion, these short outings are often high-octane sufferfests that would cripple even a seasoned climber. And Honnold will push himself past the limits that most of us require for sleep and recovery in order to squeeze in an epic day. In the summer of 2025 in South Lake Tahoe, Honnold made regular outings to The Incredible Hulk, a 2,000-foot vertical wall in California’s Sawtooth Ridge, with routes rated up to 5.13. The trailhead is a two-hour drive from the family cabin, so Honnold would wake up at 2 A.M. in order to complete the round trip and be home for dinner. Longtime friend Chris Weidner would sometimes call Honnold on the phone during the drives to and from the spire for a friendly chat.

“It was such a crazy schedule—for most of us, doing that climb fresh would be extremely challenging, and here he is doing it after hard days of climbing in his home gym,” Weidner said. “Yes, he is very committed to his family, but his intense drive and relentless need for climbing volume is still the same.”

And climbing still provides Honnold with an emotional salve, much as it did when he was in his twenties. In the 2015 book, Alone on the Wall, Honnold described soloing the 5.12b Rainbow Wall in Nevada’s Red Rock Canyon, and how the ascent helped him work through a relationship that was not going well.

I ask if he still seeks out tough climbs to help process the frustration of middle-aged life. Honnold shrugs.

“I actually have a healthier approach to that type of thing now than when I was younger, because I would basically channel my inner angst to do climbs I was proud of,” he says. “It almost became a process, because I’d have to harness those moments of peak feeling to do a climb I cared about. I eventually realized I can just go out and climb the same way, without the angst.”

Honnold adds, “Being in a committed and healthy relationship for ten years also helps.”

But there are still times when Honnold’s emotions and his climbing intertwine. He recounts the story of a recent free solo gone wrong near the family’s full-time home in Las Vegas.

“For whatever reason, I was upset about something. Home life was hard, whatever, kids and stress,” Honnold says. “So, in the middle of the afternoon, I’m like, ‘Fuck it, I’m going soloing’ as a reason to get out of the house.”

But alas, there was a high-wind advisory in Vegas. The smart thing to do was to climb indoors, but Honnold was undeterred. He drove to Black Velvet Canyon trailhead in Red Rock Canyon and pointed himself up a towering route. He began free soloing a wall rated 5.10d—impossibly dangerous for most climbers without a rope—and soon realized that conditions were less than optimal.

“I’m up there just getting hammered by the wind. It’s insane,” Honnold says. “And I turn the corner and all of a sudden this huge gust, this updraft, blows chalk in my face, and the gusts are even harder, and I’m on a prow in the center of this 2,000-foot rock face.”

A gust billowed up Honnold’s shorts and knocked his iPhone out of his pocket. The device tumbled hundreds of feet to the valley floor, breaking on impact. The phone is the only car key for Honnold’s Rivian SUV, which was also parked at the foot of the cliff. He didn’t have enough daylight to summit the climb and then hike back down. Instead, he downclimbed the wall’s 16 pitches without a rope—a sketchy undertaking. He eventually found the shattered device in the sand. Miraculously, it still turned the car on.

“The next day I’m at the Apple Store buying a new phone and it hits me, what an idiot I was,’’ Honnold says. “I was like, ‘I should have just gone on a jog or something.’”

The whole ordeal sounds horrifying. But Honnold laughs and grins as he recounts the memory.

Gym time, dad adventures, and the occasional death-defying free solo simply hold Honnold over through the busiest part of the year: the spring and summer. Every fall, he carves out two weeks or so for a hard-charging adventure on rock. In 2025, he spent two weeks in Yosemite, where he and Tommy Caldwell completed a free ascent of the Platinum Wall on El Capitan, among other hard climbs. In August 2026, Honnold will spend two weeks in Europe, completing ascents on the Eiger, Matterhorn, and tough rock walls in the Dolomites.

I ask Honnold if his relationship with climbing has changed at all, now that it’s something he has to schedule into his complicated lifestyle.

“In some ways, climbing is like my only opportunity to feel like I’m good at something,” he says. Being a celebrity, a paid speaker, and even a parent, Honnold admits, doesn’t always come easily. “With parenting, especially, it’s like you always feel like you’re bad at it. You know what I mean?” he adds.

It’s something I know all too well. I then ask Honnold if, at age 40, he’s just as good at rock climbing as at any point in his twenties or thirties.

“I’m probably a better climber today, in some ways, than I’ve ever been,” Honnold says. “I’m probably stronger than I was at age 30. I suspect I could do most of the climbs that I’ve ever done, if I had the time to do them again.”

Every 40-year-old weekend warrior can relate. If I only had the time. Honnold shrugs and takes it all in. He’s philosophical about the push and pull of middle age. And he tells me that, in so many words, this phase of life, with the kids, the commitments, the schedule, was, to a degree, planned. It’s the life that he and McCandless Honnold sought for themselves when they became a couple a decade ago.

“Yeah, I’ve probably given up some of my best years of climbing,” he says. “Some doors open, others close. But it was always part of our plan.”

Honnold didn’t develop this adult mindset overnight, of course. The journey from van-dwelling dirtbag to committed father capable of prioritizing family and career over recreation took time and effort. And it hasn’t always been easy, Sanni McCandless Honnold tells me.

“I think, like anyone, he misses the freedom of having all the time in the world and no responsibilities, and not having to juggle the complexities of his life,” she says. “And at the same time, I don’t think he’d go back to that life.”

McCandless Honnold, 34, met Honnold at a book signing in 2016 in Seattle, and the two quickly became a couple, traveling to climbing trips in Europe and California together. When directors began filming Free Solo six months later, they chronicled the early stages of their relationship, and captured the tension of how Honnold’s lifestyle—you know, the van and all of that climbing—collided with the requirements of partnership.

Some outdoor athletes never grow out of a lifestyle that prioritizes daily doses of adrenaline and adventure. But Honnold has, she says, even if he’s had to work at it. “Alex doesn’t suffer from the Peter Pan syndrome that I’ve seen a lot of adventurous people have,” she says. “Even back then, he had the ability to see his life longterm, like, ‘Yeah, I’ll probably get married and have kids. I want to provide for my family.’”

The first turning point in this evolution, McCandless Honnold says, occurred immediately after Free Solo. Honnold had spent years scouting El Capitan for the ascent, and then months meticulously preparing for the climb. When it was over, she says, he faced a big question: What’s next? The answer included marriage, buying a house, and traveling around the world to speak about the film to audiences.

Another major turn occurred in 2022 after the birth of June. McCandless Honnold underwent an emergency cesarean section, and June spent time hooked up to a ventilator in the intensive care unit. Perhaps the biggest turning point, she says, happened in 2024, when Summer was born. McCandless Honnold suffered a painful infection just after delivery and did two stints in intensive care. Then, several weeks after returning home, she fell and broke her elbows and spent more time in the hospital.

“I handed him a two-week-old and a two-year-old, and he went home without Mom,” she says. “It was one of those periods in life where you have to learn ruthless prioritization.”

Over the course of several months, Honnold molded his life around the needs of the kids, staying up late to mix formula, waking up early to change diapers, and sacrificing workouts to keep his wife off her feet. Months turned into years, and as McCandless Honnold explains, Honnold learned how to juggle the many demands in his life in a way that didn’t put the entire burden of child rearing on her shoulders.

He also underwent a period of emotional growth, she says, progressing from a left-brained rationalist into a person capable of acknowledging and understanding the feelings of others. The antithesis of a robot, she assures me.

“He was never emotionless; he was never a robot,” she says. “He’s now more willing to communicate his feelings and to engage with the feelings of others.”

When I present this question to Honnold (Are you more empathetic now than you were at age 20?), he shrugs and delivers a hard no. “I’ve always understood what people are thinking, or why they’re thinking it, even if I don’t always care all that much,” he says.

But others in his life disagree. Weidner, who has climbed with Honnold for more than two decades, said he often suffered the wrath of Honnold’s impatience early in his career. Honnold was a better climber, and would chide Weidner when he took too long to ascend a pitch.

In the fall of 2025, the two reconnected for an ascent of the Middle Cathedral Rock in Yosemite, and Weidner noticed a major shift in Honnold’s vibe.

“He used to be like, ‘C’mon dude, it’s only 5.12, what’s your problem?’” Weidner says. “Now he is a lot more willing to be a kind and patient climbing partner. Instead of scolding me, it’s more like, ‘C’mon Chrissy-Poo, I’m ready to climb again.’”

Maybe getting older isn’t that bad. Still, Honnold’s balancing act between climbing and middle-aged life is ever-present, and every few months brings a new wrinkle for the Honnold family to navigate. McCandless Honnold spent much of 2026 writing her first book, a self-help title called Mosaic Theory: How to Craft a Life of Many Summits. She says that, as her own ambitions and career have grown, Honnold has had to take on more family responsibilities.

“On any given day or week, things may not appear that balanced between us, but over months and years it gets balanced out,” she says. “And I trust my partner is going to understand when I’m like, ‘OK, it’s my turn for free time.’”

Thus, in some ways, they were prepared for the dramatic increase in non-climbing opportunities that came Honnold’s way after Skyscraper Live beamed across Netflix. The streaming giant aired the ascent live on January 24, and over the course of the next week, it generated 11.4 million views. In the weeks afterward, requests for interviews and engagement poured in from all corners of the globe.

But in other ways, Skyscraper Live changed Honnold’s life in ways he was not prepared for—something I witness as he pulls up to the girls’ school on the western corner of South Lake Tahoe. Honnold helps his daughters walk up to the cute green building, and then briskly strolls back to the Rivian. Just as he’s about to reach the car, an enthusiastic passerby stops him, extends his hand, and begins talking. Honnold smiles and nods as he casts a few nervous glances at his watch.

“Going out in public has gotten harder,” Honnold says after getting back to the car. “Fan sightings happen all the time now. I’m still not used to it.”

It used to be that only other climbers or outdoor rec aficionados would stop him for a handshake or a photo. After Skyscraper Live, it’s everyone. Several days earlier, he tells me, while cycling on the local bike path with June, Honnold was stopped by a series of tourists, all of whom wanted his photograph. The encounter ended the bike ride prematurely. Losing the ability to blend into a crowd, Honnold says, has been a struggle.

“Every once in a while, you’re like, ‘Did I make the wrong choice? Did I botch this whole thing? Should I still be living alone in my van?’” Honnold says. He laughs. “And the obvious answer is no, because I don’t want to be a 65-year-old guy living in my van alone.”

Honnold did not foresee the changes to his life when he summited the metal spire atop Taipei 101 and waved to the tens of thousands of awestruck onlookers below. Like free soloing El Capitan, ascending Taipei 101 was the finish line of a years-long process. Honnold first had the idea to climb a building live on television in 2013. The following year, he worked with National Geographic on the idea, eventually homing in on Taipei 101, which at the time was the world’s fourth-tallest building. But the project fell apart when the media empire learned the cost of staging the stunt.

But Honnold never forgot about the crazy idea. After the pandemic, he began working with UK production company Plimsoll Studios, eventually filming two successful adventure series: Arctic Ascent and The Devil’s Climb. The company’s head of adventure filmmaking, James Smith, told me that Honnold frequently brought up the skyscraper idea during pitch sessions.

“Alex and I end up spending a lot of time together in these remote places,” Smith told me. “And ever since I’ve known him, he has mentioned the idea of climbing a skyscraper on TV.”

Honnold and Smith connected for a preparatory meeting about the project in 2023, and the following year Smith traveled to Los Angeles to present the ideas to producers at Netflix. Netflix bought the idea, but Smith’s work wasn’t done—they had to choose a building. By 2023, Taipei 101 was no longer one of the world’s top-ten tallest skyscrapers. Once they winnowed the group down to four, Smith reached out to see if they could have access.

After months of outreach, Taipei 101 emerged as the final choice. Then, Smith had to figure out how to film the climb in a way that would make it compelling to the masses. His plan involved a small army of cameramen, riggers, ropes professionals, and a safety crew.

“We put together one of the most ambitious camera rigs in TV history,” Smith said. “The camera is 1,700 feet high, and we had cameras covering every foot of it.”

The crew suffered a minor catastrophe when, on the January 23 shoot date, they awoke to see the building shrouded in fog and buffeted by steady rain. After pushing it back a day, they enjoyed perfect conditions for Honnold’s climb. The cameramen strapped into their harnesses and zipped up ropes on the building’s facade. Honnold and McCandless Honnold stepped out of a white van at street level and embraced, and then Honnold began climbing the building’s metal frame toward the top, and toward a new future.

“You could tell he was having a lot of fun on a worthwhile endeavor,” Smith says. “The success of Skyscraper Live at some point comes down to Alex’s authenticity.”

The first inkling I got that Skyscraper Live was about to boost Honnold into a new level of fame came a few days before the stunt when I watched a 30-minute interview with him on CNN. Then, in the days after the climb, Honnold was everywhere: Jimmy Kimmel Live, the pages of Hollywood trade magazine Variety, and even on the popular podcast hosted by comedian Hasan Minhaj. The media crush kept rolling, and in fast succession Honnold did lengthy one-on-one interviews with Rich Roll, British celebrity Jay Shetty, entrepreneur Steven Bartlett, and others.

In addition to interview requests, requests for paid public speaking engagements also rolled in, many of which Honnold agreed to. After a few months, Honnold and his team decided to raise his speaking rates. In 2019, Honnold charged a low five-figure fee to speak. For 2026, Big Speak, a website that allows you to book a motivational speaker, lists Honnold’s fee at above $50,000. Another site, WSB, estimates his fee at $70,000.

Prior to Skyscraper Live, the New York Times reported that his fee for the ascent would be $750,000—noticeably low for a professional athlete. But Honnold emphasized that he would have happily climbed the building for free. The comparatively low upfront pay, he says, has also generated a substantial uptick in revenue afterward.

“Every segment of earning has grown a bit each year: sponsorship, corporate speaking, and then TV and film stuff,” Honnold says. “Taipei 101 just turned everything up quite a bit.”

In many ways, Honnold is charting his own path as a crossover outdoor athlete, who earns paychecks from doing jobs that are only tangentially related to his sport. In a famous scene in Free Solo, Honnold tells a class of schoolchildren in 2016 that his earnings from rock climbing are comparable to what a successful dentist would earn. Now, Honnold says, that’s no longer the case.

“I think I’ve exceeded that of any normie jobs,” he says. “Maybe if you owned a dental franchise.”

Honnold’s long-term financial plan has been to earn enough from climbing to never have to work a normal job in his life. Honnold created that goal long before he had three other mouths to feed and a personal charity to back. The Honnold Foundation supports solar power programs across the globe, and in 2026 the foundation committed $11 million in donations, its largest amount ever.

Now, Honnold says, the lifetime earning goal is within reach.

“I’ll hit that lifetime savings goal in the next year or two,” he says.

I ask Honnold if the flying, the showing up, and the stress are worth it.

“The pro climbing thing is going to wind down at some point—I’ve always known it,” he says.

For three whole hours Honnold is a remarkably good model for the camera lens. He clings to the side of massive boulders, tilting his head this way and that, as a photographer belts out curious orders. The monotone, mechanical personality parodied by Instagram memes is nowhere to be seen. Honnold cracks jokes and tells stories as though he’s addressing a room full of software salesmen in Orlando.

I ask Honnold if the strategic dad adventures, scheduled between work obligations, are a permanent part of his life as an adventurer. Honnold shakes his head: this phase in his life doesn’t last forever, he reminds me. The babies will get older, the corporate speaking engagements may evaporate, his free time to climb will someday return.

“The things I’m stressed about, I know that they are temporary,” Honnold says. “At some point I’ll be able to slow things down a bit.”

Whether or not that’s true is, of course, up for debate. Those who know him best are dubious that Honnold will ever back away from the breakneck balancing act. In many ways, the demands of family and work, when added to climbing, give Honnold the level of commitment he needs to thrive.

“With Alex it’s gonna be nonstop—not just with climbing but also with work and family,” Weidner says. “He has always seemed really uncomfortable with any downtime. To him, it’s just a waste.”

Wright puts it more bluntly: “Alex would be miserable with an easy life.”

“Alex wants to be charging headlong at all times at everything he’s doing,” Wright adds. “He’s just taken that ethic for climbing and is now applying it to the crazy opportunities that are open to him. I think it’s impossible for him to say no.”

Thus, there are likely to be more flights, more hurried ascents, and more gray hairs in Honnold’s future—in addition to another media project that showcases his skill and bravery to the widest audience possible. As we drive back to the family cabin, I ask Honnold if he’s done soloing buildings, to which he replies with an emphatic no. The next chapter of Skyscraper Live hinges on finding the right skyscraper. The world’s highest building, the 2,717-foot Burj Khalifa in Dubai, is the obvious choice. But the war with Iran has made that structure abnormally complex.

“There are five or six others in China that are also a bit of a risk,” he says. “Not off the table, but not preferred.”

The energy is high at the family cabin when we arrive: neighbors buzzing by to say their goodbyes, a cadre of relatives in town to share the afternoon, and McCandless Honnold preparing afternoon snacks for the kids. Honnold and I leave the chaos for the garage climbing gym to continue our conversation. As we bounce from climbing to money to fame, I can tell that the pull of his life is feeling more like a strong tug. The babies will be home soon. Then, houseguests will arrive. Then, packing for Europe. His phone buzzes with texts and missed calls.

We wrap up the interview, and Honnold gives me a firm handshake. He’s been more than generous with his time. And now it’s off to the next family outing, corporate speaking event, or climbing crag, to do what he has always done best, which is being Alex Honnold.
