American science is broken. Meet the people fixing it

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

After a massive realignment of federal science priorities, researchers are rebuilding — and reimagining — what has been damaged

The February sun was setting outside her family’s West Virginia home when Rebecca Lindsey found out she was being fired in 75 minutes. Lindsey, who until early 2025 managed the federal government’s climate.gov website, had just begun to think her job might be safe during the second Trump administration.

“Every week, it was like, ‘This is the week we’re all going to get fired,’ ” says Lindsey, who had worked as a contractor and later a civil servant at the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration for nearly 15 years. “Every time you would hear the rumor and then it wouldn’t happen, you would think … maybe this is all just talk.”

But at 3:45 p.m. on February 27, 2025, Lindsey and around 800 of her NOAA colleagues received a mass termination email saying they no longer “fit the Agency’s current needs.” In June, Lindsey’s sister and the data visualization lead for the project, Mary Lindsey, was fired alongside the remaining climate.gov contractors. The website URL was redirected, hiding the site — and its decades’ worth of climate visualizations, educational tools and extreme weather trackers — from the public.

The Lindsey sisters decided to fight back. Along with another terminated NOAA employee, they split a $65,000 grant from the nonprofit Surge Climate Talent and launched a crowdfunding campaign to rebuild and update climate.gov. The site, now called climate.us, notched one million views in its first month.

But as proud as they are of their work, the project is a patch, not a fix. Rebecca Lindsey predicts it would take at least seven employees and $1.8 million annually to resume 80 percent of the old site’s work. “Climate.gov was more than just a website; it was a climate literacy program,” she says. To rescue the full mission, they still have a long way to go.

The Lindsey sisters are far from the only tale of makeshift resilience. Over the last year, untold numbers of U.S. scientists and their supporters have rebuilt data dashboards, formed recommendation groups for childhood vaccines and screenings, and doled out research funds for hundreds of stranded early-career scientists. They’ve reconstituted committees to advise space missions, rehomed key reports on climate and Arctic science, and even turned to social media to spread public health alerts in the wake of a massive realignment of the federal science enterprise.

“I don’t think anyone could have foreseen the amount of destruction and deliberate breaking down of norms and infrastructure” over the last two years, says Christina Madison, a clinical pharmacist and public health communicator known on social media as The Public Health Pharmacist.

Since January 2025, the second Trump administration has frozen or rescinded billions of dollars in research funding on topics from climate change to vaccine hesitancy and is trying to redirect it — often without congressional approval — to administration priorities such as artificial intelligence and quantum computing. During deliberations for the FY2026 and FY2027 budgets, the White House proposed slashing future science funding by one-third.

“When this was first starting, it really, truly felt like an alternate universe,” says Sean O’Leary, a pediatric infectious disease researcher at the University of Colorado Anschutz. “The science was always what drove the decision making, and now we’re at a place where it’s not.”

In 2025 alone, the federal government shed some 20 percentof its science workforce. And in May 2026, the Office of Management and Budget proposed a sweeping set of rules to expand the authority of federal political appointees in selecting science grants.

Yet some researchers see the destruction as an opportunity to rebuild their corners of U.S. science — or even reimagine it, says Michael Osterholm, an epidemiologist at the University of Minnesota in Minneapolis. “If there’s any good that can come out of what has been a horrible situation, it’s that it’s given us time and provided a pause to think through clearly: Is this the way we want to continue?”

University of Ottawa ecologist Julien Martin spent a decade building a respected lab that probed how social behaviors affect the evolution of bushy-tailed, cat-sized rodents called marmots. So when the National Science Foundation began its sweeping grant cuts, Martin found himself doing something unexpected: uploading cute marmot videos to OnlyFans, an online video subscription platform better known for hosting pornography.

The effort, called OnlyMarms, netted $6,000 — a mere drop in the funding bucket. But the project went viral, leading outside supporters to launch a cryptocurrency token that earned the lab an additional $150,000. And direct donations, including an anonymous $300,000 gift, gave the lab enough to fund one Ph.D. student’s entire degree.

“When you see that so much money could be obtained by doing essentially nothing about science, it’s quite shocking,” Martin says. He welcomes the support, but having to spend so much time on non-research tasks has also felt demeaning. “Sometimes, you have to question: Is the science funding broken? Are we just competing for pennies?”

In 2025, the National Institutes of Health and the NSF canceled or suspended more than 7,800 existing grants, totaling billions of dollars. (Some 5,200 grants were later reinstated.) Each agency issued roughly a quarter fewer grants in 2025 than their previous 10-year averages.

Universities and research centers were hit hard. “It was really dire,” says Emily Young, who leads medical research grantmaking at the San Diego-based Prebys Foundation. In phone calls with researchers, many told her they had halted ongoing experiments, such as developing measles vaccines for people with autoimmune disorders.

But the thing that really stood out, Young says, was how the sudden drop in funding was affecting postdocs and early-career researchers. “Young people were going from feeling like they had an avenue for a really promising career … to feeling like they were in a worthless career.”

So, in May 2025, the foundation awarded $7 million total in emergency grants to seven local institutions, including San Diego State University, the University of California, San Diego and Scripps Research. The grants targeted more than 80 research programs involving early- and mid-career scientists that were facing hundreds of millions of dollars in lost federal funding.

Larger science philanthropies also stepped into the breach. The Gordon and Betty Moore Foundation gave $55 million total to 30 universities to support 400 postdoctoral researchers. The Alfred P. Sloan and Spencer foundations, among others, partnered to provide 83 quick-turnaround grants of around $25,000 each to researchers whose NSF education grants had been canceled.

Some wealthy universities also offered bridge funding to their researchers; Johns Hopkins University gave awards up to $250,000 to individuals with unexpected funding gaps, and the Yale School of Medicine arranged to fund up to 90 percent of terminated or frozen NIH grants.

After the first bout of funding chaos, science philanthropy Schmidt Sciences announced a more ambitious approach: to develop the science themselves. In January 2026, they revealed plans to construct three ground-based observatories and a space telescope larger than NASA’s Hubble by the end of the decade. The space telescope alone will cost hundreds of millions of dollars and provide open-access data to scientists worldwide, with a special focus on exoplanets and transient events like supernovas.

In November 2026, the philanthropy opened its first round of funding for early-career scientists using the observatory system; individual awards will total up to $2.5 million each.

“Originally, the hope was we would play a more classic philanthropic role,” says Arpita Roy, an astronomer who directs the Astrophysics & Space Center at Schmidt Sciences. But given the federal funding landscape, she says, “it became pretty clear to us, if we really think these things need to exist in the world … then we need to build them out.”

While private funding can be a lifeline, Young says, it’s still a temporary measure at best. “We are not going to fill the vacuum of federal funding,” she says. “There is simply not enough philanthropic resource out there to do that.”

In 1995, the American Academy of Pediatrics and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention made a consequential decision: Rather than publish independent recommendations for childhood vaccines, as they had since the 1960s, they would issue one set jointly. Over the next 30 years, a group called the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices advised the CDC, with academy input, to expand its recommendations to include chickenpox, hepatitis A and pneumococcal infections. Cases of the three dropped by more than 90 percent, saving thousands of lives.

But in June 2025, Secretary of Health and Human Services Robert F. Kennedy Jr., an outspoken vaccine critic, fired all 17 members of ACIP and replaced many with vaccine skeptics. Kennedy also dismissed the academy representatives that informed the group.

“Essentially, we had no voice anymore,” recalls O’Leary, the pediatric infectious disease researcher at the University of Colorado Anschutz. He also serves as the AAP’s immunization schedule editor. “So we made a decision to not participate until we could say, ‘OK, this is a body that can be trusted.’ ”

In July 2025, the academy and other public health groups sued over the ACIP firings. The committee was unable to meet due to the lawsuit, and over the next six months, the CDC removed recommendations from its childhood vaccination schedule including hepatitis A and B, COVID-19 and meningococcal disease. The American Academy of Pediatrics issued its own schedule including those vaccines in January 2026.

The loss of trustworthy federal recommendations leaves “an impossible gap to fill,” O’Leary says. But when it came to stepping in, “from my perspective, there was not an alternative.” The academy has since collaborated with the Vaccine Integrity Project, a new group led by Osterholm, to conduct the scientific reviews that inform vaccine recommendations.

Vaccines weren’t the only childhood health matter to get a shakeup. In April 2025, HHS terminated the Advisory Committee on Heritable Disorders in Newborns and Children, which formulates newborn screening recommendations for genetic disorders, many of which can be deadly or disabling. Currently, some 98 percent of U.S.-born infants are screened at birth.

When the genetics community raised concerns, Cynthia Powell, a retired pediatric geneticist at the University of North Carolina School of Medicine in Chapel Hill, gathered around 15 representatives of professional medical societies to create the Newborn Screening Collaborative. With support from the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics, the group began meeting in early 2026.

While the collaborative cannot give direct recommendations to HHS, it hopes to conduct evidence reviews for states and the small, parent-led advocacy groups that form around rare diseases, Powell says. It plans to focus on conditions that already have treatments under development, like a rare metabolic disorder known as Sanfilippo syndrome type A.

“This Newborn Screening Collaborative is another effort not to leave a complete void,” says Jewel Mullen, an epidemiologist at the University of Texas at Austin who was lead editor on a May 2025 newborn screening report for the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine.

Other researchers are filling gaps in awareness about public health threats. A philanthropy-backed group called the Evidence Collective posted about the diarrhea-causing cyclosporiasis outbreak before the CDC’s Health Alert Network did. The collective, made up of more than 30 medical communicators, has more than 10 million followers across social media and notched 11 billion impressions in 2025.

“I don’t think that we will ever be able to fully replace the infrastructure that was there through the federal government,” says Madison, a founding member of the collective. But “there is a concerted effort now to look at: How do we take the momentum that we have with these organizations, and then make it sustainable?”

Although stopgap measures are succeeding so far, scientists see them as Band-Aids at best. “We’re going to do it for as long as we’re needed,” Osterholm says. But “I look forward to the day when we’re not going to be needed anymore.”

In April 2025, nearly 400 scientists working on the National Climate Assessment, a recurring, congressionally mandated report, received an email that it was being reevaluated and they were being released from their work. One of them — Rutgers University climate scientist Bob Kopp — wasn’t surprised. “I don’t think you have to speculate very far” to know why, Kopp says. He cites a directive in the White House’s 2027 budget request to “stop wasting biological and environmental research resources on climate change.”

However, “there was an immediate and deep sentiment by the science community to continue the work,” says environmental engineer Costa Samaras of Carnegie Mellon University in Pittsburgh, who had also been involved with the climate report. He and colleagues asked the American Geophysical Union and the American Meteorological Society to help organize the U.S. Climate Collection, a new set of papers from scientists about climate research, risks and solutions that will keep the public informed until federal assessments resume.

While the work is promising, “a volunteer effort of 170 scientists cannot substitute for a $30 million effort of 600 people,” Kopp says.

The American Geophysical Union similarly stepped in with crucial financing for the Arctic Report Card, an annual report from NOAA that has provided information about the changing Arctic since 2006. Its editors were blindsided when they found out during a routine meeting in August 2026 that NOAA was withdrawing its support.

AGU’s in-kind offer to support the project just one month later was “tremendous,” says Twila Moon, a glaciologist at the National Snow and Ice Data Center in Boulder, Colo., who edits the report card. “But [it] is not a kind of model that is sustainable into the future.”

AGU President Brandon Jones agrees. Nevertheless, at the time, the organization “had this sense of what was moral, what was right, what was ethical,” Jones says. And given AGU’s resources, “it seemed like it was our turn to show up and step up and act like the union that we’ve always been.”

The Planetary Society, a nonprofit space advocacy organization based in Pasadena, Calif., took the lead on several committees that NASA dropped. In January, the agency cited executive orders as it withdrew financial and logistical support from eight groups of space scientists and experts that advised NASA’s planning teams on exploring asteroids, comets and planets.

“It was pretty well-managed and organized [before], and then really fell apart because of the intrusion of the executive orders,” says Jack Kiraly, chief of advocacy at the Planetary Society. The organization now hosts the advisory groups’ websites, documents and meetings. “We’ve basically been having to reinvent this whole system.”

Climate.us isn’t the only site saving federal data. In November 2024, many environmental organizations “started preparing for the worst,” says Brittany Janis, executive director of the Open Environmental Data Project. Her group and nine others banded together to create the Public Environmental Data Partners, a network of 400 volunteers that archive environmental data and tools they suspected “would probably be on the chopping block.”

In February 2025, the Environmental Protection Agency deleted a tool called EJScreen, which maps places where people face high levels of risk from environmental burdens such as hazardous waste and ozone. Within 48 hours, the partnership had stood up EJScreen on a new website, using open-source files to reverse engineer and refine the data pipelines. It has since received 1,000 views a month, Janis says.

Nearly $2 million in philanthropic funding has helped the project pay staff, host data and hire engineers. It has restored the Federal Emergency Management Agency’s Future Risk Index, which projects county-level economic losses from climate change; the White House Council on Environmental Quality’s Climate and Economic Justice Screening Tool, which identifies communities overburdened by pollution; and the Department of Homeland Security’s Homeland Infrastructure Foundation-Level Data tool, which maps infrastructure at risk of natural disasters.

“We should not misunderstand the administration’s closing off of this data [to mean] that they don’t think data is important,” Janis says. “They recognize how important it is — and how valuable it is to causes that they disagree with.”

At the University of Chicago, the Data Mirror Project preserves datasets larger than one terabyte, including those from the U.S. Census Bureau and the National Park Service. The project has a guiding principle: LOCKSS, which stands for lots of copies keeps stuff safe, says colead Lisa Chinn, who is head of research data services at the university library. An agency doesn’t have to delete its data to create problems, she adds. Firing website staff can lead to wonky links, corrupted files and pages that degrade into Wingdings gibberish.

The thought of losing all that data can be stressful, Janis says, but “there’s a lot of parachutes that have gone out.”

Even as researchers, volunteers and scientific organizations pick up the pieces of the federal science enterprise, some are already imagining new ways of shaping it. “The world was not perfect in 2024,” says Rutgers’ Kopp. “Reconstruction is an opportunity to improve upon the way things are done.”

To Mullen, the epidemiologist at the University of Texas at Austin, transparency is key. New bodies like the Vaccine Integrity Project and the Newborn Screening Collaborative should make their meetings and deliberations as open as possible, she says, especially when “people think that those with the loudest voices or the biggest wallets are going to be the ones that have the greatest influence behind the scenes.”

Powell, of the Newborn Screening Collaborative, is hoping for greater efficiency in the disease recommendation process. Previously, it could take as long as three years to bring a condition from an initial vote to a final recommendation; with the collaborative, she hopes it could take one to three months.

Janis, of the Open Environmental Data Project, hopes to develop more creative and flexible data tools for researchers based on their needs. And Madison from the Evidence Collective and Martin of OnlyMarms believe that the science infrastructure needs to diversify its funding sources to prevent future shocks.

“If we really think these things need to exist in the world … then we need to build them out.”

However, Janis notes that there are inherent risks in having the private sector support public goods such as open information. And Kopp warns that “attempts to replace something that was well-functioning … are not going to necessarily end up with something that is better or even as good as what was there before.”

At the end of the day, one of the key elements in establishing a new science system is “faith that you will still exist in a year or 10 years or maybe even 20 years,” says Katharine Jacobs, an environmental scientist who directed a previous National Climate Assessment under President Obama. Without stable institutional funding, “we are all essentially wasting our time, bashing our heads against the wall, trying to create a system that we can’t support,” Jacobs says.

For the Lindsey sisters, being involved in public service — even outside the government aegis — has been empowering, especially during what they see as an otherwise dark period for science.

They have raised more than $600,000 from individual donations since August 2025. And in February, they received a one-time grant from the Doris Duke Foundation and others that will cover their salaries and benefits until February 2027.

“If we can get the money, I can see us doing this indefinitely,” Rebecca Lindsey says. She still hopes to retire one day, and she fights off worries that the government might come knocking about their unsanctioned work. But “we had a public that trusted us,” she says about jumping into the fray. “It was a risk worth taking.”
