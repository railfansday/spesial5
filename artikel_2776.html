One of the world's most contested seas is proving too much for Asia's leaders to resolve

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

One of the world's most contested bodies of water continues to cause problems for Asia's leaders.

A substantive Code of Conduct in the South China Sea does not seem to be feasible despite ASEAN nations expressing optimism that the negotiations could finally conclude after more than two decades, experts told CNBC.

The ASEAN summit in the Philippines failed to resolve it, despite Manila saying it intended to conclude the Code this year.

The crux of the issue is that China claims virtually all of the South China Sea as its own territory, despite international rulings saying it's not the case and historical claims from many other countries.

China's "nine-dash line" claim was ruled as invalid by the Permanent Court of Arbitration in 2016, although Beijing has refused to recognize that ruling.

Over decades, China has been building shoals into structures that strengthen its territorial assertions, and has become increasingly aggressive in policing the territory against nations such as the Philippines, that claim traditional rights over the maritime areas.

The Code of Conduct for the South China Sea aims to address areas where multiple ASEAN nations and China have disputes and competing claims.

Manila has reported that it had completed 80% of the negotiating text, with negotiations to continue under next ASEAN chair Singapore. But, experts are skeptical that a "substantive and effective" agreement can be realized, at least in the short term.

"It's an annual ritual. The ASEAN Chair commits to completing the Code of Conduct in January, but by November is reporting that — although great progress has been made — they didn't quite get there this year. Rinse, repeat," Ray Powell, executive director at gray-zone activities tracker SeaLight Foundation told CNBC.He described the 80% number as "a meaningless statistic," given that the issue at hand was complex and contentious.

ASEAN secretary general Kao Kim Hourn still sees potential for resolution. "I don't think it's a failure at all," he told CNBC's JP Ong. "There's still a lot of commitment and a lot of leadership on both sides to move ahead and get it done."

But Collin Koh, senior fellow at the Institute of Defence and Strategic Studies in Singapore's Nanyang Technological University, told CNBC that even if 80% of the text has been set, the 20% remaining contain the most persistent problems, including geographical scope, competing interests among ASEAN member states, and enforcement.

Koh said the original aim was ASEAN to negotiate with China "one-to-one," but, it has never been the case.

"It has always been 10 against one. Now you add Timor Leste. Now 11 against one. So, because of that, there is never any unified ASEAN position that they will bring to the table with with China," he pointed out.

Disputes in the South China Sea stretch at least as far back as the 1970s, with various countries such as Brunei, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines and Vietnam claiming various zones and islands in the critical maritime region, which houses rich natural resources and fishing areas.

Back in 2015, Singapore has positioned itself as a "neutral broker" in ASEAN-China dialogue, with then-Prime Minister Lee Hsien Loong saying that Singapore will deal "fairly and openly with all parties" in order to "preserve peace and stability in the region."

But while the city-state may be able to broker some compromises, the disagreements that have held up the code for almost a quarter of a century will persist, according to Lynn Kuok, Lee Kuan Yew Chair of Southeast Asia Studies at the Brookings Institution.

"Beijing, moreover, has little incentive to accept meaningful restraints while it continues to consolidate its position in the South China Sea," Kuok added.

Her view was echoed by Joshua Kurlantzick, senior fellow for Southeast Asia and South Asia at the Council on Foreign Relations, who said that a code of conduct is unlikely to happen soon, if ever. "China at its heart doesn't really want a code of conduct, and it continues to push more assertively into the South China Sea." He pointed out Beijing's actions against the Philippines in the region, including around the disputed Scarborough Shoal.

Chinese coast guard vessels have most recently been accused of ramming Filipino fishing vessels near the Spratly islands in September, and have used lasers and water cannons against Filipino ships in the past.

Kurlantzick also said that there are multiple countries within ASEAN that are close to China that are willing to hold up any agreement on the Code, adding that the move toward China is also happening as the U.S.' commitment to defense in the region becomes less clear.

"I do not think there is really any chance of a code of conduct happening with Singapore as ASEAN chair — or really, anyone as ASEAN chair," he said.

At the same time, a rushed or flawed Code would be worse than none at all, Powell said."In fact, the worst outcome is not a failure to agree, but rather a weak Code that would enable Beijing to undermine UNCLOS," he said. The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, which came into force in 1994, provides a comprehensive legal framework for oceans and seas globally.

"The narrative from China could then be 'see, now we have this shiny new regional agreement that overrides that problematic law invented by outsiders who don't understand East Asia and shouldn't have a say here anyway,'" Powell added.

—CNBC's Joanna Ossinger contributed to this article.
