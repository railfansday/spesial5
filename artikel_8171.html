The World’s Most Cinematic Trail Running Is Hidden Above the Arctic Circle

Trail Runner’s travel columnist Andy Cochrane has run all over the world, including five trips to the Lofoten Islands above the Arctic Circle in Norway. He shares his hard-earned recommendations on where to run, stay, and eat, what to pack for the unpredictable weather—and how to get there.

As a travel writer, I fly an embarrassing amount. Over three quarters of a million miles in the last decade, or roughly three laps around the Earth each year. That’s a lot of time in a pressurized tube sitting next to strangers, but perhaps a fair price to pay for the privilege of running trails all over the world, from Patagonia to Morocco to Greenland. Picking a favorite destination is difficult, but often actions speak louder than words. I’ve returned to one more than any other: the Lofoten Islands in Norway.

Hidden a hundred miles above the Arctic Circle, Lofoten is best known for its Viking heritage, red fishing  villages, and dramatic coastline—sandy beaches, turquoise waters, and walls of rock that rise 3,000 feet out of the Norwegian Sea. There are very few places in the world where mountains collide with the ocean in such a cinematic way, giving it the best of both worlds—and incredibly unpredictable weather.

On my first trip to the Lofoten Islands in 2017, a snow storm shut down trails in the alpine, so I explored the lowlands, dabbled in fishing and kayaking, and enjoyed a nightly sauna. Since then I’ve returned four times to explore different corners of the islands and am planning to go back again next year, because there is still so much to explore, especially if you love empty trails and technical running. Here’s why I think Lofoten could be the next great trail destination and how you can visit before it gets too crowded.

The Lofoten Islands are an archipelago in northern Norway that stretch from the mainland into the North Atlantic. At 68 degrees 54 minutes north latitude, they sit further north than most communities in Greenland and all but a dozen or so towns at the top of Alaska. The chain consists of five main islands and hundreds of smaller ones, connected by bridges and underwater tunnels. (Oh, the joys of Norwegian infrastructure!)

Combined, the islands cover a total of 474 square miles, roughly the size of the city of Los Angeles. However, they are home to just 25,000 full-time residents, most of whom live in Svolvær and Leknes. Suffice to say, the interior is remote and rugged; it’s common to find yourself on a trail without a single human in sight, despite the lack of trees and views in all directions.

Although there are offshore oil reserves nearby, drilling is prohibited in Lofoten (unlike much of Norway), which has allowed cod fishing and tourism to flourish. The landscape is a mix of granite spines and tussocked tundra, with a few birch and mountain ash stands sprinkled in. When storms roll in—which they do often—there is very little protection, which can be a humbling experience.

The trail network in Lofoten is a patchwork of everything from unmaintained user trails to heavily engineered routes like Reinebringen’s 1,978 stone steps. As a whole they skew towards the technical end of the spectrum; most are steep, rocky, and stout. It’s common to gain a thousand feet per mile or more.

The vast majority of trails start near sea level and ascend through bogs and brush, climbing into the alpine via scree fields and off-camber granite slabs. Expect your shoes to get muddy and shirt to be saturated in sweat as you climb to the summit. Some marshes have wooden planks and some ridgelines have cairns, but they aren’t guaranteed. It’s best to always bring a good map and a better attitude.

While there isn’t an official count of trails on the islands, a good estimate is around 150, including everything from short beach hikes to high ridge lines. For ambitious trail runners, the Lofoten Crossing is an unofficial 100-mile thru-route from Delp to Å, and uses a mix of local paths, beaches, and roads. It’s a great way to see the entire chain of islands, even if it skips some of the serene trails. A few of my other favorites are the steep climb up to Himmeltindan (2,700 feet in 2.1 miles), crossing the interior of Vestvågøya from Leknes to the eastern end, and almost any of the remote trails at the end of the archipelago near Å, many of which reside inside Lofotodeen National Park.

Lofoten sees over a million tourists each year, heavily focused on the summer months. The crowds get so bad in July and August that iconic trails like Ryten, Fløya, and Mannen are probably not worth visiting. In the spring and fall Lofoten becomes a lot more enjoyable, because it offers more solitude on the trails, open spots at restaurants, and generally friendlier locals. I have a slight preference for the fall, mostly because I reserve most of the spring for skiing, which also is pretty great in Lofoten.

It’s also worth considering the weather and daylight. For about a month from early December to early January, the sun doesn’t rise above the horizon. Days lengthen dramatically in the spring, from 6 hours in February to over 18 by the end of April. The Midnight sun rises in late May and sets in mid-July, offering 24 hours of daylight for a month and a half straight. Almost too much!

Weather is fickle all year but especially in the spring and fall, as temps float in the 40s and 50s Fahrenheit. Snow usually melts in May and the mountains undergo a rapid transition to green. In the fall the foliage turns to a golden and red mix, storms begin to batter the coast, and the Northern Lights illuminate the night sky as early as September. Thankfully saunas are common and help make cold weather running more enjoyable.

Getting to Lofoten takes more time than most destinations in Europe. The drive from Oslo is around 20 hours and the combination of train and ferry takes even longer. The fastest way to get to the islands is a flight to Bodø and then a puddle hop prop plane to Svolvær or Leknes. This saves time and helps avoid weather-dependent ferries, but is often more expensive.

I prefer to fly from Oslo to Narvik, which sits on the mainland adjacent to the islands, and rent a car. The drive takes between 2-4 hours, depending on where I’m staying, but is always worth it for the views. Plus, public transit is limited in Lofoten, so having a car allows me to get to quieter places. For example, the cinnamon rolls at Unstad are to die for—but the only way to get there is to drive (or go on a 80-mile round-trip bike ride).

Traditional fishing lodges, called rorbuer, are built on poles over the water. Many have been converted to cozy places to stay. My favorite is Hattvika Lodge in the town of Ballstad. The restaurant next door, Fangst, is one of the best in the area. For those wanting to stay further off the beaten path, Nusfjord would be a great option. For a wider selection of restaurants, consider a stay in Svolvær or Leknes. Regardless, it’s important to book early, because the best places fill up months in advance.
