The 8 Best Beginner Snowboards of 2027

What to know: This guide covers the eight best snowboards for beginners, including the Editors’ Choice Salomon Lotus (women’s) and Salomon Pulse (men’s). Testers evaluated the boards based on how well they flex, carve, and hold an edge. Keep reading for our tips on choosing the best snowboard if you’re just starting out or are looking to progress.

Bunny hills might not be steep, but the snowboard learning curve definitely is. You’ve got to pay your dues and eat shit snow a couple times. But after a lesson or two and some good old-fashioned repetition, things will start to click.

One of the biggest mistakes beginners can make is riding the wrong equipment. We constantly see beginners riding boards that are too stiff, too big, or too small. That’s why we pulled together this guide: to help you find the right snowboard for you, so that the path from beginner to intermediate is a little smoother—and a little less painful.

This past spring, we put 44 snowboards to the test. We asked our crew of snowboard testers—including instructors and coaches—to identify boards that were easy to ride and approachable for beginners. Below, you’ll find the result of months of testing and countless laps in the Sierra, Wasatch, and Rockies: our favorite beginner snowboards of 2027.

Sizes: 142-163 cm (Pulse); 135-155 cm (Lotus) Genre: All-mountain

Pros and Cons + Versatile and fun + Beginner-friendly shape + Encourages learning fundamentals – Not ideal in choppy conditions – Overwhelmed by high speeds

If you’re looking for a snowboard that will help you learn solid fundamentals without encouraging bad habits, the Salomon Lotus (women’s) and Pulse (men’s) are hard to beat—and easy to ride. They’re soft-flexing and approachable, proving to be “exceptionally intuitive for any skill level,” according to a Tahoe tester.

Gentle camber underfoot supplies grip in firmer snow, and a touch of pop makes it approachable to learn how to ollie and cruise through the park. The Pulse and Lotus are also directional twins, with a bumped back stance for float in powder and stability at speed, while the nose and tail are the same width for solid switch handling.

In slush and hardpack, one tester appreciated the camber underfoot, which flows into a rockered nose and tail. “You would have to try very hard to catch an edge. This is a confidence-building board,” she reported. The Pulse is similarly predictable, stable, and easy to manipulate.

The Salomon decks showed their weaknesses at high speeds, on bumpy, icy snow, and in technical moguls—terrain and conditions most beginners will want to avoid anyway. For newer riders, though, this board is an ideal first buy.

Sizes: 146-161W cm Genre: All-mountain freestyle

Pros and Cons + Easy to turn + Buttery, playful for stepping into the terrain park + Rides switch well – Soft flex and rocker-dominant profile make it less of a long-term investment

For a solid beginner board that doesn’t break the bank, the Rossignol Ampage Vol. 2 is an easy-riding, rocker-dominant, twin shape that rides regular and switch equally well.

The Ampage is soft—way too soft for riding hard off-piste—but the flex is perfect for learning the ropes, practicing on groomers, and getting a feel for buttering, jibbing, and jumping in small terrain parks. “Both the nose and tail are flexible for buttering,” a Tahoe tester reported, speaking to the Ampage’s inherent playful nature. The rocker is designed to make turns feel smooth and catch-free, while a touch of camber adds moderate grip while carving.

Even at full retail ($400), the Ampage is a decent deal. But last year’s version is currently on sale, making it an incredible steal for boarders on a budget. Because of the core malleability and easy-riding rocker profile, progressing beginners might outgrow this board more quickly than other decks on this list.

Sizes: 150-167W cm Genre: All-mountain/Freeride

Pros and Cons + Floaty freeride shape + Playful freestyle chops – Not as reliable straightlining – Undergunned in chop

The redesigned Jones Frontier 2.0 is softer, more buoyant, and friendlier than ever, making it a solid option for powder-chasing beginners, intermediate riders learning to navigate the deep stuff, or cruisy shredders who appreciate surfy, laidback turns.

The exaggerated, elongated, rockered nose and a touch of taper (8 millimeters) help the Frontier perform well in powder, while three-dimensional contouring facilitates smooth and natural carving. Camber beneath your boots, a medium sidecut, and Jones’ serrated Traction Tech edges help the shape bite into piste on rail. “It lays sweet, soft trenches, and allows for nuanced wiggling underfoot,” applauded a hard-to-please Colorado tester who was surprised by how much he enjoyed the Frontier’s cruise control.

An approachable combo of biaxial fiberglass and a poplar and paulownia core yields a softer, predictable flex pattern. Testers had mixed feedback on the resulting ride. Some found the Frontier 2.0 “effortless and forgiving.” Multiple harder-charging riders called it a “dad cruiser,” with a 30-year-old ripper joking he’d happily buy it—in 20 years. But for beginners and intermediates especially, that flex makes for an intuitive, easy-to-direct deck that facilitates flowy turns and forgiving forays into powder.

Sizes: 151-170W cm (men’s); 139-154 cm (women’s) Genre: All-mountain freestyle

Pros and Cons + Approachable flex + Decent float in powder yet still solid riding switch + More butter than Land O’Lakes 𑂾 Overwhelmed at high speeds in variable conditions 𑂾 Not the most reliable for bigger drops

The Jones Rally Cat combines a capable, all-mountain directional twin shape (a touch of setback, zero taper, full camber, and spooned-out nose and tail) with a softer, forgiving flex pattern. The pliable feel comes from a poplar/paulownia wood core and a dual-direction, dual-layer, stitched fiberglass laminate.

A progressing, all-mountain Tahoe tester judged the flex “loose enough to be playful but stiff enough to hold an edge.” The shape inspired confidence and elevated her riding to a “more fun level,” and she recommended the board for “new riders, park riders, and playful freeriders.”

Despite its setback stance, the Rally Cat’s lack of taper made it equally adept at jibbing through the park and riding switch, according to one Mammoth snowboard coach. She also dug the tight, 6.6-meter sidecut for the 148-centimeter length we tested. Combined with the spoon-shaped nose and tail, she said the Rally Cat facilitates “effortless turn initiation and quick and efficient edge-to-edge transitions.”

However, a harder-charging North Lake Tahoe freerider and snowboard instructor warned that the buttery flex in the tail that makes the Rally Cat a blast on side hits and park laps can be overwhelmed by overhead drops and chop. This won’t be an issue for true beginners, but worth keeping in mind as you progress.

Sizes: 151-164X cm (men’s); 139-151 cm (women’s) Genre: All-mountain

Pros and Cons + Versatile twin + Most upside for progression out of any board in this guide + Durable build – Expensive – Too much for true beginners

Never Summer’s Proto Type 3 is more expensive and stiffer than the other options in this guide, but it’s also more stable and reliable, providing excellent edgehold, carving chops, and a solid backbone for handling sizeable jumps, cliff drops, and higher speeds. It’s too much for true beginners, but it can be a smart investment for late-stage beginners committed to progression and who want a board they can use for years to come.

The all-mountain twin features a classic Never Summer camber concoction, utilizing three camber bumps running from inserts to inserts, with rocker at the nose and tail. Combined with an adaptable sidecut and a medium-flexing, carbon-bolstered poplar-paulownia-birch core, the Proto Type 3 rips on edge. “Edgehold feels very locked in,” said a Tahoe tester. “Incredibly balanced and easy to shift from toe to heel,” commented a Mammoth rider who appreciated the Proto’s ability to tackle smaller to larger turns well.

The carbon reinforcements help the Proto Type 3 pop aggressively off lips like pubescent pimples. “Stable through the air even on the Palisades Gold Coast jump line,” commented a Tahoe freestyler who also praised the camber for keeping him upright on tail-heavy landings and the rubber laminates and UHMW sidewalls for dampening. The Mammoth rider agreed, saying the board smoothed out bumps that typically irritate her bad knees. Her verdict:  “After you’ve mastered making it down a green run, this board would be great for a late beginner to intermediate rider looking to perfect carving and increase confidence.”

Pros and Cons + Wide ride is perfect for riders with bigger boots + Turns confidently and floats well + Solid freestyle flavor – Can be sluggish compared to narrower boards

The Cardiff Lynx is a wide ride that’s a game-changer for boarders with bigger boots who have a penchant for powder. However, it’s more suited to progressing, late-stage beginners and aspiring intermediates than day-one newbies.

The generous width combined with rocker in the nose and tail, as well as a touch of taper, help the Lynx float extremely well in powder. A versatile, blended sidecut enables the Lynx to tackle a variety of turn sizes for true all-mountain capability, while subtle serrations along the edges help grip firm snow like the claws of the Cardiff’s namesake. “Edges lock into turns,” voiced a Tahoe snowboard instructor. “High-speed carving is fun.”

The Lynx is Cardiff’s softest offering, with a pliable cedar and aspen core that can handle rails, boxes, butters, and playful small-to-medium jumps. One Tahoe tester loved taking the board all over the mountain with a freestyle approach, noting that it handled switch riding better than expected for a tapered shape with a setback stance.

That said, the flex is stiffer and ultimately more capable in steeper all-mountain and freeride terrain than many of the other decks in this guide, although the Lynx’s build is not the most shock-absorbing in chunky moguls. Not to mention, that width can make the Lynx sluggish at slower speeds. But no one took issue with the cool graphics— testers loved the black, big cat-emblazoned topsheet.

Fit Tip: To make sure the Lynx will work for your boots, check the technical specs. In particular, look at the underfoot width at the front and rear foot. Compare that to the length of your boot sole, remembering that your boots may be turned slightly at an angle, depending on your stance preferences.

Sizes: 148-161W cm Genre: All-mountain freestyle

Pros and Cons + Approachable all-mountain cruiser + Excels hitting rails and boxes + Fun, buttery, and easy to maneuver – A bit soft for high-speed all-mountain ripping and freeriding

If you’ve already fallen in love with sliding boxes and rails, CAPiTA’s Outerspace Living might be a perfect fit. This directional twin rides switch well, with a sliver of setback for all-mountain riding en route to the park, so it can easily be your daily driver. It features gentle camber underfoot for pop and hardpack proficiency. Rocker in the nose and tail reduce common catch points while carving and facilitate easy jibbing and buttering. “It’s forgiving and light,” commented a Tahoe park regular. “You won’t catch an edge easily.”

The board’s minimal weight comes courtesy of a slender, malleable poplar and beech core. Despite carbon strips running parallel down the length of the deck, it rides on the softer side. Our testers loved that flex while hitting rails, spinning lazily off rollers, and sniffing out side hits, but our most advanced riders overpowered the Outerspace Living at high speeds in steeper terrain. It’s a great tool for playful all-mountain riders looking to develop their jib skills, or progressing riders looking for a stepping stone to more advanced boards.

Sizes: 135-162W cm Genre: All-mountain, park, powder

Pros and Cons + Great pop and landing gear for jumps + Decent jibber + Rides switch well – Too much for true beginners – Not our favorite in powder

When one of our crew test-drives a deck and instantly buys one for themselves, it’s a damn good sign. And that’s precisely what happened when one of our Mammoth testers put the Burton Good Company to use in her local terrain park. A true twin with camber underfoot and a slightly stiffer-than-advertised medium flex, this is a board that loves to snap ollies, power off lips, and stomp landings with authority.

“Handled medium-sized jumps like a dream,” reported that Mammoth rider, noting she felt confident and comfortable in the jump line. “Riding switch was a breeze,” voiced another Mammoth tester, who appreciated the Good Company’s grip on edge, which translated to boosting prowess in the mini pipe, too. She was also pleased by the board’s ability to spin on and off rails.

Because the Good Company felt a little stiffer than advertised, testers said it’s better for more advanced beginners or intermediates interested in park jumps. It’s also more capable than anticipated in all-mountain terrain, preferring medium and large radius turns to quicker, tighter ones. It doesn’t have a penchant for buttering, and it’s not our top pick for powder. But for progressing all-mountain riders, the Good Company delivers at a very fair price.

Shopping for a snowboard can be overwhelming, especially as a beginner. We’re not going to dive too deep on snowboard tech here (you can check out our additional snowboard coverage here for that), but we want to quickly help you understand a few key concepts discussed in this guide.

“Beginner” is a broad and admittedly unhelpful phrase. If you’re a true first-timer or close to it, you should focus on the first four snowboards in this guide. The best beginner snowboards are on the softer side, making them more approachable and easier to learn the basics on. They’re also generally a little cheaper than the next four boards we’ve included in this guide, which are boards we recommend for more experienced beginners, those approaching intermediate status, or riders who are hellbent on progression or excited to explore a certain style of riding.

Snowboards come in all shapes and sizes, but they can be considered true twins, directional boards, or directional twins.

True Twins, like the Rossignol Ampage, have a symmetrical nose and tail, which look the same and have the same flex. This ensures that the board feels the same whether you’re riding regular or switch. Twins are popular with park riders, as riding switch is a keystone of freestyle snowboarding. True twins are also a sweet choice for beginners, as they make learning “falling leaf” down steeper slopes a piece of cake.

Directional snowboards, like the Jones Frontier 2.0, have a purpose-built nose and tail. They’re designed to be ridden primarily in one direction, with the nose pointing downhill. They often have bigger, floatier noses and shorter, stubbier tails, a combination that can supply stability at speed, buoyancy in deep snow, and landing prowess. Directional snowboards often have taper, which means the nose is wider than the tail, and a setback stance that is bumped toward the back of the board. Both setback and taper can contribute to float and stability.

The main downside of directional boards is that they don’t ride switch nearly as well, which explains why many freeriders and all-mountain riders prefer directional boards, but freestylers stick to twins.

Directional twins combine certain elements from twin boards and others from directional boards to create hybrids that perform across a variety of conditions. For instance, the Jones Rally Cat is a directional twin in the sense that it features a nose and tail that are exactly the same width, enabling it to ride switch well, like a twin. But the nose is longer than the tail, helping the Rally Cat to float in powder and tackle steeper terrain like a directional board.

When you lay a snowboard flat on the ground and look at it from the side, you’re getting a feel for its profile. Profile dictates much of a snowboard’s riding qualities, as it influences how you weight, flex, and engage the board’s sidecut on snow.

Cambered snowboards, like the Burton Good Company Camber, look like an upside-down “U” when you lay them flat on the ground. There’s a raised central section between your feet, and two points of contact with the ground toward the nose and tail. Camber provides grip when you’re carving on firm snow, reliability and edgehold in technical terrain, and power when you’re snapping ollies. For some beginners, camber can be a touch trickier to learn on, and  can feel catchier than rocker. However, camber also helps snowboarders learn fundamentals the right way. You can’t cheat on a camber snowboard, whereas a rocker board is more forgiving and can subsequently let snowboarders develop sloppy habits. For that reason, we mostly recommend camber-dominant shapes in this guide, like our top pick, the Salomon Pulse and Lotus.

Rocker is the opposite of camber. The “U” is right side up this time, as rockered boards have one central point of contact with the ground between the feet, and the nose and tail are raised off the ground. Rocker-dominant boards, like the Rossignol Ampage Vol. 2, are less catchy than their camber counterparts, and they’re generally more playful, easier to butter, and more buoyant. However, they’re also less stable and don’t ollie as well.

Just like directional twins combine aspects from twins and directional shapes in search of a balanced ride, hybrid profiles merge attributes of camber and rocker. For instance, the CAPiTA Outerspace Living pairs grippy camber underfoot with catch-free, floaty rocker in the nose and tail.

Now that you’ve narrowed in on the board you want, you face an equally important question: What size should you get? Sizing a snowboard correctly can make or break your season, but the truth is that there’s no perfect formula. Here are a few things to consider to make the best possible choice for you.

Even as a beginner, you can learn about your preferences from snowboards you’ve already ridden. For instance, if you rented a 155 and it felt too squirrely and unstable, you might want to size up a touch. If you demoed a 160 and it felt too beefy and you weren’t able to turn it, sizing down is a smart idea. Especially as you’re progressing from a true beginner towards intermediate status, it’s important to demo lots of snowboards, as it’ll help you develop your personal taste and your snowboard vocabulary.

Always pay close attention to specs provided by manufacturers, as many brands provide helpful sizing parameters, including ranges for rider weight, boot size, and height. Use this information as a potential starting point.

Between two sizes? If you’re a beginner or riding park, gravitate toward the smaller size, which are easier to turn and spin. If you’re getting more advanced or you’re riding powder frequently, consider the bigger size, which provides more float and stability.

Some brands won’t give you a range of rider heights, but they will give you a range of rider weights, as weight influences how a rider engages flex and how much a board will float. For example, there are skinny 6’3” riders who like 158s, and squat, powerful 5’8” riders who dig 160s.

If you have average-sized feet for your height and weight, you don’t need to worry too much about boot compatibility. But if you have big feet—say, you’re 5’9” and 150 pounds, but you have a size 11.5 boot—it’s important to consider wider snowboards like the Cardiff Lynx. Wide boards help riders with big feet avoid dragging their toes in the snow, which is at best annoying and at worst can cause avoidable crashes. When you scan those manufacturer-provided specs, pay attention to the width and, if possible, ideal boot size ranges. It’s also worth noting that certain snowboards, like the Never Summer Proto Type 3, are available in standard sizes, as well as wide sizes, which Never Summer denotes with an “X.”

This past spring, we sent 44 snowboards out to testers across the Wasatch, Rockies, and Sierra. Those testers aren’t beginners—far from it. These are advanced to expert riders who live and breathe snowboarding, with a few progressing intermediates to mix things up. This crew is intimately familiar with snowboard design concepts, and many of them have been testing snowboards for a decade. A handful of testers are currently instructors or coaches, or they’ve held those jobs in the past. Needless to say, they know what beginners need under their boots to learn the ropes.

After testing snowboards at their home mountains all spring, those testers filled out detailed snowboard review forms. They scored boards quantitatively on aspects like flex, responsiveness, pop, playfulness, stability at speed, etcetera. Then they dove deep on additional qualitative questions, among them: Would you spend your hard-earned money on this board? What conditions or terrain does this board excel or struggle in?

And then, most importantly, we ask these testers to rewind the clock and think like a beginner. Will the board in question encourage a beginner to progress and step up their game? Is it easy to ride? Or is it a hard-to-command bronco that’ll buck a less experienced snowboarder?
