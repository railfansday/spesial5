I Bonds Track Inflation and Never Lose Value – but Are Locked for a Year

Series I savings bonds earn the inflation rate plus a fixed rate, and the total can’t fall below zero, so they never lose value. You can buy up to $10,000 a year at TreasuryDirect but can’t cash one for 12 months. Use them for savings you won’t need that soon, not your emergency fund’s first few months.

That floor sets I bonds apart from bond funds and TIPS, the Treasury’s other inflation-protected bonds, whose prices tend to fall when interest rates rise. An I bond’s value only grows or holds steady from one month to the next.

The fixed rate in effect when you buy stays with the bond for all 30 years it earns interest, so the month you buy decides it. The Treasury resets the inflation rate every May and November from the Consumer Price Index, a government measure of consumer prices, and your bond switches to the newest one every six months. When prices rise faster, your bond earns more.

Interest is added to the bond’s value rather than paid out, so you collect it when you cash the bond. State and local income taxes never apply to it, and federal tax can wait until then or until the bond stops earning at 30 years. Cash it to pay college tuition, and the interest can be tax-free under IRS rules that include an income limit.

You can’t cash an I bond in its first 12 months, and cashing one before it’s five years old costs you the last three months of interest. So I bonds fit money you can leave alone for at least a year, and ideally five, such as savings beyond your emergency fund’s first few months or a down payment you’ll make in a few years.

Since buying paper bonds with a tax refund ended in 2025, the only way to buy I bonds is online at TreasuryDirect, the Treasury’s own site. The limit is $10,000 per person each calendar year, so your spouse can buy that much too in their own account, and you can buy the same again for each child under 18 in an account linked to yours.

Before you buy, set aside what you could need in the next 12 months, then put only the rest into I bonds.

Keep the money you set aside for the next 12 months in one of the best high-yield savings accounts.
