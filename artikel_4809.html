Can Virtual Reality Make Systemic Racism Easier to Recognize?

Imagine sitting near the front of a bus in the American South, hearing someone recall a time when occupying that seat could expose a Black passenger to aggression. You know the bus is not real, and you can choose to remove your headset whenever you wish. Yet, for a moment, you stand in the same space where an ordinary journey once carried the threat of an assault.

This scene occurs in Traveling While Black, a virtual reality documentary that tells the history of racial discrimination in the United States. The documentary was used in a recent psychological experiment studying a deceptively hard question: Can experiencing someone else's story change how we recognise injustice, including the kind that operates through whole institutions?

While racism is often imagined as an individual act—an insult, an unfair decision, or an openly prejudiced remark—systemic racism operates through laws and institutional practices, contributing to established customs that disadvantage particular groups, sometimes without requiring anyone to manifest explicitly racist intentions.

Acknowledging this broader pattern requires something more than identifying a prejudiced individual. It requires connecting individual experiences to the arrangements that make them possible. This is where VR might have something unusual to offer.

A discriminatory law can be described in a textbook, but its consequences are harder to convey. A historical report might tell us that Black Americans faced violence while travelling through segregated states. What did it mean, though, to sit on a bus knowing that choosing the wrong seat might put you in danger?

VR allows researchers to approach such questions differently. Rather than presenting discrimination as a sequence of facts, it places people within the circumstances in which those facts acquire meaning.

In Traveling While Black, viewers find themselves at Ben's Chili Bowl, a historic restaurant in Washington, DC, listening to Black Americans tell their experiences of racial injustice. Then, they are transported into the settings being discussed. As civil rights activist Courtland Cox describes the dangers of sitting at the front of a bus, viewers find themselves seated among its passengers.

Viewers do not become Black passengers; they remain observers, witnessing another person's account from within the virtual setting. This distinction is particularly interesting, as several VR studies have sometimes placed participants inside virtual bodies belonging to another social group, with mixed results for prejudice reduction. Here, the researchers asked whether proximity to someone else's experience, without pretending to become that person, could influence how people think about racism.

In a 2026 study published in Media Psychology, Elisabeth Noland, Christopher Agnew, and Margo Monteith recruited 313 non-Black university students in the United States.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of three experiences: watching the 19-minute Traveling While Black documentary through a VR headset, listening to the same documentary's soundtrack, without images, or hearing an unrelated recording about automobile history.

Afterward, everyone answered questions about racial discrimination, their willingness to address it, and their reactions to the accounts they had encountered.

The researchers distinguished between personal prejudice and systemic racism, while separating recognising discrimination from wanting to do something about it. After all, admitting that an institution treats people unevenly does not inevitably mean someone feels responsible for correcting it.

The differences between groups were telling: Participants who experienced the VR documentary showed greater willingness to tackle their own prejudiced behaviour than either audio group. They were also more inclined to identify racism in individual incidents, including situations in which discrimination might not be instantly evident.

The results regarding systemic racism were promising, although less clear-cut. VR viewers reported greater perception of institutional racism than either comparison group in individual statistical comparisons, but the broader statistical test narrowly missed the threshold of significance. They also stated greater willingness to reduce systemic racism than participants who heard the unrelated recording; the difference from those who heard the documentary soundtrack alone was less evident.

Meanwhile, the audio documentary did not produce comparable differences in these attitudes and intentions relative to the unrelated recording.

Why might experiencing a story in VR produce reactions that listening to it does not?

The researchers investigated two forms of empathy—one involving grasping another person's perspective, the other implying sharing, or feeling concern about, their emotional experience.

Both increased after the VR documentary compared with the audio version. But the statistical analyses identified emotional empathy, rather than perspective-taking, as the more reliable link between the VR experience and participants' reactions to racism.

In other words, acknowledging that another person endured discrimination may be different from becoming emotionally affected by that suffering. The latter was more closely associated to participants' readiness to address injustice. These statistical associations, however, cannot determine that empathy itself produced their responses.

There was another, rather uncomfortable finding: VR viewers became more receptive to recognising racism within institutions (although the statistical evidence was not entirely clear-cut), yet they did not become more inclined to recognize their own prejudices. Nor did they steadily classify broader examples of racial inequality, including the underrepresentation of Black people in media or professional sports coaching, as racist.

Someone may therefore recognise that racism exists within society and still resist to identify their own involvement in it.

For psychologists, VR offers an opportunity beyond transmitting emotionally affecting stories. It lets participants experience carefully controlled situations, with the same surroundings, events, and timing. Researchers can then compare how particular experiences affect people's judgements and decisions.

This could be particularly valuable for investigating systemic bias, as researchers might, for instance, alter the rules governing an institution while keeping everything else constant, observing whether unequal treatment becomes easier to recognise when its costs are experienced rather than described.

The most interesting potential for VR, then, may be its capacity to bring institutional arrangements into the sphere of personal experience, where their consequences become easier to observe. It supplies researchers with a way to explore how people connect individual suffering to wider patterns of inequality.

But the experiment also leaves us with a concerning question: If someone can recognise the injustice of an entire system while remaining hesitant to recognize their own prejudices, what does it take to bridge that final distance?
