Here’s the latest.

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Markwayne Mullin, the secretary of homeland security, says Trump administration officials blamed a telecommunication outage but said they did not know its cause and that it would “take some time” to fix the issues grounding flights.

The Federal Aviation Administration announced a rare nationwide ground stop on Friday afternoon, in what appeared to be a major outage affecting its air traffic control system.

Markwayne Mullin, the secretary of homeland security, blamed the ground stop on a telecommunication outage but said Trump administration officials did not know the cause, adding that it would take an extended period of time to fix the issues. “This isn’t going to just be resolved and turned around within the hour,” he said. “This is going to take some time.”

Sean Duffy, the secretary of transportation, confirmed the outage in a social media post, citing “a communication issue impacting all Air Route Traffic Control Centers.”

“FAA technicians are investigating the issue,” he added. “We will provide immediate updates as we have them.”

The agency posted an advisory stating that “all airports are ground stopped,” citing “extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety” as the reasons.

“All departing traffic should remain ground stopped until further notice,” the official advisory read, adding that the landings of inbound international flights would be managed on a case-by-case basis.

Shortly after the advisory was posted, an F.A.A. website that lists air traffic control system notices appeared to have gone down, erasing dozens of notices that previously had been listed.

The stoppage, which began shortly before 4 p.m., could affect hundreds of flights already airborne, in addition to airplanes on the ground waiting to take off. As of about 4:45 p.m., there were more than 7,800 flights delayed in the United States and about 350 cancellations, according to FlightAware, a flight tracking website.

More than 2 million people pass through airport security checkpoints on a typical day, according to Transportation Security Administration data. On Thursday, the agency screened nearly 2.5 million people. Amid delays and cancellations on Friday evening, many passengers were taken off flights or told to go home.

According to FlightRadar24, an aviation tracking site, there were more than 1,000 airborne aircraft bound for U.S. airports during the nationwide ground stop. According to the flight tracking app Flighty, there were more than 30,000 departures and arrivals scheduled for the remainder of the day.

At least some of the airborne flights appeared to be landing amid the stoppage. About half an hour after the ground stop was declared, planes could be seen landing at O’Hare International Airport.

But passengers waiting to take off were stuck in limbo.

In the Denver airport, passengers were taken off their flights with their suitcases. A handful began rebooking travel for the next day. But most waited patiently for any kind of update. A Southwest flight attendant announced that the airline expected an update in 30 minutes.

Before the advisory was published, the F.A.A. halted flights to Chicago’s O’Hare and Midway airports, citing equipment problems, and reported equipment-related departure delays at Newark, Las Vegas, Teterboro, Orlando, Houston and Phoenix.

Friday’s ground stop is only the third such nationwide moratorium on flights in recent history. The F.A.A. issued nationwide ground stops previously on Sept. 11, 2001, in the wake of the terrorist attack on the World Trade Center in New York, and on Jan. 11, 2023, due to an outage in the F.A.A.’s system to alert pilots to safety issues.

Christine Chung, Niraj Chokshi, Lisa Lerer and Robert Chiarito contributed reporting.

Some international flights from the United States have been allowed to depart amid the ground stop, according to Flightradar24, an aviation tracking site. Planes headed from Boston and New York to Europe and Asia have taken off, after delays, since the ground stop was announced, the site showed.

A spokeswoman for the National Weather Service said a screenshot circulating online that claimed its weather radar was down was “outdated.”

The Weather Service “has not experienced a disruption to its radar systems today,” said Kim Doster, the spokeswoman.

Across the United States, more than 10,000 flights were delayed and another 1,700 were canceled as of 6 p.m. Eastern, according to FlightAware, a flight-tracking service.

In the middle of national aviation challenges, President Trump is posting on social media about his hopes for tax incentives to lure filmmaking to the United States.

Nationwide ground stops are exceptionally rare in the United States, but they are not a distant memory for many travelers.

The last nationwide ground stop came in the early morning of Jan. 11, 2023, and lasted around 90 minutes. The disruption came after a system to alert pilots of real-time dangers went down.

The NOTAM alerts, short for Notice to Air Missions, include updates on hazards like closed runways, airspace restrictions and signal disruptions. The F.A.A. said at the time there was no evidence the outage was caused by a cyberattack. The agency ordered airlines to halt departing flights just before 7:30 a.m., and the pause was lifted about 9 a.m. Flights already airborne were allowed to land at their destinations.

But the travel headaches spilled over to many passengers as airlines struggled to get their schedules back on track. By that afternoon, about 9,000 flights had been delayed and 1,300 had been canceled.

More than two decades earlier, the Sept. 11 terrorist attacks led to what was at the time an unprecedented ground stop. All airborne planes were ordered to land at the closest available airport.

Flights may be grounded, but that is not stopping SpaceX’s countdown for its Starship launch, which is scheduled to lift off at 5:47 p.m. Eastern time from the SpaceX site in South Texas. This test flight will aim to be the first where SpaceX will attempt to catch both parts of the rocket, a major step toward a fully reusable space vehicle.

A spokeswoman at Heathrow Airport in London, one of highest-traffic gateways between the United States and Britain, said in a phone interview on Friday that most U.S.-bound flights had already departed. Officials at Heathrow were in touch with U.S. authorities and airlines to plan for tomorrow’s service, the spokeswoman said. Heathrow’s site showed a New York-bound flight scheduled to leave at 10:30 p.m. local time had been canceled.

This ground stop has occurred at the end of the first week of a change in staff in the White House. The new chief of staff, Stephen Miller, has been involved in many aspects of policy, but transportation and A.I. policy were not among them. The F.A.A. has been adopting a new A.I. model into its system, and it is unclear if that is part of the problem here.

Some people are lining up to rebook their flight at the Southwest terminal in Denver International Airport.

The Republican staff of the Senate Commerce Committee, which oversees the F.A.A., pointed out that the agency had not built any new air traffic area control centers — the facilities that appear to be at the heart of the outage — in decades.

“No new ARTCC has been built since LBJ was in office,” they wrote on social media, noting that Congress recently passed legislation dedicating $1.9 billion to replace and consolidate the aging facilities.

The outage comes about two weeks before Thanksgiving, one of the busiest times of the year for air travel. On average, U.S. airlines are expected to fly about 2.7 million passengers each day over the holiday period, according to industry trade group Airlines for America.

As major airports fill up with planes that can’t depart as well as incoming aircraft, flights are likely to be diverted to other, less-crowded airports.

Friday’s outage comes just a few days after the F.A.A. announced it would begin rolling out an artificial intelligence tool nationwide to help controllers avoid delays. That tool was first unveiled for the Washington, D.C., area airports two months ago, on a day when problems with air traffic communications failures also grounded hundreds of flights, after a circuit failed and it was discovered that contractors had accidentally ripped through a fiber line that was supposed to serve as its backup.

The new A.I. system had previously been implemented in a limited test program in late September. SMART, short for Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories, was made by an A.I. company called ASI, or Air Space Intelligence, which won an $875 million contract for the program.

In a social media post on Friday, the company said, “The current ground stop is not based on or a result of the SMART system, any product of ASI or our integration with FAA and ATC.”

Toronto’s Pearson International Airport, which handles a large volume of flights in and out of the United States, said Friday that its operations were impacted. The airport added that no scheduled flights from the United States were arriving Friday afternoon, and advised passengers to contact airlines for more information.

In Mexico City, the Benito Juárez International Airport, one of Latin America’s main hubs connecting the region to the United States, said in a statement that scheduled flights to and from the United States were affected. The airport added that travelers should check the status of their flights with their airlines.

Even though he said that federal officials do not believe this was a terrorist attack, Mullin acknowledged he did not know the cause.

“Do we know what has caused it yet?” he said. “No, we’re going to go through it. But I’m not going to make assumptions.”

Typically, more than 2 million people pass through airport security checkpoints on a given day, according to Transportation Security Administration data. On Thursday, the agency screened about 2.5 million people.

Microsoft said that it has no indication that the F.A.A. ground stop is related to its global outage, from which it is still recovering.

American Airlines said in a statement that its airborne flights can continue to their destinations, but that the airline anticipated disruption to its schedule for the rest of the day.

Mullin emphasized again to reporters that this is not a terrorist attack.

“This isn’t another 9/11,” he said. “This is a telecommunication outage.”

Markwayne Mullin, the homeland security secretary, said it would take an extended period of time to fix the issues grounding flights.

“This isn’t going to just be resolved and turned around within the hour,” he said. “This is going to take some time. But once again, everybody is safe. Flights are accounted for, and there should be no fear to the public at all.”

A congressional official, speaking on condition of anonymity, said that both the White House National Security Council and Pentagon told their office it was not yet clear what had caused the outage.
