Tammy Shipley was supposed to be monitored in prison. Instead she died after drinking 20L of water

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

If the Aboriginal woman had been checked at regular intervals as required, her ‘excess consumption of water’ would have been prevented, inquest finds

Warning: This article contains images of and references to Indigenous Australians who have died

Get our breaking news email, free app or daily news podcast

The death of an Aboriginal woman who collapsed after drinking at least 20 litres of water in a New South Wales prison cell was preventable and the result of systemic failures, a coroner has found.

Tammy Michelle Shipley, 47, died at Silverwater women’s correctional centre on 20 December 2022.

State coroner Teresa O’Sullivan ruled on Thursday that her death was caused by low blood sodium levels resulting from excessive water consumption and complications of schizoaffective disorder.

“I’m confident in finding that Tammy’s death was preventable,” O’Sullivan said.

The mother of five had been taken into custody on remand 11 days earlier, after being charged with shoplifting offences, but didn’t disclose her mental health issues to police. Police also didn’t double-check her answers about her health, despite the fact she had attended a police station two weeks earlier and told them she heard her neighbour talking in her head.

O’Sullivan found there had been a missed opportunity to recognise the mental health issues that were present, and to potentially divert Shipley away from the criminal justice system.

When she appeared in a local court over the shoplifting charges the next day, the magistrate was not told she had been refused bail on other charges, so made orders they thought would secure her release.

“There was a clear systems failure,” O’Sullivan found.

Shipley should have been continually monitored – both physically and electronically – after twice attempting self-harm in custody.

It was a serious failure that this had not been done, O’Sullivan said.

“If observations had been carried out at the intervals required, it was likely that Tammy’s excess consumption of water would have been … prevented,” she said.

She deemed it unsatisfactory that Shipley had not been seen by a psychiatrist or mental health nurse at any point during the 11 days before her death.

She added that Shipley should have received medical care from the time she started vomiting, after 10am on the day of her death. Instead, she said, Shipley experienced seizures shortly before noon and was seen lying face down on the floor of her cell around an hour later.

A period of 23 minutes lapsed between when she was first seen lying on the ground and the commencement of resuscitation attempts, which the coroner found was unacceptable.

One corrective services officer had hastily closed the door to her cell after smelling faeces in the room earlier in the day.

“It was completely inappropriate that Tammy, who was known to be suffering a mental health condition, was left in that condition in her cell,” O’Sullivan said.

O’Sullivan made 10 recommendations to NSW police, Corrective Services NSW and Justice Health, including a review of the “austere and depressing” unit in which Shipley was held, which was intended to be a therapeutic space but left the coroner gravely concerned.

She also recommended regular monitoring of footage inside the jail and improved maintenance and cross-checking of records related to mental health conditions.

The coroner acknowledged the jail had already made changes after Shipley’s death.

Outside the court, Shipley’s mother, Vicki, said she hoped the findings would force the system to assess how people are treated.

“I honestly thought that at this stage of our country’s development, we have moved past how convicts were treated,” she said.

“The system – every system – eventually caused trauma to my daughter, until eventually it killed her.”

Vickey Shipley urged the NSW government to quickly implement the recommendations.

“If they’re not made, there will be some other family standing here doing exactly the same thing that I am,” she said.

“All too often we hear the cry: ‘There’s not enough money.’
