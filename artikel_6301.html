A honey bee’s stinger keeps pumping venom after the bee flies off, and now scientists know how

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Video shows that a honey bee stinger works as a tiny, double-piston pump, with collapsing valves that keep pushing venom after the bee has gone.

A honey bee stings once, leaves its stinger in your skin, and flies off to die. It looks like the end of the attack. It isn’t.

The stinger left behind is a self-contained unit, with its own venom sac, muscles and nerves. It keeps digging in and pumping venom on its own after the bee has gone.

Scientists have now filmed that happening at high speed. Fiorella Ramirez-Esquivel of Macquarie University in Sydney and her colleagues watched detached stingers push into a clear gel that stands in for skin. The gel is not living tissue, but it lets a camera see what skin would hide.

The stinger, they found, works as a pump but is unlike most pumps that people build: two flexible pistons sliding in opposite directions inside a single chamber.

“We’ve known since as early as 1910 that the venom sac of a honey bee stinger empties into a chamber with little pumping elements called valves, but until now we didn’t know exactly how they work,” said Ramirez-Esquivel.

The honey bee is one of only a handful of insects whose stinger is built to tear away from its body. When the bee pulls free, the stinger stays put, along with its venom sac, several glands, a cluster of nerve cells, and the muscles that drive it.

The team, from Macquarie and UNSW Canberra, took stingers from live worker bees. Each tip went into a small block of clear gel about a sixth of an inch (4 millimeters) long.

A stinger is partly see-through, so a strong light shone through it let cameras running at up to 500 frames a second record the parts moving inside.

The stinger only pumped properly when it had something to push into. Without gel, its pumping parts barely moved. In the gel, strong pumping lasted from 15 seconds to just over a minute in a sample of 10 stingers.

It then faded to twitches that went on for two to five additional minutes. In an interview with Earth.com, Ramirez-Esquivel said the speed of the stinging made it hard to film.

“There is no time to adjust, you either catch it or you don’t,” she said. “I still did not expect the sheer number of attempts we had to go through to capture this footage.”

Inside the stinger, the venom sac empties into a small chamber at the base. Two long, sharp blades called lancets slide back and forth through that chamber along a rail.

Their backward-facing barbs anchor the stinger in skin and work it deeper with each stroke. Scorpions and spiders deliver venom differently, by squeezing muscles around a venom gland.

Each lancet carries a small, flexible flap, called a valve, inside the chamber. The two valves move in opposite directions: when one travels down toward the tip, the other travels up.

A single stroke moves a valve about a sixtieth of an inch (0.45 millimeters), roughly the thickness of four sheets of paper, in about seven tenths of a second. The venom then leaves through a canal about half the width of a human hair.

On the downstroke, each valve inflates like a parachute and pushes venom ahead of it. On the upstroke it collapses, so venom can flow past it and refill the chamber below while the other valve is pushing.

The top of the chamber is also wider than the valves can seal, which leaves a space where fresh venom keeps entering even while pumping goes on.

Then the two valves meet. “Finally, and perhaps most dramatically, as the valves meet in the middle of the stroke, they change shape again to push past each other,” said Ramirez-Esquivel.

“This is a remarkable strategy to pump fluid since maintaining a seal is vital to transmit pressure effectively.”

There are no muscles inside the chamber or the valves, so every change of shape is passive.

To test the idea, the team scanned stingers from a Canberra hive at the Australian National University and 3D printed a scaled-up chamber with flexible plastic valves pumping glycerol. Its valves moved like the real ones.

A syringe shows why that matters. Its one rigid piston can’t refill without pulling back, so it can’t deliver a steady flow. The stinger’s two collapsing pistons pump and refill at once.

“The stinger pump design is quite unusual in having two pistons in a single pumping chamber,” Ramirez-Esquivel told Earth.com.

She said that, as manufacturing advances, the design may become practical to produce at scale for uses such as medical pumps for drug delivery.

The stinger has already inspired microneedle designs, and mosquito mouthparts have inspired 3D printing nozzles.

Asked by Earth.com the best way to remove a stinger, Ramirez-Esquivel said the stinger empties the venom sac within about 30 seconds to two minutes.

Taking it out as quickly as possible limits how much venom gets into the skin, she said. Near a hive, though, it is better to get away calmly and quickly first.

The reason is in the stinger itself. It carries glands that release alarm pheromones, chemical signals that recruit other bees to sting. “If there is something worse than getting stung once it is getting stung multiple times,” she said.

The stinger’s independence also makes sense for the bee. A worker that leaves its stinger behind dies, but the stinger keeps delivering venom whether or not the bee survives.

The authors wrote that this suits an insect that defends its colony with its life.

The team could not directly see venom flowing inside the chamber. The refill space at the top is inferred from the shapes and timing of the valves.

Whether the collapsing valve fully stops venom from being sucked back up is also unknown, and the authors wrote that more work is needed to measure that.

How the valves squeeze past each other is unresolved too. The video suggests the cup-shaped part of each valve collapses sideways, while in the printed model the valve’s wings flipped upward. The authors wrote that future work is needed to settle which happens in the real stinger.

In addition, the authors have not measured what the valves are made of, because each one is small and fragile enough to damage on removal. And every stinger was filmed in gel, not living skin.

The next step the authors proposed is to freeze stingers in liquid nitrogen in mid-sting and scan them, catching the valves in whatever shape they take as they pass.

The scans from this study are public on MorphoSource for anyone who wants to look.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
