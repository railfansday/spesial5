Keep Your Paid-Off Car Until a Year of Repairs Costs More Than Replacing It

Keep fixing your paid-off car while a year of repair bills stays below what a replacement would cost you each year in lost value and loan interest, plus any rise in your insurance. Replace it sooner if it’s no longer safe or keeps leaving you stranded.

A big repair bill lands all at once, which makes a new car look like the cheaper way out. Count what each option costs over a year, and the old car usually wins.

Depreciation, the value a car loses as it ages, is a new car’s biggest cost. AAA’s 2026 Your Driving Costs study puts it at $4,422 a year for top-selling new cars, averaged across their first five years and 75,000 miles. Interest on a five-year loan with 15% down adds $1,184 a year in the same study. That’s about $5,600 a year, before insurance and the rest of what owning a car costs.

A paid-off car has no loan and far less value left to lose. So when a $2,400 transmission repair hits a car worth $6,000, fix it. Even a repair that size every year would cost about $3,200 less than a new car’s $5,600 in yearly depreciation and interest.

To run the comparison on your own car, add up the past year’s repair bills, towing, and rental car costs, plus any work your mechanic says is coming. Compare that total with a year of depreciation and interest on the car you’d actually buy.

Replace it whatever the math says if rust or damage makes it unsafe to drive, or if it breaks down often enough to make you miss work. When one repair costs more than half of what the car is worth, get a second opinion and price a replacement before you pay it.

Find an independent mechanic you trust before the next breakdown, not during it.

Each month, set aside what you’d otherwise pay toward a car loan. Putting $300 a month into a high-yield savings account covers a $2,400 repair in eight months.
