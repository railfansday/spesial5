I Thought I Was Getting Slower Because of Age. Then an Incidental Scan Revealed the Real Problem—and Likely Saved My Life.

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

A longtime runner reflects on an unexpected diagnosis and the chance to start over.

Last year, a lung scan—ordered even though I’d ground out my last butt decades earlier—incidentally picked up a bulge in my aorta. Because I smoked heavily when I was young, my doctor sent me for a screening after the age eligibility had been lowered, just in case. While the scan didn’t find any signs of cancer, it detected something else. An aneurysm: a weak spot in the body’s main artery that causes it to balloon.

I was baffled. I was in my mid-fifties and healthy. I had no family history of cardiovascular issues. I was a longtime runner. I ate my veggies and hadn’t had a drink in 27 years. My resting heart rate was borderline smug.

Further tests revealed the diameter of my aorta to be twice the normal size. Putting me at risk of an aortic dissection or rupture, both life-threatening emergencies that demand immediate treatment.

Damn. How long had I been walking—and running—around with this fault line trembling in my chest?

While waiting for an appointment with a cardiologist, I was seized with anxiety. I stopped exercising. I practiced randomly unlocking the emergency feature on my phone and quickly making a mental note of my location.

An echocardiogram later revealed that the bulge had also pulled apart the leaflets of my aortic valve, preventing them from sealing tightly. With every heartbeat, some of the blood my heart pumped out leaked backward, forcing it to work harder to circulate the same blood twice.

“Given that you’re only in your fifties and live an active lifestyle,” the cardiologist said, “I’d suggest you consult with a cardiac surgeon.”

The last sentence I thought I’d ever hear. Until I was met with, “Let’s schedule you for open-heart surgery.”

Looking back, I wondered if there had been signs. I had tried to run at least one marathon or ultra a year, along with some shorter distances. In recent years, no matter how hard I trained, I could never improve my times, my marathons creeping up from low four hours to a whisper below five. I increasingly had to run/walk the final miles. I tried a vegan diet to improve my energy and switched to forefoot running for more spring in my stride. Nothing budged. Maybe I was just getting older.

Gradually, and then seemingly all at once, running became a grind. I started cutting workouts short. The hills felt steeper. The part I looked forward to most was stopping. The joy I had always found in movement was supplanted by fatigue and frustration and more frequent visits to my physical therapist. After plodding across the finish line at the 2025 Falmouth Road Race, I hit the pause button and wondered if I’d ever press play again.

Running has been part of my identity for most of my adult life. After years of flailing, it showed me I was capable. It soothed the anxieties of sobriety and helped me believe I was more than the drunken jerk many people knew when I was in my teens and twenties. It was a sandbox for role-playing difficult conversations, reckoning with my trespasses, and cultivating humility. It was where the idea for my first book took shape and stories took root. It was a way to find people like me, if only through those knowing waves as we passed. Running was more than a healthy habit. It was a partner, my better half.

After all these years, had I fallen out of love? And if I had, who would I be without it?

Open-heart surgery involves slicing through the chest, sawing the sternum in two, parting the halves with a retractor, and stopping the heart. The task of keeping you alive is outsourced to a machine. Even though doctors have been successfully performing this procedure for decades, when it’s you going up on that table, it feels like science fiction.

As the date of my surgery crept closer, fear and anxiety filled me like a helium balloon. The idea of intubation kept me up at night. What if I wake gagging and rip the tube out?

I calmed myself the way I would before a big race: meditation, yoga, and Peggy Huddleston’s pre-surgery relaxation techniques. I studied “the course” and sought out people who’d been through it, looking for any kind of mental edge. And though I was not a religious person, I asked friends and family for prayers, having read that they’d been shown to influence outcomes.

Early one morning in February, I was wheeled into an operating bay at Massachusetts General Hospital, knocked unconscious and chemically paralyzed. Over the next five hours, my surgeon removed my diseased aortic root and ascending aorta, replaced them with a synthetic graft and carbon valve, and hooked my coronary arteries back up to the new plumbing.

When they took me off bypass, my heart forgot how to beat. Instead of pumping, it quivered, seized by electrical chaos. Ventricular fibrillation. The doctors had to shock my heart back into rhythm. Four times. Inside the cardiac OR, the team was ready. Anywhere else, it would have been goodnight, Irene.

I knew none of this when I groggily opened my eyes in the ICU that evening. I felt trampled. I could see the breathing tube that had haunted me for weeks protruding from my mouth, my hands bound to the bedrail to keep me from reaching for it, but I felt no urge, couldn’t feel it. The second time I woke up—the first just brief enough to realize where I was—a voice said, “Squeeze my hand.” My eyelids too heavy to lift, I managed, then drifted back into blackness. When I stirred again, the tube was gone. Relief. But drainage lines snaked from my chest into canisters, while wires and catheters tethered me to a forest of blinking monitors.

Later, in the step-down cardiac ward, I needed a walker to cover even the shortest distance, the drainage boxes hanging on the sides like hard saddlebags. I was carrying 15 extra pounds of fluid, my fingers fat as cocktail wieners. I shuffled down the hall in grippy socks, a tech beside me and a chair trailing behind should I need to sit.

I spent a week at Mass General, three months of recovery ahead of me. In the early weeks, the focus was on walking. Three minutes at a time. Twice a day. Getting to the end of the block took effort. My wife (stone-cold angel!) set up chairs on the landing so I could stop and catch my breath going upstairs in our house outside of Boston. Instead of crushing miles, I was crushing naps.

At six weeks, I met with my surgeon for a follow-up. She was pleased with my progress and lifted some restrictions and cleared me for cardiac rehab. She showed me a picture of my valve leaflets riddled with holes. Told me the aneurysm had likely been there for a decade, the aortic wall “paper thin.” I felt dizzy as if peering over the edge of a tall building. You hear about runners collapsing at the end of marathons, about shovelers going down after a snowstorm. Had it only been a matter of time?

As she described the repair—a Bentall procedure, one of the more complex operations in cardiac surgery—a seductive thought wormed its way into my brain. Sure, aging may have started to slow me down, but a leaky, punctured valve must have been robbing my body of strength and energy, too. Now that a set of brand-new carbon leaflets was opening and closing tightly, blood safely moving in the right direction, could I get my running back on track? Maybe emerge stronger and faster, start breaking PRs again. Could I fall back in love?

I stared at my surgeon, the woman who had held my heart in her hands. Had she seen that old spark still flickering in there?

Cardiac rehab is part safety net, part exercise boot camp. A mini gym with EKG leads and blood-pressure cuffs. When I walked in, an elderly woman in jeans pedaled a recumbent bike while a silver-haired man in a rugby shirt and mask worked an arm cycle. Nearby, an old man plucked one of the resistance ropes that hung from hooks like a swarm of eels. Not everyone had undergone surgery. Some were recovering from heart attacks or stent procedures, others from dangerous arrhythmias or heart failure. I didn’t see anyone else my age.

A physical therapist named Andi asked about my surgery and fitness history, then my goals.

“The seven-mile Falmouth Road Race in four months,” I said. Returning a year later, with a sternum fused by wire, felt like a symbolic milestone. For good measure, I added, “and a marathon next spring.”

I leaned back, waiting for her eyes to light up with amazement. I bet she didn’t hear that very often. She kept scribbling. Probably too stunned, I figured.

She handed me a laminated workout card. “No treadmill?” I frowned. “Soon. Let’s see how you do with these exercises.”

I climbed onto a stationary bike and quietly turned the resistance a few notches above the prescribed setting. I watched my heart rate climb on the monitor above the nurse’s station: 100, 110, 120. Sweat prickled my forehead. That old feeling. For almost two months, my body had belonged to surgeons, nurses, and restrictions. Now, little by little, it would be returned to its rightful owner.

Andi kept her promise. A few weeks later, she prescribed one minute of running. Sixty seconds at a 14-minute pace, followed by two minutes of walking. Five intervals. Three-quarters of a mile. The longest I’d run in eight months. It felt every bit as satisfying as finishing a marathon.

As I built from one-minute intervals to two, then three, I stopped thinking so much about future PRs and training plans. I was running again. The minutes stretched. The walks grew shorter. The weeks passed. By the third month, I was running two miles continuously and sweating so much that the EKG leads kept popping off my chest. Andi would laugh and hand me a fresh set.

When I arrived, I’d seen those sticky leads as signs that I was still a patient. Now they wouldn’t stay on, blanking out my little section on the wall monitor.

On August 16, 2026, I stood among the heaving throngs on Water Street in Woods Hole, the staging area for the Falmouth Road Race on Cape Cod. I stroked my chest. Still tender six months later. Amid shouts and laughter, selfies and the sharp medicinal smell of Bengay mixed with sunscreen, my mind hummed with questions: Was I still the runner I used to be? Did we have a future together? Had my slump been caused by my heart—or “my heart?”

Every few minutes, the crowd surged forward, then stopped, then surged again—an electrical current passing through thousands of bodies as waves of runners were slowly released onto the course.

At last we crossed the starting line. As I set off, an old familiar voice rose to taunt me: This doesn’t count as your big comeback if you take walk breaks, and Dave McGillivray ran the entire Boston Marathon six months after heart surgery.

But then the race gave me something else to listen to. The pounding of rubber soles like golf applause. Spectators shaking cowbells and waving signs. The theme song from “Rocky” pouring from unseen speakers at Nobska Light. The cheers rose around us, and somewhere beyond them the waters of Vineyard Sound flashed in the sun, the island a squiggle on the horizon. Then an emptiness settled over the salty air.

I found myself thinking about who I had become. Or maybe who I’d spent my adult life trying to become. The abstinent runner. The marathoner chasing his father’s PR. The ultrarunner. The trail runner, leaping logs and picking my way across rocks with my trusty dog trotting beside me. Every few years, another version of myself to reel in.

Maybe, for the first time, I needed to stop fishing for some future version of myself. Maybe that was the gift of starting over: the chance to be a beginner again. Who I was 20 years ago. The overweight lug, freshly sober, shuffling down a dirt road in Vermont in the predawn blackness, listening to cicadas and coyotes, watching for flattened frogs and gravel stones the size of baby shoes. The accretion of feeling, competence, and the desire to be a better person. I didn’t know what a PR was. Or a starting corral. My finish line was the sunrise over the Green Mountains. Something I could never reach, only watch in awe. Back when distant light was all I needed.

Every few miles of the race, I walked for a minute. No earbuds. A friend ran beside me, an easy presence, and we chatted, and sometimes we didn’t. The race was everything I’d hoped it would be, which is to say: nothing. No expectations. No aspirations. Pace didn’t matter. I only checked my watch to make sure my heart rate didn’t climb above 180. Falmouth reminded me of a few things: that we are remarkably resilient, that the ocean never fails to awe, and, according to one spectator’s sign, that you should never “trust a fart after mile 6.”

When I crossed the finish line, I raised my arms perfunctorily and smiled for the photographers, lifting my shirt to flash my scar like I was at Mardi Gras. But on the drive home, the emptiness lingered. No epiphany. No transformation. No cinematic moment of rebirth.

I had run seven miles. And somehow, that was all.

That old blah feeling persisted. It took me longer to get out the door, my pace stuck in the basement. So I put a half marathon on the books.

A rare fall nor’easter blew into town one Friday and was still raging on Sunday: rain and wind blowing sideways, tree branches nearly horizontal, deck furniture scattered. I was behind in my training and needed to get a long run in that weekend. I hemmed and hawed for a couple of hours, hoping it would let up. It didn’t.

Finally, I got dressed. My Garmin had died the week before, so I set off blind. No pace, distance, or heart-rate information. By the time I reached the bike path along the Charles, my clothes were plastered to my skin. Broken branches and leaves littered the pavement. It looked like the aftermath of a house party. Rain hammered the river. With a playlist in my ears, I felt as if I were running through an adventure movie.

Cars sent waves of water rolling over the guardrails. Enormous puddles swamped the path. At first I tried to avoid them. Then I realized there was no point, and it became fun. I splashed through them like a kid.

I was the only one out there. Despite the wild weather, everything felt quiet, abandoned. And somehow that spurred me on. A small tree had fallen across a wooden footbridge. I had to wrestle it out of the way. Storylines bubbled up. I was racing to the next town after a catastrophic event, seeking help; lives depended on me. I had to keep going.

Finally, I saw another runner, both of us looking like we’d been fished out of a lake. We waved—not perfunctorily, but with a knowing look. Was he running for help, too?

The slap of my feet on wet asphalt took over the soundtrack, along with the gusts of my own breathing and the faint ticking of my mechanical valve in my throat. I had no idea how far I’d gone or how much farther I had to go.

For once, I didn’t need to know. I felt inside and outside everything at the same time, the rain somehow lighting the way.
