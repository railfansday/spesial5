What Is a Reconditioning Fee on a Used Car?

Used-car dealers may charge you a reconditioning fee to cover their costs of getting cars ready for sale.

A reconditioning fee is an added dealer charge you might find when buying a used car. It’s intended to cover any costs the dealer incurs to make sure a used car is showroom-ready, from mechanical inspections to detailing.

The reconditioning process varies between dealerships, but typically includes these steps:

A visual exterior inspection. An auto tech does a quick walk-around the vehicle and notes any corrosion, damage or wear.

An interior inspection. The engine and auto interior are examined and tested, and any issues are documented.

A test drive. The car is taken out for a drive to check that it runs well and that the radio, brakes, A/C and other components are all functioning properly.

Service and repair. Any interior or exterior mechanical, functional or cosmetic issues that were found are repaired, including paint, dents, oil changes and major engine work.

Inspection. Once repairs are completed, the car is inspected again to make sure everything looks and functions as it should.

Detailing. This includes deep interior, engine and exterior cleaning as well as any necessary leather refurbishing, bumper repair or wheel restoration.

Reconditioning fees are legal, but that doesn’t mean you have to pay them. Dealers can charge whatever they want for a reconditioning fee, and it’s not uncommon to find them ranging from around $500 to over $2,000.

The good news: Reconditioning fees, like most dealer extras, can be negotiable. Ask the dealer for a breakdown of the reconditioning fee to see what kind of work was done to prepare the car for sale. If the work was minor but the fee is high — for example, if they’re asking for $1,995 but replaced the floor mats only — that’s a good sign it’s a bogus charge.

However, when it comes time for negotiation, don’t just haggle over the reconditioning fee or other individual fees. The best way to negotiate is to focus on the out-the-door price, or the total amount you’re paying for the car. This simplifies the negotiation process and keeps you focused on the true price of your car.

All dealers invest some amount in reconditioning, but those costs are often built into the dealer’s asking price for a vehicle, rather than as a separate charge.

Some dealers opt to add a reconditioning fee to the bill because it allows them to advertise a lower asking price online, which doesn’t include fees. It’s a tactic to get you into their dealership, since the asking price is lower than that of other dealerships.

However, once you arrive, you may find that the out-the-door price is the same — or more — compared with what other dealers are asking.

Some car-buying experts recommend avoiding dealerships that charge reconditioning fees altogether. But remember that the out-the-door price is what matters at the end of the day, so it’s worth comparing prices at multiple dealerships to find the best deal.

Reddit is an online forum where users share their thoughts in “threads” on various topics. The popular site includes plenty of discussion on financial subjects like car buying, so we sifted through Reddit threads to get a sense of how users feel about reconditioning fees. People post anonymously, so we cannot confirm their individual experiences or circumstances.

Reddit users are generally skeptical toward reconditioning fees. Many users view these fees as a bait-and-switch tactic: dealers advertise an attractive price online and then tack on the fee at the dealership, inflating the actual out-the-door cost well above what was listed. Car buyers frequently share stories of being hit with reconditioning fees ranging from a few hundred dollars up to $2,000 or more, often with little or no evidence of what work was actually done.

Car-buying experts on Reddit point out that most dealerships already build reconditioning costs into the sticker price, so if the fee is listed separately, the dealership may be double-dipping. Several users suggest that car buyers ask their dealer for an itemized breakdown of the work done, but most users recommend negotiating the total out-the-door price rather than individual fees.
