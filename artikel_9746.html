A faster algorithm for extracting hexadecimal digits of π

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

This post introduces a new algorithm for extracting hexadecimal digits of \(\pi\) without computing the preceding digits. It beats the standard term-by-term evaluations of Bailey–Borwein–Plouffe (BBP), Bellard, and Huvent on operation count. Even against Huvent’s eight-term formula, the savings at large offsets are about 61% fewer divisions and 38% fewer full modular exponentiations.

The key is a relationship among the four powers of two in this identity:

The common denominator \(M=2k+1\) means we can add four remainders and divide once. The surprise is that we don’t have to compute those remainders independently either. As we will see, the gaps between them stay small multiples of \(k\) plus constants, so one starting power and the step \(2^k\bmod M\) let us reach the others by multiplication.

Even better, working modulo \(3M\) lets the same two exponentiations serve both \(M\) and \(3M\). Eight powers of two over two different denominators can share two full exponentiations and a single division. For every million hexadecimal places skipped, that saves about a million full exponentiations compared with Huvent, before any machine-specific optimization enters into it.

To see where the savings come from, recall the BBP trick. To skip \(n\) hex digits, shift \(\pi\) left by \(d=4n\) bits and take the fractional part. For a term \(2^E/N\) with \(E\ge 0\), we only need \((2^E\bmod N)/N\) because the difference is an integer which doesn’t contribute to the fractional part. So even at \(d=4{,}000{,}000\) we never have to construct a four-million-bit numerator. The terms with negative exponents still count; they decay geometrically, so we can sum their tails directly, keeping enough guard bits to settle the last digit we want.

Last time, I counted the work by counting fractions. Bellard’s formula advances 10 bits for every seven fractions; Huvent’s formula advances 12 bits for every eight. After finding Huvent’s formula independently, I’ve feverishly tried to find one with a better ratio, without success. So I looked toward ways of optimizing existing series.

One clue tipped me off. There’s a choice hiding in the last step of Bellard’s derivation. He has one sum in powers of \(1/4\) and another in powers of \(1/1024\). Since \(4^5=1024\), splitting the first sum into five progressions lets him write both sums in powers of \(1/1024\). What if, instead of lining up the powers, we lined up the denominators?

Notice what we’re dividing by. Since \(4m+2=2(2m+1)\), we can halve the numerator of the middle fraction and leave \(2m+1\) in the denominator. As \(m\) runs over the nonnegative integers, \(2m+1\) gives every positive odd integer. The other two progressions, \(4m+1\) and \(4m+3\), split that same list between them. So each odd denominator appears twice in each sum.

For example, consider the denominator 13. The first progression hits it at \(m=3\); the middle one hits it at \(m=6\), where the original denominator was 26. That’s two contributions from the sum in powers of \(1/64\) and two from the sum in powers of \(1/1024\). The powers are different, but all four numerators can be added before we divide by 13. We can collect terms this way for every odd denominator.

For an odd denominator \(N=2k+1\), the two contributions in each logarithm come from \(j=N\) and \(j=2N\). All further doublings give multiples of four, where \((1+i)^j\) is real and contributes nothing. At \(j=N\) the imaginary part of \((1+i)^N\) is \((-1)^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}2^k\); at \(j=2N\) we’re looking at \((2i)^N=(-1)^k 2^N i\). At \(j=N\), the first logarithm multiplies the imaginary part by \(8/2^{2(2k+1)}\), giving the numerator term \((-1)^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}2^{1-3k}\). Collect the other three contributions in the same way and we have the boxed formula.

Call a power active if its exponent is nonnegative. The slowest-decaying exponent is \(E_1\), which stays nonnegative up to \(k=\lfloor(d+1)/3\rfloor\). That’s about \(d/3\) odd denominators, and we divide just once for each one. Even the largest modulus is only \(2d/3+O(1)\), compared with about \(d\) for Bellard or Huvent, and \(2d\) for the original BBP formula.

As for the remainders \(r_j=2^{E_j}\bmod N\), notice that the middle two exponents differ by \(k\). If we already have \(r_3\) and \(x=2^k\bmod N\), then \(r_2\) is just \(xr_3\bmod N\). In fact, we can step from the smallest exponent up to the largest:

Start at the smallest active power. We pay for one full exponentiation to get it and one to get \(x\); the rest are a handful of multiplications. Past \(k\approx d/5\) only \(E_1\) is active, so don’t bother computing \(x\).2 That’s two exponentiations for the first \(d/5\) denominators and one for the remaining \(d/3-d/5\), or about \(8d/15\) altogether.

There’s one more thing we can do with 13. Suppose we’re going to compute remainders modulo 39 anyway. A remainder modulo 39 is good modulo 13 too, and \(A/13+B/39=(3A+B)/39\), so we only need a single division by 39.

To avoid counting a denominator twice, we start a pair only at an odd \(M\) that isn’t divisible by three. If \(3M\) is in range, we handle it together with \(M\); otherwise we handle \(M\) alone. So 39 gets handled with 13, and we don’t also pair 39 with 117. Multiples of nine, like 117, get their own turn.

The two numerators can share the exponentiations, too. The index for \(M=2k+1\) is \(k\); for \(3M\) it’s \(3k+1\). For 13 and 39 those indices are 6 and 19: once we have \(2^6\bmod 39\), we get \(2^{19}\bmod 39\) just by cubing and doubling. In general, all eight exponents have the same \(d\), so their differences are small multiples of \(k\) plus constants. We compute just two powers modulo \(3M\): the smallest active power across the pair and the step \(2^k\). Multiplication gives us the rest. The largest exponent for \(3M\) is \(d-2-9k\), so it drops out near \(k=d/9\), before \(M\) stops needing the step near \(k=d/5\). Whenever there’s a partner, \(M\) would already need two exponentiations on its own.

In any nine consecutive odd denominators, three are divisible by three. One of those is divisible by nine and keeps its own turn, while the other two are absorbed into their smaller partners. That leaves seven trips through the loop instead of nine, both where we need one exponentiation and where we need two. Multiplying the counts above by \(7/9\) gives about \(7d/27\) divisions and \(56d/135\) full exponentiations, compared to \(2d/3\) of each for Huvent.

Returning to the pair 13 and 39, while 39 is active, I never reduce modulo 13: changing the numerator for 13 by a multiple of 13 changes its tripled contribution by a multiple of 39. So I do all the squaring and multiplying modulo 39. Since 39 is odd, I can compute its inverse modulo a power of two once and reuse it for every Montgomery multiplication in the pair. I triple the numerator for 13, add the one for 39, and convert back from Montgomery form just once for the final division.

There’s a nice trick inside each exponentiation, too. Suppose a Montgomery reduction leaves \(-14\) modulo 39. Normally I’d add 39 to get 25. But if I’m going to square it next, 14 is just as good: \(14^2\equiv25^2\pmod{39}\). Even if the current exponent bit calls for doubling first, the next square still erases the sign. So for all but the last bit, I use vector absolute values instead of correcting the sign. On the last bit I do the usual reduction, since the numerator needs the actual residue rather than its negative. That change made the exponentiation loop about 8% faster on Zen 5.

Each vector lane handles a different odd modulus. I batch moduli with the same set of active powers so that one lane doesn’t sit idle while another steps through three extra residues. The hardware multiplies 52-bit inputs, so the largest modulus matters: ours is about \(2d/3\), compared with \(d\) for Huvent. That lets us reach about 50% farther into \(\pi\) before we need wider arithmetic.

I originally went looking for a series with fewer fractions per bit. If you evaluate every fraction separately, Euler’s series actually needs more fractions per bit than Huvent’s, so it has been easy to pass over. Sharing the modular arithmetic across common and paired denominators is what makes it faster, and I suspect similar tricks apply to other BBP-type series.

There’s a way to thin out the far end of the loop even more. At \(j=4m+3\), the real and imaginary parts of \((1+i)^j\) are opposites.
