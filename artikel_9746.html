Does Options Delta Really Predict the Probability of Expiring In the Money?

Before you read on, take a guess. A put option on Bloom Energy, a volatile stock, has a delta of 0.30 and about three months to expiry. How likely is it to finish in the money? Keep your number in mind.

Many traders would say 30%. Delta runs from 0 to 1 for calls and from 0 to −1 for puts, so it is often read as the chance of an option finishing in the money: a 30-delta put, a 30% chance. Some broker platforms even show a "probability in the money" next to delta, and for short-dated options the two are usually within a couple of points.

I wanted to know how far that rule of thumb holds. First in the textbook model, then in four real option chains: SPY, Apple, Broadcom and Bloom Energy.

Delta measures how much an option's price moves when the stock moves by one dollar. It is a sensitivity, not a probability. In the Black-Scholes model (Black and Scholes, 1973), the two quantities have closely related but different formulas:

The two numbers are only equal when σ√T is close to zero, that is, for low volatility and little time. The higher the volatility and the longer the expiry, the further they drift apart. For puts, the probability ends up above delta. For calls, it ends up below.

At 20% volatility and 30 days, the rule of thumb works: a 30-delta put finishes in the money 32% of the time. At 80% volatility and six months, the same 30-delta put does so more than half the time, and a 30-delta call only 14% of the time. I checked the formulas against a Monte Carlo simulation of 400,000 price paths, the approach first applied to options by Boyle (1977), and the values matched.

The textbook model uses one volatility for all strikes. Real option chains do not. Each strike trades at its own implied volatility, and on most stocks, and especially on indexes, out-of-the-money puts carry a higher volatility than calls. This pattern is the volatility skew, and on index options it has been a fixture since the 1987 crash (Rubinstein, 1994).

So, your guess: the 30-delta Bloom Energy put had a 43% chance of finishing in the money, more than 40% higher than its delta suggests. With five months to run, it was 48%.

Not even an option with a delta of 0.50 is a coin toss. With the skew included, the 0.50-delta put was at 44% on SPY and at 64% on Bloom Energy.

The same delta stands for very different odds, depending on two properties of the underlying: how steep its skew is, and how high its volatility is. A steep skew pushes the odds of puts below delta and those of calls above it. High volatility and long expiries push them the other way.

"Overstates" means the probability of finishing in the money is lower than delta suggests, "understates" means it is higher. With a steep skew and high volatility at the same time, the two effects work against each other, and which one wins depends on the stock.

Two more points matter for anyone who uses delta to pick strikes. First, the broker-style probability, computed with each strike's own volatility, does not show the skew effect at all. On SPY it was off by about ten percentage points for a 30-delta put. Second, all of these numbers describe where the option finishes, not what happens on the way. A stock reaches a strike before expiry far more often than it finishes beyond it, which matters for stops and rolls. I measured that gap on 469 stocks in a separate probability of touch study.
