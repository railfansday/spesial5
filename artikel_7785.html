ABA tracks new challenges affecting Public Service Loan Forgiveness borrowers

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

The ABA is closely monitoring new problems affecting borrowers working toward loan forgiveness. (Image from Shutterstock)

For lawyers and other student loan borrowers working in public service jobs, the path to having their remaining federal student loan balances discharged under Public Service Loan Forgiveness program after 120 months of qualifying payments is becoming increasingly difficult to navigate.

Under the PSLF program, created by Congress in 2007 with the support of the ABA, borrowers who have made 120 timely payments on their federal direct loans while employed full time in qualifying public service jobs are eligible to receive forgiveness of whatever portion of their loan remains after completing the payments.

But the ABA is closely monitoring new problems affecting borrowers working toward loan forgiveness. Issues include processing delays, payment record errors, growing application backlogs, and changes in Department of Education policies and procedures—some implemented with little or no public notice. Significant staffing cuts at the department, including a 40% reduction in Federal Student Aid office staff, have added to concerns about whether problems can be resolved promptly.

For reasons that are not yet clear, many borrowers have not received credit for qualifying loan repayments they’ve made since June, which means those payments cannot yet be counted toward their 120-repayment requirement. The Department of Education has not offered information or explanation on the missing information.

Separately, the Department of Education confirmed in August that it has reduced the number of some borrowers’ qualifying payments, potentially delaying when their remaining loan balances will be forgiven. After initially describing the falling payment counts as a “data issue,” the department subsequently said the changes were intentional, correcting coding errors during the Biden administration that resulted in inaccurate payment counts for some borrowers.

For example, some of the PSLF credit reversals may have been for payments made in a repayment plan that didn’t qualify for PSLF, although without an explanation from the Education Department about this process, some borrowers believe their payment counts were removed erroneously.

Without a formal announcement, the department also in July quietly eliminated the Biden-era 15-day grace period on whether PSLF-eligible payments counted as timely. As of July 1, a payment must be made “on or before the due date” to count as a qualifying payment under PSLF.

Also, a “clarification” by the department about the PSLF Buyback program is causing confusion and concern among borrowers.

The PSLF Buyback program allows public service employees to get credit toward the required 120 payments for months their loans were, e.g., in deferment or forbearance and thus not eligible for PSLF. Borrowers can apply to “buy back” those months, however, by paying what they would have paid had they been in repayment during those months.

But under updated Department of Education guidance, borrowers will not be able to buy back credit for payments made while under either of two new repayment plans—the Repayment Assistance Plan or the Tiered Standard Repayment Plan. These two new repayment plans were created in legislation signed into law by President Donald Trump in 2025.

Further causing concern is the significant application backlog for the PSLF Buyback program. As of April, the application backlog was between 70,000 to 90,000, but no new data has been released since that time. Even if the backlog has been reduced, some borrowers have waited two years or more to get credit for retroactive payments. The delay requires them to remain in their jobs and repay their loans for longer than required, thus functionally ending the buyback program.

On Oct. 21, the Department of Justice dropped its appeal of two court rulings that stayed the Department of Education’s controversial proposed rules to PSLF, which would have narrowed PSLF eligibility by excluding employees of organizations engaged in activities the department considered as having a “substantially illegal purpose.”

In August, the department had appealed the separate decisions by federal judges in Massachusetts and Washington, D.C., that had vacated the rules.

The ABA vigorously opposed the department’s proposals. In letters to Secretary of Education Linda McMahon, ABA Presidents Bill Bay and Michelle Behnke cited a lack of statutory and other legal authority. The ABA House of Delegates reinforced that opposition through adoption of Resolution 505 at its February 2026 midyear meeting, which opposes efforts to politicize this politically neutral program.

“Any perception that benefits are awarded based on political affiliation or ideology undermines the legitimacy of programs such as PSLF, discourages participation, and decreases the requisite public confidence they require to be successful,” the report accompanying the resolution states.

The ABA recognizes the crucial role PSLF plays in encouraging individuals to pursue careers that provide much-needed public services, including public defense services and civil legal aid representation to underrepresented communities such as senior citizens and veterans.

Since the program’s creation, the ABA has consistently responded to attacks and efforts to repeal or cut the program, including direct lobbying efforts through multiple Congresses and administrations; major grassroots campaigns including as a priority at past ABA Day fly-in events in Washington, D.C.; and working alongside its allies in the PSLF Coalition, a collaboration of more than 100 nonprofit and public service organizations.

In all, the ABA has adopted three policies supporting the program, including one in 2002 calling for the program’s creation. The most recent, Resolution 505, urges removal of the requirement that applicants be in qualifying employment at the time of applying for discharge of their student loans—a current program requirement that holds borrowers in limbo, unable to change jobs until the paperwork is filed. Once the required service and payment obligations are met, the resolution states, “there should be no further obligation than to apply for the benefits owed.”

As new issues with the program continue to emerge, the ABA will continue to work with allies to identify issues affecting borrowers and press for solutions. To learn more as significant developments occur, follow us at @ABAGrassroots on LinkedIn.
