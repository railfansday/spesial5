The Reason Some Parking Lots Have Angled Spaces Instead of Straight Ones

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Angled parking spaces aren't just a design choice. Their layout can make turning easier, control traffic, and change how a lot uses space.

Navigating a new parking lot can be confusing and stressful. Some have one-way traffic, some have different parking rules. Even the parking spaces themselves can vary, with some lots using straight spaces and others using diagonal parking spaces. It turns out that small difference is intentional.

Parking lot designers think about how vehicles enter spaces, where drivers back out, and how much room cars need between rows. Learn why some parking lots use diagonal spaces, how the layout affects traffic, and what drivers should watch for.

Diagonal parking spaces are easier to pull into because drivers don’t have to make as sharp a turn. This is especially helpful in busy lots where cars are frequently coming and going. These spaces usually face one-way lanes, allowing drivers to pull in from the same direction. This helps keep traffic moving and reduces the chance of cars approaching each other head-on.

Drivers still need to follow the arrows, though. Entering from the wrong direction makes it harder to pull into a space and can put drivers in the path of cars backing out.

Not always. According to the Iowa Statewide Urban Design and Specifications, “One-way angled parking provides fewer spaces than perpendicular for the same length of aisle.”

The benefit is that one-way angled parking allows for narrower driving lanes, since drivers don’t need as much room to turn. Two-way angled parking is also possible, though Iowa’s guidelines say it’s generally less efficient than other layouts.

There’s also a downside to consider. Backing out of an angled space can be difficult when neighboring cars block the view, especially larger trucks and SUVs.

Some streets use back-in angled parking to address this problem. Drivers back into a space so they can pull forward when leaving. According to the University of Kentucky, Kentucky Transportation Center, “With the front of their vehicle facing the street, a motorist has improved sight distance when leaving a parking space.”

Back-in parking also lets passengers get out toward the sidewalk and keeps loading and unloading away from traffic. For regular diagonal spaces, drivers should back out slowly and watch for approaching cars and pedestrians. Backup cameras can help, but they shouldn’t replace checking the surrounding area.
