How to reduce screen time and improve your digital well-being

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

Once a week, your phone sends you a screen time report. You wince at the number, then swipe it away with the same thumb you were scrolling with.

Screen time adds up fast when work emails and news alerts share a device with your group chats and favorite shows. By evening, you can feel worn out even if the day wasn’t particularly busy.

Conventional wisdom might say “just put the phone down.” But you don’t have to completely give up screens to feel more in control of how you use them. A few small tweaks to your daily routine are a good place to start. Below, you’ll find some realistic ways to cut back, plus a look at why it can feel so hard to put your phone down.

There's no set amount of time that counts as too much. What matters more is how your screen use affects you. Signs worth noticing include strained eyes, restless sleep, reaching for your phone out of habit, and feeling worse after scrolling. If any of these sound familiar, the tips below may help.

If “one more video” keeps turning into one more hour, you’re in good company. Many apps are designed to keep you scrolling, so you’ll need more than willpower to cut back. Start with one or two tips that feel doable right now. You can come back to the rest later.

Bright colors can make apps harder to put down, and notification badges and thumbnails are built to draw you in. Switching your screen to black and white takes away some of that appeal, which can make scrolling feel less rewarding.

Try: Search “grayscale” or “color filters” in your phone’s settings. You’ll usually find it under Accessibility or Well-Being.

Grayscale may look odd for a day or two. Some people keep it on all day, and others only turn it on in the evening to wind down. You can turn it off whenever you need to, like when you’re using maps or looking at photos.

Time can slip by while you scroll. Set a daily limit on your one or two most-used apps, and your phone will nudge you when you reach it.

Try: Search “app limits” or “app timers” in your phone’s settings. You’ll usually find it under screen time.

Start with a limit slightly below how much you use those apps now, then lower it gradually. Smaller steps are often more sustainable than cutting back all at once.

Notifications like news alerts, app promos, social likes, and game reminders mostly add noise. Keep the ones you rely on, such as texts, calls, and reminders, and turn off the rest.

Try: Search “notifications” in your device settings, then toggle off the alerts you don’t need, starting with the apps that send the most.

Constant check-ins can keep you glued to your screens all day. Pick a few set times to go through your messages, emails, and feeds.

Try: Limit your check-ins to lunchtime and after work, and keep your personal devices out of reach in between.

Before you open your phone, take one slow breath and ask yourself why you’re reaching for it. If the answer is just habit, that brief pause may be enough to snap you out of autopilot.

Try: Breathe in for four counts and out for six, then see if you still want to unlock your phone.

When you feel the urge to pick up your phone, try turning to an offline activity instead. Keep an activity you enjoy within reach, like a book on the couch or a sketchpad on the table.

Choose one or two spots to keep screen-free, such as the dinner table or your bedroom. A clear boundary often works better than a vague promise to use screens less. These zones also give you room to be more present, like during a family meal or your evening wind-down.

Try: Place your phone in a dedicated spot, like a basket by the door. If you share a home, invite everyone to join.

Pick a cutoff time for all your screens, such as 30 minutes before bed, and stick to it as best you can. Late-night screen use can keep your mind active and make it harder to fall asleep.

If you usually watch a TV show before bed, choose one episode and turn off autoplay.

Try: Schedule a nightly reminder for your cutoff. Swap your screen time for a calming activity, like journaling or a short meditation.

A few common app features can keep you hooked:

Infinite scroll: New posts keep loading, so you never reach a natural stopping point.

Autoplay: The next video or episode starts on its own, which keeps you watching unless you stop it.

Unpredictable notifications: Each message or like feels like a small reward, and not knowing when the next one will pop up keeps you checking.

Your emotions play a part too. A screen can be a quick escape when you’re stressed or putting something off. It can bring relief in the moment, even if you feel more drained afterward.

If you want to cut back on screen time without giving up after a few days, start small. Choose one change, like keeping your phone off the dinner table, and give yourself time to adjust before you make another tweak to your routine. If you slip back into old habits, you can always start again the next day.

To set screen time limits that stick, build them into your routine so you don’t have to rely on willpower in the moment. You could use an app timer or set aside a screen-free time each day, like the first half hour after you wake up.

There isn’t a daily screen time limit that works for every adult. If you’d like to use screens less, a good starting point is a limit slightly below your current daily average, and you can lower that limit gradually as you get used to it.

How you spend your screen time matters just as much. An hour on a video call with a friend often leaves you feeling better than an hour of doomscrolling.

You can reduce your screen time at night by setting a cutoff time before bed and charging your phone across the room. Swap your late-night scroll for a book or a short meditation to help you relax before sleep.

To help your family reduce screen time together, make it a shared plan. You could agree on screen-free times, such as during meals or the first hour after school or work. Try leading by example, rather than telling your kids or partner what to do.

No, you don’t have to quit screens completely to feel better. Technology connects you to the people and things you care about. You can cut back on the screen use that leaves you feeling worse, like aimless scrolling, and keep what you value.
