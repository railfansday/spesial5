Crostata Di Mele (Italian Apple Pie) Recipe

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

While working as a young pastry chef in an Italian trattoria, I had the great fortune of spending 3 weeks eating my way through Italy in an attempt to discover the most unique and tempting desserts each region had to offer. Although I tasted complex and ethereal cakes, pastries, and frozen delights, I kept coming back to the simple fruit crostatas: buttery crusts molded into tart pans or baked free-form in rustic rounds, topped with glistening fruits and cakey or custardy fillings. These desserts tasted like perfection in their simplicity and purity of flavors.

My recipe for crostata di Mele (Italian apple pie) is a bakery-worthy homage to those delightful desserts, and it became a seasonal fixture on my pastry menu. A traditional pasta frolla (Italian short dough) crust is filled with a lemon and vanilla-scented creme patissiere (pastry cream custard) and topped with tart, crisp apples and crunchy turbinado sugar. The warm crostata is given a finishing glaze of melted apricot preserves, then chilled for serving.

Learning how to make the dough and filling for this crostata will give you a solid foundation for making other Italian pastries. The dough is easy to work with, and the pastry cream is a versatile filling for other treats, such as profiteroles, layer cakes, or napoleons.

There are 3 main components in the crostata di mele: the crust, the custard filling, and the toppings. For the crust, you will need all-purpose flour, granulated sugar, sea salt, cold unsalted butter, a large egg plus 1 yolk, vanilla extract, and the zest of a large lemon. The difference between this crostata dough and regular apple pie dough is the addition of eggs, which makes the dough slightly less flaky but more pliable for rolling and shaping. For the pastry cream, you will need whole milk, a vanilla bean, more lemon zest, 4 yolks, granulated sugar, and cornstarch. You will split the vanilla bean lengthwise to release the tiny black seeds into the milk, and the cornstarch acts as a thickening agent. To top the crostata, I like using apples that are crisp and tart, but that also have a balance of sweetness. Honeycrisp, Pink Lady, and Granny Smith are perfect apple varieties for this recipe. You will toss your apple slices with fresh lemon juice to prevent browning before baking, and use some melted butter to secure a crunchy sprinkling of turbinado sugar. To give your baked crostata a shiny finish, you will need apricot preserves to melt down and strain.

Combine the flour, sugar, and salt in a stand mixer bowl.

Add the butter a few pieces at a time and mix until pea-sized clumps form.

Add the egg, yolk, lemon zest, and vanilla, and mix until a dough forms.

Shape the dough into a disk, wrap in plastic, and refrigerate it for at least 30 minutes.

While the dough is chilling, heat the milk, vanilla bean, and lemon zest strips in a medium saucepan until it barely starts to simmer.

Remove the pan from the heat, cover, and steep for 10 minutes.

Place a medium glass bowl on a damp kitchen towel or mat to prevent slipping.

Add the egg yolks, sugar, and cornstarch and whisk until thick and pale yellow.

Remove the vanilla bean and zest strips with a slotted spoon, then discard.

Gradually add the warm milk to the egg mixture, whisking constantly.

Transfer the mixture back to the saucepan and cook over low heat, stirring frequently until it comes to a boil. Turn the heat to low and whisk constantly for 1 minute.

Remove the pan from the heat, then scrape the filling into a heat-proof bowl and press a piece of plastic wrap to the surface. Chill for at least 2 hours.

In a large bowl, toss the apple slices with the lemon juice.

Roll out the chilled dough to a 12-inch circle on a floured surface.

Roll out the chilled dough to a 12-inch circle on a floured surface.

Press the dough into the pan and trim the excess flush with the edge of the pan.

Spread the cooled pastry cream evenly over the bottom of the pastry shell.

Arrange the slices in concentric overlapping circles over the pastry cream, starting from the outer edge and working inward.

Brush the apples with the melted butter and sprinkle on the turbinado sugar.

Bake the crostata di mele for about 50 minutes until the crust is golden brown and the apples are tender.

While the crostata is baking, heat the apricot jam with 1 tablespoon of water in a small saucepan until warm and liquid.

Strain the jam if any large pieces of fruit remain.

Remove the crostata from the oven and immediately brush on the apricot glaze.

Cool the crostata completely in the pan on a rack before removing from the pan.

Slice the crostata and serve at room temperature or chilled.

Our bakery-worthy crostata di Mele (Italian apple pie) is filled with a lemon and vanilla-scented creme patissiere and topped with tart, crisp apples.

The crostata is one of the most continuously made dessert forms found in Italian baking, dating back to the 1400s. Not only is the crostata found in pastry shops and on restaurant menus, but it is also the one dessert that many people make at home with whatever ingredients are on hand. This kind of humble tart was likely consumed at breakfast, lunch, and dinner.

The crostata di mele probably originated in northern Italy, where apples are grown in abundance. In order to highlight the fresh flavor of the apples, crostata de mele is spiced with a light hand. Subtle cinnamon and a hint of nutmeg might be used, along with the zest of a lemon, as in this recipe. With only a few simple ingredients, the recipe is also dependent on using a light hand in preparation. The dough should be minimally handled to prevent a tough or shrunken crust. The pastry cream must be carefully tempered to prevent lumps from forming. And nothing should get in the way of the apples being the star.

Even though there may seem to be a lot of steps to this recipe, there are lots of ways to do advance prep to save time. First, your crostata dough can be mixed, shaped into a disk, wrapped, and chilled for several days in the refrigerator. You can also freeze this dough for up to 3 months. Once you are ready to roll out the dough, allow it to come to cool to room temperature before you shape it. You can also roll out and fill your tart pan, then wrap the unbaked shell and refrigerate or freeze until it is time to fill and bake.

The pastry cream should only be made 1 or 2 days in advance, and this custardy filling should not be frozen unless it is layered into a dessert (too much separation will occur). Wait until just before baking to spread the pastry cream into your tart shell so that the dough will not become soggy.

From experience, I know that this crostata di mele will stay fresh, tasty, and sliceable for almost a week under refrigeration. That said, it is crucial to always keep the crostata chilled to prevent bacterial growth in the custard layer. Typically, 3-5 days is the best range for serving this dessert.
