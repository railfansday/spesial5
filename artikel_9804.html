The short answer: To measure AI ROI in software engineering, look past how fast code gets written. Count the engineering hours your team spends after the code exists, on triage, diagnosis, prioritization, review, and release for escalated customer issues. Then estimate how much of that time AI could recover, and value it at a loaded engineering rate.

AI coding tools help engineers implement features and explore solutions faster. But faster code only gets teams partway to a positive return on AI. Teams still need to review, test, and release changes, then diagnose and resolve defects that reach customers.

That work depends on understanding how the code behaves in production, which customer configurations matter, and what previous failures revealed. When each team has to reconstruct the same production context, time saved in the editor gets spent again downstream.

A more complete way to measure AI ROI includes the effort required to verify, release, and support the software teams produce: the work of production engineering. Drawing on what we've seen with customers, we built an AI ROI calculator that estimates the capacity tied up in escalated customer work, and how much of it an engineering world model can recover by connecting the evidence teams need to cut redundant investigation and coordination. Together, they bring a broader opportunity into focus: preserving more of the gains from faster coding and making each resolution useful beyond the issue that prompted it.

Faster implementation gives teams room to test ideas, refine features, and respond to customers. Those gains become harder to realize when the surrounding workflow still depends on people rebuilding the same explanation.

DORA's 2026 report, The ROI of AI-assisted Software Development, emphasizes that faster coding doesn't automatically translate into business value. It identifies reducing unnecessary rework as a way to reclaim engineering capacity and increase output without expanding headcount.

For teams already investing in AI code generation, that opens another investment question: how much capacity could AI recover from the work triggered by production defects? Customer escalations still require investigation, fixes need prioritization, and corrective changes need verification before release. Those responsibilities create an opportunity to extend AI's value beyond producing code.

An escalated customer issue makes those dependencies visible. Support rebuilds the customer's history. Engineering traces the failure through code and runtime evidence. Product seeks a reliable account of its impact, while QA establishes the conditions the fix needs to satisfy. When those findings stay scattered across tickets and conversations, each handoff adds another round of explanation.

Much of this effort is easy to miss because it appears inside otherwise necessary work. Investigating a defect is necessary; reconstructing evidence that another team has already assembled adds avoidable effort. Reviewing a change is necessary; asking its author to recover the reasoning behind its scope adds more.

The loss extends beyond labor cost: slower resolutions, interrupted feature work, less time for prevention, and senior engineers diverted into repeated explanations. Recovering that capacity means addressing repeated work across the full production workflow, from the first customer report to a verified release.

Support's contribution begins well before a backend engineer picks up a ticket. L1 support clarifies the report and responds to the customer; support engineers connect it to sessions, documentation, related tickets, and technical evidence.
