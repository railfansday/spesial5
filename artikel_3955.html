Opinion: Onimusha vs Phantom Blade Zero - The Insane Sword Fight for Game of the Year

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Onimusha: Way of the Sword and Phantom Blade Zero are two of the best action games I've played in years.

I've always been a sucker for cool sword fights and I'd argue that these two games do sword fights better than almost any other (Ghost of Tsushima and Ghost of Yotei deserve a big shoutout, mind).

I genuinely feel like I've hit the jackpot, having both of these blade-wielding masterpieces drop within the space of two months.

And I've got very little doubt: it's going to be Onimusha and Phantom Blade Zero vying for my eventual Game of the Year vote. And at the time of writing this article, I've got no idea which way I'm going to swing.

To be clear, I know Grand Theft Auto 6 is just around the corner — and yes, it could absolutely throw a spanner in the works — but taking my own personal taste into account, it would have to be properly mind-blowing to top the two titles I've already mentioned.

So I wanted to try and take a step back, and re-examine both Onimusha and Phantom Blade Zero. Break them down a bit and at least attempt to decide on which one I like better.

Admittedly, we might not get to that point today, but it should still make for a good discussion on what actually makes a great action game.

Believe it or not, enjoyable action is crucial to making a top tier action title — but picking a winner in this category isn't going to be easy.

That's mainly because the two games operate so differently. While they share a focus on defensive techniques that subsequently open your opponents up to retaliation, the overall feel is completely different.

Onimusha is very measured and, for lack of a better word, slow. Comparatively, Phantom Blade Zero is lightning quick. Their rhythms couldn't be much more distinct from one another.

Interestingly, though, both games prioritise cinematic flair — but critically, they do it without sacrificing responsive controls. I think this is a huge factor in why they stand apart from many of their peers; both games are a masterclass in providing eye-popping but very readable action.

Although I will say, Phantom Blade Zero pushes the limits of that last point at times. Where Onimusha affords itself clarity through its more methodical clashing of swords, Zero can appear a little too busy during its more frantic battles.

And I think this translates, very subtly, into Phantom Blade Zero feeling more unwieldy. You press a button once in Onimusha and Musashi swings his sword once. You do the same in Zero and Soul might slash three times depending on the weapon he has equipped.

That's part of Zero's wuxia identity, of course, but the seemingly never-ending combos can sometimes come off as being flashy for the sake of flashiness. In contrast, every strike in Capcom's offering feels like it has a purpose and a weight to it.

So suddenly, I find myself leaning towards Onimusha here... but then Zero takes the cake with regards to variety.

I absolutely love the weapon system in S-GAME's debut. There are 24 of them to find throughout the game, and they're all unique, with their own movesets and accompanying perks. Coming across a new blade is genuinely exciting, because you know the developer's put serious time and effort into designing them.

Onimusha just can't compete — but that's also by design. Capcom clearly wanted to flesh out Musashi's single sword to an obsessive extent, and that's exactly what it did. He's got one primary weapon, and you learn to master it over the course of his adventure.

And this is reflected in the sheer number of unique, contextual animations found in Onimusha. You can't really delve into that level of detail if you're animating for 24 weapons — although S-GAME certainly tries, with each blade boasting its own finishers and standard combos.

It's a real 50-50, then; the action is absolutely brilliant in both games, but for different reasons. There's part of me, though, that just adores Capcom's commitment to weight. Whether it's Monster Hunter, Devil May Cry, Dragon's Dogma, or Onimusha, no one does satisfyingly crunchy combat like the Japanese company.

And so purely in terms of feel, I think Onimusha sneaks the win. It's a more refined experience overall.

Bosses play a gigantic role in this debate. They're the highlights of both games, punctuating pivotal story moments, providing sheer spectacle, and putting your skills to the test.

Numbers-wise, Phantom Blade Zero wins this one hands down. The game's got a really, really impressive lineup of unique boss battles; outside of several big bad guys that share select attack animations, there are no repeats to be found.

I honestly can't think of another action title that can equal its roster.

You could argue, however, that Onimusha's more focused approach makes each boss feel more memorable. I do think Zero's myriad opponents blend together a touch; I'd struggle to recall a lot of them if you were to put me on the spot.

But Onimusha also has a repeat problem. It reuses certain boss encounters multiple times, and even though the act of clashing with them is always engaging because of how strong the combat system is, it does get to a point later on where you find yourself asking "this guy again?".

The bottom line here is that both games have a range of incredible boss encounters — Onimusha in particular's got a few of my all-time favourites, like the brothers — but ultimately, it's so hard to look past the variety that's on display in Phantom Blade Zero.

When it comes to story and characters, I don't think there's any competition.

Phantom Blade Zero's narrative is arguably its weakest aspect. I think the plot itself is quite interesting once events start falling into place in the game's latter half, but until then, it's a mishmash of often incoherent flashbacks and exposition-heavy dialogue.

The story's sloppily told, and there are way too many notes and memory flowers — basically audio logs — to find in order to understand the full context of what's going on.

By contrast, Onimusha tells a story that's smaller in overall scope, with a greater emphasis on character interactions. The narrative stuff never outstays its welcome, with snappy dialogue and frankly fantastic cutscenes.

Plus, Musashi is a deeply endearing protagonist.

Phantom Blade Zero's got some amazing cinematics as well — the animated ones are superb — but they can't have the same impact when the context surrounding them is often so murky.

Placing great gameplay within a world that's lovely to look at and a joy to explore can be what truly elevates an action game.

The recent Ninja Gaiden 4, for example, is an absolute blast to play — but its drab, samey environments prevent it from feeling like a memorable journey.

Phantom Blade Zero certainly doesn't have that problem. Each chapter takes place within a visually distinct region. Most are very linear in structure, but when all the emphasis is on the action, that's a positive — all you really need is a nice backdrop.

Onimusha stumbles slightly in that sense. Between main story segments, you get to roam freely around a kind-of open world city map which gradually expands as you progress through the game.

The problem is it just gets a bit dreary; there are only so many times you can run down the same grey and brown streets before they start to feel like filler.

And while I think Phantom Blade Zero makes you backtrack a touch too often, the region-based structure promotes a sense of adventure and travel across a more fleshed out world.

Yep, as expected, I'm having a seriously tough time trying to separate these two titles. If anything, writing this article has just made me appreciate their individual strengths even more.

Onimusha is the action connoisseur's pick, I reckon. It's the refined choice, a more nuanced but masterful take on the genre from the veterans at Capcom.

Meanwhile, Phantom Blade Zero is the blockbuster. It's bigger in scope and ambition, and its action is consistently extreme. It's arguably the more exciting release, from a relatively new studio that could become a giant of the genre.

It's a good job I've got another month or so to think this one over...
