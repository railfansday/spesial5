BlackRock holds 123,056 Strategy shares ahead of MSCI exit, but how much must it sell?

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Three BlackRock ETFs reported 123,056 Strategy shares ahead of MSCI's Nov. 30 removal, raising questions about selling pressure.

When you buy an index fund, you're paying a manager to follow a particular group of stocks. MSCI creates these indexes and sets their membership rules, while companies such as BlackRock manage the funds that follow them.

MSCI is removing Strategy from several of its indexes after Nov. 30. Funds following those indexes will then be trying to match the performance of a group that no longer includes Michael Saylor's Bitcoin-buying company.

It's easy to see why investors expect selling: a fund that bought Strategy to follow an index loses that reason to own it when the stock drops out. But two BlackRock funds tracking the same MSCI index show why we can't simply add up all their investments and treat the total as a sell order.

One owns thousands of Strategy shares, while the other uses contracts with financial institutions to get the index's performance. Both managers have to account for the same change, but they don't have to do it through the same trades.

Their fund documents and published holdings let us follow part of that adjustment, including 123,056 Strategy shares reported by three BlackRock funds on Nov. 13, without pretending we know exactly how much stock will be sold at the end of the month.

MSCI's indexes put together stocks that meet a set of rules, such as company size and location, and assign each a share of the index's overall value.

A stock with a larger weight has more influence over how the index performs. The index serves as a benchmark, or performance target, for funds that follow it.

The MSCI USA Index includes large and midsize US companies, and Strategy's Class A shares are scheduled to leave it at the close of trading on Nov. 30, with the change taking effect Dec. 1. MSCI applied new eligibility rules for companies it classifies as non-operating, and Strategy was among the stocks selected for removal.

Other changes to specialized versions of MSCI's indexes are due to be announced on Nov. 18.

BlackRock's iShares MSCI USA ETF (MLRG) follows that US index and trades under the symbol MLRG. As an exchange-traded fund, investors buy and sell shares on a stock exchange while the manager handles the investments inside. Its published holdings included 5,227 Strategy shares on Nov. 13, worth about $583,000.

MLRG uses what's called representative sampling: the manager chooses a collection of investments designed to behave like the index without promising to own every stock on the list at every moment.

Its prospectus, the document setting out how the fund invests, says it intends to keep all or substantially all its assets in index stocks at roughly their index weights. It also allows some investments outside the index when they help the fund follow its performance, including around expected membership changes.

That gives the manager some flexibility while keeping the fund tied to its performance target. Strategy's departure is a reason to adjust the portfolio, but the documents don't require all 5,227 shares reported on Nov. 13 to be sold together at the Nov. 30 close.

BlackRock has another fund that follows the very same index: the iShares MSCI USA Swap UCITS ETF, traded in Europe under symbols including MUSD. This one owns a separate collection of developed-market stocks and uses contracts called total return swaps.

Under its investor document, the fund exchanges that stock portfolio's performance with financial institutions for the performance of the MSCI USA Index.

You're still buying a fund designed to follow MSCI USA, but the contracts provide that connection rather than a portfolio that reproduces the index stock for stock. When Strategy leaves, it stops contributing to the index performance those contracts are meant to deliver.

That doesn't tell us whether MUSD owns Strategy in its separate stock portfolio or whether it will sell any shares.

The counterparties of those contracts may also adjust their own investments to manage the risk they've taken on. Their trades aren't disclosed in these fund documents, so multiplying MUSD's assets by Strategy's index weight wouldn't tell us how many shares the fund has to sell.

BlackRock's iShares MSCI World ETF (URTH) follows companies across developed markets, including the US, and reported 29,345 Strategy shares, worth about $3.3 million, on Nov. 13.

Its iShares MSCI ACWI ETF follows companies in both developed and emerging markets and reported 88,484 shares, worth about $9.9 million, on the same date. These broader indexes include the US segment, so Strategy's removal from MSCI USA also affects them.

Together with MLRG, those funds held 123,056 Strategy shares valued at $13.74 million. We're looking at three identified portfolios on a particular day, rather than every fund affected by MSCI's decision.

Both URTH and ACWI also use representative sampling, so their managers have some flexibility in how they match their indexes.

MSCI's announcement for those specialized indexes is still due Nov. 18, so USXF isn't included in the three-fund subtotal.

Investors holding Strategy through a fund tied to another index don't automatically face the same change. For example, Invesco QQQ follows the Nasdaq-100, which has its own provider and membership rules.

Managers paid to choose stocks themselves can also keep assessing Strategy on their own terms, as can anyone who owns its shares directly.

In its response to MSCI's consultation, Strategy said that funds following the provider's global investable indexes held about 13 million of its shares, equivalent to 3.1% of basic shares outstanding.

That's a much broader estimate than the three-fund sample here, but the published statement doesn't identify the individual funds or give a current observation date for their holdings.

After the index changes, daily holdings files will let us compare what these funds owned before and afterward. Those files can show that a portfolio has fewer Strategy shares, but they won't necessarily show how the shares left it or why.

ETFs can grow or shrink as investors put money in or take it out. When a fund grows, its Strategy holding could increase simply because it needs more of the stocks it already owns.

Comparing the number of Strategy shares with the number of the fund's own shares helps separate fund growth from changes in how much of its portfolio it assigns to Strategy.

When an ETF shrinks, some stocks can leave through the process used to redeem its shares. Financial institutions can hand fund shares back to the ETF and receive underlying securities in return.

Strategy stock could leave the ETF without the fund itself selling it on an exchange, and the institution receiving that stock would then decide what to do with it. The next holdings file would record the smaller position without telling us about those later decisions.

MUSD adds another limit to what we can observe because its contracts deliver the index performance. Its counterparties could adjust their investments as Strategy leaves, but following only the fund's stock holdings wouldn't capture those decisions.

Even when we can see holdings change, comparing two dates won't show how much of Strategy's share-price movement was driven by MSCI, especially when investors can trade ahead of the announced change.

Strategy will still lose a place in the performance target these funds are paid to follow.

That could weaken demand for its stock and make raising money less attractive for a company that sells securities to finance Bitcoin purchases, although the index removal doesn't require Strategy to sell any Bitcoin itself.
