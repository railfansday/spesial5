Taxes may be the last thing on your mind right now. The kids are studying for mid-terms and the holidays are almost upon us. But with less than 3 months left in 2026, you have a small window to implement strategies to minimize your tax bill for 2027.

According to Medscape’s 2026 compensation report, physicians earn, on average, $386,000 a year. Primary care physicians earn $298,000 and specialists make, on average, $417,000.

In 2026, a single filer’s taxable income of $256,225 puts them in the 35% bracket. Same for an income of $512,450 MFJ (married filing jointly). The 37% bracket starts above $640,600 for singles and $768,700 for couples.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) made permanent many provisions that had been scheduled to expire after 2025. It also added limits and phaseouts that affect higher earners specifically. In this guide, I’ll cover the changes that matter to physicians and what you can do before the year is up.

You can’t always choose when you receive income but some form of income like bonuses, self-employment income, Treasury bill income, and voluntary retirement distributions can be timed to reduce your tax bill for 2026.

Deferring income means you receive it in 2027 instead of 2026, and thus pay the tax a year later. Accelerating work in the opposite way. You can also defer or accelerate deductions to either lower this year’s bill or leave it on the table for next year.

If your employer pays a $20,000 bonus on Dec. 20, it counts as 2026 income. If your employer pays it on Jan. 5, it counts as 2027 income. At a 35% rate, that single date change delays $7,000 of federal tax by a full year.

If you expect to be in the same bracket with lower rates next year, you can defer the bonus. If you think you’ll be in a higher bracket next year, go ahead and take it in 2026.

According to this comprehensive guide by Plante Moran, pushing income into 2027 and pulling deductible expenses into 2026 delays tax, which usually helps. A higher bracket next year means you should take the opposite approach because you pay tax on 2026 income at your lower current rate.

If you’re starting as an attending in 2027, you fit the second group. Taking taxable gains or converting pre-tax IRA money to Roth this year will cost you less than doing it after your pay jumps.

Employed physicians don’t have much control but ask your group whether it can pay a year-end bonus in January. Locums and 1099 physicians can, however, control invoicing and equipment purchases.

Check your Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT) exposure before you defer anything. AMT works like a second tax calculation.

First, you calculate your regular tax, then, you recalculate with certain deductions added back, including your SALT deduction. Then you subtract an AMT exemption, which in 2026, is $90,100 for single filers and $140,200 for joint filers. You then must pay whichever tax is higher.

The OBBBA shrank that exemption for high earners in 2026. It starts the reduction at $500,000 of AMTI for singles and $1,000,000 for couples. It then cuts the exemption by 50 cents for every dollar above those thresholds.

The exemption disappears at $680,200 for singles and $1,280,400 for couples.

All that is to say that each extra $1,000 of income inside the phaseout range costs you $500 of exemption, so $1,500 becomes taxable at 28%. That equals a 42% marginal rate, which beats the 37% top regular rate.

Once the exemption disappears, your marginal AMT rate drops to 28%. Estimate your marginal rate for 2026 and 2027, then move income toward the lower-rate year.

Your employer withholds the additional 0.9% Medicare tax only on wages above $200,000, regardless of your filing status or your spouse’s pay. The tax itself applies above $250,000 for joint filers.

Two married physicians who each earn $300,000 have an employer who starts withholding the extra 0.9% Medicare tax once that physician’s pay passes $200,000. Each physician has $100,000 above that line, so each employer withholds $900. Together, the couple has $1,800 withheld.

With a combined wages of $600,000 the joint cutoff is $250,000, so the couple owes 0.9% of the rest of the $350,000. That’s $3,150. After subtracting the $1,800 withheld by the employers, the  couple owes $1,350 more when they file.

You can raise your W-4 withholding before Dec. 31 to cover it. This will require you to submit a new W-4 to your employer and enter the extra amount per paycheck in Step 4(c).

Withholding counts as paid evenly across the year so it beats a late estimated payment. Penalty protection requires payments of at least 90% of your 2026 tax or 110% of your 2025 tax. The 100% safe harbor covers only taxpayers with 2025 AGI of $150,000 or less, which rules out most attendings.

The annualized income installment method helps when income skews toward year-end, as locums and bonus income often do.

The 3.8% net investment income tax (NIIT) starts at $200,000 of MAGI for single filers and $250,000 for joint filers.

Those thresholds have stayed the same since 2013, so every raise adds more of your investment income into the tax. Anything that lowers MAGI, including retirement contributions and HSA deposits, lowers your NIIT too.

The IRS raised the 2026 employee limit for 401(k), 403(b) and governmental 457 plans to $24,500. Catch-up contributions add $8,000 at age 50 or older, for a total of $32,500.

There’s also a bonus catch-up of $11,250 for ages 60 to 63, which brings the total to $35,750. If your hospital also offers a governmental 457(b), that plan carries its own limit of $24,500.

SECURE 2.0 now requires employees who earned more than $150,000 in 2025 wages to direct catch-up contributions to Roth accounts. That’s all attendings. If your plan offers no Roth option, you can’t make 2026 catch-up contributions at all.

Direct Roth IRA contributions phase out between $153,000 and $168,000 for single filers and between $242,000 and $252,000 for joint filers.

Employed physicians can contribute $7,500 to a nondeductible traditional IRA and convert it. This works best when neither spouse has funds in a traditional IRA, and you owe tax only on growth between the contribution and the conversion.

A pre-tax IRA balance makes part of the conversion taxable, so roll it into your 401(k) before Dec. 31 if your plan accepts rollovers.

Practice owners have bigger options. A profit-sharing plan allows total contributions of $72,000, or $80,000 with catch-up, or $83,250 at ages 60 to 63.

A SEP allows $72,000 and requires at least $360,000 of self-employment income before the deduction.

A defined benefit plan can allow larger deductible contributions than either, depending on your age and target benefit. The 2026 maximum is $290,000 or 100% of your highest 3-year average earned income, whichever is lower.

Self-employed physicians can set up a plan and make 2026 contributions as late as the tax return due date, including extensions. If you have employees, you likely must let them participate.

For 2026, the HSA limits are $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage, plus $1,000 at age 55 or older. Contributions reduce AGI and qualified withdrawals come out tax-free. A family contribution of $8,750 can save you about $3,063 in federal tax at a 35% rate.

A health FSA is limited to $3,400 and you can carry over up to $680 of unused funds to 2027. The dependent care FSA limit rose to $7,500 from $5,000, which saves you $2,625 at a 35% rate. Those contributions reduce the expenses you can claim for the child and dependent care credit.

Today’s relatively low rates raise the odds that your retirement rate will be higher.

Permanent rates have no expiration date but Congress can still raise them. Converting in chunks over several years keeps each year’s income from jumping into a higher bracket. But a Roth conversion adds to your taxable income in the year you convert so confirm you can pay the tax from outside the IRA.

Early-retired and part-time physicians must now deal with expiration of the enhanced premium tax credit. This means that the ACA cliff now applies to them.

If you earn $1 above 400% of the poverty line, you lose all marketplace subsidies. The threshold is $62,600 for a single person and $129,000 for a family of 4 in 2026.

Tommy Lucas, a CFP at Moisand Fitzgerald Tamayo recommends pairing a high-deductible plan with HSA contributions, which lower your AGI.

Two new rules have reduced the value of itemized gifts.

A 0.5% of AGI floor is now in effect which means a physician with $500,000 of AGI can’t deduct the first $2,500 of donations.

The OBBBA also caps the tax benefit at 35 cents per dollar for filers in the 37% bracket.

Bunching charitable giving can fix most of the floor issue. The floor applies once per year, so 5 years of $10,000 gifts at $500,000 of AGI forfeit $2,500 each year and leave $37,500 deductible.

One $50,000 gift forfeits $2,500 once and leaves $47,500 deductible. The extra $10,000 saves $3,500 at a 35% rate.

Charissa Anderson, a CFP at Ferguson Wellman Capital Management, says that bunching works best when you pair a donor-advised fund with appreciated stock. A donor-advised fund lets you make 1 large gift now and send grants to charities over time.

Plante Moran warns that you should get written acknowledgment that the sponsoring organization holds exclusive legal control of the assets.

If you itemize, shares held for more than 1 year before donating let you deduct full market value and skip the capital gains tax you’d pay otherwise. Most physicians pay 15% on long-term gains plus the 3.8% NIIT, which totals 18.8%.

Physicians with taxable income above $545,500 (single) or $613,700 (joint) pay 20% plus the NIIT, which totals 23.8%. If you donate $50,000 of shares with a $20,000 basis, you can avoid up to $5,640 at 18.8% or $7,140 at 23.8% of the $30,000 gain.

The deduction is capped at 30% of AGI for public charities and you can carry the excess forward 5 years.

Donors can use tax lot selection, as in choosing which specific shares to give. Lots with the largest gains usually make the best candidates. Never donate shares that trade below your basis. Sell them, claim the loss and donate the cash.

Physicians who take the standard deduction ($16,100 single, $32,200 joint) can now deduct up to $1,000 of cash gifts or $2,000 for couples. A couple in the 35% bracket can save $700.

The Section 199A deduction is permanent and equals up to 20% of qualified business income.

S corporation owners can take part of their income as distributions instead of salary, which avoids payroll taxes and the 0.9% Medicare tax. The IRS scrutinizes this closely, so make sure your salary is reasonable.
