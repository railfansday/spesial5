Mental health’s short-term mindset problem

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

When I struggled with anorexia as an adolescent, I learned that the traditional healthcare ecosystem had a simplified view of recovery: stabilize vitals and clear the patient out to keep costs down.

Now, as an eating disorder treatment founder and CEO, I spend my days working with collaborative health insurance plans to fix these historical dynamics. Together, we’ve made incredible progress toward value-based care, a system that rewards treatment providers for getting patients better rather than for how often patients see them.

But as the mental health industry works to measure real clinical impact, providers and health plans share the same roadblock: overreliance on immediate, short-term financial ROI.

Because health plans and employers usually operate on 12- to 24-month budget cycles, standard financial models naturally hunt for quick wins, like preventing an ER visit within that same year. Behavioral health doesn’t fit into a short-term box.

This isn’t about bad intentions; it’s just how the financial system was built. In practice, though, it leaves out people managing on the outside who still need real support before things get worse.

THE STRUCTURAL CHALLENGE

Real health savings take time. We already spend money upfront to manage conditions like diabetes, because it prevents far more expensive health emergencies years later. Behavioral health needs that same long-term investment.

A major roadblock is that behavioral and physical health costs are tracked in separate silos. On paper, investing in mental health looks like an extra expense, even when it directly drives down physical healthcare costs, like expensive hospital admissions.

I lived this firsthand: I appeared as a “zero-cost” member on claims data until I was hospitalized, leading to months of treatment. Early intervention could have prevented immense suffering and expense, yet data shows nearly 40% of pediatric eating disorder patients first enter the healthcare system under general medical complaints rather than a behavioral health diagnosis. When hospitalization does occur, the median time from diagnosis to admission is under a month.

THE MYTH OF THE “LOW-RISK” MEMBER

The true cost of mental illness isn’t always apparent in claims data. For example, millions of people struggling with things like severe eating disorders, OCD, major depression, and anxiety arrive at work or school every day, meet their daily goals, turn in homework, and maintain a general outward appearance of being well.

Because they aren’t ending up in emergency rooms or generating high costs, they’re viewed as low-cost.

Evaluating mental healthcare strictly on one-year cost savings makes it hard to justify giving high-functioning people the full support they need. On paper, their immediate medical costs look minimal, so why invest in specialized care before something terrible happens?

Under standard short-term budget cycles, care is too often delayed until it becomes a crisis, costing both the person and their health plan much more in the long run.

CO-CREATE THE NEXT PHASE OF VALUE-BASED CARE

Health plans are the indispensable engine of healthcare innovation, and real systemic change happens only when health plans and care providers agree on incentives—something I’ve seen firsthand with some of the partners we work with at Equip. Moving beyond short-term cost containment toward long-term value on investment (VOI) requires new frameworks:

  • Health plan partnerships could look at whether people are regaining their lives—such as staying symptom-free, doing well at work, and feeling good—instead of just counting how many people stayed out of the hospital. 
  • Health insurance plans and employers can work together on long-term contracts, giving treatment the time it needs to pay off in real, cumulative savings.
  • Getting people help early keeps them healthy and stops small problems from turning into bigger ones. The crazy thing is, the current system only rewards fixing things after they break. So when someone actually steps up and chooses to get help, the system sees that as an extra bill instead of a huge win.

Fixing this doesn’t mean ignoring financial reality, but we need to look at the bigger picture together. To fulfill the promise of value-based care, we need models that match the reality of recovery.

Resilience should open doors to care, not close them, and together, we can build a healthcare system that keeps people well before they break.

Kristina Saffran is cofounder and CEO of Equip.
