An old origami pattern inspires a new approach to construction

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Researchers developed origami-inspired aluminum blocks that form lightweight, rigid structures, offering a potential new way to build emergency shelters.

Origami is usually something people do with paper. Using a folding pattern from the 1960s, researchers have turned flat sheets of aluminum into rigid building blocks.

Once they’re connected with ordinary hand tools, the blocks form a stiff, lightweight shell that holds itself up without any columns inside.

Every piece starts flat, so a whole structure could be shipped as a stack of panels and shaped by hand where it’s needed.

That makes the approach a candidate for relief shelters, set up quickly at the site of a natural disaster such as an earthquake or flood.

The method was developed by Alfonso Parra Rubio, a graduate student at the MIT Center for Bits and Atoms, with colleagues in Italy, Austria, and Japan. The center studies how to turn digital designs into physical things.

So far, the team’s strength figures come from computer simulations, and the approach hasn’t yet been tried on a full-size building.

“This work shows that the power of digital manufacturing can go beyond personal fabrication projects and start touching the real world, enhancing the way we design houses and large structures,” said Parra Rubio, the paper’s lead author.

“We are demonstrating structural origami on building scales.”

Parra Rubio’s design builds on the folded patterns that the artist and scientist Ron Resch developed in the 1960s.

Resch repeated small many-sided cells across a surface to build complex 3D shapes, and each shape came from a single folded sheet.

The new method folds each small unit on its own and connects the units afterward. Building from separate blocks makes the parts simpler to produce, and it lets the team create more complicated shapes.

Working in the lab of Tomohiro Tachi at the University of Tokyo, Parra Rubio found a way to pair two tripod-shaped units from a Resch pattern into one block.

Pairing the units locks their folds in place, creating a rigid block.

Joined together, the blocks form a hexagonal grid of interwoven beams. The grid is extremely stiff and stable, yet it weighs very little.

“This helps us build strong structures while minimizing the amount of material we are using,” Parra Rubio said.

Designing every block by hand, so that the finished structure matches the shape a builder wants, would be enormously difficult.

Parra Rubio teamed up with Erik Strand, another graduate student at the center, to write a program that does the job automatically.

The program designs a set of blocks that can be cut, folded, and joined to form the requested shape.

The resulting surface can curve in one direction or in two directions, as a dome does.

From there, the design files are sent to a computer-controlled milling machine, which cuts the blocks out of flat sheets of material such as aluminum.

“On the computational part, we break this big problem down into many small subproblems. Now, we solve each cell independently to make sure that it is foldable, and then we globally assemble them after that,” Strand said.

Builders fold each unit into shape and put the structure together with hand tools such as screwdrivers.

Assembly requires no welding equipment, which could make construction simpler, faster, and cheaper.

The units are also designed so that each one takes its correct shape as the structure goes together.

“The way the unit cells are designed, the structure can only exist in one configuration. The connection is the same everywhere, even though each unit cell is different,” Parra Rubio said.

In the team’s computer simulations, shells built this way came out about 3.5 times stiffer than those made with some other origami-based methods. They also needed only about two-thirds as much material.

Because the method could reduce metal use, it may be a more sustainable form of construction.

Parra Rubio said the finished structures could be strong enough for some architectural uses.

The method could also produce strong, lightweight parts for airplanes and for modular robots, along with panels that dampen sound and the outer walls of buildings.

To show that the method works beyond small prototypes, the team assembled a 23-foot (7-meter) sculpture from folded aluminum.

It was hung from the ceiling of the atrium at the Boston Public Library, as part of an exhibition opened for the annual Fab Lab Conference and Symposium.

A team with no professional construction experience built the entire sculpture in a matter of days, Parra Rubio said.

“It was really fun to work on something at such a quick pace where the theory was really meeting a practical use for it. We had to think a lot about how we could tweak the design to achieve each objective,” Strand said.

The researchers next plan to test the approach on larger architectural structures.

The team also wants to try units with more complex shapes, which might offer more strength or stiffness. And they hope to build a design program that’s easy to use.

Neil Gershenfeld, an MIT professor who directs the center, was the paper’s senior author.

“This work shows that folding metal is a method that is ready to be used for building structures,” Gershenfeld said.

“We can do it big enough and strong enough, now that we can bring it out of the lab and to the construction site.”

Larger tests will show whether folded aluminum performs as well in a real building as it did in the simulations.

The full study was published in the journal International Journal of Solids and Structures.
