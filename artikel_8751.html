We Tested 15 Electric Mini-Inflators. These Tiny Pumps Are Fast Enough to Replace CO₂ on Many Rides

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

From 90-gram pocket pumps to premium inflators, these editor-tested picks make flat fixes easier.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

Pumping up tires is a constant of cycling. Whether you’re checking pressure before a road ride, fixing a mid-ride flat on your gravel bike, fine-tuning your air pressure at the trailhead, or topping up tires on your kid’s bike in the garage, every cyclist needs to put air in their bike’s tires

Some riders don’t worry about the exact psi in their tires, but others constantly check and analyze their air pressure, and the latter group may be onto something. In his story, “More Speed and Comfort? The Right Tire Pressure Can Give You Both,” Test Editor Dan Chabanov explains that having the correct pressure in your tires provides more confidence and control on rides.

Small, portable electric inflators have recently taken the cycling world by storm and quickly gained popularity with riders. For several decades, traditional pumps (floor, frame, and mini), CO₂ cartridges, and air compressors have been the go-to methods for filling tires. While mini-inflators don’t fully replace these other inflator types, these small electric units are now finding their way into riders’ toolboxes, gear bags, jersey pockets, and seat bags.

Over the last two seasons, we’ve subjected 15 electric mini-inflators to the same tests. We originally tested 11 inflators in 2025. In November 2026, we tested 4 models (from Cyclami, PDW, State, and Lezyne) and updated our Best Mini-Inflators ranking. A handful stand out for specific use cases or rider types. But these are only a few of the great pumps out there; choose the one that best fits your needs and budget.

With svelte dimensions and a 106-gram weight, the Ultra Tiny is small enough to stash in a jersey pocket, flat kit, or bar bag. Plus, it quickly inflates tires of all sizes to pressure. The AS2’s super-accurate gauge also makes it worth considering for your home shop or carrying in a race-day tool kit. However, the pump runs a little hot.

At the media launch for the Colnago G-5X, someone asked the brand representatives if any inflators fit inside the internal frame storage. That led me to search for options that can fit inside frames. The Cyclami A5S came up in some discussions, so I ordered one on AliExpress to test.

Overall, it’s a solid little inflator that performs well—sub-one-minute inflation times and a 122-gram weight. The rounded profile and placement of the air chuck (on top rather than the side) help it squeeze into many frames’ internal storage doors. Of the five road, gravel, and mountain frames in the office I checked, the A5S fit into four (it didn’t fit into the Cervélo Caledonia).

Several Bicycling editors rely on the Air Rush on rides and at home. Overall, it’s a solid and dependable pick. It has a nice display, runs cool, and inflates road and gravel tires in under a minute. It also filled five road tires; that’s better than most units. I only wish it were slightly smaller for carrying in a seat bag or internal frame storage.

Like many RockBros products, this mini-inflator isn’t the best at any single thing, but it does everything pretty well overall. It gets tires up to pressure in under 50 seconds, doesn’t run too hot, isn’t too loud, and isn’t super heavy.

The most expensive model in our test, the Ultimate, lived up to its name, hype, and steep price tag. And matching its durable appearance, this inflator is a beast—it gets tires up to pressure in under 30 seconds and inflates eight tires on a single charge. I tested Silca’s claim that the Ultimate will seat tubeless tires—surprisingly, it succeeded.

The Ultimate was the quietest inflator in our testing and tied for the second-lowest temperature. Other than price, the biggest negatives are the Ultimate’s weight and size. One note: after almost a year of frequent use, an O-ring inside the pump failed. However, Silca offered to replace the unit under warranty.

The Pocket SE doesn’t feature a display or gauge, but at only 90 grams, it weighs less than a single CO₂ cartridge and head. And though it takes roughly a minute to inflate a tire to full pressure, I can fill four tires on one charge. The Pocket SE’s one downside is that without a pressure gauge or a 72 psi cutoff, I’d be cautious using it on hookless tubeless road wheels.

For a year, I’ve carried the Pocket SE in my seat bag for flat fixes, and it has gotten me out of plenty of jams. My only complaint is that the button activates easily; a bump or pothole can trigger the unit if it isn’t carefully positioned in your bag.

Portland Design Works makes well-crafted, usable gear for everyday cyclists. The brand doesn’t take itself too seriously, and its products sometimes have whimsical or quirky elements (like its water bottle cages).

PDW’s Essentials inflator follows this mold—it’s well-designed, looks good, and functions well. The Essentials inflates tires quickly, and the gauge is pretty accurate. Its best feature is presets for different bike types (labeled road, gravel, mountain, and city). You can use these to set your tires for your quiver of bikes, or program them for different rides, wheelsets, or riders in your house.

The screw-on chuck is slightly tricky to use and leaks some air if not fastened correctly. While the unit’s body doesn’t run hot, the hose’s base certainly does: heed that yellow warning tag.

Mini-inflators are pint-sized electric air compressors. Larger electric pumps have been around for many years and are commonly used at home, in the garage, or the workshop. Some people carry electric pumps in their vehicles to air up car or truck tires and fill inflatable rafts or SUPs. Cordless air inflators have been a common sight around World Cup mountain bike team paddocks and cyclocross pit zones for several years.

Yet cyclists only started using these inflators within the last three years, when the prices dropped, the form factor shrank, and battery life increased. Riders soon found electric inflators perfect for the garage or the trailhead. Plus, their smaller size makes them much easier to toss in a bike bag or luggage than a floor pump when flying with a bike.

Several cycling equipment brands have released models tailored to bicycle riders’ tastes and preferences. Some companies have even cut enough weight and reduced the proportions to make electric mini-inflators a viable option for carrying in a jersey pocket or flat kit on rides.

The current crop of bike-specific mini-inflators can be broken down into two groups:

1) Small enough to bring on a ride, and 2) Everything else.

Of course, your definition of “small enough” might vary greatly from another cyclist’s. If you ride with a bar bag or frame pack, an inflator like the Portland Design Works Essentials or Trek Air Rush might be sufficiently tiny. However, if you carry everything in a ride wallet or obsess about weight, even Silca’s 112-gram Elettrico Micro might be too large for you.

For our testing, we focused on mini-inflators sold by cycling-specific brands and ones marketed for bicycle use. This isn’t an exhaustive test of every inflator available; instead, it provides context to help you choose one best for your needs.

In two years of testing, we’ve evaluated fifteen models across a wide price range ($15 to $160). These include mini-inflators from respected cycling brands, options from smaller companies, and those that fly under the radar.

To test if size matters, I picked some based on their tidy proportions and low weight. I also chose a couple of larger, heavier options to see whether they push air significantly faster or fill more tires than other mini-inflators. I also bought the cheapest mini-inflator available on Amazon ($14.90 on the day I was shopping) to test it against the pack and see if spending more on a pump really matters.

As with other Bicycling head-to-head product tests (like insulated water bottles, flared handlebars, and wide road tires), we evaluated electric mini-inflators based on real-world requirements, not intricate lab tests. Riders can replicate at home without much fancy equipment.

We inflated the tires to air pressures for a 180-pound cyclist riding a road, gravel, and mountain bike in average conditions (as recommended by Wolf Tooth’s app). For road riders, we recorded the time to inflate a 28mm-wide tubeless tire to 65 psi. We also measured the time to reach 30 psi on a 45mm gravel tire. For mountain bike riders (or folks riding new-school gravel bikes), we clocked the time to 20 psi on a 29x2.3-inch tire. To evaluate the pumps without built-in gauges, we measured the pressure they reached after 50 seconds.

After timing inflation speeds for each category, I fully recharged all devices. The next morning, I tested how many times each electric mini-inflator could fill a 28mm road tire to 65 psi from fully flat. To prevent overheating, I let each inflator cool down for ten minutes between fills.

In addition to real-world tests, we also measured three common concerns of electric pump buyers: Temperature, noise, and gauge accuracy. For these tests, I borrowed equipment from my colleagues on the Popular Mechanics test team.

This included a specially designed fixture that measures bike pump gauge accuracy. Gauge accuracy is important, especially for riders using hookless tubeless road wheels. Since the maximum allowed pressure for these wheel systems under ETRTO standards is 5 bar (72.5 psi), you don’t want to risk a tire blowing off a rim due to a wildly inaccurate pump reading.

Like other users, I have personally experienced noise and heat build-up issues with electric mini-inflators. Early one morning at Sea Otter Classic, my inflator woke the cyclist in the hotel room next door—my sincere apologies to him! Later, on an All Bodies on Bikes group ride, the inflator I borrowed from another cyclist to change a flat melted the plastic valve of my spare TPU tube.

Lesson Learned: Due to excess heat, always use an extension hose when filling plastic-valved tubes with an electric mini-pump.

“Do I plan to carry this on rides?” you ask when purchasing a mini-inflator. If you answered “yes”, look for lighter-weight and smaller-profile models. If you only plan to use the inflator at home, at the trailhead, or at races, don’t focus on size or weight.

Unless you only need to get out of a jam on the side of a road or trail, look for models that fill more than two tires. This allows you to adjust tire pressure and inflate tires on other bikes or spare wheels (and for friends to borrow before a ride—because you know they will need it).

After evaluating these mini-inflators, my key takeaway is that price doesn’t exactly make it the best for your needs. The most expensive pump in our test has brilliant inflation speed, is the quietest, and doesn’t run hot—plus it fills lots of tires. But it’s too heavy and large for most cyclists to carry on rides. However, spending more doesn’t guarantee better performance either, as some pricier models didn’t outshine the low- or mid-priced models. And, as I found out from testing, you probably don’t want to buy the absolute cheapest possible option either (luckily, this model is no longer available).

Skimming listings for different devices, you’ll notice (from similar design details and parts) that a select few vendors manufacture many electric inflator models for every brand. But even if a mini-inflator shares the same outer silicone protector, case, buttons, or display, it doesn’t mean they’re identical. For example, in our testing, the Rockbros model outperformed the extremely similar-looking Muc-Off Air Mach pump.

Unless you specifically need a lightweight, compact mini-inflator for on-the-bike use, spend a little more for a model with a built-in gauge. A display is super handy, and you can pre-set your desired pressure within 1 psi (some even read to tenths).

Pro Tip: If you buy an inflator without a built-in gauge, time how long it takes to reach your optimal ride pressure from fully flat before bringing it out on the road or trail. Then, when you’re airing up a flat fix, you can count off (or use a timer) to get you relatively close to your riding pressure.
