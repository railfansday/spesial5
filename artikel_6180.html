Hyatt Guest Charged $50 For A “No-Show” Despite Staying—Points To The Bigger Risk Of Booking With Points

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

A Hyatt guest booked two nights with points, arrived in the middle of the night, and says the hotel charged an extra $50 for being a no-show — even though they showed up and stayed. Hyatt kept the points for both nights, too. After a month of calls, the guest still can’t get a straight answer. The customer service explanation they’ve received doesn’t match Hyatt’s award no show terms. But those terms are bad enough on their own: when you cancel an award past the deadline, or fail to arrive, you are supposed to get your points back and get stuck with a cash bill of an indeterminate amount instead. I’ve covered one of these charges for more than $11,000. The amount isn’t disclosed when you book. They Showed Up And Want Their $50 Back The guest describes a two-night points booking at a Hyatt in Palm Springs. They arrived in the wee hours after the scheduled arrival date. They’re happy to pay the points for both nights that they reserved and stayed. They’re disputing the additional $50: Yeah crazy right! I am not trying to get my night refunded at all, just the no show fee. In the customer service conversation the guest shared, Hyatt’s agent says the first night’s points were forfeited because they didn’t show up, and that a cash no show fee still applies. The agent compares this to checking in and then leaving early, when unused points wouldn’t be returned. The guest says repeated calls to the hotel and Hyatt have ended with being placed on hold and disconnected. The chat ends with a case number and a promise of an email within 72 hours. Is This Right? from r/hyatt If you’re arriving in the middle of the night, tell the hotel and have them note that you’re still coming. Otherwise their overnight audit can mark you as a no show. Here it seems like the reservation was marked as a no show and then reinstated when the guest arrived, without the billing getting properly reconciled. Hyatt’s Agent Appears To Be Mixing Up Two Different Rules Under Hyatt’s award terms, a late cancellation or no-show generally means a cash penalty under the property’s policy, with the points or award returned. The rate is the hotel’s Standard Rate being offered when the charge is made. If Hyatt can’t collect payment, it retains or deducts the points instead. Guests need to find a way to make the cash uncollectible! That’s different from the rule for someone who checks in and departs early. Hyatt’s terms do provide for forfeiting unused award nights in that situation. The agent appears to think they’re applying that separate rule to a supposed no show. If the hotel honored both nights as an award stay, using both nights’ points makes sense. There’s no coherent explanation for the $50, and it’s clearly not the room rate penalty for a no show or late cancel which would be much higher. It might even be an internal award reimbursement amount incorrectly charged to the guest. You Book With Points But The Cancellation Penalty Is (A Lot Of) Cash Most people reasonably assume that if they book a nonrefundable award—or cancel after the deadline they’ll lose the points they used. That’s what they agreed to spend. Instead, major hotel programs return the points and let the hotel charge their credit card instead: Marriott: Section 3.2.d provides for the property’s applicable cash cancellation fee and redeposit of the points. Hilton: terms expressly permit cash penalties on reward stays. The hotel can specify its fee; otherwise the fallback is one night’s room and tax at its Best Available Rate for that date. IHG: terms authorize a cash no-show charge under the hotel’s policy. Six Senses is an exception: its award rule provides for forfeiting the points or free night certificate instead. A Hilton guest reported getting 20,000 points back and being charged £99 after arranging a morning arrival and actually using the room. And there was a Holiday Inn placing a €103.69 hold on an award guest’s card specifically to secure a potential no-show charge. That guest stayed and the hold was released. You’re Never Told The Amount You’re On The Hook For I once booked a Hyatt stay for 90,000 points. The confirmation said the cancellation penalty was “50 PCT OF STAY.” I’d read that as 45,000 points. Instead, I was told I’d owe half the paid rate. My concierge ultimately got it waived. I had Covid and said that if they were going to charge for the late cancel, I’d be forced to show up. And while I was in the lobby I’d have to be honest with other guests that the hotel pressured me to come and expose them. Specifying that the penalty is one night doesn’t put guests on notice for what Hyatt actually intends to charge – and their rules just reference the rate when the charge is made, whatever that may be. I’ve also received a Marriott hotel’s warning of a $1,999-plus-tax penalty on an award reservation. At least that property told me the amount. It did so after I’d booked. I don’t see how a guest meaningfully agrees to thousands of dollars in liability when the booking never tells them the amount. These booking disclosures seem ripe for a class action. Some of the examples I’ve covered: $11,212.50 at Montaneros in Vail: The guest cancelled 58 days before arrival, just inside a 60-day deadline. Hyatt ultimately confirmed a full refund and said it was examining the cancellation language for Destination Residences. $5,500 after a death in the family: A guest had to cancel a 63,000-point Lake Como stay because his father’s funeral conflicted with the trip. $3,690 at Thompson Central Park: A 58,000-point booking was noncancellable from the outset. The guest cancelled months ahead, got the points back, and then received the cash invoice three weeks later. A flight cancellation can trigger this just as easily as a change of plans. You may be perfectly willing to lose the points for the night you missed. That doesn’t mean you budgeted for buying the room all over again at a cash price you weren’t quoted. Starwood Had A Better Way Of Handling This The underlying problem is that the hotel and loyalty program are separate parties. The hotel doesn’t keep your points as payment. The loyalty program does, and it pays cash to the hotel. It doesn’t want to pay for an award stay that never happened, because that’s ripe for fraud – the hotel redeems a bunch of no show stays on nights they’re near full occupancy to scam the program for increased reimbursement rates. As I explained about Starwood years ago, reimbursing unused award reservations could let hotels collect for stays that weren’t real. High occupancy can make award reimbursements much more valuable, creating an incentive to manipulate the numbers. Starwood’s own representative acknowledged the need to verify stays to prevent fraud, while distinguishing that from the cancellation penalty imposed on members. And the Parker Meridien litigation was about inflated occupancy to extract higher award reimbursements. But making customers pay an undisclosed retail rate isn’t the only way to control reimbursement. It can even leave the hotel better much off when a guest misses the stay than when they actually arrive because the cash penalty will generally exceed what the program would have paid by a lot. At the Andaz Papagayo, I helped get a $7,000 penalty reversed after a cancellation six months ahead. The property had all that time to sell the room again. Starwood eventually allowed members to substitute points forfeiture for the cash charge. You could request the cash refund within 60 days of the scheduled arrival date and give up the points instead. That gave the member a choice, rather than simply letting the hotel claim a cash windfall. Marriott didn’t preserve that protection when they combined programs, but Hyatt could adopt it. Hyatt’s Customer Service Should Be Able To Untangle A $50 Bill In June 2025, I reported Hyatt’s major U.S. customer service cuts. The company confirmed a reduction of about 30% across its Americas guest services and support teams. Nebraska’s layoff listing records 286 affected jobs associated with Omaha and remote work. I’ve noticed a decline in central customer service, especially through Twitter direct messages. My experience with that team immediately after the cuts was still good. More recently, it hasn’t been as good. Here, instead of sorting out whether both award nights were honored, what happened to the no show transaction, and what the $50 actually was for, the agent offers an explanation that mixes together different rules and sends the guest back to the hotel. If the hotel used both nights as an award stay, I’d expect the points to remain deducted and the unexplained $50 to come off. If it insists the first night was a no show, Hyatt needs to reconcile that with its points return rule (and the fact that the guest was literally there and stayed in the room). A member shouldn’t need a month of calls to get someone to sort this. I don’t see how a guest meaningfully agrees to thousands of dollars in liability when the booking never tells them the amount. These booking disclosures seem ripe for a class action. Some of the examples I’ve covered: $11,212.50 at Montaneros in Vail: The guest cancelled 58 days before arrival, just inside a 60-day deadline. Hyatt ultimately confirmed a full refund and said it was examining the cancellation language for Destination Residences. $5,500 after a death in the family: A guest had to cancel a 63,000-point Lake Como stay because his father’s funeral conflicted with the trip. $3,690 at Thompson Central Park: A 58,000-point booking was noncancellable from the outset. The guest cancelled months ahead, got the points back, and then received the cash invoice three weeks later. A flight cancellation can trigger this just as easily as a change of plans. You may be perfectly willing to lose the points for the night you missed. That doesn’t mean you budgeted for buying the room all over again at a cash price you weren’t quoted. Starwood Had A Better Way Of Handling This The underlying problem is that the hotel and loyalty program are separate parties. The hotel doesn’t keep your points as payment. The loyalty program does, and it pays cash to the hotel. It doesn’t want to pay for an award stay that never happened, because that’s ripe for fraud – the hotel redeems a bunch of no show stays on nights they’re near full occupancy to scam the program for increased reimbursement rates. As I explained about Starwood years ago, reimbursing unused award reservations could let hotels collect for stays that weren’t real. High occupancy can make award reimbursements much more valuable, creating an incentive to manipulate the numbers. Starwood’s own representative acknowledged the need to verify stays to prevent fraud, while distinguishing that from the cancellation penalty imposed on members. And the Parker Meridien litigation was about inflated occupancy to extract higher award reimbursements. But making customers pay an undisclosed retail rate isn’t the only way to control reimbursement. It can even leave the hotel better much off when a guest misses the stay than when they actually arrive because the cash penalty will generally exceed what the program would have paid by a lot. At the Andaz Papagayo, I helped get a $7,000 penalty reversed after a cancellation six months ahead. The property had all that time to sell the room again. Starwood eventually allowed members to substitute points forfeiture for the cash charge. You could request the cash refund within 60 days of the scheduled arrival date and give up the points instead. That gave the member a choice, rather than simply letting the hotel claim a cash windfall. Marriott didn’t preserve that protection when they combined programs, but Hyatt could adopt it. If the hotel used both nights as an award stay, I’d expect the points to remain deducted and the unexplained $50 to come off. If it insists the first night was a no show, Hyatt needs to reconcile that with its points return rule (and the fact that the guest was literally there and stayed in the room). A member shouldn’t need a month of calls to get someone to sort this.
