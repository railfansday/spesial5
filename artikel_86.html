Love made a fool of Beau Lamarre-Condon but did not make him a murderer, defence argues

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Crown says accused former officer developed ‘jealous obsession’ but defence says ‘he was a poorly behaved boyfriend’

Love made a fool of Beau Lamarre-Condon, but it did not make him a murderer, his lawyer has told a court.

Lamarre-Condon is accused of fatally shooting Jesse Baird and his new partner, Luke Davies, with his police service weapon in Baird’s Paddington home on 19 February 2024.

The former officer has pleaded not guilty to two counts of murder and one charge of aggravated break and enter, claiming the two men died while he acted in “self-defence”.

Lamarre-Condon developed a “jealous obsession” with Baird when the two were casually and non-exclusively dating in late 2022 and 2023, the prosecution argued.

The accused has admitted to going through Baird’s phone to delete and block other men, to entering the Paddington home one night and running off with Baird’s wallet and phone, and to watching Baird have sex with another man through his bedroom window.

But in his closing statement to the New South Wales supreme court, defence barrister John Stratton SC claimed this was because he had fallen “head over heels” in love with Baird and quoted English novelist William Thackeray.

“‘Love makes fools of us all,’ and you certainly might think that that very much applies to all of us whether young or old, straight or gay, male or female,” Stratton said.

“There’s no doubt the accused, Beau Lamarre-Condon, did plenty of foolish things and you might think the level of them was unusual, that he was a poorly behaved boyfriend.

“There’s a long distance between his admitting that sort of behaviour, and you finding him guilty of murder.”

But crown prosecutor Brett Hatfield SC urged the jury to reject Lamarre-Condon’s account and convict him on all three counts, as he finished his closing statement on Monday.

“The case is that the accused, driven by this jealous obsession, drove to Paddington in a hire car, scoped the house and waited for his opportunity,” he told the court.

Lamarre-Condon entered the house with a gun loaded with stolen bullets, and shot Baird in the back and temple, Hatfield alleged.

“It was not self-defence or an accident,” he said. “The evidence speaks only to an execution.”

Lamarre-Condon had testified he shot Baird after he lunged at him with a knife.

During this struggle, his gun was then flung across the room and picked up by Davies before the officer grabbed his arm and pushed the gun towards Davies.

Davies then pulled the trigger and shot himself, Lamarre-Condon told the court.

But this defence rests entirely on his own account and even if Baird had picked up a knife, it would not have assisted him against a Glock pistol, Hatfield said.

“You might think he is a proven and persistent liar and such a scenario is completely contrary to the weight of the evidence,” he told the court.

“The accused’s account is not the truth. It is a story made up after the fact in an attempt to meet the evidence.”

But Stratton said there were two serious problems with the crown case about Davies’ death that the prosecution “hasn’t overcome and can never overcome”.

Though Davies’ DNA was found on the trigger of the gun, there was no blood, the jury heard.

Davies being the source of the majority of DNA on this part of the gun is “powerful” evidence his finger was on the trigger when the third shot was fired, Stratton said.

The prosecution also failed to locate the bullet or any bullet fragments of the shot that struck Davies, and this is enough to leave jurors with reasonable doubt about the crown case, the barrister said.

“The forensic and ballistic evidence makes it more likely that the accused’s version is true, than the version the crown seeks to persuade,” he told the court.

The trial before Justice Natalie Adams continues.
