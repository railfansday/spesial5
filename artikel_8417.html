A Bed at Muir Trail Ranch — John Muir Trail Day 14

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Lying in my sleeping bag in the early morning, I rattled off a few thoughts as I lay there:

It’s 5:09, and I should be thinking about getting up, but I’ve decided this is a morning I don’t need to push for an early start.  It is a relatively short day ahead, and I have an actual cabin for tonight at Muir Trail Ranch!

I was thinking about yesterday’s fall, and the similarity with last year’s fall on the Long Trail.  In both cases it was so instantaneous, from being upright and moving, to being flat on my face!  I have been in a few car accidents, not a lot, but a few, either as driver or passenger.  I remember in those cases feeling like time kind of slowed down, as if I were watching what was happening and what was about to happen, almost like a movie in slow motion.  Yet my falls were completely different sensations.  Just immediate:  Bam!  Face on the ground!  Done!   So weird.

Trail injuries seem to fall into three broad categories.  First, the extreme.  Things like death or a broken leg, where the possibility of continuing is zero.  Third, the minor.  Cuts and scrapes, bumps and bruises, these are all inevitable in hiking.  But leaving the trail for these reasons is unthinkable.  If you choose to leave, these injuries may be excuses but would not be the real reason.  The middle group is the most complicated.  They are injuries where you have some disability, but not a crippling one; where you have some pain, but it can be made manageable.  Continuing or leaving becomes a personal negotiated choice.

Yesterday’s fall was obviously not extreme.  I think (hope?) it was more like a severe version of a type three injury.  Today will be the test.  Can I push my sleeping bag into its stuff sack with my wrist injury?  Will there be severe pain in my ribs when I walk?

Before I dozed off last night, I looked through my open tent flap at the sky.  The constellation Orion was framed by the trees.   “A brave hunter,” I thought, “battles adversity and perseveres.”  As I fell asleep, I resolved to battle my adversity too.

When I finally left my tent, I was still alone at my site by the river.  My wrist felt worse, my chest felt better.  I took an Ibuprofen as protection before the day even started.

With under ten miles to go and a two p.m. check-in time at Muir Trail Ranch (MTR), I wasn’t too worried about my starting time.  As I packed, it seemed my wrist could do almost anything I needed except – as I predicted — stuffing the sleeping bag. That was hard.  I pretty much had to rely on my right hand for actions that that required force and torque.  But my fingers worked and I was able to hold my poles.  I headed off in the sunshine.

A little way down the trail I met a woman who told me she was Russian.  She had three big mushrooms beside her, arrayed on a log.  She said, “Oh yes, I know my mushrooms from Russia.  These are very tasty.  The other ones I find yesterday you can eat them right away, but these you have to boil them first and then you fry them and eat them.  They’re delicious.”

She was so sure these were edible that I believed her. (But I would have let her take a few bites before trying them myself!)

I replied, “Well, I don’t know if you have the same mushrooms in the Sierras of California as you do in Russia.  Isn’t it a kind of risky proposition to assume that they’re the same?”

She was very confident.  “No, no, I know for sure.  Exactly.  Very good.”

Very good.  Okay, I wasn’t going to eat them.  After all, I did eat the green onions the day before and was not the worse for wear.  Somehow this felt a little different though.

The Russian was with her husband and two kids, a daughter, about 15 or so, and a son who might have been 18.  I left them with their mushrooms and continued.

Just then Sally appeared!   She clearly pitched camp some ways behind me but got up much earlier and now we were at the same spot.  It was great to see her, and so funny how we keep leapfrogging.

We walked together and less than a half mile later, we came to the crossing for Evolution Creek.  For the first time there were no steppingstones!  I hate hiking in soggy, soppy, shoes and socks.  So, I was finally going to use my water shoes for their intended purpose!  I began changing footwear.

Sally was changing into flip-flops for her traverse as I headed across first.  The water was quite cold but felt refreshing rather than painful.  The crossing was perhaps 25 yards; it got to my knees, but never above them.

As Sally started across, I noticed the Russian family assembling on the far bank.  I got some good pictures of Sally crossing but realized too late that it would have been perfect for a video.  Sorry Sally!

The Russians were wearing big, heavy hiking boots or the kind I wore 30-40 years ago.  All but the daughter, they marched straight into the water without taking them off.  The daughter arrived at the bank after they crossed and seemed unwilling to emulate them.  Finally, she took off her shoes and crossed barefoot, carrying shoes, socks, and poles in her arms, which seemed a bit risky to me.

I would have said something, but it was a pretty mild current.  After she crossed over though, I spoke to her, “You know, if you encounter this situation again, especially if it is deeper and faster, you should tie the boots to your pack and use your poles for balance, rather than carry your poles.”

I switched back into my dry hiking footwear, and off I went.  I went out faster than the others and, as the trail started to go down steeply, I enjoyed a few waterfalls along the way.

I also began to understand the weird zigzag I had seen on the map.  We were approaching a confluence where a river valley meets another river valley.  The zigzag entailed going down one side of the incoming valley, taking a very nice bridge across to the other side, and then coming back around to the original valley where there was yet another very nice bridge to cross to the third side.

I know that sounds confusing because it is, but trust me, there were two beautiful bridges constructed to get us across.  As I continued downhill, I was appreciative of my wrist.  I was able to hold the hiking pole all right.  My chest did hurt a little, but it didn’t seem to be anything serious.

Having crossed the bridges, I emerged from shadow into bright sunshine.  It was distinctly warm and even getting hot, a function of both the sun and the lower elevation.  The mountains above seemed to tower brightly over the valley.

On the rocky trail, I saw a fellow walking toward me moving fast, wearing a strange colorful something-or-other around his neck.  We stopped to exchange greetings, and I found out he was Cam, trail name “Blair Witch,” and the “something” around his neck was a Hawaiian lei!

Cam was wearing almost nothing on his feet, but he was planning to complete the John Muir Trail – the route I had already walked for 13 days – in the next five days!  He was remarkable.  I continued for about 50 yards then turned around, calling out, “Hey!  Blair Witch!  Can I take your picture for my blog?”  This was his answer:

I just noticed, Blair Witch is not only wearing a stunning lei, but his hand is in shaka position!

Sometimes the short days you expect seem longer.  It is probably the anticipation of arrival.  I hiked by the river and it got warmer.  I saw a lizard, I saw some deer, and I crossed another bridge.  Finally, I came to the fork – to the right I would stay on the John Muir Trail but would miss the Muir Trail Ranch.  I took the left fork.

Hiking along the trail at this lower elevation, it was notable how large the trees were, especially after spending so much time above tree-line.  (See my cover photo!)  On the approach trail to MTR I stopped for lunch by the stream, relaxing with the time I had available.

The South Fork of the San Joaquin River. This is where I had lunch and…

After the meal, as I got close to the Ranch, there was another fork.  Fortunately, I had been told to follow the sign that says “pickup, resupply pickup.”

Arriving, there was a building with a porch.  A whole bunch of people – fellow hikers — were milling around on the porch and around the building.   There were a great many resupply buckets that had stuff in them that people didn’t want.  They were leaving the contents behind for other hikers to rummage through.  The principle:  “One hiker’s trash is another hiker’s treasure!”

On the porch was a table covered in a jumble of wires and plugs and charging devices and phones and it all seemed on the brink of toppling over in a chaotic mass of electronics.  Behind that was a window that connected the room and store inside with the hikers outside.  An earlier arrival told me, “Oh, yeah, the woman who staffs the window just left.  She’ll be back in a few minutes.”

While I waited, I started looking through the hiker boxes to see if I could find some much-needed hand sanitizer.  Sure enough, I found some.  It had a screw top rather than a cap, so I hoped it would not cause the same leakage problem as the one I was about to throw away.

Around then, Sally showed up and decided to try to get a cabin for herself too.  My wife had secured the booking for me a couple of days before, for the princely sum of $375 a night.  I thought they might now be full up, but figured Sally should give it a try.  Nothing to lose.

Finally, the woman returned to the window.  She had disappointing news for Sally.  But she confirmed my reservation and brought me inside to help her look for my resupply bucket.  This took a while.  As we looked through rows and rows of buckets, she explained that they had a system for organizing them, but that sometimes, when a lot arrive all at once, the system breaks down.  In other words, it was a free-for-all.

Just as I got nervous that my bucket was not there at all, she found it.  Then she took me to my cabin and gave me a tour of the place.

She introduced herself as Stitches, a trail name she had earned when she fell and… you guessed it, needed stitches.   The camp was very rustic and basic.  My room had no climate control, no sink, and no running water.  Yes, for $375.  At least it had electricity and a plug for my electronics, so I was much better off than the chaos on the porch!

View of the door. Note the decorative cowboy boots and the fancy trash can. Also see that I am already charging my electronics! (On the floor.)

Stitches showed me the open-air guest showers up the hill and the nearby privy pit toilet.  Further away was the dining hall and the lounge.  The kitchen was where we had to leave all our personal food to protect it from bears at night.  It also had a big container of lemonade and ice, available all day long (and delicious!).

Beyond the lounge were a couple of hot springs for people to soak in, but two girls were scrubbing them, so they were temporarily unavailable.  Stitches did not show me the fancy cabins, but they had a two-night minimum.  I guess that was to discourage grubby distance hikers from messing them up?

As I settled in. I started charging my electronics.  It was blazing hot in the room.  To get out of it, I decided to do my laundry and stripped down to a pair of shorts, as I planned to wash everything else.

Where was the laundromat?  Well, it was outdoors on a platform, and it was one of those old timey hand wash tubs with a roller above it.  A wringer-washer.  I was alone when I got there, but the tub portion was full of very dirty water.  I was like, “I’m not gonna wash my clothes in that!”

My phone was charging so this is NOT the one I used. But you get the idea…

I followed the tube that came out of the tub and looked around.  Behold, there was a wire nearby!  So, I snaked the wire up the tube and sure enough, the filthy water started to flow out.

In the meantime, a woman, who introduced herself as Smiles, came up to do her laundry too.  Eventually somebody else came, creating a kind of a queue.

They were all appreciative that I’d figured out how the thing worked and got it to empty out.   Fortunately, there was some detergent available.  I washboard scrubbed my clothes and in no time the tub water became even grimier than the water I had cleaned out.

So, I cleaned it out again, scrubbed everything again, and then squeezed most of the water out with the wringer.  It actually worked pretty well, even if I did feel like Willa Cather.

Behind the platform was the electric dryer.  Oops, I mean the clotheslines.  There were a lot of them.  Not too many clothespins though.  I tried not to use too many pins so the others could use a few too.  Luckily, it was a nice sunny day and not too windy!

Smiles told me she had done the John Muir Trail when she was 40; now she was doing it again for her 50th birthday.  Her fun and sunny disposition matched her trail name.  She was staying in the cabin on one side of mine.  On the other side were Jay and Jim, two retired New York oncologists.  After doing my laundry and showering to wash my own body, I returned to my cabin and sat in a chair on the porch to relax and enjoy feeling clean.  Aaaaah!

I heard someone calling.  I wondered who they were calling.  Then I heard it again, louder.  Finally, I paid more attention and realized the person was calling ME.  It was Jay looking at me through the slats in his cabin.  Jim had reflexively thrown the outside latch on their door when he left.  Jay was a prisoner!  Yes, I rescued him.

We were called to dinner at six and we all made our way up to the dining room.  The food was simple, but delicious.  The main course was a stew of beef and lentils with big carrots and potatoes – so good!  We had rice, very fresh salad, and garlic green beans that were super al dente and fantastic.  Having hiker hunger, I basically went through the line twice with large helpings both times.  Then someone said, “Oh, you know, there’s dessert too.”

Ooops!  But I still made room and ate my whole dessert.

The company was congenial too.  Our table had Jim and Jay (still on speaking terms after the imprisonment) and Smiles.  There was another J, who was a young woman from England who was solo hiking.  To my surprise, the last person was Lauralyn, whom I had encountered on my way up Forester Pass.  She and her husband owned the Mount Williamson Motel in Independence, the place where they did not have space for me.  On trail I told Lauralyn that if I stayed in her hotel I would recite The Raven for the guests.

We shared stories and joked around, and I even had half a mug of coffee to wash everything down.

Then I went to my cabin to bring up my resupply bucket.

The problem is, they are very strict about keeping no food in these little cabins.  My feeling is like, “Just put a frigging thick bolt on the door so that, you know, no bears are going to get in.”

But their response is, “No, the bears can always get in.  They’re very clever, blah, blah, blah.”

I decided not to even open my resupply until the next morning.  I just took the whole bucket, still taped with duct tape, up to the kitchen where they lock up all the food.  The next day I would sort what I wanted and what I didn’t.  I knew without looking that I did not want everything in that heavy bucket!

The view from the door into my spacious cabin. Well, it was a LOT bigger than my tent anyway!

Back in the cabin, I bundled under the blankets.  A bed, glorious!  I fell asleep.
