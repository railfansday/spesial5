Is There Ever a Case for Sleeping With Your Food While Backpacking?

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

I’ve noticed that the thru-hiking community has a tendency to point the finger at day hikers and section hikers whenever there’s a debate about food and trash left out at campsites. “Thru-hikers aren’t the ones doing this” is the inevitable comment on any photo of a trashed campsite. That may even be true in many cases, but if there’s a food-related practice that many thru-hikers are guilty of, it’s sleeping with their food in their tents.

We all know the reasons you’re not supposed to: food attracts wildlife, and animals that get into people-food can come to associate campsites with an easy meal and start approaching people more often and more persistently.

In the case of small critters like rodents, this is annoying, possibly unsanitary, and can result in damaged gear and lost food supplies. In the case of large mammals like bears, it can lead to dangerous encounters, and food-conditioned bears may be relocated or killed as a result. Proper food storage is at least as much about protecting wildlife from people as it is about protecting people from wildlife.

Yet I can sympathize with the food-in-tent crowd: thru-hikers are always tired at the end of a long day, and proper food storage methods aren’t easy or automatic. Soft-sided bear-resistant bags like the Ursack can work well, but they’re expensive and somewhat heavy. Bear canisters are the most foolproof, but they’re heavy and inconvenient. Bear hangs require time, skill, and suitable trees to do properly, and they mean getting out of your warm bed to retrieve your food in the morning before breakfast. They’re arguably also more fallible than canisters and Ursacks.

In contrast, sleeping with your food is, well, easy. You don’t have to fuss with anything after dinner, you’ll have it right there with you if you wake up with a craving in the middle of the night, and you can make your morning coffee from the comfort of your sleeping bag.

It’s easy to imagine how, after weeks on trail with no food-related incidents, thru-hikers start getting comfortable with life in the mountains and start questioning whether all those cumbersome food storage steps are actually worth it.

So it begs the question: is there ever a case for sleeping with your food in the backcountry? Andrew Skurka has said that he does it on the regular, provided he’s camping in a low-use area where animals are unlikely to enter his camp and there are no specific food storage regulations there.

Beyond mere convenience, Skurka also argues that animals that have never become habituated to human presence tend to give us a wide berth, meaning food stored inside the tent might actually be safer than food stored outside.

I also want to add the caveat that “not sleeping with your food” should mean actually securing it somewhere else, e.g., in a bear canister. Not just leaving it lying on the ground outside your tent, which is the worst option of all.

Personally, I always store my food outside the tent, whether in a canister, Ursack, or bear/rodent hang. Even in low-risk areas, for me it’s simply not worth the small chance of a chewed-through tent, let alone a full-on bear encounter. Securing my food is a minor inconvenience that gives me more peace of mind, so I’ve just gotten used to it.

Is there a valid argument for sleeping with your food in some circumstances? Sure, but emphasis on some circumstances. For instance, a decent share of The Trek’s readers are Appalachian Trail people, and the AT is a prime example of a trail where I feel strongly that you should not sleep with your food. People do it even there, but “I sleep with my food and I’ve never had a problem!” is not empirical proof that a problem cannot happen.

Also, since fairly reliable food storage methods do exist, why not just use them and err on the side of keeping wildlife wild?

I’m not going to tell you all how to live your lives, but storing your food elsewhere is not the end of the world, and especially on a trail like the AT, it’s the more prudent choice for you and the bears.
