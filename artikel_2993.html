Invesco QQQ Is 14% Below Its October Record. Here's Where I Think It Will Be in 2030.

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

The Nasdaq-100 fund trades around 14% below its October record. Can it pass that peak by the end of 2030?

By Daniel Sparks – Nov 18, 2026 at 5:23PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Invesco QQQ Trust ›

The Invesco QQQ Trust (QQQ +0.39%) closed at a record $759.66 on Oct. 6. Since then, the exchange-traded fund that tracks the Nasdaq-100 has fallen to roughly $654 as of this writing, about 14% below the high.

It's still up about 6% in 2026, but the drop has erased most of the year's gains.

Even so, I think QQQ will end 2030 at roughly $890 per share. That would put it about 17% above the October record and about 36% above today's price -- a rise of close to 8% a year, before the fund's small dividend.

That would also be a slower pace than the fund's recent run. The Nasdaq-100 itself climbed around 12% a year in the five years through October, according to Siblis Research. And my number rests on one assumption more than any other: how fast the index's companies keep growing their profits.

Showing how much profit growth has powered the Nasdaq-100, the index's total earnings climbed around 50% in the last year, according to Siblis, which removes big accounting gains. That was up from about 28% a year in the past three years and about 19% a year in the past five. In those five years, earnings outpaced the index's price by more than 6 percentage points a year. That means the valuation dropped even while the index rose.

At the end of October, the Nasdaq-100 traded at about 33 times earnings, and QQQ has slipped roughly 5% since then. By my math, this leaves the fund near 31 times earnings now.

That's a bit under where the index finished 2024 and 2025 (about 32 times earnings both years). But it's well above the 24 or so times earnings it hit at the end of 2022.

The fund isn't cheap. Still, its price-to-earnings ratio is arguably more reasonable than its recent gains would suggest, because earnings have carried most of them.

I started with the earnings. Going by the end-of-October numbers, every QQQ share represents about $21 of its holdings' earnings in the last year.

I'm assuming these earnings rise about 12% a year through the end of 2030, well below the five-year rate and a fraction of the past year's. That still puts them about 60% higher, near $34 a share.

The other piece is what investors will pay for these earnings at the end of 2030. I'm assuming 26 times earnings, about what big U.S. companies as a group sell for today. It's also under the Nasdaq-100's average of about 29 times earnings at its past 10 year-ends.

In other words, my estimate assumes the Nasdaq-100 winds up valued about like the broader market is now, with its normal premium gone. At 26 times earnings of about $34 per share, QQQ would trade at about $890 a share. Its dividend, which yields about 0.5%, could add a bit on top.

The earnings growth rate worries me more. I've already set the final valuation multiple under the index's recent average, but part of the past year's profit surge came from companies whose earnings can swing hard.

Micron Technology (MU -1.00%), one of the fund's 10 biggest holdings, is the clearest example. The memory chipmaker's revenue more than tripled to around $133 billion in its fiscal year that ended Sept. 3.

Its net income was around $85 billion, almost 10 times its net income a year earlier. But Micron's own annual report says that in the past five fiscal years, yearly changes in the average selling prices of its memory chips ranged from a rise of about 200% to a drop of about 50%.

Profits that climb so fast can fall quickly if the supply of memory chips catches up to demand, as Micron itself warns.

At 8% growth, though, the fund's earnings would reach only about $29 a share by 2030. Even at 26 times earnings, it would trade near $760. That price would just bring QQQ back to its October record after four more years.

All told, I expect QQQ to finish 2030 around $890, over its October peak but at a slower pace than investors have gotten used to. The forecast doesn't need this year's profit surge to repeat. It just needs the Nasdaq-100's earnings to keep growing at a double-digit rate once the memory chip boom cools.

Compared with the last five years, that's a modest outlook. But I think roughly 8% a year from a fund trading 14% below its record would still be a decent return.
