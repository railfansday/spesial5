A virtual escape may help children feel less scared before surgery

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Children reported less fear after brief virtual reality sessions before surgery, offering early evidence for a possible drug-free calming option.

Before an operation, plenty of children are scared, and a common way to calm them is a sedative that carries risks of its own.

Researchers tested a drug-free option: a virtual reality headset showing a calm playroom or a quiet beach.

After a short session in it, children said they felt less upset and less scared, while their heart rates went down.

Whether the headset deserves the credit is still an open question.

Walaa Abu Rukbah, a pharmacist at the University of Tabuk in Saudi Arabia, is the study’s first author.

“Sometimes, the best support we can offer a child before surgery isn’t a medicine at all,” said Abu Rukbah.

The project ran for nine months at a children’s hospital in Virginia. Every child in it used the headset, so there was no group of children who waited without one to compare against.

Between half and three-quarters of children feel anxious before surgery, by the researchers’ estimate.

A scared child isn’t only unhappy. Very anxious children are more likely to struggle when it’s time to go under anesthesia.

They’re also more likely to wake up confused and agitated, a state doctors call emergence delirium, and to have more pain and a slower recovery.

The calming drug used most often is midazolam. It can make some children more agitated instead of calmer, and it can slow breathing or delay the trip home.

Drug-free options such as play therapy and music have helped children in earlier studies, but they need trained staff that not every hospital has.

The team spent 10 months designing its own virtual world for children.

The main scene is a playroom in soft blues and greens, with toys that respond when a child looks at them and slow music in the background.

A second scene shows a beach with waves and palm trees.

Some people experience motion sickness in virtual reality because what they see does not match the motion their body senses.

To avoid that, children hop from spot to spot instead of gliding around. When 15 children tried it early on, none of them reported feeling sick.

From October 2023 to June 2024, 80 children aged 6 to 15 used the headset while waiting for planned surgery at Children’s Hospital of Richmond at VCU.

Their parents could watch on a tablet what the child was seeing. A typical session lasted about nine minutes, though some ran as long as 20.

Before and after each session, children rated how upset they felt from 0 to 10, much like the pain scales doctors use.

They also pointed to one of five faces, ranging from calm to very scared.

The typical child’s score dropped from 3 to 1 on the 0-to-10 scale. On the faces, the typical child pointed to the second face before the session and the calmest face after it.

Not every child changed much. By the team’s measure of a change big enough to matter, 46 of the 80 children felt noticeably less upset afterward and 44 felt noticeably less scared. That’s a little more than half.

Bedside monitors picked up a change too. Heart rate dropped by nearly six beats a minute on average.

Systolic blood pressure – the top number in a blood pressure reading – fell by about 4 points on average.

Only three children had any side effects, and all of them were minor.

Dayanjan Wijesinghe, a researcher in the Department of Pharmacotherapy and Outcomes Science at Virginia Commonwealth University (VCU), was also involved in the study.

“In a busy children’s surgical unit, a short virtual reality session proved easy for nurses to run, was safe, and was very popular: nine in ten children wanted to use it again, and nearly every parent would recommend it,” said Wijesinghe.

All 29 nurses involved said they would recommend it too.

When parents wrote about the experience, they mentioned distraction more than anything else.

“He was distracted and didn’t notice when the needle went in,” one parent wrote.

Not every parent was equally enthusiastic.

“If my child chose it, sure. To me it’s a bit overwhelming to have so much going on,” another parent wrote.

Waiting rooms can get easier on their own. A child might calm down simply from getting used to the room, or from having an adult pay close attention for a while.

The most scared children had the biggest drops.

However, unusually high initial scores can fall on a second measurement even without an effective treatment, potentially making the headset appear more helpful.

Scientists call this regression to the mean, and it can make health programs look better than they really are.

The team’s own checks suggested it explains much of why the most anxious children improved the most.

Families also knew they were trying something new, and the study team collected their ratings in person. That can lead people to give kinder answers.

Once the children left the waiting room, the researchers stopped tracking them.

Whether a calmer wait leads to an easier start to anesthesia or a faster recovery wasn’t measured.

All of the children came from a single hospital, so a hospital with different staff or patients might see different results.

In the researchers’ view, the next step is a randomized trial that compares the headset with usual care and with other drug-free options.

Such a trial could also show whether a headset costs less than giving children a sedative.

The full study was published in the journal PLOS Digital Health.
