AI’s new business model: Break the internet, then sell the fix

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

People who work in cybersecurity or work on the fundamentals of digital infrastructure do not want to live in “exciting times,” but right now, they don’t have a choice.

Binance was just hacked in what is, so far, the largest cryptocurrency heist in history. Anthropic, the company behind Claude, narrowly averted a major attack through a third-party extension that would have allowed hackers to upload worms to the affected computers. A number of major airlines were affected when the Spanish travel-tech company Amadeus was hacked, leaking customer information on the web (mercifully without payment info, this time). At the center of this are generative AI companies like Anthropic and OpenAI, which all at once are empowering hackers while billing themselves as the only line of defense. They’ve found a new niche, and it’s to become a protection racket. 

The threat is quantifiable: Severe bugs are classified as CVEs (common vulnerabilities and exposures), and around 400,000 have been officially logged since 1988. Of those, 13,000 happened in the last month. PHP2Shell is the perfect example of this. If you spent any time on social media in the last few weeks, you probably heard some chatter about PHP2Shell and just how dire the threat was. Roughly 70% of websites rely on code written using PHP, and the announcement of a fundamental bug in the language—akin to discovering that millions of buildings share the same faulty lock, potentially giving intruders easy access—had people panicking. Websites such as Wikimedia preemptively went offline until a fix was pushed through.

These bugs were rediscovered separately by two cybersecurity firms: Calif and Wiz. The bugs are very difficult to spot. They had to do with how the host computer accesses memory while an application is running: If a malicious actor forced an out of memory condition by whatever means, it would be possible to interrupt the runtime, leave a partially initialized object behind, and have the application treat it as valid. It would be a stepping stone for a hostile takeover of the host server, while a separate bug could leave instructions from a request that could be read again subsequently instead of being reset. This can be an apocalyptic threat if somebody can actually do those things. They would gain remote control over the host computer, and at that point they can do whatever they want with that machine and what’s on it.

Notably, developers at the PHP Foundation knew about this bug in 2024, but couldn’t agree on a solution for it. Every proposed fix had associated costs, and in any event, nobody actually knew how to act against these bugs. The threat was completely theoretical. It sat there, which wasn’t the optimal workflow, but it also wasn’t an omega-level threat until generative AI came into the picture. Forcing the condition while also creating the takeover wasn’t easy to do. Once the vulnerability was publicly announced, what had been a trickle of attacks became a flood. Fixes were rapidly pushed (in some cases by volunteers) and anybody running a website on PHP was urged to quickly upgrade to protect themselves.

This was just one bug, but pretty much everybody expects that more will be found. The people talking loudest about the danger are, surprisingly, generative artificial intelligence companies. AI companies are all too happy to make clear how incredibly dangerous all of this is and how many more bugs and vulnerabilities they expect to find as they continue to look around. Sam Altman doesn’t think we’ll be able to patch all the bugs, while Jason Clinton of Anthropic terms it a “vulnerability tsunami.” They’re promising years of instability following this as they struggle to discover and patch all of these bugs—an ongoing process, because software development is itself a process of continual updates. 

Why are they talking this way? They’re emphasizing how dangerous their product is, which seems like terrible salesmanship. But they’re also peddling the solution to the problem they helped to introduce.

And to force the issue, they’re going to look for the bugs themselves. That’s the point of OpenAI’s Patch the Planet initiative, which is all about looking for defects and vulnerabilities in open-source software. Nor are they just running around bug-hunting and letting chaos ensue. They’re trying to step into the cybersecurity space and set themselves up as the best solution to the problem.

OpenAI unveiled a new security-focused model in September, GPT-6 Cyber. OpenAI’s Daybreak program is intended to be available only to eligible organizations so that only “good actors” can take advantage of this, though in the long term as models become open access it’s not clear how that distinction will matter. Anthropic has its own model, Claude Mythos Preview. Google has Gemini Carbon 4 as its cybersecurity model. As with all models, you pay per token. Access to Daybreak is subsidized, although if you’re a believer in Cory Doctorow’s theory of enshittification, the long-term business model will be to make this more expensive over time as customers grow to depend on it. These companies are desperate for new sources of revenue even as overall revenue growth slows.

They’re not being subtle about this. Sam Altman told a group of business students at Stanford that “I think we’re going to have to completely switch the defense model to using the same technology for a new kind of defense shield.” In other words: Gotta use my products to keep yourself safe. What they’re doing verges on extortion; they’re trying to monetize the chaos their products are introducing. But in addition to that, they’re taking aim at a fundamentally vulnerable digital space.

A threat to the open-source commons

Let’s take a step back and consider how the internet, much of which runs on open-source software, is really maintained. PHP is supported by the PHP Foundation, which has just over a dozen employees and relies on donations. It is in effect a passion project for a few developers. There’s an Xkcd comic that sheds a great deal of light on this: Random critical pieces of digital infrastructure are being maintained by people entirely on their own, thanklessly and often without compensation. The modern open internet has been built up in ad hoc ways that often deprioritized security, but that mattered less when only human beings were doing the building or the hacking. To take advantage of those vulnerabilities would require not just expertise, but a great deal of time, effort, and computational power in many cases. PHP2Shell’s exploits were hard to spot, even if you really knew what you were doing and understood PHP down to the source code.

Yes, these potential threats are dire, but this isn’t a tsunami or a natural disaster that simply could not have been prevented. This is entirely manmade, and the people shrieking about what a dire emergency it is are also the ones who have helped to inaugurate it. This exploit sat virtually unknown by anybody for more than a decade, and even when it was recognized for what it was, the threat was purely theoretical. Now because of generative AI, cybersecurity is starting to favor the attack rather than the defender. People like Sam Altman helped to create the problem, first by developing the models, and now by going around and actively looking for weak points in our ramshackle internet. They broke it, and now they’re here to offer a solution: “Nice open internet you’ve got right there; it would be a shame if something happened to it.”

And what are the costs associated with this? For individual consumers of course, there’s the threat of hacks, theft, and general instability on the internet. For businesses, outages, lost revenue, and theft. That’s severe. But I think that there’s a more fundamental risk to the whole of the internet, at least as we know it. Groups like the PHP Foundation or the individual developers who sustain free and open-source software (FOSS) are going to be under even greater strain in the years to come, especially when compared to companies like Anthropic that, for the moment at least, have very deep pockets. Those 13,000 CVEs I mentioned at the beginning of the piece? Those largely fall on volunteers and overworked organizations to remediate, which will burn them out and in the long run likely push people away from the whole FOSS project. 

Turning to generative AI is probably what Anthropic and every other company wants, as another use case for their products, but it poses its own severe risks. Large language models (LLMs) are already being used on open-source projects that call into question the whole license-free structure and system of attribution that has made FOSS possible. That’s one leg of our stool that’s being kicked out from under us.

On top of that though, as AI-generated code winds up in more FOSS projects, is it going to introduce more vulnerabilities that will then have to be hunted down and eliminated? The answer seems to be yes. Whether it’s from vibe coding or simply the fact that LLMs trained on code with security vulnerabilities will tend to reproduce those vulnerabilities, we’re likely going to be running in place for years on end. And even if it can be introduced without undermining the security of the code itself, it’s another avenue to eat away at the licensure and copyright-free status that makes FOSS possible.

So this protection racket that’s forming is more than just a tax. It’s going to massively destabilize the ecosystem of the internet as we know it. I don’t know what comes next if the delicate FOSS sandbox falls apart. Maintainers could abandon their projects, which in turn creates more security vulnerabilities as things fall out of date and become obsolete. Even stably running pieces of infrastructure will eventually be unsupported by the languages that they run on; people can wake up one morning and find that some odd dependency powering their website stopped working. Who’s responsible at that point for fixing it? 

Nor do I want to see it entrusted to for-profit entities. Maintaining this work is basically thankless as it stands; it’s unprofitable to take on because the whole point is that everybody can freely use it. The only reason for them to do so in the long run would be to monetize it, which costs the rest of us. And as they make these into their own proprietary systems, will they wall off what is theirs from everything else? Why shouldn’t they; Microsoft spent years fighting the FOSS movement because it saw open-source software as a threat to its copyrighted systems. What has been up until now a true commons, maintained by people and organizations largely doing it out of passion, would become something else entirely: less able to talk to each other, and more expensive to use.

What’s the solution? This is probably a whole other essay, but in the long run it’s going to have to be government. Whether by directly assuming the burden or offering cash and resources to private groups, governments need to take steps to ensure that an open and free internet continues to exist. Could something like the United Nations do it, ensuring that it’s a truly global movement instead of under one national or regional umbrella? Failing that, could the European Union take it on—a prudent move, especially as Europe tries to get out from under the thumb of U.S. big tech. Food for thought, because under the shadow of all of these scary cyberattacks, that’s where I think the real threat lies.
