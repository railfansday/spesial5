Can digital tools be a force for good?

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Nearly every day, I see a headline about the downsides of AI and other digital tools, from deepening divides to the impact of data centers on the environment and communities. It’s critical to address the challenges, but we also need to focus on the good that technology can do when developed and used responsibly.

Why? Showing what’s possible can inspire the next solution, steering investments toward digital tools as a force for good.

Recently, I’ve seen several examples of technology being used to address social and environmental issues. From a data-driven initiative to save the North Atlantic right whale to grief-education built inside a kids’ gaming platform, here are a few highlights.

SAVE SPECIES

The North Atlantic right whale is critically endangered, and we have the power to prevent one of its greatest threats, vessel strikes. The Canadian Space Agency’s smartWhales initiative, developed with Fisheries and Oceans Canada, Transport Canada, and WSP (one of our clients), uses satellite data and AI to predict where the whales will be, so ships can slow down.

In Australia, ghost nets entangle hundreds of turtles each year. Instead of relying on costly, polluting helicopter surveys, Indigenous rangers are now working with Charles Darwin University to fly drones over remote beaches to pinpoint ghost nets for removal.

And in Yellowstone National Park, Yellowstone Forever teamed up with the Colossal Foundation to analyze wolf bioacoustics using AI, helping identify individual howls and monitor population changes.

EXPAND ACCESS TO MEDICAL CARE

In under-resourced regions, our client Smile Train’s Virtual Reality Operating Room (VR OR) app enables surgeons to virtually “scrub in,” either solo or alongside colleagues from around the world, to learn and practice cleft-related procedures. Building on Smile Train’s Virtual Surgery Simulator, an earlier web-based training platform that has reached healthcare professionals in 141 countries, the VR OR app further expands access to advanced surgical training, helping surgeons bring high-quality care to communities where it might otherwise be out of reach.

Language barriers leave roughly 30 million people in the U.S. more likely to be misdiagnosed and to stay in the hospital longer. Lexi AI is tackling the problem with an AI medical interpreter that can translate conversations in more than 30 languages. 

ADAPT TO CLIMATE CHANGE

Google launched Flood Hub a few years ago, designed to forecast river floods around the globe. Now, the tool can also track urban flash floods using data from Groundsource, a novel attempt at transforming millions of local news articles into standardized flood records.

Similar early warning tools are available for wildfires. NOAA’s HWP index combines predicted winds, humidity, and soil moisture to assess vegetation flammability and flag risks before a spark hits dry brush.

Environmental issues also carry real financial implications. WSP’s Nature Vista platform combines AI-powered analytics with environmental intelligence to help organizations understand how nature-related risks could affect their business.

SUPPORT EDUCATORS

In the U.S., teachers are paid 25% less than others with the same level of education. And with tight school budgets, many spend hundreds of dollars of their own money on classroom supplies, leaving costlier items like lab gear out of reach. Northwestern University’s Baxter Box program (another of our clients) helps bridge that gap with an online lending library that connects teachers from under-resourced Chicago-area schools with free biotechnology kits.

Lesson planning can also absorb a lot of unpaid time outside of the classroom. This is where Khan Academy’s Khanmigo comes in. The AI assistant is completely free for teachers to use and can give them back hours each week by speeding up their prep work.

FIGHT GLOBAL HUNGER

Technology is helping herders across West Africa’s Sahel locate better grazing conditions through Action Against Hunger’s “GPS for goats,” called PEWS. Our client’s technology combines satellite imagery of vegetation and water levels with ground reports on animal disease, bushfires, and conflict to issue regular guidance.

Soybean farmers may soon be able to spot hidden signs of disease thanks to researchers at Southern Illinois University who built a robot that captures images of crops from the ground. The photos produced are much more accurate than the ones taken by overhead drones, even showing the undersides of leaves, where symptoms can hide.

DESIGN SAFER CITIES

Protecting pedestrians isn’t easy in crowded urban areas. To ensure that essential infrastructure like crosswalks, street lights, and bike lanes are built where they’re most needed, fitness platform Strava captures de-identified movement data from millions of app users in Strava Metro. More than 4,000 partners now rely on it, from small research teams to major cities.

Cambridge Mobile Telematic’s StreetVision takes a similar approach for drivers, using AI to analyze anonymized phone data and flag roads where speeding, hard braking, and distracted driving are most common. Cities can then fix hazards before crashes happen.

CULTIVATE SOCIAL COHESION

Many people carry grief for a lifetime, yet most kids who’ve lost a close family member say support fades within three months. GRIEF SUCKS, Experience Camps’ digital platform for grieving teens, is carving out space to normalize tough conversations, using personal stories, celebrity interviews, and even humor to help spread awareness online. Our client, Experience Camps, also created Grief Quest, a scavenger hunt built inside Roblox, to help teach players how to support a grieving friend.

These are all reminders to focus on the promise as well as the peril. Digital technology is evolving faster than any innovation in history. For companies and organizations seeking the next challenge ripe for technological innovation, focusing on any of the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals is a great place to start.

Whether bridging a global gap or addressing a local community need, the same principles apply: Listen and engage beneficiaries early and seek out diverse perspectives; pair implementation with clear communication and training; and maintain the relationship long after launch. Notice that none of these principles are about the technology itself, rather the people and strategy behind impactful initiatives. While the downsides to digital social impact activations will keep making headlines, every example like these makes the case for investing in digital tools as a force for good. What will your organization add to the list?

Carol Cone is CEO of Carol Cone ON PURPOSE.
