The Great Bear Rainforest: How Fashion Rallied to Stop the Deforestation of an Ancient Rainforest

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

This story is part of ‘The Business Case for Nature’, a series exploring fashion’s impact on nature and vice versa. Read more here.

The Great Bear Rainforest, spanning 6.4 million hectares from the Alaskan border to just above Vancouver Island, is the largest coastal temperate rainforest in the world, a critical carbon sink, and the only known home of the extremely rare white-furred spirit bear. Whales migrate through the rainforest year-round, and it is a major breeding ground for all five species of Pacific salmon. Thanks to the region’s heavy rainfall and nutrient-rich ecosystem, it also houses an array of ancient trees over a thousand years old, including sitka spruce (among the tallest trees in the world) and red western cedar (which hold deep cultural significance for the 26 local First Nations).

Until 2016, 90% of the Great Bear Rainforest was open for logging, and fashion was one of the industries profiting from it. The industry uses large quantities of man-made cellulosic fibers (MMCF) like viscose, modal and lyocell, which typically come from wood pulp. It also has a direct link to deforestation through wood-based packaging, and an indirect link through leather and cotton, which are often grown on deforested land.

Now, thanks to fashion brands joining anti-logging campaigners at a crucial moment, 85% of it is legally protected from deforestation and logging, the First Nations whose territories overlap with it are co-decision makers in its conservation, and there is a $120 million fund helping them transition to conservation-based economies. This is a rare example of fashion rallying around nature restoration and using its purchasing power for good, in partnership with other industries. Experts say it’s a blueprint the industry could apply elsewhere.

This “seismic” win took decades to achieve, says Nicole Rycroft, founder and executive director of non-profit Canopy, which works to conserve ancient and endangered forests around the world. While anti-logging efforts in the Great Bear Rainforest pre-date Canopy, the non-profit joined the campaign at a critical junction, leveraging fashion industry partners including H&M and Zara, to secure the groundbreaking land use agreement when momentum was slowing. “Canopy was one of several organizations that started working with international corporate customers of forest products coming out of that region to send a very clear signal that we don’t want to be sourcing from clear cuts of massive, ecologically critical, temperate rainforests, and we don’t want to be sourcing from spirit bear habitat,” she explains.

A decade on, Vogue Business invited Rycroft to reflect on what the Great Bear Rainforest can teach fashion about nature conservation, and using its purchasing power for good.

Logging happens at the beginning of the supply chain, several steps upstream from where brand traceability programs tend to stop. With such a complex, sprawling supply chain, and such a small number of brands on the hook to comply with traceability regulations, few brands have visibility beyond Tier 1 (garment manufacturing) or Tier 2 (fabric production). Logging would come under Tier 4. That’s why meaningful action starts with traceability, says Rycroft, so brands can understand where their deforestation risks are.

But brands don’t need a direct link to deforestation to make a difference in conservation campaigns. “Even if it’s not a direct or immediate sourcing link, the adjacency is really relevant,” says Rycroft. “Brands are still seen as important even if they’re not existing customers, because there is the potential for them to be future customers.”

Anti-logging efforts in the Great Bear Rainforest started in earnest in the mid-1990s, with conservation organizations standing shoulder to shoulder with local First Nations. But the forestry industry was powerful, so media coverage was largely limited to British Columbia, framing the conflict as a domestic “war in the woods” and failing to break through on the international stage. The turning point came when international brands began sending clear signals to the forestry industry that they did not want to source from ancient and endangered ecosystems. This included home improvement companies such as Home Depot and Lowe’s, as well as major retailers like Sainsbury’s.

But even after the various parties agreed to work toward a land use agreement, progress ebbed and flowed. It took more than a decade to reach a legal agreement, during which time brands and conservations continually applied pressure to keep negotiations on track. “It took consumer companies making phone calls and writing letters to individual forestry companies,” says Rycroft. Fashion joined in later. “The process was long and arduous, and for a long time, there was a blindspot around fashion’s footprint in the Great Bear Rainforest.”

It was Canopy that rallied the fashion industry to get involved. In 2013, it created Canopy Style, an initiative where leading fashion brands commit to end sourcing from ancient and endangered forests. One of the early adopters was Stella McCartney. Within a few months of signing onto the pledge, McCartney canceled her contract with a dissolving pulp mill on Vancouver Island, which was sourcing directly from the Great Bear Rainforest, Canopy discovered. “It sent a very clear signal that fashion is a force to be reckoned with,” says Rycroft.

Later down the line, around 2016, progress stalled again. The land use agreement was 18 months overdue, and even the most ardent supporters were starting to feel stuck. Canopy teamed up with national newspaper The Globe and Mail to host a customer-investor roundtable. The date was set for a month ahead. They invited leading politicians, the forest industry and the First Nations leaders that had been involved in the negotiating process, as well as conservation partners and 70 people representing 70 international brands, including Stella McCartney, Eileen Fisher, H&M, and Zara. “Within days of issuing the invitation, the spirit and intent at the negotiating table changed,” says Rycroft. Twenty-four hours before the roundtable was due to take place, the British Columbia government announced the draft land use agreement.

“Brands have incredible social capital and influence, in addition to being an important economic sector,” says Rycroft. “They can’t be afraid to lean in and flex that muscle.”

Brands that sign up to Canopy Style — or Canopy’s equivalent packaging initiative, Pack4Good — commit to stop sourcing from ancient and endangered forests. That’s the baseline, says Rycroft, the Great Bear Rainforest case study shows that fashion’s nature strategies need to go one step further. They also need to include scaling next-gen and circular alternatives that can ease the industry’s burden on nature, and the risk that burden poses to supply chain resilience.

“Next-gen and circular alternatives are the off-ramp from deforestation and forest degradation,” explains Rycroft. “Brands will not be able to eliminate — or even substantially mitigate — their risk of sourcing from high-carbon, high-biodiversity forests unless we have scaled alternatives.”

At last month’s Textile Exchange conference, Canopy walked the industry through Next Gen Warp Speed, its new initiative that makes the business case for brands to convert 5% of their MMCF basket to next-gen alternatives by 2028. Fashion’s struggle to scale next-gen materials has been well-documented, but Canopy is hoping that a 5% commitment is enough to tip the balance without putting a substantial burden on brands to fund the transition. “A big focus for us now is: how do we create the right conditions and pre-competitive vehicles to scale next-gen production?” says Rycroft.

Moving forward, Canopy is applying its learnings from the Great Bear Rainforest win to other critical ecosystems around the world. Top of its list are the Amazon rainforest and the Leuser Ecosystem in Indonesia, where it already has brand buy-in from the likes of Eileen Fisher and Stella McCartney. Rycroft says she is hopeful that fashion can make a significant difference in these landscapes, just as it did in British Columbia.

A few weeks ago, Rycroft traveled up to the Great Bear Rainforest to see the ecosystem Canopy had worked for so many years to protect. She sat on a riverbank and watched a spirit bear pick salmon out of the stream. “So many people dedicated their lives to this cause, or made a call at a critical juncture, to enable that to happen. It has made such a dramatic difference to the landscape. And now the Great Bear Rainforest truly is a landscape of hope.”
