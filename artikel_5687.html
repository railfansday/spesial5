Restoring trust: Embattled CDC, HHS officials on their priorities for 2027" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Federal health officials are focused on rebuilding trust with the public and working across the aisle with the new Congress.

Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Director Erica Schwartz, M.D., and Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr., spoke at the Milken Institute’s Future of Health Summit last week in Washington, D.C. Over 1,200 attendees gathered at the event, the theme of which was “better health within reach.”

In a fireside chat, a chipper Schwartz tried to answer a tough line of questioning about Americans’ loss of trust in her agency, controversial vaccine changes, measles and other public health issues.

When asked about her potential disagreements with Kennedy, Schwartz said, “I disagree all the time. He’s a lawyer, I’m a lawyer. […] What I love about the secretary is that he listens to those disagreements. He's not someone who just says, ‘No, I don't have room for that.’ He really does value differing opinions.”

The U.S. has lost its measles elimination status, global health officials announced just this week. On that issue, Schwartz stressed the importance of supporting CDC’s state partners on awareness, prevention and surveillance efforts.

“MMR is the number one way to prevent measles,” Schwartz said. Today’s outbreaks “happen in communities that are very close-knit communities … that typically do not and have not vaccinated their children,” she said. Most cases are currently among kids over the age of five and adults, according to Schwartz.

Under Schwartz’s leadership, the CDC changed its method of reporting measles deaths from real-time reporting by state public health departments to death certificate data, which can face multi-month lags. Schwartz is being investigated by Energy & Commerce Committee Democrats who, during a briefing on CDC’s alleged suppression of the total number of national measles deaths on its dashboard, have called her unresponsive and misleading.

“As the crisis worsens, we are concerned that you continue to direct an effort at CDC to downplay the outbreak and artificially reduce the number of measles deaths that CDC reports to the public, concealing the fact that 10 people have died of measles-related deaths this year alone,” the Committee leaders wrote (PDF) in a letter on Tuesday. “We are expanding our investigation to examine the threat your actions pose to Americans’ health and whether changes to your statutory authority are required.”

During her fireside chat, Schwartz acknowledged that "we've lost trust not only externally with the American public, but also internally with the team that works at the CDC,” she said. To address this, Schwartz wants the public to understand that CDC follows the science, is being transparent and “leading with humility.” She pointed to COVID-era “arrogance” from institutions, directing at instead of conversing with the public.

“I want to be a CDC director that really has a conversation with the American public,” Schwartz said.

Internally, the first priority is to add stability to the body, including hiring leaders within the agency so they have a permanent leadership role. The CDC has also created a strategic hiring plan for 2027 with the input of staff and is looking to its “force extenders” like epidemic intelligence officers and its Public Health Associate Program.

On how she’s planning to work with the new majority-Democratic Congress, Schwartz noted she was encouraged to see more Congress members coming in with a healthcare background, “who understand how these policies will impact patients.” She said is eager to work with the Congressional Doctors Caucus and to work with both sides of the aisle.

“I know that we may not agree on every issue, but I do think there's a common ground where we can actually focus on really trying to make Americans healthy again,” she said.

One of her priorities for 2027 is having a “21st-century CDC” by bringing innovation into the public health sphere. She called out the Biothreat Radar, a proposed wastewater surveillance program about which she recently spoke with Sen. Andy Kim (D-New Jersey). The program’s proposed (PDF) budget is $52 million. The CDC has also tentatively put out a call for new Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) members, which will depend on a final ruling from the appellate court in the agency’s favor.

At the Summit, Schwartz’s comments did not necessarily satisfy some of the attendees. When asked by Fierce Healthcare for his impressions of the interview, a public health worker in the room who did not wish to be named called her a “traitor."

In his fireside chat closing out the Summit, Secretary Kennedy primarily spoke about food and obesity.

One of HHS' most consequential accomplishments during his tenure, Kennedy said, has been changing the dietary guidelines to be “science-based.” Earlier this year, the White House released new federal dietary guidelines, calling for prioritizing quality protein, healthy fats, fruits, vegetables and whole grains, while avoiding highly processed foods and refined carbs.

The agency is focused on transforming food across a number of sectors, including in Head Start programs—free federal early childhood programs—which have received about $151 million in supplemental HHS awards to strengthen nutrition services. HHS is also focused on rolling out healthy food options in the military, Kennedy said, and within SNAP by allowing states to request waivers to restrict what SNAP dollars are spent on. As of this summer, HHS has launched a Make Hospital Food Healthier Pledge, challenging hospitals to put nutrition at the center of patient care. HHS has also encouraged medical schools to increase their number of education hours on nutrition from an average of two to 40 hours, Kennedy said.

“Real” food sales in grocery stores, Kennedy claimed, went up 8% this year, while obesity rates have dropped by 7%. He acknowledged that the drop in obesity rates is more likely due to GLP-1 access rather than healthier eating. “If we can reduce obesity, we can reduce our healthcare costs,” he said, praising Trump’s Most Favored Nation negotiations for lowering the price of drugs like Ozempic.

“We reward people at every level—the hospitals, the providers, other providers, doctors, insurance companies, pharmaceutical companies—we reward them financially for keeping people sick. And as long as those financial incentives are in place, we are going to be operating a sick-care system rather than a healthcare system,” Kennedy said. HHS has started to change that by shifting more to value-based care, he added, so stakeholders get paid to keep patients healthy “rather than being paid to keep them sick.”

When asked what he wants his legacy as Health Secretary to be, Kennedy echoed Schwartz on the idea of following science. “What I want to do is to end corporate capture and the politicization of science … that Americans once again trust the regulatory agencies, they trust the science that’s coming from them,” he said.

He referred to newer guidelines meant to encourage extra transparency from researchers. In 2026, the HHS Scientific Integrity Policy was updated (PDF) to support publishing raw data and code in publicly accessible repositories, preregistering study protocols and transparently reporting all results.

He said he wants "to make sure that we’re not kowtowing to the food industry, the pharmaceutical industry, all these people who are at the trough taking on the $2.3 trillion that my agency gives out every year. And that instead, that Americans believe that this agency is devoted to their health, that they trust it, and that we are doing the science that we need to find out what is making them sick so that we can all eliminate it as a society.”

Kennedy's interview at the event did not touch on measles or vaccine policy, where the secretary's policies and communications have garnered broader criticism and controversy than his views on nutrition. Tuesday's announcement that the U.S. had lost its measles elimination status garnered a fresh call for Kennedy's resignation among his critics, including the Democratic Doctors Caucus.

Also coinciding with Milken's event was the announcement of the inaugural 2027 cohort of the Elevate Investment Initiative. The effort, part of the Milken Institute’s Women Health Network, aims to mobilize $1 billion in net-new capital for women’s health innovation.
