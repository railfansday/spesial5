12 Speaker of the House firsts, from Frederick Muhlenberg to Kevin McCarthy

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These Speaker of the House firsts, from Frederick Muhlenberg in 1789 to Kevin McCarthy's 2023 removal, trace how the office gained, lost and reshaped its power

The U.S. Constitution names only one officer of the House of Representatives. Article I says the House shall choose its Speaker and other officers. It says nothing more about the job. Everything else that defines the speakership has been settled by the House itself since 1789. That includes how much power the office carries, how a member wins it and how a Speaker can lose it.

As a result, the office's history reads as a record of improvisation. Practices that look fixed today often began as a single Speaker's ruling, a floor fight or an election result without precedent. Many of these firsts mark a moment when the balance of power inside the chamber shifted. Sometimes it moved toward the Speaker, and sometimes away.

The Speaker presides over the House and controls much of its agenda, including which bills reach the floor and when. The office also stands second in the presidential line of succession, behind the vice president. That placement comes from the Presidential Succession Act of 1947.

This timeline gathers 12 Speaker of the House firsts in chronological order. It is a selection, not a complete record of every precedent the office has set. It begins in April 1789, when the 1st Congress reached a quorum and elected Frederick Muhlenberg of Pennsylvania. It ends in October 2023, when the House removed a sitting Speaker through a motion to vacate for the first time.

In between are the entries that trace the job's reach. One Speaker won the post on his first day as a member. Another later moved to the White House. One changed what counts as a quorum. A later Speaker saw his powers cut by insurgents in his own party working with the opposition. The list also covers the longest total tenure and the longest unbroken run. It includes a party's return to the majority after decades in the minority. It also marks the first woman to hold the gavel and her return to the post years later.

Each entry stands on its own. Readers can start with the founding-era Speakers or jump to the 2023 fight over the gavel, which took 15 ballots to settle. The entries show how the House has expanded, limited and contested the Speaker's authority over time.

Frederick Muhlenberg, a Pennsylvania representative, became the first Speaker of the House in April 1789. Members chose him once the chamber gathered a quorum during the 1st Congress.

The new Congress was due to open in March 1789 in New York, then the seat of the federal government. Too few representatives had arrived to conduct business. The House could not organize itself or elect officers until enough members reached the city. Once a quorum assembled in April, choosing a presiding officer was among its first acts.

Muhlenberg brought prior experience in running a legislature to the post. He had served in the Continental Congress and had presided over the Pennsylvania Assembly. He was also president of the Pennsylvania convention that ratified the Constitution in 1787.

Muhlenberg was born in 1750 in Trappe, Pennsylvania. He was ordained as a Lutheran minister before turning to public office. His father, Henry Melchior Muhlenberg, organized the first Lutheran synod in North America. His brother Peter Muhlenberg served as a general in the Continental Army.

Henry Clay, a Kentucky representative, was elected Speaker of the House in 1811 on his first day as a House member. He held the post in three separate stretches, the last ending in 1825.

The vote came on Nov. 4, 1811, when the 12th Congress convened. Clay was not new to national politics. He had already served two short stints in the U.S. Senate, filling unexpired terms. The House was a different setting. Members chose a newcomer to lead them before he had cast a single vote in the chamber.

Clay arrived as a leader of the War Hawks. This group of mostly Southern and Western members pressed for a harder line against Britain. As Speaker, he used his authority to advance that agenda. Congress declared war on Britain in June 1812, starting the War of 1812.

Thomas Brackett Reed, a Maine Republican, was Speaker of the House from 1889 to 1891. In January 1890, he ruled that members sitting in the chamber but not voting would count toward a quorum. The ruling ended the minority's ability to stall the House by staying silent.

The tactic Reed confronted was known as the "disappearing quorum." The Constitution requires a majority of the House to be present to do business. Minority members could attend a session and simply decline to answer the roll call. They were then recorded as absent.

If enough of them stayed quiet, the count fell short of a majority. The House could not act, even with most of its members physically in the room. When the majority party held a narrow edge, a determined minority could freeze the legislative calendar this way.

Sam Rayburn, a Texas Democrat, holds the record for the most total time as Speaker of the House. Across three separate stretches between 1940 and 1961, he held the gavel for about 17 years. No one else has served longer in the office.

His first stretch began in September 1940. Rayburn, then the House majority leader, was elected Speaker after the death of Speaker William Bankhead of Alabama. He kept the post until 1947.

The breaks in his tenure came from elections, not from any challenge inside his own party. Republicans won control of the House for two Congresses during his career. The first ran from 1947 to 1949, and the second from 1953 to 1955. Each time, Rayburn led House Democrats as minority leader.

His second stretch as Speaker ran from 1949 to 1953. His third began in 1955 and lasted until his death from cancer in November 1961. He was still Speaker when he died.

Newt Gingrich, a Georgia Republican, became Speaker of the House in January 1995. His party had won control of the chamber in the 1994 midterm elections, ending a run of Democratic majorities that dated to the 1950s.

Across those decades, every Speaker was a Democrat. Democrats also chaired every standing committee and set the floor schedule.

Gingrich first won his seat in 1978 and took office in January 1979. Through the 1980s, he built a reputation as a combative critic of the Democratic leadership. House Republicans elected him minority whip in 1989, making him the party's second-ranking leader in the chamber.

In September 1994, House Republicans unveiled the Contract with America, a campaign platform Gingrich helped shape. More than 300 Republican House candidates signed it. The document promised votes on 10 bills within the first 100 days of a Republican-led Congress. Its proposals included a balanced budget amendment, a line-item veto and changes to welfare.

Nancy Pelosi was elected Speaker of the House again on Jan. 3, 2019. The vote made her the first former Speaker to win back the gavel after a break since Sam Rayburn did so in 1955.

Her return came eight years after she lost the post. Pelosi first held the speakership from 2007 until January 2011. Republicans won control of the House in the 2010 midterm elections, and she became minority leader.

Two Republicans held the gavel during her years out of the office. John Boehner of Ohio served as Speaker from 2011 until he resigned in 2015. Paul Ryan of Wisconsin followed him and served until the end of the 115th Congress in January 2019.

Democrats regained a House majority in the 2018 midterm elections. That result set up Pelosi's second election as Speaker on the opening day of the 116th Congress.

Kevin McCarthy became the first Speaker of the House removed from office by a vote of the full chamber. On Oct. 3, 2023, the House ousted the California Republican through a motion to vacate the chair. He had held the gavel for less than nine months.

A motion to vacate is a resolution that declares the Speaker's office empty. If a majority of members voting approves it, the Speaker loses the post immediately.

Rep. Matt Gaetz, a Florida Republican, filed the motion on Oct. 2, 2023. It came two days after the House, under McCarthy, passed a stopgap spending bill with Democratic votes. That measure kept the federal government open and averted a shutdown.

The path to the vote ran through the rules McCarthy accepted to win the speakership that January. Those rules let any single House member force a vote on removing the Speaker. Under the previous Congress, a motion to vacate needed the backing of a party caucus or conference.

The 1st Congress had a heavy agenda. It created the departments of State, War and the Treasury. It set up the federal court system through the Judiciary Act of 1789. It also approved 12 proposed constitutional amendments. As Speaker, Muhlenberg signed the enrolled resolution that sent them to the states in 1789. Vice President John Adams signed it as well, as president of the Senate. The states ratified 10 of the amendments, which became the Bill of Rights.

Muhlenberg did not hold the gavel in the 2nd Congress, which met from 1791 to 1793. Jonathan Trumbull Jr. of Connecticut served as Speaker instead. Muhlenberg then returned to the chair, serving a second stretch from 1793 to 1795. That return made him the first person to hold the speakership on two nonconsecutive occasions.

The early speakership was mainly a presiding role. The Speaker kept order, recognized members during debate and ruled on procedure. Muhlenberg stayed in the House until 1797. He died in 1801 in Lancaster, Pennsylvania.

His main tool was committee assignments. House rules at the time let the Speaker appoint committee members. Clay placed allies on the committees that handled foreign affairs and military matters. That gave the Speaker a direct hand in shaping which bills moved and how.

Clay's approach turned the speakership into a center of political power. Earlier Speakers had acted mostly as presiding officers. Clay used the chair to steer legislation and policy as well as to run debate.

His first stretch ended in January 1814. He resigned the speakership to join the U.S. delegation that negotiated peace with Britain. That delegation produced the Treaty of Ghent, signed in December 1814.

Clay returned to the House and was elected Speaker again in 1815. During his second stretch, Congress took up the dispute over Missouri's admission as a state. Clay played a central role in the Missouri Compromise of 1820. He left the chair later that year.

His third stretch began in 1823. It ended in 1825, when Clay left the House to become secretary of state under President John Quincy Adams. He later returned to the Senate and ran for president three times without winning.

James K. Polk is the only Speaker of the House who went on to become president. The Tennessee Democrat held the gavel from 1835 to 1839, then served in the White House from 1845 to 1849.

Polk was born in North Carolina in 1795 and built his political career in Tennessee. He entered the House in 1825. He was a close ally of Andrew Jackson. Supporters called him "Young Hickory," a nod to Jackson's nickname, "Old Hickory."

His speakership covered the 24th and 25th Congresses. Polk won the post in 1835 by defeating John Bell, a fellow Tennessean. The chamber he led was split between Jacksonian Democrats and the new Whig Party, which had formed in opposition to Jackson.

Those divisions made the job contentious. Whig members challenged his rulings and pressed him on procedure. In 1836 the House also adopted the "gag rule," which tabled antislavery petitions without debate. Fights over that rule ran through his time in the chair.

Polk left the House in 1839 to become governor of Tennessee. He then lost races for that office in 1841 and 1843. Those defeats left him without a public post as the 1844 presidential contest approached.

The Democratic National Convention met in Baltimore in 1844. Martin Van Buren arrived as the front-runner but could not secure the nomination. Delegates turned to Polk, a compromise candidate, on the ninth ballot.

In the general election, Polk defeated the Whig nominee, Henry Clay. He pledged to serve a single term and kept that promise.

His presidency expanded U.S. territory. The 1846 Oregon Treaty with Britain set the northwestern boundary at the 49th parallel. The Mexican-American War ended with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in 1848. That treaty brought California and much of the Southwest under U.S. control.

At home, Polk restored the Independent Treasury system. He also signed the Walker Tariff, which lowered import duties.

Polk left office in March 1849. His successor was Zachary Taylor, a Whig. Polk died of cholera in Nashville that June.

Reed stopped relying on roll-call responses alone. He directed the clerk to record the names of members who were present but refusing to vote. Those members were then counted toward the quorum. Democrats protested loudly from the floor, but the ruling stood.

The House then wrote the principle into its rules for that Congress. The package became known as the Reed Rules. It also gave the Speaker more room to rule out motions offered only to delay business.

The new method was tested in court. In United States v. Ballin, decided in 1892, the Supreme Court upheld the House's power to set its own way of determining whether a quorum was present.

The episode is tied to Reed's nickname, "Czar Reed." Critics used the label to describe the control he exercised over the floor. The ruling shifted power away from the minority and toward the Speaker and the majority party.

Democrats dropped the counting rule after they took control of the House following Reed's first speakership. They adopted their own version of it in 1894. Reed later returned to the chair, serving as Speaker again from 1895 to 1899.

In March 1910, a coalition of insurgent Republicans and Democrats removed Speaker Joseph G. Cannon from the House Rules Committee. The vote broke the Speaker's control over which bills reached the floor and how they were debated.

Cannon, an Illinois Republican, held the speakership from 1903 to 1911. Colleagues and the press knew him as "Uncle Joe." In that era, the Speaker appointed members to House committees and also chaired the Rules Committee.

Those two powers worked together. Committee assignments let Cannon reward allies and sideline critics. Control of the Rules Committee let him decide when legislation came up for a vote and under what terms.

Cannon used that leverage to slow measures he opposed, including proposals backed by progressive members of his own party. Republicans from the Midwest and West who wanted reform found their bills stalled. They became known as insurgents.

The decisive challenge came from George Norris, a Republican representative from Nebraska. He introduced a resolution to change how the Rules Committee was chosen. Cannon's supporters argued that the resolution was out of order. The dispute carried through several days of floor debate.

Norris's measure passed when Democrats joined the insurgents. It expanded the Rules Committee, gave the full House the power to elect the committee's members and barred the Speaker from sitting on it.

Cannon answered the defeat by inviting a motion to declare the chair vacant. Albert Burleson, a Texas Democrat, offered it. The motion failed. Many insurgents who had voted to curb Cannon's powers would not vote to remove him from office.

Cannon remained Speaker until the end of that Congress. Democrats then won control of the House in the 1910 midterm elections. Missouri Democrat Champ Clark became Speaker in 1911. The new majority also moved committee assignment power away from the Speaker.

The Speaker has not served on the Rules Committee since the 1910 vote. Later Speakers shaped the panel through their party's leadership rather than by holding a seat on it.

Cannon's name remains on Capitol Hill. The Cannon House Office Building, completed in 1908, is the oldest of the House office buildings.

Rayburn worked through personal relationships with members. He hosted lawmakers in a private room in the Capitol known as the "Board of Education," where they gathered after hours. Truman, then vice president, was in that room on April 12, 1945. There he received a call summoning him to the White House. At the White House, he learned that Roosevelt had died.

Rayburn also served as a mentor to Lyndon B. Johnson, a fellow Texan. Johnson began his House career in the 1930s before moving on to the Senate.

Rayburn first entered the House in 1913. He represented a district in northeast Texas that included his hometown of Bonham. Before Congress, he had served as speaker of the Texas House of Representatives.

John McCormack of Massachusetts succeeded him as Speaker in January 1962. The Rayburn House Office Building, which houses congressional offices on Capitol Hill, carries his name.

Thomas P. "Tip" O'Neill, a Massachusetts Democrat, served as Speaker of the House from 1977 to 1987. His 10 years in the chair are the longest continuous tenure in the office's history.

O'Neill took the gavel in January 1977, at the opening of the 95th Congress. He succeeded Carl Albert of Oklahoma, who had retired. O'Neill had already moved up through the Democratic leadership. He served as majority whip and then as majority leader under Albert.

O'Neill was born in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1912. Before reaching Congress, he served in the Massachusetts House of Representatives and rose to become its speaker. He then won the U.S. House seat that John F. Kennedy gave up to run for the Senate. O'Neill took that seat in 1953.

His speakership spanned five consecutive Congresses, the 95th through the 99th. Sam Rayburn of Texas holds the record for total time as Speaker. Rayburn's service, though, was broken into separate stretches by periods of Republican control. O'Neill held the gavel without interruption from his first election as Speaker until his departure.

O'Neill's decade as Speaker overlapped two presidents. His first four years came under Jimmy Carter, a fellow Democrat. The remaining six came under Ronald Reagan, a Republican. Reagan drew on votes from conservative Democrats to pass his 1981 tax and budget plans over O'Neill's opposition.

The two men also reached agreements. In 1983, O'Neill and Reagan backed a bipartisan package of Social Security changes. The package built on recommendations from a commission chaired by Alan Greenspan.

Live television coverage of the House began during his speakership. C-SPAN started carrying House floor proceedings in March 1979, early in the 96th Congress.

O'Neill did not run for reelection in 1986. He left Congress when his term ended in January 1987. Jim Wright of Texas, the majority leader, succeeded him as Speaker. O'Neill died in Boston in January 1994.

His memoir, Man of the House, written with William Novak, came out in 1987. The phrase "All politics is local" became the saying most closely associated with him. It also became the title of a book he wrote with Gary Hymel, published in 1994.

The 1994 losses extended to the Democratic leadership. Speaker Tom Foley, a Democrat from Washington state, lost his own seat. He was the first sitting Speaker defeated for reelection since 1862.

Once in charge, the new majority rewrote House rules. Committee chairs became subject to a limit of three consecutive terms. The Speaker's own tenure was capped at four consecutive terms. The House also abolished three committees and cut committee staff by about a third. The eliminated panels were the District of Columbia, Merchant Marine and Fisheries, and Post Office and Civil Service committees.

Gingrich's speakership also brought clashes with President Bill Clinton over the federal budget. Two government shutdowns followed. The first lasted from Nov. 14 to Nov. 19, 1995. The longer one ran from Dec. 16, 1995, to Jan. 6, 1996.

In January 1997, the House reprimanded Gingrich over an ethics case and assessed him a $300,000 penalty. After Republicans lost House seats in the 1998 midterms, he announced he would step down as Speaker. He left Congress in January 1999.

Nancy Pelosi became the first woman to serve as Speaker of the House on Jan. 4, 2007. The California Democrat won the post on the opening day of the 110th Congress, more than two centuries after the first Speaker took the chair in 1789.

Her election followed the 2006 midterm elections, in which Democrats won control of the House. Pelosi took the gavel from Dennis Hastert, an Illinois Republican who had held it since 1999.

Pelosi reached the speakership by rising through her party's House leadership. Her Democratic colleagues elected her whip in 2001. She became the party's top leader in the House in January 2003. That made her the first woman to lead a major party in either chamber of Congress.

Her congressional career began in San Francisco. She won her House seat in a 1987 special election and represented a San Francisco district for decades afterward. She also became the first Italian American to serve as Speaker.

Her roots are in Baltimore, where she was born. Her father, Thomas D'Alesandro Jr., represented a Maryland district in the House and later served as mayor of Baltimore.

The 2007 vote also changed the presidential line of succession. No woman had ever stood as high in that line as Pelosi did once she took the gavel.

The milestone closed a long gap for women in the House. Jeannette Rankin, a Montana Republican, became the first woman elected to Congress in 1916. She took her seat the following year. Women served in the House for about nine decades before one held its top post.

Pelosi's first stretch as Speaker covered four years and two Congresses. During that time, the House passed the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act, which President Barack Obama signed in February 2009. The House also passed the Affordable Care Act, and Obama signed the health care law in March 2010.

That stretch ended after the 2010 midterm elections, when Republicans won back the House majority. John Boehner, an Ohio Republican, succeeded her as Speaker in January 2011.

Her return required support from critics inside her own party. A group of House Democrats pushed for new leadership after the midterms. Pelosi won over enough of them in part by agreeing to limit how long she would serve as Speaker. Several Democrats still voted for other candidates when the full House chose its Speaker.

The gap between the two comebacks spans more than six decades. Rayburn regained the office in 1955 after Republicans controlled the House for two years. Pelosi's absence lasted four times as long as his.

Pelosi held the gavel for two more Congresses, the 116th and the 117th. During that stretch she presided over the House as it impeached President Donald Trump twice. The first impeachment came in December 2019 and the second in January 2021. After the 2020 elections, she led a narrow Democratic majority.

Republicans won the House in the 2022 midterm elections. Pelosi then announced she would not seek a leadership post in the next Congress. Hakeem Jeffries of New York succeeded her as leader of House Democrats.

Her second stretch as Speaker ended in January 2023. Across her two stints, she spent eight years in the office.

Kevin McCarthy, a California Republican, became Speaker of the House in the early hours of Jan. 7, 2023, on the 15th ballot. He reached the gavel only after 14 earlier rounds of voting failed to produce a winner.

The contest opened on Jan. 3, 2023, the first day of the 118th Congress. Electing a Speaker is the first order of business in a new House. Until that vote is settled, members-elect cannot take the oath of office. The chamber also cannot adopt its rules, form committees or pass legislation.

Republicans had won a narrow House majority in the 2022 midterm elections. McCarthy had led the House Republican conference since 2019, and he was the party's nominee for Speaker. To win, a candidate needs a majority of the members who vote for a named person. With so few seats to spare, a small bloc of Republicans could keep him below that line.

That bloc formed on the opening day. A group of conservative Republicans withheld their support and cast votes for other names instead. Democrats backed their own leader, Hakeem Jeffries of New York, on every ballot. Neither candidate could reach a majority, and the House kept voting.

The standoff stretched across several days of roll calls. McCarthy and his allies negotiated with the holdouts between votes. Several of those members moved into his column on Jan. 6. The rest did not switch outright.

On the 15th ballot, the final holdouts voted present rather than naming another candidate. A present vote does not count toward the total of members voting for a named person. That lowered the number McCarthy needed, and he cleared it shortly after midnight.

Rep. Hal Rogers of Kentucky, the dean of the House, administered the Speaker's oath to McCarthy. McCarthy then swore in the other members-elect as a group. With that step done, the House could adopt its rules and begin its formal work for the new Congress.

An earlier attempt to remove a Speaker this way never reached the floor. In 2015, Rep. Mark Meadows of North Carolina filed a motion to vacate against Speaker John Boehner. The House never voted on it. Boehner announced his resignation later that year.

McCarthy's removal left the chamber without an elected Speaker. Patrick McHenry, a North Carolina Republican, stepped in as Speaker pro tempore. His name sat at the top of a confidential list of successors McCarthy had filed with the House Clerk. A House rule adopted after the Sept. 11, 2001, attacks requires each new Speaker to submit such a list.

House Republicans put forward several nominees for the job over the following weeks. The search ended on Oct. 25, 2023, when the House elected Mike Johnson, a Louisiana Republican, as Speaker.

McCarthy did not run to reclaim the post. He left Congress at the end of 2023.
