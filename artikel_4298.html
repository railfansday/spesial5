Strange new materials can crawl, walk, and dig like living creatures

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Most materials simply react when something pushes, pulls, or bends them. But a very different category of matter can draw on its own energy to change shape and respond to outside forces in surprising ways. Known as active matter, these unusual materials are attracting growing scientific interest. Now, physicists from the universities of Amsterdam, New South Wales, and Cambridge have uncovered remarkable new behaviors that could change how future machines and robots are designed.

Everyday materials such as metal, concrete, glass, and rubber are generally considered passive. They can stretch, bend, shift, or change shape when forces act on them, but the energy needed to produce those changes comes from outside the material.

Active matter works differently. It contains an internal source of energy that allows it to respond to its surroundings in ways ordinary materials cannot. Many examples occur naturally in living systems. A flock of birds, for instance, can move together as a coordinated group, adjusting its behavior in response to wind, changes in the landscape, available food, or a suitable place to rest.

Scientists can also recreate some of these remarkable properties in the laboratory. Researchers from the University of Amsterdam (The Netherlands), the University of Cambridge (UK), and the University of New South Wales (Sydney, Australia) have spent several years developing active materials from surprisingly simple components, including miniature motors, rods, and rubber bands. Their experiments have revealed unusual mechanical behaviors with potentially valuable applications, leading to two recently accepted scientific papers.

Scientists Create Materials That Can Crawl, Walk, and Dig

One of the discoveries involves two familiar mechanical behaviors known as buckling and snapping.

Imagine squeezing a paper ticket between your fingers. As the pressure increases, the ticket becomes unstable and bends to one side. If you then press against the curved surface, it initially pushes back before suddenly flipping in the opposite direction.

This is how ordinary passive materials behave. External forces supply the energy needed for the ticket to buckle and snap, and each action occurs only once in response to the applied force.

The researchers discovered that adding internal activity can fundamentally transform this behavior. They assembled a series of connected rods into a flexible chain, placing small motors at the joints where neighboring rods meet. These motors created interactions that were not symmetrical. Moving one rod could affect its neighbor in a different way than moving that neighbor would affect the original rod (by rotating over a different angle, for example).

The result was striking. When external forces acted on these active chains, they still bent and snapped, but the movement no longer had to end after a single event. Instead, the structures could repeatedly buckle and snap, producing ongoing oscillations.

The underlying physics involves a transformation of the system's critical point, where snapping normally takes place, into what researchers call a critical exceptional point. This unusual behavior makes it possible for the chains to generate movements resembling crawling, walking, and even digging.

The findings were published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, with Sami Al-Izzi of the University of New South Wales and Yao Du of the University of Amsterdam serving as joint first authors. One of the team's buckling chains was also featured on the journal's cover.

Beyond revealing unexpected physics, the discovery points toward a promising approach to building materials capable of performing multiple tasks autonomously. Such structures could be especially useful in flexible, "soft" robots, potentially allowing their bodies to produce complex movements without relying on a centralized control system.

Active Matter Challenges a Fundamental Engineering Principle

A separate investigation by the same international research team uncovered another surprising property of active materials, this time challenging a principle widely used in engineering.

Whether designing large bridges or tiny nanomechanical devices, engineers depend on established rules that connect the properties of individual components to the behavior of an entire structure.

One such rule is Le Chatelier's Principle. In the mechanical context described by the researchers, it suggests that changes made to individual parts of a system should produce corresponding changes in the overall structure. For example, making a structure's individual components stiffer would generally be expected to increase the stiffness of the entire assembly.

But the researchers found that active matter does not always follow this expectation. In some cases, increasing the activity of individual components can actually reduce the activity of the material as a whole.

To investigate this counterintuitive effect, the scientists built another structure using motors and rods. Rather than arranging the components in a chain, they connected them into a flat, two-dimensional network resembling a lattice. They then examined how the elastic behavior of the entire structure changed depending on the properties of its individual elements.

Their experiments revealed that the overall response depended on more than simply how active each component was. What mattered was whether the active elements formed connected pathways capable of transmitting mechanical responses throughout the structure.

Why More Activity Can Produce Less Response

The researchers identified a process called percolation as the key to understanding this unexpected behavior.

A useful comparison is the movement of water through ground coffee. If the coffee grounds are packed too tightly, water cannot easily travel through the entire layer. The arrangement of the grounds determines whether the water can find a continuous path.

Something similar happens inside active materials. If too many of the interconnected components have relatively low activity, they can prevent elastic responses from spreading through the structure. Even when other components are extremely active, their influence may not extend across the entire material.

This explains why making some parts more active does not necessarily produce a more active structure overall. The way the components are connected and distributed can be just as important as their individual properties.

The second study, led by first author Jack Binysh from Corentin Coulais's research group at the University of Amsterdam, was published in Physical Review X.

The researchers believe that this unexpected departure from Le Chatelier's Principle could have important implications for understanding and designing systems built from active microscopic structures. These include biological gels, epithelial monolayers, which are sheets of closely connected cells, and neuromorphic networks that mimic aspects of biological information processing.

Together, the findings reveal how dramatically materials can behave when their internal components actively consume energy and interact in unconventional ways. The discoveries could influence research across physics, soft matter science, mechanical engineering, the life sciences, and robotics, while opening new possibilities for creating adaptable materials and smarter robotic systems.
