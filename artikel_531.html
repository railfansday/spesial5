DNA’s secret sunscreen may have helped life survive on early Earth

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

How did life emerge and survive on Earth when the planet was exposed to extreme conditions? Scientists have spent decades trying to answer this fundamental question, including how the earliest biological systems endured an environment that would have been dangerous to many living organisms today.

Researchers at Texas A&M University are now using advanced techniques that study how molecules interact with light to investigate a surprising clue about the evolution of DNA. Their findings could help explain how early genetic material survived intense ultraviolet radiation from the Sun.

The research was led by Dr. Peter Rentzepis, a professor in the university's Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, along with research assistants Keyvan Khosh Abady and Negar Karpourazar. Their study, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS), provides new insight into a longstanding mystery surrounding molecular evolution and the origins of life.

Why DNA's Evolution Has Puzzled Scientists

One of the major changes in the evolution of genetic systems involved the transition from RNA, which is typically a single-stranded molecule, to DNA as the primary means of storing genetic information. An important difference between the two molecules is that DNA uses a chemical building block called thymine in place of uracil, which is found in RNA.

This substitution has puzzled researchers for decades. Thymine is more susceptible to certain forms of ultraviolet (UV) damage, making its adoption seem like an evolutionary disadvantage. The problem would have been especially significant on early Earth, before a protective ozone layer developed and shielded the planet's surface from much of the Sun's harmful radiation.

Why would evolution favor a component that appeared to make genetic material more vulnerable under such harsh conditions? Rentzepis and his colleagues set out to investigate whether thymine might offer an advantage that had previously been overlooked.

To explore this possibility, the researchers exposed thymine and uracil to carefully controlled UV radiation intended to reproduce conditions on primordial Earth. They analyzed the molecules using biophotonics, a field that examines biological materials through their interactions with light. Their experiments employed several techniques, including absorption, fluorescence, and Raman spectroscopy.

DNA's Surprising Defense Against UV Damage

The results revealed an unexpected possibility. Although thymine can be vulnerable to UV radiation, it appears to favor types of molecular damage that are more readily reversed. This suggests that an important advantage of thymine may lie not in preventing damage, but in limiting its consequences and making recovery easier.

"We found that thymine tends to direct UV damage into a more reversible form, suggesting that early genetic molecules may have survived intense sunlight not by avoiding damage, but by controlling it and making it easier to self-repair," said Khosh Abady. "In that sense, thymine may have acted as a molecular sunscreen, absorbing harmful UV energy and keeping the damage confined to the genetic bases rather than allowing it to harm more vulnerable parts of the molecule."

This finding offers a possible explanation for why thymine became a fundamental component of DNA despite its apparent weakness. By concentrating UV damage in parts of the molecule where it could be more easily reversed, thymine may have helped protect the integrity of early genetic systems.

The discovery also suggests that surviving the extreme conditions of early Earth may have depended on more than simply resisting environmental threats. The ability to manage and recover from damage could have played an important role in the evolution of the molecular systems that eventually supported life.

New Possibilities for Pathogen Detection and UV Disinfection

Beyond its implications for understanding life's beginnings, the research could have practical applications in several scientific and technological fields.

The team established a new set of reference measurements describing how these molecules respond to light. These measurements could eventually help researchers identify harmful microorganisms more efficiently and assess the effectiveness of systems that use UV radiation to disinfect water and other materials.

"The distinct spectral signatures we measured could support applications in faster, more sensitive pathogen detection and in monitoring whether UV disinfection has effectively inactivated microorganisms, particularly in water-treatment systems," said Khosh Abady.

These potential applications demonstrate how investigating fundamental questions about molecular evolution can also provide tools for addressing modern challenges in health and environmental safety.

Could the Building Blocks of Life Have Come From Space?

With these findings in hand, Rentzepis and his team plan to investigate an even earlier chapter in life's history. Their next goal is to understand the forces and chemical processes that brought thymine, uracil, and other essential components of primitive genetic systems together.

A central question is whether these ingredients originated entirely on Earth or whether some arrived from beyond our planet. Meteorites may have delivered organic compounds that later became incorporated into the chemical systems from which life developed.

"We hope to explore whether life's chemical building blocks formed and assembled entirely on Earth or whether some were delivered to our planet from elsewhere by meteorites and later incorporated into early genetic systems," said Khosh Abady. "We are grateful to Texas A&M University and our research sponsors for supporting this work and enabling us to explore fundamental questions about life's origins."

By examining how DNA's molecular components respond to radiation and how those components may have first assembled, the researchers hope to uncover more of the chemical history that made life on Earth possible.
