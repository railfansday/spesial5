Larry Ellison Has Lost $145 Billion in Net Worth Over the Past Year as Oracle Stock Fell 59% From Its Peak. Here's Why Oracle's Heavy Concentration in OpenAI Has Wall Street Nervous.

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Oracle is estimated to have over $300 billion in orders from OpenAI, and Wall Street thinks that could be a bad thing.

The last year has been brutal for Oracle (ORCL -3.38%) stock investors. The company's share price is down roughly 44% over the stretch and roughly 50% from its high, and you can count founder and CEO Larry Ellison among those impacted by the stock's big sell-offs.

As of the company's most recent public disclosures, Ellison held roughly 1.16 billion shares of Oracle stock -- giving him a roughly 40% stake in the business and making him its largest shareholder. As a result of the large valuation drawdown, Ellison has seen his net worth decline roughly $145 billion compared to where it stood at his company's valuation peak.

Broader valuation trends affecting companies in the artificial intelligence (AI) industry have played a role in the stock's collapse, but the market is also concerned about Oracle's heavy reliance on OpenAI as a customer. Here's why.

At the end of its last quarter, Oracle reported a backlog of roughly $664 billion. A company's backlog represents products or services that have been contracted for but not yet delivered and recorded as revenue. Of Oracle's backlog, at least $300 billion is widely estimated to come from OpenAI -- the company best known for its ChatGPT artificial intelligence model.

In other words, around half of Oracle's backlog likely comes from just one customer. While both Oracle and OpenAI would like to see the entirety of Oracle's massive backlog fulfilled, there's no guarantee these contracts will be realized.

If OpenAI falls on hard times, there's a major risk that the majority of its backlog with Oracle could be voided. The specific terms of Oracle's contracts with OpenAI aren't public, and it's reasonable to expect that Oracle would receive cancellation fees if OpenAI backs out of large orders, but there's no getting blood from a stone. Oracle has effectively married its future to the success and scaling of OpenAI, and it could face catastrophic setbacks if its bets on the frontier AI model leader don't pan out.

Heavy customer concentration presents significant risks because sales and earnings performance can deteriorate rapidly if a major customer scales back or cuts its orders entirely. The risk of customer concentration for many players in the AI infrastructure space, and Oracle in particular, is even higher due to the highly cost-intensive nature of data center build-outs and operations.

As of this writing, Oracle is trading at roughly 15 times expected forward earnings. If you look at the company's forward price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio in the context of the tech giant's recent business performance, there's a solid case to be made that the stock is significantly undervalued based on recent sales and earnings momentum.

In the first quarter of the company's 2027 fiscal year, which ended Aug. 31, Oracle's overall revenue grew 30% year over year to reach $19.3 billion. The biggest growth driver by far was the company's Oracle cloud segment, which saw sales rise 62% annually to reach $11.6 billion. Meanwhile, adjusted earnings per share were up 30% compared to the prior-year period. These levels of sales and earnings growth support the thesis that Oracle stock is cheap right now, but the picture is more complicated than that.

While Oracle recorded operating cash flow of $23 billion last quarter, this figure does not factor in the company's $28 billion in capital expenditures. All told, the business recorded free cash flow of -$5 million last quarter.

When a company invests in building out a resource for the business, such as a data center, these costs aren't recorded as normal expenses that affect its bottom line. Instead, capital expenditures are recorded as assets. So while the company's earnings growth has been impressive, it also doesn't fully reflect current spending dynamics and related risks.

Adding another layer of complexity, Oracle is relying on debt and stock sales to fund its capex. The company has said it expects between $90 billion and $95 billion in capex this year, and it's also likely to continue spending heavily to expand its data center capacity in subsequent years. With roughly $125 billion in debt and the business likely to continue posting negative free cash flow in the near term, Oracle's massive data center spending to support its OpenAI contracts comes with added risk.
