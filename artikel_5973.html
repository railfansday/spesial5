How Amadeus Pulled Away From Sabre and Travelport

Amadeus gained distribution share and built a bigger airline IT business. Sabre and Travelport remain weighed down by heavy debt and a heavier reliance on distribution.

For decades, the companies that controlled airline distribution occupied one of the most important positions in the travel industry. But making money from travel bookings and building a profitable technology business around them have turned out to be very different propositions. The contrasting fortunes of Amadeus, Sabre, and Travelport show just how different.

In 2000, Amadeus accounted for an estimated 26% of global GDS air bookings, compared with 34% for Sabre, including Abacus. Galileo and Worldspan, then separate systems that were later combined into Travelport, together had about 40%. By 2023, the estimates were 47% for Amadeus, 32% for Sabre, and 21% for Travelport, according to Redburn Atlantic data.

Three companies that once split global airline distribution between them now look nothing alike. Amadeus has moved from an estimated 26% of GDS air bookings in 2000 to 47% in 2023, according to Redburn Atlantic data, while Sabre slipped from 34% to 32% and the Galileo-Worldspan combination that became Travelport fell from about 40% to 21%. The bigger divergence is in what each company sells. Amadeus generated €6.52 billion ($7.15 billion) in 2025, with only €3.12 billion from air distribution and the rest from airline IT and hospitality technology built through Altéa, Navitaire, Newmarket International, and TravelClick. Sabre generated $2.77 billion from continuing operations, 80% of it distribution, and sold Hospitality Solutions to TPG for $1.1 billion in 2025 to pay down debt. Travelport reported $1.44 billion, nearly 90% of it booking fees. All of this played out in a channel that shrank: Amadeus processed 580.4 million travel-agency air bookings in 2019 and 484.5 million in 2025, a decline of about 17%, even as airline passenger traffic recovered. In mid-November 2026, Amadeus carried a stock-market value of roughly $29 billion against less than $1 billion for Sabre.
