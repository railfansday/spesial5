Compass, MLSs Jockey Over Jurisdictions as Lawsuits Sprawl

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

In the ongoing legal battle between Compass and more than a dozen large MLSs across the country, both sides are maneuvering to control how and where the cases are tried, as the mega-brokerage seeks to spread the litigation as widely as possible.

The Arizona Regional MLS (ARMLS), one of 15 MLSs sued by Compass last week, filed a letter with the Southern District of New York on Nov. 18, arguing that Compass’s motion to “splinter one dispute across multiple courts” should be denied.

“Compass’s effort to scatter this dispute across multiple courts would create unnecessary complexity and expense without changing the fundamental issue at stake,” said Jeff Bosch, CEO of Arizona Regional Multiple Listing Service, in a statement shared with RISMedia. “We are defending cooperation rules that promote fair competition, give buyers broader access to available homes, and help sellers reach more potential buyers. We believe these rules are lawful and procompetitive, and we look forward to defending them in court.”

Simultaneously, Compass is grappling with court rules that require it to notify judges of related cases as it pursues a larger goal of maximizing pressure on each MLS, arguing that the lawsuits should proceed separately despite their many similarities.

“The overlap between the cases concerning the subject of the challenged rules and Compass’s phased-marketing program does not justify coordinating cases that involve different defendants, markets, witnesses, and damages,” Compass wrote in a letter filed in the Southern District of California.

The back-and-forth is the latest key maneuver as Compass and the MLSs battle over rules that prevent pre-marketed or off-MLS listings from being shown on the MLS.

As the mega-brokerage pursues a broad campaign to remake real estate, it has not shied away from using its significant size and resources to lean on MLSs that have stuck to their policies (specifically, restrictions on off-MLS marketing). In a September letter sent to the MLSs it would eventually sue, Compass explicitly pointed to the legal costs of defending a lawsuit, and promised to spend “millions of dollars to sue” MLSs that defied it.

In at least three of its lawsuits targeting MLSs, Compass informed federal judges of the other two-dozen lawsuits it has filed, while simultaneously arguing those lawsuits should not be consolidated or reassigned, claiming “each case turns on a distinct defendant, rule, and local market.”

Still, Compass acknowledged that the cases “overlap…in a limited respect,” but wrote in one letter it would not “concede…that consolidation, coordination, reassignment, or transfer would be appropriate.”

With around 350,000 federal civil lawsuits filed every year, judges are often highly motivated to find efficiencies and avoid redundancies in their caseloads. To that end, many districts require that attorneys notify the court of cases with similar factual elements or common parties filed in other districts, allowing judges to coordinate, consolidate or otherwise smooth out the litigation process.

In one of its letters, Compass cited this rule but said it was only informing the judge out of “an abundance of caution,” while still arguing the the other lawsuits “do not arise from the same or substantially identical transactions, happenings, or events.”

Forcing each MLS to defend a separate lawsuit will likely increase litigation costs overall, with Compass better able to bear that burden as a well-funded public company. At the same time, judges are also likely to consider geographic factors, as any consolidated case would force some MLSs to travel further for proceedings.

On November 12, Compass sued 15 MLSs in 15 separate actions in 11 districts, claiming their longstanding broker cooperation rules violated antitrust law. The en masse filing was preceded by a letter Compass sent to several MLSs in September, warning them to cease enforcement of their broker cooperation rules or face federal antitrust claims.

In response, six MLSs, later joined by one more, filed a lawsuit in New York seeking to prevent Compass from suing them along with a declaration that their cooperation rules are lawful under federal antitrust laws (and state-law analogues).

The MLSs have also sought to rally together, with at least two separate legal defense funds in the works—one by CRMLS, and another through the Council of Multiple Listing Services (which has not been named in the litigation, though many of the targeted MLSs are members).

In its November 13 motion, Compass claimed that the action filed by the MLSs should either be dismissed or handled individually in the jurisdictions where each MLS resides.

ARMLS counsel Gregory S. Skidmore argued in his letter that trying the cases individually would create “massive inefficiencies and duplication of effort.”

“The MLS Parties sued Compass where it is headquartered, and where Compass itself has chosen to litigate its own Cooperation Rules–related suit,” wrote Skidmore, referring to the brokerage’s injunction hearing against Zillow in November of 2025. “Compass has not shown any ‘indication that nonparty witnesses would refuse to appear’…and Compass’s executives reside in New York,” he continued. “Nor is any other district the locus of operative facts: Compass’s campaign originated at its New York headquarters and reached nationwide.”

Compass, in its separate letters, argued that each case is more about the local circumstances and specific rules of each MLS, and directly disputed that consolidation would create more efficiencies, and noted that potential damages will depend on sales prices in local real estate markets.

Skidmore, on the other hand, urged the SDNY court to to focus on “judicial economy” and claiming that Compass “bears the burden” of showing that severing the case is necessary.

“Transfer would create seven suits in five districts, each replicating Compass’s evidence,” he added. “Courts need not indulge an ‘onslaught of litigation.'”
