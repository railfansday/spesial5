Cancer-associated mutations in PIK3CA underlie congenital overgrowth disorders that frequently involve the vasculature1,2. Among the affected tissues, the endothelium commonly exhibits aberrant expansion, leading to vascular malformations3. Notably, these lesions arise predominantly in veins and capillaries, whereas arteries are largely spared, for reasons that remain unclear. Here, using genetic lineage tracing, we show that endothelial expression of PIK3CAH1047R drives profound clonal expansion of capillary and venous endothelial cells, whereas mature arterial endothelial cells are refractory to pathological PI3K activation. Expression of PIK3CAH1047R in arterial precursors disrupts arterial differentiation and promotes the acquisition of venous features, providing a mechanistic explanation for the paucity of arterial lesions after PIK3CA genetic perturbation. Mechanistically, these effects are mediated by two complementary downstream branches of PI3K signalling: increased proliferation, which drives vascular overgrowth, and upregulation of the venous-specifying transcription factor NR2F2 (also known as COUP-TFII), which suppresses arterial differentiation. Together, our findings reveal that pathogenic responses to PIK3CAH1047R are dictated by endothelial differentiation state and fate trajectory, and demonstrate that arterial endothelium is intrinsically resistant to oncogenic PI3K signalling. Arteries are therefore shielded from PIK3CA mutations, explaining the rarity of arterial malformations in PIK3CA-driven disease.

Somatic and germline pathogenic variants in cancer-associated genes cause diverse congenital disorders1,2 and are also found in phenotypically normal tissue4,5. Germline mutations may be inherited or arise de novo, whereas somatic mutations, acquired during embryonic development, generate mosaicism and a broad spectrum of phenotypes1. Understanding how identical mutations produce distinct outcomes across tissues is crucial for defining cellular susceptibility to disease and explaining the phenotypic heterogeneity.

PIK3CA encodes p110α, the catalytic subunit of phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3Kα), which regulates cell growth, proliferation, migration and metabolism6 through AKT and downstream effectors, including mTOR and forkhead box O (FOXO) transcription factors7,8. PIK3CA is an oncogene that is frequently mutated in both mosaic congenital disorders9 and phenotypically normal tissue4. Activating mutations occur throughout the coding sequence, with recurrent hotspots including E545K, E542K, H1047R and H1047L3. Mesoderm-derived tissues, including the endothelium, are commonly affected in PIK3CA-related congenital disorders, which manifest as aberrant tissue overgrowth and vascular malformations10,11,12,13. Although PIK3CA mutations have been extensively studied in adult epithelial tissues14,15,16,17, their effects on cellular heterogeneity, differentiation and clonal dynamics in mesoderm-derived lineages remain poorly understood.

Vascular malformations are congenital vascular anomalies classified as slow-flow lesions, affecting capillaries, veins and lymphatic vessels or fast-flow lesions, often involving arteries18. Somatic activating PIK3CA mutations occur in the endothelium of all major slow-flow vascular malformations, where lesion expansion requires mitogenic signals, even though the mutation alone is insufficient to confer a proliferative advantage19,20,21,22. By contrast, no evidence currently implicates PIK3CA mutations in fast-flow arterial malformations3. Whether arterial endothelial cells have these mutations but resist their effects, or mutant clones are negatively selected or eliminated in arterial vessels remains unclear.

Here we demonstrate that endothelial responses to oncogenic PIK3CAH1047R are governed by cellular identity and developmental trajectory. Combining mosaic genetics, lineage tracing and single-cell analyses, we identify a differentiation-stage-specific window of susceptibility to pathological PI3K signalling, while fully differentiated arterial endothelial cells are resistant. These findings provide a framework for the tissue and vessel-type specificity of PIK3CA-driven vascular malformations.

Postnatal expression of Pik3caH1047R (Pik3catm1.1Waph/+) from the endogenous locus in mouse retinal endothelial cells using Pdgfb-creERT2 results in venous and capillary malformations, whereas arteries remain unaffected19 (Extended Data Fig. 1a–e). To correlate these phenotypes with the distribution of mutant cells, we introduced the R26-mTmG reporter allele into the Pik3caH1047RPdgfb-creERT2 mice (pan-endothelial (pEC)-GFP-Pik3caH1047R; Fig. 1a). Tamoxifen-mediated Cre activation induced recombination of the endogenous Pik3caH1047R allele and the reporter, resulting in membrane-bound GFP expression that marks recombined cells and therefore approximates Pik3caH1047R-expressing endothelial cells. Pdgfb-creERT2R26-mTmG mice were used as controls (pEC-GFP). After low-dose administration of 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) at postnatal day 1 (P1), retinas were analysed at P6 (Extended Data Fig. 1c). Mosaic recombination was observed throughout the vascular plexus in both genotypes (Fig. 1b). Although GFP+ mutant cells were present in all vessel types, vascular malformations occurred exclusively in capillaries and veins (Fig. 1c,d). In these vessels, mutant GFP+ endothelial cells underwent marked clonal expansion, generating enlarged vascular tubes (Fig. 1d,e and Extended Data Fig. 1f), accompanied by increased endothelial cell proliferation during early lesion formation (Extended Data Fig. 1g–i). By contrast, Pik3caH1047R GFP+ cells in arteries neither expanded nor altered vessel width (Fig. 1d,e and Extended Data Fig. 1f). Consistent with these phenotypes, capillary and venous mutant GFP+ cells displayed increased PI3K pathway activation, as assessed by staining of phosphorylated S6 (p-S6), whereas arterial mutant cells showed no detectable increase relative to wild-type (WT) cells (Fig. 1d,f). These data show that Pik3caH1047R endothelial cells selectively expand in capillaries and veins, while arteries remain structurally and molecularly normal despite containing mutant cells.

The apparent resistance of arterial endothelial cells to Pik3caH1047R prompted us to directly investigate the behaviour of mutant cells in arteries. To this end, we used the Bmx(PAC)-creERT2 mouse line, which specifically targets arterial endothelial cells in retinal vessels. WT and Pik3caH1047R arterial endothelial cells (aEC) were traced by combining Bmx(PAC)-creERT2 and R26-mTmG with or without the Pik3caH1047R allele (aEC-GFP and aEC-GFP-Pik3caH1047R; Fig. 1g). Bmx(PAC)-creERT2-mediated tracing revealed robust arterial recombination after 4-OHT administration at P4 or P7, whereas induction at P1 did not label arterial endothelial cells (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). Thus, recombination was induced at P4 or P7 for subsequent experiments. In both settings, the proportion of GFP+ cells and arterial vessel width were comparable between the mutant and control retinas (Fig. 1h–j and Extended Data Fig. 2c–f). Arteries also displayed similar smooth-muscle-cell coverage and maintained expression of arterial markers UNC5B and CX37 (Extended Data Fig. 2g–j). Moreover, GFP+ arterial endothelial cells of both genotypes lacked expression of NR2F2, a key transcription factor that suppresses arterial identity23,24,25,26 (Extended Data Fig. 2k,l). Consistent with earlier observations, mutant GFP+ cells showed low PI3K pathway activation, with no increase relative to controls (Fig. 1i,k and Extended Data Fig. 2m,n). To assess the long-term effects, we analysed retinas at P21. No differences were detected in the GFP+ cell distribution, arterial morphology or NR2F2 expression between WT and Pik3caH1047R cells in main arteries and arteriolar branches (Extended Data Fig. 2o–q). These findings show that mutant cells persist in arteries or arteriolar branches without detectable changes in arterial identity, morphology or PI3K activation, supporting the resistance of differentiated arterial endothelial cells to Pik3caH1047R.

To investigate why arterial endothelial cells are protected against Pik3caH1047R, we pharmacologically disrupted key arterial features in aEC-GFP and aEC-GFP-Pik3caH1047R pups. As arterial endothelial cells experience higher shear stress, we first reduced systemic blood pressure with the angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor captopril27. Although captopril reduced KLF4 expression and induced dilation of the vascular plexus28, it did not alter the proportion of GFP+ cells or arterial width. Furthermore, endothelial proliferation was not increased in mutant arteries (Extended Data Fig. 3a–d). We next inhibited Notch signalling, a key regulator of arterial differentiation and maintenance, using N-[N-(3,5-difluorophenacetyl)-l-alanyl]-S-phenylglycine t-butyl ester (DAPT). Despite a pronounced response in sprouting capillaries, DAPT likewise did not elicit any response in arteries to Pik3caH1047R expression (Extended Data Fig. 3a,e,f). Collectively, these results indicate that inhibition of major arterial identity programmes alone is insufficient to overcome arterial resistance to Pik3caH1047R.

To test whether resistance to Pik3caH1047R emerges during arterial differentiation, we traced tip endothelial cells—a pre-arterial population that contributes to arterial growth29,30. We used the Esm1(BAC)-creERT2 mouse line, which specifically marks tip cells, crossed with R26-mTmG (tEC-GFP) alone or in combination with Pik3caH1047R (tEC-GFP-Pik3caH1047R; Fig. 2a). 4-OHT was administered at P1, and Esm1-GFP+ progeny were analysed at different timepoints (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 4a). Then, 24 h after induction, GFP+ cells were predominantly localized at the sprouting front (Fig. 2b). By P6, GFP+ cells contributed extensively to arteries in tEC-GFP retinas, and the GFP+ arterial area further increased by P8 (Fig. 2b). Consistent with previous reports30, a subset of Esm1-traced cells also contributed to the capillary plexus (Fig. 2b). Pik3caH1047R expression markedly reduced the GFP+ area within arteries (Fig. 2b–f and Extended Data Fig. 4b,c). Instead, most mutant GFP+ cells were retained in the capillary bed, where they expanded (Fig. 2d,f and Extended Data Fig. 4b–f) and formed multiple vascular malformations (Fig. 2b,c). Moreover, mutant, but not control, GFP+ cells were frequently detected in veins (Fig. 2b–f and Extended Data Fig. 4b,c). The few Pik3caH1047R GFP+ cells that reached arteries did not induce pathology (Fig. 2c). Consistent with lesion formation, Pik3caH1047R GFP+ cells displayed increased p-S6 levels in capillaries, but not in arteries (Fig. 2e,g and Extended Data Fig. 4g,h). By P21, Pik3caH1047R GFP+ clones persisted in capillaries and veins, although they did not further expand (Extended Data Fig. 4i–l). These data indicate that Pik3caH1047R expression impairs mobilization of arterial precursors to arteries, resulting in their retention within capillaries and the diversion of a subset towards veins, where they expand and form vascular malformations.

To directly compare WT and mutant endothelial cells within the same tissue, we generated a multispectral genetic mouse model, termed iFluMosaic-PIK3CAH1047R (Rosa26CAG-LSL-H2B-Cherry/H2B-GFP-2A-HAHuPIK3CAH1047R). Cre-mediated recombination stochastically generates either His-H2B-Cherry (WT) or H2B-eGFP-V5 together with HA-PIK3CAH1047R (Extended Data Fig. 5a). Validation in primary endothelial cells confirmed preferential generation of Cherry+ cells, as reported for related constructs31, and increased proliferation and PI3K pathway activation in eGFP+ cells (Extended Data Fig. 5b–h). To fate-map pre-arterial endothelial cells in vivo, we crossed iFluMosaic-PIK3CAH1047R mice with Esm1(BAC)-creERT2 mice (tEC-iFluMosaic-PIK3CAH1047R) (Fig. 2h). As the recombination efficiency was lower than in Pik3catm1.1Waph/+ mice, we increased the 4-OHT dose, and retinas were analysed 7 days after induction (Extended Data Fig. 6a). Cherry+ cells contributed to both capillaries and arteries (Fig. 2i–k). By contrast, eGFP+ cells were predominantly located in capillaries, including those adjacent to arteries, and in veins, where they expanded and formed vascular malformations (Fig. 2i–k and Extended Data Fig. 6b). eGFP+ cells greatly outnumbered Cherry+ cells (7,680 versus 511) and displayed increased Ki-67 positivity and p-S6 levels (Fig. 2j,l,m and Extended Data Fig. 6c,d). Despite their expansion, only 0.47% of total eGFP+ cells was incorporated into arteries, compared with 23% of Cherry+ cells (Fig. 2j). Overall, mutant endothelial cells were detected in only 16% of arteries containing recombined cells (Fig. 2n). Co-staining for eGFP, Cherry and ERG confirmed that lesions consisted exclusively of mutant cells (Extended Data Fig. 6e).

In contrast to Esm1+ cells expressing Pik3caH1047R from the endogenous locus, which did not induce arterial pathology (Fig. 2c,e), all eGFP+ cells that reached arteries in the iFluMosaic-PIK3CAH1047R model formed small lesions (averaging nine cells per lesion) (Fig. 2k). We therefore tested whether forced overexpression of PIK3CAH1047R in arterial endothelial cells would produce a similar phenotype. To this end, we combined iFluMosaic-PIK3CAH1047R with Bmx(PAC)-creERT2 and induced recombination between P4 and P6, or between P7 and P9 (Extended Data Fig. 6f–i). Five days later, the expected proportions of Cherry+ and eGFP+ cells were detected (Extended Data Fig. 6h). Mutant arterial cells remained non-pathogenic after late recombination; however, targeting at P4–6 resulted in mild arterial overgrowth in around 20% of arteries containing recombined mutant cells (Extended Data Fig. 6f–i). Across models, arterial width inversely correlated with endothelial differentiation state at the time of PIK3CAH1047R expression (Extended Data Fig. 6i). eGFP+ arterial endothelial cells showed increased PI3K signalling activation compared with neighbouring Cherry+ cells under all conditions (Extended Data Fig. 6g,j). By contrast, endogenous Pik3caH1047R expression did not increase arterial p-S6 levels (Figs. 1d,f,i,k and 2e,g). Nonetheless, eGFP+ arterial cells still lacked NR2F2 expression (Extended Data Fig. 6k), consistent with maintained arterial identity. Together, these data indicate that arterial resistance to PIK3CAH1047R depends on endothelial differentiation state and mutant expression level. Forced overexpression partially overcomes this resistance at earlier developmental stages, promoting mild arterial overgrowth without inducing NR2F2 expression.

To investigate how Pik3caH1047R impairs arterial mobilization, we analysed endothelial cell transcriptomes using single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis. We profiled endothelial cells from tEC-GFP (6,564 cells) and tEC-GFP-Pik3caH1047R (19,168 cells) retinas, enabling molecular tracing of Esm1+ tip cells and their progeny (Fig. 3a). We first defined endothelial transcriptional states in WT retinas by clustering scRNA-seq data from tEC-GFP retinas. Unsupervised analysis identified nine populations: tip, pre-arterial angiogenic, metabolic angiogenic, proliferative 1, proliferative 2, venous, capillary, arterial capillary and arterial endothelial cells (Extended Data Fig. 7a–c and Supplementary Data 1). Notably, hierarchical clustering based on average gene expression profiles grouped tip, proliferative and metabolic angiogenic populations together, whereas pre-arterial angiogenic endothelial cells were more similar to arterial and arterial-capillary populations, supporting their arterial bias (Extended Data Fig. 7d). Traced cells were identified by GFP transcripts (Extended Data Fig. 7e). Projection of GFP expression onto the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) revealed Esm1-traced cells in all clusters except for the venous population (Extended Data Fig. 7f–h). Approximately half of Esm1-traced cells localized to arterial populations, whereas around 20% remained proliferative, indicating that Esm1-derived endothelial cells normally retain proliferative capacity while progressively differentiating into the arterial lineage (Extended Data Fig. 7h).

We next examined whether Pik3caH1047R affects this developmental trajectory. To address this, we jointly analysed WT and Pik3caH1047R endothelial cells. Integrated analysis resolved seven endothelial populations: tip (C1), metabolic angiogenic (C2), proliferative 1 (C3), proliferative 2 (C4), venous (C5), arterial capillary (C6) and arterial (C7) endothelial cells (Fig. 3b,c, Extended Data Fig. 7i and Supplementary Data 1). GFP expression revealed a marked redistribution of Esm1-traced cells (Fig. 3d and Extended Data Fig. 7j,k). Whereas WT GFP+ cells predominantly localized to arterial capillary and arterial clusters (C6 and C7), Pik3caH1047R GFP+ cells were instead enriched within angiogenic, proliferative and venous clusters (C2, C3, C4 and C5) (Fig. 3d,e and Extended Data Fig. 7k). Quantification confirmed selective expansion of mutant GFP+ cells in proliferative, angiogenic and venous clusters, but not arterial clusters (Fig. 3e and Extended Data Fig. 7k). Importantly, Pik3ca expression was comparable across all endothelial clusters (Extended Data Fig. 7l).

To determine whether this redistribution also reflected altered endothelial transcriptional identity, we performed cluster-specific differential expression analysis comparing GFP+ WT and GFP+ Pik3caH1047R endothelial populations. We identified transcriptional alterations between WT and mutant cells in all clusters, with only limited changes in arterial cells (C7), consistent with the resistance observed in differentiated arterial endothelial cells (Extended Data Fig. 7m). The tip cluster (C1) contained too few cells for robust differential expression analysis (Extended Data Fig. 7k). Affected clusters showed a consistent shift towards venous-associated gene expression, accompanied by reduced tip, arterial, guidance and capillary-specialization programmes (Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 7n). This pattern remained after correcting for differences in cluster size (Supplementary Fig. 1). Hierarchical clustering further showed the greatest genotype-dependent divergence in angiogenic and venous populations (Extended Data Fig. 7o). We next performed RNA velocity analysis on subclustered GFP+ cells, which revealed altered cell-state dynamics in Esm1-traced mutant endothelial cells (Extended Data Fig. 7p,q and Supplementary Data 1). Whereas WT GFP+ cells displayed coherent trajectories toward capillary and arterial states, mutant cells showed altered cell-state trajectories, with prominent progression toward proliferative and venous-associated populations and reduced progression toward the arterial lineage (Extended Data Fig. 7r). Together, these findings indicate that Pik3caH1047R promotes endothelial proliferation while diverting differentiating endothelial cells away from the arterial programme towards venous-associated cell states.

To determine whether this programme is initiated rapidly after Pik3caH1047R expression, we analysed cultured sister endothelial cells derived from Pik3caH1047RPdgfb-creERT2 mice using scRNA-seq (Extended Data Fig. 8a,b and Supplementary Data 2). Endothelial identity across clusters was confirmed by uniform Pecam1 expression, and Pik3ca expression was comparable between clusters (Extended Data Fig. 8c,d). Acute Pik3caH1047R expression recapitulated the in vivo transcriptional changes, with expansion of proliferative and venous-like populations and reduction of tip/arterial endothelial cells (Extended Data Fig. 8e–g). Mutant cells also expressed higher levels of venous markers (that is, Nr2f2 and Nrp2) and lower levels of tip markers (that is, Esm1 and Angpt2) (Extended Data Fig. 8h,i). These data indicate that Pik3caH1047R rapidly and cell-autonomously initiates a programme of proliferation and loss of arterial identity.

We next examined how this transcriptional transition unfolds during arterial differentiation in vivo by staining tEC-GFP and tEC-GFP-Pik3caH1047R retinas at P4 and P6 for UNC5B and NR2F2. Control GFP+ cells expressed UNC5B by P4 while remaining NR2F2 negative at both timepoints (Fig. 3g–j and Extended Data Fig. 8j–l). Mutant GFP+ cells were similar to controls at P4 but, by P6, showed reduced UNC5B expression at the sprouting front and near arteries, along with ectopic NR2F2 expression (Fig. 3g–j and Extended Data Fig. 8j–n). These findings validate the scRNA-seq data and demonstrate that Pik3caH1047R progressively disrupts arterial differentiation in vivo.

To establish the temporal relationship between hyperproliferation and arterial differentiation defects, we examined the proliferative behaviour of Esm1-traced cells at P4 and P6. EdU incorporation showed that Esm1-traced cells normally retain proliferative capacity at P4, with proliferation beginning to diminish by P6 (Fig. 3k–m). Pik3caH1047R mutant Esm1-traced cells exhibited the same temporal pattern, although proliferation remained markedly higher at both timepoints, indicating that hyperproliferation precedes ectopic NR2F2 induction (Fig. 3k–m). The absence of a further increase in proliferation after NR2F2 induction at P6 argues against NR2F2 driving endothelial hyperproliferation.

To test whether hyperproliferation accounts for defective arterial differentiation, we treated tEC-GFP and tEC-GFP-Pik3caH1047R mice with the CDK4 and CDK6 inhibitor palbociclib. Palbociclib partially reduced endothelial proliferation and vascular hyperplasia in tEC-GFP-Pik3caH1047R retinas but did not restore arterial differentiation or suppress NR2F2 expression (Fig. 4a–h). Similar results were obtained with mitomycin C, which robustly inhibited endothelial expansion without restoring arterial incorporation or reducing NR2F2 expression in mutant GFP+ cells (Extended Data Fig. 9a–i). Consistently, palbociclib inhibited cell cycle progression in vitro but did not prevent Pik3caH1047R-driven NR2F2 upregulation (Fig. 4i–k and Extended Data Fig. 9j–m). Together, these findings show that suppressing proliferation does not prevent NR2F2 induction or restore arterial differentiation, supporting distinct effects of Pik3caH1047R signalling on proliferation and arterial specification. Consistent with this interpretation, scRNA-seq analysis revealed no association between Nr2f2 expression and cell cycle status in mutant GFP+ cells (Extended Data Fig. 10a).

As Pik3caH1047R induces NR2F2 expression in pre-arterial endothelial cells, and NR2F2 suppresses arterial identity23,24,25,26, we hypothesized that NR2F2 mediates the arterial differentiation defect downstream of Pik3caH1047R. To test this hypothesis, we crossed the Nr2f2flox/flox allele with tEC-GFP-Pik3caH1047R mice (tip-cell-specific Nr2f2 deletion; hereafter, tEC-GFP-Pik3caH1047RNr2f2ΔEC/ΔEC mice) and optimized 4-OHT dosing to maximize recombination of both alleles (Fig. 5a). NR2F2 expression was efficiently reduced in tEC-GFP-Pik3caH1047RNr2f2ΔEC/ΔEC retinas (Extended Data Fig. 10b,c). Deletion of Nr2f2 alone neither impaired mobilization of Esm1-traced Nr2f2ΔEC/ΔEC-GFP+ cells into arteries nor altered vascular density (Fig. 5b–d and Extended Data Fig. 10d). Importantly, Nr2f2 deletion restored the ability of Pik3caH1047R cells to contribute to arteries (Fig. 5b,c), whereas endothelial hyperproliferation and vascular expansion remained essentially unchanged compared with tEC-GFP-Pik3caH1047R mice (Fig. 5d–f and Extended Data Fig. 10d).

We confirmed these findings using the complementary tEC-iFluMosaic-PIK3CAH1047R clonal tracing model (Extended Data Fig. 11a–i). Whereas Cherry+ and Cherry+ Nr2f2ΔEC/ΔEC endothelial cells contributed similarly to arteries, eGFP-PIK3CAH1047RNr2f2ΔEC/ΔEC cells were detected in 35% of arteries, compared with 10% for eGFP-PIK3CAH1047R cells (Extended Data Fig. 11c,e). Moreover, most eGFP-PIK3CAH1047RNr2f2ΔEC/ΔEC cells that reached arteries did not form lesions, whereas eGFP-PIK3CAH1047R cells consistently generated small arterial lesions (Extended Data Fig. 11c,f). Nevertheless, eGFP-PIK3CAH1047RNr2f2ΔEC/ΔEC cells continued to overexpand within the capillary plexus (Extended Data Fig. 11g–i). Together, these findings demonstrate that NR2F2 mediates the PIK3CA-driven block in arterial differentiation but is dispensable for mutant cell hyperproliferation in non-arterial vessels.

To investigate how PIK3CAH1047R activation induces NR2F2 expression, we analysed endothelial cells in culture. Nr2f2 transcript levels increased within 24 h after 4-OHT-induced recombination and remained elevated thereafter (Extended Data Fig. 12a). NR2F2 protein levels increased by 72 h (Extended Data Fig. 12b–d). To test whether Nr2f2 induction depends on PI3K signalling, endothelial cells were treated with the PI3Kα-selective inhibitor alpelisib (BYL719). PI3Kα inhibition reduced Nr2f2 mRNA and protein levels in a dose-dependent manner, accompanied by reduced p-AKT levels (Extended Data Fig. 12e–h). Similarly, alpelisib reduced NR2F2 transcript and protein levels in patient-derived endothelial cells with activating PIK3CA mutations (Extended Data Fig. 12i,j).

To investigate the transcriptional mechanisms underlying NR2F2 induction, we focused on FOXO transcription factors, major downstream effectors of PI3K signalling7,32, with FOXO1 the predominant family member in endothelial cells33. PI3K–AKT signalling inhibits FOXO1 through phosphorylation-dependent nuclear exclusion7,32 and, consistent with this, FOXO1 was excluded from the nucleus in vascular lesions of Pik3caH1047R mutant mice (Extended Data Fig. 12k,l). We next examined whether FOXO1 directly regulates NR2F2. Analysis of published RNA-seq, assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC–seq), FOXO1 chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP–seq) and H3K27ac ChIP–seq datasets from human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) expressing constitutively nuclear, PI3K-resistant FOXO1(A3)34,35 revealed sustained repression of NR2F2 transcripts and protein, reduced chromatin accessibility at the NR2F2 locus and FOXO1 binding to a vein-specific NR2F2 enhancer (Fig. 5g and Extended Data Fig. 12m–v). FOXO1(A3) increased occupancy at this enhancer while reducing H3K27 acetylation and chromatin accessibility, consistent with direct transcriptional repression (Extended Data Fig. 12p).

Conversely, endothelial deletion of Foxo1 markedly increased Nr2f2 transcript and protein levels (Fig. 5h,i). Finally, induction of PIK3CAH1047R in HUVECs increased NR2F2 expression, whereas the PI3K-resistant FOXO1(A3) suppressed this response (Fig. 5j). Together, these findings establish FOXO1 as a transcriptional repressor of NR2F2 and define a PI3K–FOXO1–NR2F2 axis through which oncogenic PIK3CA disrupts arterial differentiation.

Using the vasculature as a model, we show that the consequences of PIK3CAH1047R are dictated by endothelial cell context. Depending on the differentiation state of the affected endothelial cell, the mutation promotes pathological proliferation, remains phenotypically silent or disrupts differentiation (Extended Data Fig. 13). This context-dependent model provides a mechanistic explanation for the marked predominance of capillary and venous lesions, and the rarity of arterial lesions, in PIK3CA-driven vascular disease. More broadly, it may also help to explain why PIK3CA-associated overgrowth preferentially affects mesoderm-derived tissues and suggests that similar principles probably apply to other pathogenic PIK3CA variants, which exhibit comparable lesion distributions in patients3.

Genetic lineage tracing revealed that endothelial cells expressing endogenous Pik3caH1047R persist in mature arteries without eliciting a detectable phenotype. Consistently, embryonic expression of Pik3caH1047R in Tie2+ cells does not give rise to arterial lesions32. This finding is unexpected given the broad endothelial expression of Pik3ca and the prominent role of PI3Kα signalling in all endothelial cells33. However, basal PI3K signalling is comparatively low in arteries and is not increased by endogenous Pik3caH1047R expression. By contrast, forced overexpression, enhances PI3K signalling and induces mild overgrowth in arterial endothelial cells that have not yet completed differentiation, suggesting that these cells require higher signalling levels to mount a pathological response than venous or capillary endothelial cells. Nevertheless, increased PI3K signalling does not induce overgrowth or NR2F2 expression in fully differentiated arteries. Likewise, reducing shear stress or Notch signalling, key pathways in the acquisition and maintenance of arterial identity30,34,35, does not restore responsiveness to Pik3caH1047R. These findings suggest that arterial endothelial cells possess multiple layers of protection that may also underlie their broader resistance to other mutations36. Future studies are needed to determine how microenvironmental cues, including extracellular matrix composition and mural cell interactions, contribute to this resistance.

We also find that PIK3CAH1047R disrupts arterial differentiation at early developmental stages. During angiogenesis, a subset of tip cells acquires a pre-arterial identity by expressing canonical arterial markers (such as CXCR4, UNC5B, HEY1 and DLL4)30,37,38 and migrates retrogradely into nascent arteries to support arterial expansion. Pik3caH1047R disrupts this transition by inducing an early hyperproliferation response followed by Nr2f2 upregulation and loss of arterial identity. Supporting a causal role for NR2F2, Nr2f2 deletion restored the ability of Pik3caH1047R tip cells to contribute to arteries, consistent with previous studies demonstrating that NR2F2 antagonizes pre-arterial specification26. More broadly, our findings align with evidence that PI3K signalling promotes venous specification39,40, suppresses arterial differentiation41 and favours a stemness state42. Similar effects have been reported in other tissues, where PIK3CAH1047R biases ageing oesophageal progenitor cells towards self-renewal and induces dedifferentiation during mammary tumorigenesis14,15. In parallel, we show that proliferation blockade in Pik3caH1047R-expressing pre-arterial cells limits lesion formation, but neither reduces NR2F2 expression nor restores arterial contribution of mutant cells. These findings indicate that impaired arterial differentiation cannot be explained solely by proliferative exclusion43,44. Conversely, NR2F2 depletion did not block lesion growth or endothelial proliferation, despite previous reports implicating NR2F2 in cell cycle regulation25,45. Together, these findings indicate that PIK3CAH1047R-driven endothelial proliferation and the NR2F2-mediated arterial differentiation defect are functionally separable downstream outputs.

Our findings identify a transcriptional mechanism linking PI3K activation to NR2F2 induction. Previous studies showed that TIE2–PI3K signalling stabilizes NR2F2 protein during venous specification39 and that NR2F2 is elevated in PI3K-driven prostate tumours46. We show that PI3K signalling also promotes NR2F2 transcript and protein expression by inhibiting FOXO1. This is consistent with the established role of FOXO factors as nuclear effectors of PI3K signalling, including in PIK3CA-related vascular malformations47, and with the vascular overgrowth caused by endothelial FOXO1 loss48. Notably, FOXO1 has been reported to promote NR2F2 transcript and protein expression in pancreatic β cells49, whereas our data identify FOXO1 as a direct NR2F2 repressor in endothelial cells. Although the molecular basis of this difference remains to be determined, it is consistent with the context-dependent transcriptional activity of FOXO factors and NR2F2 regulation50,51 and may reflect differences in enhancer use and FOXO1 occupancy. Given that additional transcription factors regulate endothelial NR2F2 expression52,53,54, defining how they cooperate with or compete with FOXO1 will help to explain this context-dependent regulation.

Together, our findings show that the pathological consequences of oncogenic PIK3CA activation depend not only on signalling strength but also on developmental state and endothelial identity. Differentiated arterial cells resist pathological overgrowth, whereas pre-arterial cells undergo an NR2F2-mediated differentiation defect alongside increased proliferation. These functionally separable responses help to explain the vessel-type specificity of PIK3CA-driven vascular malformations.

All images shown in the figures are maximum intensity projections. Images were taken from at least three retinal areas in each retina and are representative of at least four retinas analysed for each genotype unless otherwise stated. The identification of retinal vascular lesions and their locations was performed by observing IB4 in ×10 images. Vascular malformations were defined based on abnormal vessel morphology and GFP+ clonal expansion. In capillary beds, lesions were identified as focal regions displaying abnormal vessel enlargement compared with the surrounding capillary plexus and associated clusters of GFP+ endothelial cells. In veins and arteries, malformations were defined by the presence of GFP+ endothelial cell clusters indicative of clonal expansion, together with abnormal vessel enlargement compared with control vessels. Retinal vascularity was measured using the IB4 channel by adjusting the threshold to select IB4+, followed by quantification of the percentage of IB4+ area relative to the total retinal surface. Vessel width was determined by measuring the average diameter at three different points along each vessel. GFP+ cell expansion was quantified by calculating the percentage of GFP+ area within the IB4+ area. For capillary analysis, images containing only capillary beds were used. To quantify GFP+ cells in veins and arteries, IB4 staining was used to delineate the vascular area, and vessel-specific regions of interest (ROIs) corresponding to arteries or veins were manually selected. For quantification of aEC-GFP+ endothelial cells, the analysed area was defined by a fixed ROI (200 × 550 μm (P6 retinas), 200 × 630 μm (P9 retinas) and 500 × 1,200 μm (P12 retinas)) to maintain consistent arterial length across replicates and to avoid including other vascular types. In P21 retinas, aEC-GFP+ cells were spread across various vessel types, so the ROI for quantification included the entire vascular plexus. To quantify vascular p-S6 intensity, a manual threshold was set to obtain the GFP+ area and define the ROI. The integrated density of p-S6 was then measured within the GFP+ area. In iFluMosaic-PIK3CAH1047R mice, p-S6 integrated density was quantified separately in Cherry+ (WT) or eGFP+ (PIK3CAH1047R) cells after manual delineation of IB4+ areas containing each recombinant cell population. In both cases, the background signal (mean grey values) was measured in adjacent IB4-negative regions. The corrected total fluorescence (CTF) was calculated using the following equation: CTF = integrated density – (vascular area × mean grey background value). αSMA, CX37 and UNC5B expression in endothelial cells was quantified by measuring the mean intensity within GFP+ areas. Endothelial cell proliferation was quantified as the percentage of ERG+ endothelial cell nuclei co-immunostained positive for Ki-67 or EdU relative to the total number of ERG+ nuclei within the GFP+ area. In the iFluMosaic-PIK3CAH1047R model, proliferation was quantified separately for Cherry+ and eGFP+ nuclei as the percentage of Ki-67+ nuclei relative to the total number of Cherry+ or eGFP+ nuclei, respectively. Nuclear FOXO1 positivity was quantified as the percentage of FOXO1+ERG+ nuclei relative to the total number of ERG+ nuclei within the GFP+ area. NR2F2 levels were assessed by manual scoring of nuclear immunofluorescence intensity in ERG+ endothelial cells. Endothelial nuclei with strong and unequivocal nuclear signal were classified as NR2F2high, nuclei with weak but discernible nuclear signal as NR2F2low and nuclei lacking detectable nuclear signal were classified as NR2F2negative, including after a substantial increase in display intensity during image visualization. Scoring was performed using identical acquisition settings across samples. Quantification of Cherry+ and eGFP+ endothelial cells in iFluMosaic-PIK3CAH1047R retinas was performed manually, differentiating their presence in capillaries, veins and arteries. Vascular lesions were classified based on size as small (<10 PIK3CAH1047R cells), medium (between 10 and 35 PIK3CAH1047R cells) or large (>35 PIK3CAH1047R cells) based on manual counting of PIK3CAH1047R mutant cells. The incidence of arteries populated with PIK3CAH1047R cells was determined as a percentage in relation to the total number of arteries containing any recombinant cell. The relative arterial vessel width in iFluMosaic-PIK3CAH1047R retinas was assessed by comparing vessel diameter in a central region containing recombinant cells with adjacent regions of the same vessel lacking recombinant cells.

In cultured endothelial cells, maximum-intensity projections of confocal images were analysed using CellProfiler (v.4.2.6). DAPI, eGFP, Cherry and Ki-67 signals were defined as primary objects. Nuclei were identified based on DAPI staining and used to generate secondary objects for p-S6 quantification. CellProfiler assigned p-S6 intensity to individual nuclei and classified cells as p-S6+ or p-S6−. WT and PIK3CAH1047R endothelial cells were quantified by identifying Cherry+DAPI+ and eGFP+DAPI+ cells, respectively.

FOXO1-bound Nr2f2 enhancer identification was performed according an approach described previously67. In brief, enhancer marks from three published datasets were used to identify putative enhancers: (1) open chromatin as assessed by ATAC–seq in mouse P6 retina endothelial cells (MRECs)68; (2) enriched EP300 binding in Tie2-cre+ cells from embryonic day 11.5 mouse embryos69; and (3) binding of the ETS factor ERG, which marks all known endothelial cell enhancers, in HUVECs70. Putative enhancer regions were then assessed for FOXO1(A3) binding47,65, identifying the FOXO1-bound NR2F2-1345 enhancer. For in vivo validation, the NR2F2-1345 enhancer sequence was cloned into the E1b-GFP-Tol2-Gateway vector71 using the Gene Synthesis service from Genewiz (part of Azenta Life Sciences). F0 mosaic transgenic zebrafish embryos were generated using Tol2-mediated integration72. All animal procedures were approved by a local ethical review committee at Oxford University, licensed by the UK Home Office and conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. All zebrafish (KLC strain) were maintained in groups. Embryos were maintained in E3 medium (5 mM NaCl, 0.17 mM KCl, 0.33 mM CaCl2, 0.33 mM MgSO4) at 28.5 °C. To image, all embryos were dechorionated and anaesthetized with 0.01% tricaine methanesulfonate in E3 medium. For analysis of transgenic zebrafish, single embryos were transferred into a flat-bottom 96-well plate, mounted in 0.1% TopVision low-melting-point agarose (Thermo Fisher Scientific, R0801) in E3 medium with tricaine methanesulfonate (Merck, A5040). GFP reporter gene expression was screened with a Zeiss LSM 980 (Carl Zeiss) confocal microscope at 32–72 h post-fertilization. Whole zebrafish were imaged using the ‘tile scan’ command, combined with z-stack collection, at 488 nm excitation and 510 nm emission for eGFP.

To target tip endothelial cells, recombination was induced with 25 mg kg−1 4-OHT at P1 and P2 in Esm1(BAC)-creERT2R26-mTmG (control) and Esm1(BAC)-creERT2R26-mTmG;Pik3caH1047R (mutant) mice. Retinas were collected at P6 and processed fresh for enzymatic dissociation using the Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit (P) (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-092-628). After dissociation, the cell suspension was incubated with CD31 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-097-418) and subjected to two consecutive rounds of magnetic separation using MS columns (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-042-201) and an OctoMACS Separator (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-042-109). Cells were resuspended in 0.04% BSA in PBS and processed for scRNA-seq using the 10x Genomics platform. Two independent experiments were performed on separate days using independent cohorts of control and mutant mice, with retinas pooled per genotype (Exp1: n = 5 control, n = 3 mutant; Exp2: n = 4 control, n = 4 mutant).

Mouse lung endothelial cells isolated from Pdgfb-creERT2Pik3caH1047R/+ mice were treated in vitro overnight with 2 μM 4-OHT to induce recombination and expression of Pik3caH1047R, while ethanol-treated cells were used as a control (WT cells for Pik3ca). The medium was then changed to F12 complete, and cells were collected 48 h post-induction. Cells were detached with trypsin, centrifuged, subjected to debris removal (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-109-398) and finally resuspended in 0.04% BSA in PBS. For the scRNA-seq, three independent biological replicates were pooled for each treatment condition.

Single cells were encapsulated into emulsion droplets using the Chromium Controller (10x Genomics) with the 3′ Next GEM kit (v3 chemistry) or the Chromium GEM-X Single Cell 3′ Kit v4 for in vitro and in vivo samples, respectively. Libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol, targeting a recovery of around 10,000 cells per sample. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq X 25B platform using 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger (v.7.0.1 or v.8.0.1) and aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10) using the default parameters. For the in vivo datasets, raw UMI count matrices were corrected for ambient RNA contamination using CellBender (v.0.2.1)73. This approach models background RNA from empty droplets and infers corrected expression counts for cell-containing droplets using a probabilistic framework. Corrected matrices were used for downstream analyses.

All scRNA-seq datasets (in vivo and in vitro) were processed using a unified analytical pipeline in R (v.4.2.2) with the Seurat package (v.4.9.9)74. CellBender output count matrices were imported using a custom function designed to read H5AD files in R. Each sample was initially processed independently. tEC-GFP and tEC-Pik3caH1047R samples were initially analysed separately to prevent the pronounced transcriptional changes associated with the mutation from dominating the global data structure and obscuring the physiological organization of the tEC-GFP retina. After independent quality control, dimensionality reduction and clustering, endothelial cells were identified within each genotype based on canonical marker expression. The endothelial subsets from tEC-GFP and tEC-Pik3caH1047R samples were subsequently combined and jointly reanalysed to enable direct comparison of endothelial states across genotypes while retaining an accurate representation of the WT retinal vasculature. Within each condition, control filtering was applied to remove low-quality cells and uninformative genes. Cells were excluded if they contained fewer than 250 detected genes, more than 20% mitochondrial transcripts or exhibited a complexity index greater than 0.8. Genes with zero counts across all cells were removed. Potential doublets were estimated using DoubletFinder (v.2.0.3) on a per-sample basis75. Although doublets were not removed from the dataset, they were excluded from downstream differential expression analyses. Data normalization was performed using Seurat’s NormaliseData function with the LogNormalise method and a scale factor of 10,000. Highly variable genes (HVGs) were defined based on the distribution of non-zero gene expression counts, selecting a threshold corresponding to the third quartile plus 100 genes. Data were then scaled using ScaleData, and then a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using RunPCA. To correct for batch effects across biological replicates, datasets were integrated using the Harmony algorithm (v.1.2.3)76, applied to PCA embeddings. The sample identity (ID) was used as the batch variable, enabling alignment of cells across replicates while preserving biological variability. Harmony-corrected principal components were used for downstream analyses. Graph-based clustering was performed by constructing a shared-nearest-neighbour graph using FindNeighbours, followed by community detection with the Louvain algorithm implemented in FindClusters. To specifically analyse the vascular compartment, endothelial cells were identified based on canonical marker expression (Pecam1 and Cdh5) and computationally subsetted. A secondary, endothelial-focused analysis pipeline was then applied, including re-identification of highly variable features, normalization, scaling, PCA and Harmony integration76. Multiple clustering resolutions (0.1, 0.3 and 0.5) were evaluated, with a resolution of 0.3 selected as it best recapitulated known endothelial cell subpopulations (for example, tip, arterial, venous and proliferative endothelial cells). Cluster annotation was performed using a one-versus-all differential expression approach with Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function, applying the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Bonferroni correction for multiple testing.

In the in vivo retinal endothelial cell dataset, GFP expression was used to identify recombined endothelial cells. To enable GFP quantification, the GFP coding sequence was appended to the standard mouse reference genome (mm10), and raw sequencing reads were aligned against this modified reference using 10x Genomics Cell Ranger v.8.0.1. This allowed simultaneous quantification of endogenous transcripts and GFP expression. To identify GFP-expressing cells, GFP transcript levels were examined across the dataset. Density plots of log-normalized GFP expression revealed a bimodal distribution, which enabled the application of a stringer classification threshold (log-normalized expression > 1.5). Cells with log-normalized GFP expression of greater than 1.5 were classified as GFP+, whereas cells with expression at or below this threshold were classified as GFP−.

Differential expression analysis was performed by comparing mutant (GFP+ Pik3caH1047R) and WT (GFP+ WT) cells within defined endothelial cell populations using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in Seurat (FindMarkers). To enhance robustness and account for sparsity inherent to single-cell data, a complementary pseudobulk approach was applied. Raw counts were aggregated at the sample level (grouped by ID and phenotype) using AggregateExpression, treating each mouse as an independent biological replicate. The resulting count matrix was analysed using DESeq2 (v.1.38.3)77, applying a generalized linear model based on the negative binomial distribution. Concordance between single-cell and pseudobulk approaches was assessed by correlating log2[FC] values across clusters and evaluating overlap of significantly regulated genes (adjusted P < 0.05). A high level of agreement in both magnitude and direction of gene regulation was observed. Based on this consistency, Wilcoxon-derived results were used for downstream visualization and functional analyses due to their higher sensitivity, while pseudobulk results served as an independent validation of biological signal.

Average expression profiles were calculated for each endothelial population using Seurat’s AverageExpression function and HVG. The 1,500 most variable genes were scaled by gene and hierarchically clustered using Pearson correlation distance and average linkage. Analyses included either all endothelial cells from tEC-GFP retinas, irrespective of GFP status, or only traced GFP+ endothelial cells from tEC-GFP and tEC-Pik3caH1047R retinas. In the latter, populations were additionally stratified by genotype. Heat maps were generated using ComplexHeatmap (v.2.15.4).

To control for potential biases from unequal cell numbers between conditions (GFP+ Pik3caH1047R versus GFP+ WT), we implemented a bootstrapped downsampling strategy. For each endothelial cell subtype, 1,000 iterations of random subsampling were performed. In each iteration, the condition with more cells was downsampled to match the size of the smaller group, thereby generating balanced datasets. Differential expression was recalculated in each iteration using the Wilcoxon test (FindMarkers, with logfc.threshold = 0 and min.pct = 0). For each gene, the frequency of significance (adjusted P < 0.05) and the consistency of log2[FC] direction were recorded across iterations. This approach confirmed that the identified transcriptional signatures were stable and reproducible, supporting their interpretation as robust biological features of the mutant phenotype rather than artefacts driven by differences in cell numbers.

The expression of Nr2f2 and proliferation scores across GFP+ WT and GFP+ Pik3caH1047R endothelial cells were estimated. Cell cycle and proliferation scores (the sum of S and G2/M phases) were computed using ccAFv278. Biological thresholds were established: the Nr2f2 threshold was set at the 99th percentile of reference WT arterial and artery capillary cells, and proliferation status used a cut-off of 0.1. Cells were categorized into Nr2f2 status, proliferation status and a joint status (double high, Nr2f2 high, proliferation high or low/basal).

RNA velocity was inferred with scVelo in GFP+ endothelial cells and further analysed using CellRank v.2. GFP+ WT and GFP+ Pik3caH1047R cells were analysed independently using identical parameters. For each genotype, 2,000 highly variable genes were selected, and genotype-specific neighbourhood graphs and expression moments were calculated using 30 principal components and 15 neighbours. Transcriptional dynamics and velocities were estimated using the dynamical model. CellRank was subsequently used to construct a velocity-based transition matrix using the VelocityKernel and to identify macrostates using GPCCA. Velocity vectors were projected onto the shared integrated UMAP and visualized as streamlines and cell-level arrows.

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad). Data are presented as the mean ± s.e.m. For in vivo experiments, each retina was considered an independent experimental unit. At least three mice from at least two independent litters were used per condition, except for in Extended Data Fig. 11i (n = 2). For the iFluMosaic-PIK3CAH1047R model, only retinas containing identified recombined cells were included. Experiments using mouse endothelial cells included biological replicates, whereas those using human endothelial cells were performed as independent experimental repeats. Sample sizes are indicated in the figure legends. Statistical significance between two groups was assessed using either an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests or the two-tailed Mann–Whitney U-tests. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test or two-way ANOVA with Šídák’s test was used. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample sizes; sample sizes were based on previous experiments. Reproducibility was confirmed through multiple independent experiments. The experiments were not randomized, and the investigators were not blinded during the experiments or data analysis.
