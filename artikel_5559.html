NYC pied-a-terre tax notices get an appeals court green light

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

A state appeals court dismissed a homeowners’ lawsuit that had disrupted NYC’s pied-a-terre tax notice rollout, finding no standing or injury. A separate challenge by Steve Wynn and Wilbur Ross to the underlying state law remains pending.  AI Summary

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s administration won another round on the pied-à-terre tax Wednesday, when a state appeals court dismissed a homeowners’ lawsuit that had upended the surcharge’s rollout.

Appeals judges threw out an earlier Staten Island judge’s ruling ordering the city to cancel about 17,000 notices. They found the homeowners lacked standing and weren’t injured by the notices. All four judges agreed.

The surcharge is a pillar in the city’s plan to close a $12 billion budget gap. State officials projected it would raise about $500 million a year. The comptroller estimates the impact at closer to $1 billion before exemptions and appeals.

Wednesday’s decision does not directly affect a separate suit by billionaire casino magnate Steve Wynn, former Trump administration Commerce Secretary Wilbur Ross and his wife. They have challenged the state law behind the tax. That case remains pending in Suffolk County, with hearings scheduled in December.

A Staten Island judge had ruled Sept. 29 that the city skipped individualized decisions before mailing notices. The judge called the notices arbitrary and capricious and a due process violation.

Under the order, the Department of Finance was to cancel the notices and pull its roll from the agency website. New notices were allowed after an individualized review. Within hours, the city appealed and invoked a stay, so implementation continued.

Residential brokers told HousingWire TBD that the legal back-and-forth and the tax itself have had little impact on the owner-occupied market.

Ian Slater, co-founder and CEO of Trove Partners, said the market for under $5 million slowed in September and October. “We hit a record number of contract signatures above $5 million in September and October,” he said.

Wealth from a soaring stock market, plus nerves about its froth, is pushing buyers into top-end property, Slater said. Those buyers are “fully ignoring the pied-à-terre tax.” Some want second homes in New York City, he said.

However, listings of such properties remain scarce. Jessica Chestler, co-founder of The Chestler Jacobs Team at Douglas Elliman, said bidding wars have erupted. Supply isn’t keeping pace with buyer demand, including for homes priced above $5 million.

“We recently represented a buyer in a bidding war that we ultimately won, but it required going $1 million over asking on an apartment that needed a complete gut renovation,” Chestler said.

Because the homeowners lacked standing, the court never ruled on whether the NYC Department of Finance’s rollout was lawful. The court said other arguments were either without merit or unnecessary to resolve.

Finance later used 2025 tax data to revise its determinations, the court noted. Several hundred owners learned they did not owe the surcharge. The surcharge covers only non-primary residences. Through July 2028, the measure impacts houses worth $5 million or more. Co-ops and condos face a $1 million threshold.

Wynn and the Rosses sued in late September. They argue the surcharge is an illegal property tax that discriminates against nonresidents. Wynn’s notice totaled $183,094.69, while the Rosses’ notice came to $83,531.52.

Trove Partners’ Slater does not expect the climate to change. “Political winds are clearly against the wealthy class,” he said.

He doubts judges or politicians will move away from the tax. “It’s definitely an annoyance, but it’s a bit of much ado about nothing,” he said.
