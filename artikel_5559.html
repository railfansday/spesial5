Social Security COLA, tax break could narrow spending gap for older consumers in 2027

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

A Bank of America Institute report finds baby boomer and traditionalist households have weaker discretionary spending growth than other generations despite large wealth gains among the richest older households. Because many retirees rely on fixed income sources and benefit less from recent wage growth, their budgets have been under pressure. A temporary federal tax deduction for those 65 and older and a 3.6% Social Security cost-of-living adjustment for 2027 could modestly improve real incomes, particularly if inflation cools as forecast.   AI Summary

The spending growth of older consumers has trailed other age groups for the past year despite sizable wealth gains at the top. But a 2027 cost-of-living adjustment for Social Security recipients and temporary tax break could modestly improve their purchasing power, according to a Bank of America Institute report released last week.

Bank of America aggregated credit and debit card data to show that baby boomer and traditionalist households have posted weaker discretionary spending growth than other Americans for roughly a year. That marks a reversal from 2022 and 2023, when older households were outpacing others. Boomers were defined as those born between 1946 and 1964, while traditionalists are those born before 1946.

The report ties part of this slowdown to how wealth is distributed within the older cohorts. Using 2025 data from the Federal Reserve Survey of Consumer Finances, the bank found that about two-thirds of net wealth among households headed by someone older than 65 is held by the top 10% of these households. This concentration means that stock market and asset gains may do little to support spending for most older consumers.

Overall consumer spending strength in 2026 appears to be driven primarily by wage and salary growth, particularly among lower- and middle-income working households. Older households, by contrast, depend on a broader mix of income sources and are less exposed to accelerating wages.

Fed survey data shows that as households age, a smaller share of income comes from wages or self-employment and more from pensions, Social Security, and interest, dividends or rental income. Bank of America’s internal data highlight the resulting divergence within older cohorts: Households over 62 that rely only on Social Security have average income and discretionary spending close to half that of their peers who drawing from Social Security, pensions and employment combined.

Discretionary spending is also more uneven across income tiers for older generations than for all households. Retirees are the least likely group to expect their finances to improve over the next six months, according to the study, reflecting the challenges of living on largely fixed income sources.

COLAs are based on the year-over-year change in the third quarter for the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers (CPI-W). While these increases are designed to preserve purchasing power, they can be more or less generous depending on the inflation path in the year that follows.

In 2022, for example, inflation slowed sharply after a large COLA took effect, effectively boosting real benefits and helping older generations outspend other age cohorts in 2022 and 2023.

Consensus forecasts cited in the Bank of America report anticipate that overall inflation will cool in 2027, largely as the impact of higher gasoline prices fades. If that occurs, the 3.6% COLA could outpace actual price increases next year, delivering a more meaningful real income gain for households heavily reliant on Social Security.

The survey data shows that the largest spending growth gaps between older generations and all households are concentrated in discretionary categories like electronics, lodging, restaurants, clothing, furniture and airlines. In many of these areas, older cohorts’ year-over-year spending growth lags the overall population by several percentage points.

Some of this pattern likely reflects differences in preferences and life stages. But if Social Security benefits rise faster than prices in 2027 and the tax deduction continues to support middle-income seniors, the report suggests there may be room for older households’ discretionary spending growth to narrow the gap with other age groups.
