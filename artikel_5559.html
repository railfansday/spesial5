Pregnancy is the ‘perfect storm’ for eating disorders, but doctors keep missing them

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Reporting for this story was supported by the Pulitzer Center.

REISTERSTOWN, Md. — Katye Jorgensen had been fighting anorexia and bulimia for years when she got pregnant at 25. Weigh-ins at prenatal appointments filled her with dread and certain foods terrified her, but she powered through for her baby’s sake. Then she struggled to breastfeed her son and lose weight after giving birth, and her mental health deteriorated.

After her second pregnancy two years later, Jorgensen became obsessive about exercise and fixated on comments about how well she was “bouncing back.” At her husband’s urging, she started seeing a therapist and a dietitian. Eventually, they staged an intervention and she agreed to enter intensive treatment, which she said saved her life.

“I went to my postpartum appointment, I mentioned a lot of this, and even though I really liked those doctors, it still didn’t set off any red alarms for anybody,” said Jorgensen, who is now 37.

Jorgensen is one of many women who struggle with eating disorders before and after childbirth but slip through the cracks of the U.S. health care system. Research suggests about 4.3% of pregnant women and more than 9% of new mothers experience eating disorders such as anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, or binge eating disorder, though research is limited and no one knows their exact toll.

Those rates are on par with national averages: an estimated 2.6% to 8.4% of women will experience an eating disorder over their lifetimes — that’s up to 14.6 million women and girls in the United States.

Eating disorders have been linked to health issues for pregnant women — including miscarriage, high blood pressure, anemia, and bleeding — and complications for their developing babies, including small head circumference, low birthweight, autism, and asthma. After birth, women with a history of eating disorders appear more likely to experience postpartum depression and anxiety and to report problems feeding their newborns.

The risks vary by eating disorder. Studies don’t prove that eating disorders directly cause these issues and not everyone with a perinatal eating disorder will experience them. Still, the evidence is enough to worry clinicians.

“If we don’t treat these disorders … there are risks involved there. This isn’t a neutral situation,” said Leah Graves, a registered dietitian and a clinical adviser at the National Eating Disorders Association.

Few screening tools have been validated for eating disorders in pregnancy, and none are widely used. Obstetricians typically receive very little training about these conditions, leaving them unequipped to identify or manage eating disorders. And even with a diagnosis, many pregnant women and new mothers don’t receive timely treatment due to spotty insurance coverage and a shortage of local specialists, according to research and interviews with more than a dozen eating disorder researchers, doctors, dietitians, therapists, policy advocates, and mothers.

“It’s been a huge gap,” said Elizabeth Claydon, an associate professor at West Virginia University who researches eating disorders. “People have had this; they’ve lived with eating disorders during pregnancy. We just haven’t caught them and been able to help.”

Eating disorders usually develop in adolescence or young adulthood, but they can also emerge around pregnancy, menopause, or other periods of profound physical and life change.

Several factors can raise the risks in pregnancy: the feeling of a lack of control, rapid physical and hormonal changes, negative body image, scrutiny of the pregnant person’s diet and weight for medical reasons, the need to consume extra calories to breastfeed, a new identity and family role, and societal pressure to return to pre-pregnancy weight and succeed at motherhood.

Many women struggle with body image issues or unhealthy thoughts or behaviors around food, weight, or exercise that don’t meet the criteria for a clinical diagnosis. Pregnancy can tip the scales into an active eating disorder.

Pregnancy can also hide symptoms that overlap with eating disorders, such as nausea, vomiting, appetite changes or pumping breast milk specifically to burn calories, said Christine Call, an assistant professor of psychiatry at the University of Pittsburgh.

But many women hide their symptoms, making it difficult for doctors, or even family members, to know when someone needs help.

There’s no single trajectory in the perinatal period. Some women with eating disorders fare better during pregnancy and sustain that recovery, while others relapse once their child is born. Other women, years into recovery, see their eating disorders come roaring back in pregnancy or postpartum. Some struggle throughout pregnancy, with no respite. And others develop an eating disorder for the first time in the perinatal period.

“There’s so much shame and guilt about eating and doing things right, taking care of yourself for your baby during this period,” Call said.

Jennifer Kreatsoulas was diagnosed with anorexia 15 years before her first pregnancy, but her eating disorder “came back with a vengeance” after her second child was born, alongside postpartum depression. She decided to seek treatment when she realized she was too weak to push a stroller while out on a Mother’s Day walk with her husband and 1-year-old daughter.

“It’s destructive, but I was really trying to cope, and my brain just switched back to what it knew how to do from all those years previous,” said Kreatsoulas, now 50 and living in Pennsylvania. She attributes her relapse to “hormones, lack of sleep, complete overwhelm, your identity becoming something that you don’t know — you lose yourself in it, just because that’s what’s demanded as a mother.”

In a recommendation buried in a 2019 opinion on obesity bias, the ethics committee of the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists suggests that OB-GYNs assess all their patients, pregnant or not, for eating disorders. And it recommends that all pregnant and postpartum patients be screened for anxiety and depression — as does the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force.

But ACOG’s clinical guidance on perinatal mental health doesn’t cover eating disorders, and the organization has no guidance on how to screen for or manage them during pregnancy and after birth.

“Providers don’t see it because we’re not looking for it,” said Danielle Prentice, a maternal-fetal medicine specialist at Oregon Health & Science University and a member of ACOG’s nutrition expert group.

Some states have expanded maternal mental health screening in recent years, but without a clinical standard on perinatal eating disorders, policymakers have had little to go on. In Massachusetts — where Lindsay Clancy’s high-profile murder trial drew public outrage over the inadequacy of postpartum mental health care in the U.S. — state officials recently said they would increase screening and support for depression, anxiety, bipolar spectrum disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, and postpartum psychosis.

Eating disorders were excluded because the state followed ACOG’s published guidance to decide which perinatal mental health conditions to include, according to a spokesperson for the Massachusetts Executive Office of Health and Human Services.

“Providers who have concerns about a patient’s eating behaviors, nutrition, body image, weight-related behaviors, or history of an eating disorder should use their clinical judgment and conduct or refer for further assessment as appropriate,” the spokesperson said via email.

Existing eating disorder screening tools may not be suitable for pregnant or postpartum patients. The most common one, known as the SCOFF (“Sick-Control-One Stone-Fat-Food”), has five questions, including: “Have you recently lost more than 14 pounds in a 3-month period?”

Researchers have also warned that eating disorder screening tools tend to be centered on white body ideals, suggesting they may miss women of color and feed into biases about who is at risk. Studies indicate Black women experience eating disorders, particularly binge eating, at rates similar to white women, but are less likely to be diagnosed or treated. Eating disorder experts say the “SWAG” stereotype — skinny, white, affluent girl — of eating disorder patients prevents clinicians from recognizing eating disorders in anyone who doesn’t fit that mold.

“People of color [and] trans people are not seen as having an eating disorder as an option, especially in pregnancy,” said Jessica Wilson, a registered dietitian in California.

Ideally, West Virginia University’s Claydon said, screening for eating disorders should become a standard part of perinatal health care. If the screener flags a potential eating disorder or worrisome symptoms, the patient could be referred to an expert for a final diagnosis.

Yet persuading obstetricians to adopt another screening tool could be a challenge. Screening at the six-week postpartum checkup can feel like a perfunctory exercise, though maternal mental health issues can emerge or linger much longer.

“They give you the questionnaire, which is really more like, ‘Do you want to hurt yourself or somebody else? No? OK, cool, bye.’ That’s it,” Jorgensen said.

Screening may identify a patient in need of support, but that doesn’t mean resources or specialists are available.

“Universal screening is only effective if there’s treatment,” said Marianna Thomeczek, a researcher at the University of Kansas who is developing a postpartum screening tool for Black women.

Women with an active or recent eating disorder can have complex medical needs during pregnancy, Prentice said. They may need supplementation to address nutritional deficiencies, preconception folic acid to support fetal development and prevent birth defects, and extra ultrasounds to monitor the baby’s growth, she said.

Pregnancy may also prompt women to seek eating disorder treatment, which typically calls for therapy paired with care from a doctor and dietitian, according to the American Psychiatric Association. It ranges from regular appointments to intensive day programs, residential treatment, or hospitalization, depending on how sick a patient is and her specific condition.

Health coverage can be a major barrier. Many private insurers cover visits with dietitians for patients with eating disorders, but dietitians say claims are often denied, and plans may limit the number of visits or require copays patients can’t afford, especially while preparing for a new baby. Specialized eating disorder dietitians may be out-of-network.

To get more intensive care, women often have to prove medical necessity, said Ashley Moser, a clinical education specialist at The Renfrew Center, one of the country’s oldest eating disorder treatment facilities. Pregnancy can help make the case, but it’s still difficult.

Access to care is even more difficult for low-income women who rely on Medicaid. Only 22 state Medicaid programs allow registered dietitian-nutritionists to bill independently for adult medical nutrition therapy. And while there are 384 eating disorder centers offering intensive treatment in the U.S., only about 25% accept Medicaid coverage, according to a study published earlier this year. Five states have no treatment centers at all.

There’s very little evidence about what works to prevent eating disorders or their relapse during pregnancy. Researchers in Massachusetts are currently testing whether peer group support and cognitive dissonance exercises can help, modeled on a longstanding program for teenage girls and young women called The Body Project. Early results, published this month, are promising, but bigger studies are needed to prove it actually works.

Pregnant women can also ask their doctors to adjust routine practices they find triggering. During Jorgensen’s third and final pregnancy, she pushed for blind weigh-ins and asked doctors not to relay her blood sugar levels unless there was a problem.

Overall, clinicians, researchers, and mothers said that as maternal mental health receives more national attention, perinatal eating disorders should as well. Partners and families should know the risks, they said, to help ensure women feel safe speaking up rather than suffering alone.

“We need to change the system, we need to change the policies, but women also need to know that they’re not alone in experiencing this,” Claydon said. “It’s not an unusual thing to occur during pregnancy, because it is that perfect storm.”
