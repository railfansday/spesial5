Here’s how the midterms could reset Washington’s housing agenda

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Housing affordability is expected to be a top issue for the next Congress. Lawmakers may pursue a ROAD 2.0 package and funding for ROAD programs, while clashing with the Trump administration on tariffs, immigration, and affordable housing spending.  AI Summary

The rising cost of living was a catalyst that drove voters to the polls this week, so housing, which makes up a substantial share of that burden, could gain traction as a top priority once again for the incoming Congress.

Lawmakers in both parties want to address the high cost of housing. The question always is how much they can accomplish. Democrats, who will control the U.S. Senate and the U.S. House of Representatives, may seek common ground with the Trump administration on housing policy, including streamlining regulations and funding the new programs created under the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act.

However, they could also clash with the administration over hot-button policy debates such as tariffs on building materials, immigration enforcement on construction sites and funding for affordable housing programs, among other issues.

Despite potential areas of agreement and disagreement, Andy Winkler, managing director of housing and infrastructure policy at the Bipartisan Policy Center, said voters’ focus on affordability should keep housing high on the federal policy agenda.

“Housing costs, just like other rising costs of living, were clearly top of mind as voters headed to the polls. So, it appears to me that housing would continue to be a priority in the next Congress because of that,” Winkler said in an interview with HousingWire TBD.

Many housing advocates have pointed to the possibility of a “ROAD 2.0” bill as a follow-up to the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, which became law on July 11 after securing overwhelming bipartisan support, with 85 votes in the Senate and 358 votes in the House of Representatives.

Winkler sees significant potential for another bipartisan housing package, despite potential divisions between Congress and the Trump administration. While the original legislation drew on about 60 different bills, many other bipartisan proposals failed to make the final package and could be revisited in the next Congress.

In October, the bipartisan Build America Caucus released a document previewing policy priorities that could shape a second housing bill. The bipartisan agenda included housing proposals aimed at expanding where homes can be built, lowering construction costs and improving access to financing.

The document’s focus points include establishing federally incentivized “Right to Build Zones” with by-right approvals, encouraging housing near transit, opening underused federal properties to residential development, modernizing fragmented building codes, and establishing a federal revolving loan fund for housing projects, among other proposals.

Congress will also welcome an unusually large freshman class, including many Democrats elected in Republican-leaning states and districts. As of Thursday, Democrats had already flipped seven Senate seats, including five in states Trump won by double-digit margins in 2024. Democrats may also pick up still-undecided Senate seats in Mississippi and Alaska, both of which Trump won handily in 2024.

Elections in the U.S. House of Representatives followed a similar pattern, with many Democrats winning in districts that Trump carried handily two years ago. Representing Republican-leaning constituencies could push many of these new members toward a bipartisan agenda, and housing offers a rare opportunity for common ground.

“I think you have a lot of new members coming in who will want to quickly show that they’re being productive and addressing what they ran on,” Winkler said, striking an optimistic tone. “The passage of the 21st Century Road to Housing Act in this Congress shows us that there is common ground on housing policy, and we now also have a model for how to get legislation enacted into law.”

The new Congress will be sworn in on January 3. Until then, Congress will be in a “lame duck” period. Ken Wingert, Chief Advocacy Officer at the National Association of Home Builders (NAHB), wrote in a post-election analysis that Congress will likely begin debating the bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA) during the lame duck session, offering a look at another potential federal housing bill.

BAAJA would streamline federal permitting and environmental reviews, lower land development costs and cut down on project delays that prolong the time it takes to secure federal approvals. NAHB argues the reforms could bring more affordable lots to market, accelerate multifamily development and lower costs for projects using federal housing financing and grants.

For his part, James Talarico, the Senator-elect from Texas, signaled during a Wednesday interview on Fox News that he is willing to work with Trump on several issues, including housing affordability.

“I also want to work with the President on housing. He’s a builder, and we need to cut the red tape, the regulations that sometimes my party has been guilty of, so that we can build millions of new homes in this country,” Talarico said.

Trump has also voiced a conciliatory tone since the election, signaling that he is open to working with a Democratic-controlled Congress on bipartisan legislation. Whether that holds true on housing or other critical areas remains to be seen.

“Democrats are expected to focus on increased funding for affordable housing programs, a greater emphasis on workforce training, and limits on President Trump’s tariff and immigration policies that have impacted new construction. At the same time, the Trump administration is expected to continue to pursue an agenda of deregulation that will help spur new housing supply,” Wingert wrote.

Winkler argued that federal funding and budget negotiations could become a major source of both bipartisan agreement and conflict in the next Congress, even if lawmakers can find common ground on housing policy itself.

The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act requires separate congressional appropriations to fund many of about a dozen newly enacted programs. Examples include the USDA rural housing preservation and rental assistance initiatives. Because of ROAD’s popularity, Congress could widely support appropriating funding for these programs.

“None of [the programs] have funding attached, so it will be a clear priority heading into next year if they want any of those grant programs to exist and actually work in practice,” Winkler explained.

However, disagreements over how to fund affordable housing programs and broader federal spending priorities could create fissures between Democrats and Republicans. Democrats will likely prioritize more funding for affordable housing and homelessness programs. They may also pursue oversight of the administration’s spending, particularly whether the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) and other agencies are actually distributing housing grant money that Congress has already appropriated.

Winkler also expects scrutiny of HUD’s staffing reductions, which have reportedly cut the agency’s workforce by roughly 30%. The loss of experienced HUD employees could undermine the department’s ability to implement the ROAD to Housing Act’s numerous provisions. However, Congress has limited ability to force the administration to fund and hire more employees, and any legislation would likely still need presidential support.

Democratic leadership has already signaled that they will attempt to take on Trump’s tariff agenda once they assume power in January. It’s unclear, though, how successful that effort will be and if Trump will leverage executive powers to keep certain tariffs in place.

However, Democrats in Congress have already introduced multiple bills to exempt building materials from tariffs in a bid to lower housing costs, including the Housing Tariff Exclusion Act that was introduced earlier this year. NAHB threw its support behind that legislation.

Democrats will likely push for a similar policy of tariff exemptions over the next two years. And, for what it’s worth, the Build America Caucus, which has 21 Republican members, has called for exempting building materials from tariffs as part of its housing policy agenda. If a similar provision is included in a comprehensive follow-up housing bill, it could create a potential policy clash with the Trump administration.

Other policy issues, such as the impact of immigration enforcement on the construction labor pool or a renewed push to expand an already-passed institutional investor ban on purchasing single-family homes, could also create friction between Democrats and Republicans in Congress, and the Trump administration.

Still, bipartisan support is possible in certain areas, like streamlining regulations and funding the programs created by ROAD. However, even where bipartisan support exists, Democratic leadership will, in most cases, need the administration’s backing to turn its priorities into law.

“The president has the veto, so legislation moving forward does need buy-in from everybody,” Winkler said.
