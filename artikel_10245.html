The Bambu Lab P3C is the lower-cost little brother of the H3C. It has the same type of tools and outstanding TPU printing process as its larger sibling, but drops the heated chamber, filament dryer, and automatic filament loading. If you want an upgradable tool changer on a more manageable budget, the P3C is a great place to start.

Bambu Lab didn’t even tease the arrival of the new P3C, a smaller, more budget-friendly version of the H3C, which retails at roughly half the price. In fact, this post might be the first time you’ve heard more than just a rumor about the tool changer’s mysterious little brother.

The Bambu Lab P3C combo is a tool changer with room for seven tools and a 256 x 256 x 260mm build volume, roughly the same as the P2S. It ships as either a five-tool unit, with two empty slots for later customization, or as a seven-tool “multi-material combo” with six 1.75mm tools and one 2.85mm tool. Bambu is bringing back old school 2.85mm filament in the form of super squishy TPU 75A.

The five-tool unit is priced at $1,149, while the seven-tool is launching at $1,299.

It offers the same excellent TPU printing process for both standard and phat TPU and has quickly joined the select group of send it and forget it printers in my fleet. I’ve had very few failures, even when printing more technical filaments.

The P3C is not quite as tall as the H3C, but it does require almost 4ft (1,212 mm) of vertical space for the AMS lid to fully open. So plan accordingly and have a good space picked out in advance.

Bambu’s most annoying cost-saving measure took the A right out of AMS. There’s no motor of any sort in the AMS 3P, leaving you to manually load and unload the filament all the way to the nozzle. The process takes a long time if you are loading all of the filament slots. The upside of not having powered rollers is that the AMS 3P will run almost any 1kg or smaller spool without issue.

The printer can run different-sized nozzles from .2 mm to .8 mm, and has an optional 2.85 mm TPU filament tool. The FlexFeed extruder with Active Grip Control just pushes whatever filament it’s asked to while changing on the fly. It may be the best extruder I’ve reviewed, however, the printer as a whole is not quite what we need for the best 3D printers of 2026.

The Bambu Lab P3C comes with five .4mm tools preinstalled in the bottom of the AMS 3P. Also included are a toolkit and cables needed to assemble the printer, along with quick start guides for both the printer and AMS 3P, and a few spare parts. It does not come with a USB memory stick, which is needed for recording time lapses.

We received the multi-material combo for review, which came with five tools installed, plus two additional tools I had to set up myself (one 1.75mm and one 2.85mm), along with several spools of PLA Palette and 2.85mm TPU 75A.

The P3C is missing several perks of the bigger H3C in order to save costs and keep the price reasonable. The most disappointing is the lack of a motor to drive the filament spools. This makes the “A” in the AMS 3P questionable, as you are forced to manually feed the filament all the way to the nozzle, as well as handle retraction when unloading.

The AMS 3P also does not come with a filament drier, although an optional 75C dryer will be available for $159.90.

The basic Bambu Lab P3C comes standard with only five tools and is expandable to seven. The Swaptek system keeps the cooling fans and extruder on the toolhead, with each tool having its own filament path, heater, and a hardened steel nozzle. When printing, the idle tools are preheated, allowing for extremely fast tool swaps.

The nozzles on each tool can be swapped out with different sizes, and multiple sizes can be used in the same print.

Bambu Lab will also release tools for cutting, drawing, debossing, and foil transfer in the future.

The Bambu Lab P3C supports printing TPU with a bypass port on all filament slots except 2 and 6, which allows TPU to skip the tube, reducing friction. If you have the optional 2.85mm tool, you can mount it to any slot with a bypass.

Please note that the 2.85mm tool has a wider filament path – it's not just the nozzle tip that changes dimensions.

The tools dock on a rack in front of the printer which retracts after each tool change, allowing for a clear view of the build plate.

The Flexfeed extruder is impressive. It switches from 1.75mm to 2.85mm filament on the fly and uses what Bambu Lab calls Active Grip Control with feedback from the extruder motor.

Like the H3C, there are magnetic covers on the left and right sides of the AMS and a front cover that attaches in slots to allow easy access to the chamber for maintenance. The front cover is a bit tricky, and you need to lift the AMS up to get it free. For this reason, I didn’t bother to secure the AMS to the base of the printer. You really need to remove that front panel to change out the tools.

Changing tools is one of the few frustrating things about the Bambu Lab P3C (or the H3C, as it’s the same process). There is very little room to work, and there are sharp plastic edges that left me bloodied on one occasion. I would suggest picking your favorite configuration and leaving it there if you can.

The AMS is massive and requires a lot of additional vertical space to fully open the lid to get at the spools. Bambu Lab recommends a clearance of 1,212 mm or almost four feet from the base in order to open the lid. Surprisingly, this smaller P3C is only 3.58 inches shorter than the big H3C. With the printer sitting on a standard countertop, a step-stool will be mandatory equipment for most people. Bambu offers a rolling cart for the H3C that puts the printer at a more manageable height, and I would highly recommend getting it for the P3C.

The base unit of the Bambu Lab P3C looks similar to the P and X series printers, with a plastic outer frame and a steel chassis. The build plate is a standard 256 x 256 mm, with 260 mm available on the Z-axis. The P3C does not have active chamber heating, but being enclosed with a max of 300C on the nozzle and 110C on the bed, it has the ability to print many of the more common technical filaments. The chamber is ventilated with HEPA and active carbon filtration.

The 3D printer has a great camera, which is used for AI detection and monitoring using the Bambu Handy app and the device page of Bambu Slicer. The camera can produce time-lapses of your prints but does require a USB memory stick to store them.

The Bambu Lab P3C and AMS 3P ship in separate boxes but are almost fully assembled. After removing the packaging, there are two screws holding the dock retainers that need to be removed and 6 screws securing the bed in place.

There is a helpful reminder to do this when the printer is first turned on. AMS 3P is mounted on top of the printer and requires a good deal of vertical space. There are four screws to attach the AMS 3P to the printer, but I left these off since the front panel of the AMS is easier to remove when you can tip the AMS back a little.

The individual tools attach to the dock magnetically and must be placed manually, which can take a little fiddling to get used to.

The touch screen attaches to the front of the printer without tools, and the ribbon cable to connect it is just long enough to work.

The Bambu Lab P3C levels its bed as part of its initial calibration and again at the start of every print. The system works perfectly.

The Bambu Lab P3C does not have auto-loading, as there is no motor.

When loading multiple new filaments for a print, the process can take a while and is very hands-on. When using normal 1.75 mm filament, the material is pushed into the orange tube in the AMS when called for by the printer.

When using TPU, either 1.75mm or 2.85 filament, drop the filament into a slot with a bypass port in the bottom and drag it by hand to the tool. The normal Bowden tube must be removed from the tool and clipped to the side. When running 1.75mm TPU a short piece of bowden tube must be inserted into the tool to help feed the filament correctly. The 2.85mm TPU doesn’t need the short tube and can feed directly.

The printer has a helpful filament guide to walk you through the loading operation.

Bambu Lab-branded filament with RFID tags will automatically enter the filament type and color. Other brands must be entered manually via the printer’s touchscreen.

Unloading filament is the reverse of loading. You’ll need to manually retract material once it is cut by the printer. There is a batch unloading command that will allow you to wait until all of the selected filaments have been cut.

Bambu Studio is based on PrusaSlicer and its predecessors. New for the P3C and the H3C is the ability to mix different tool sizes. It is one of the best slicers available with good filament mapping, control and print monitoring, with access to Makerworld, Bambu Lab’s file sharing service.

The P3C does not come with filament samples. Bambu Lab sent a few spools of their new PLA Palette and 2.85mm 75A TPU to try. Be sure to check out our best filaments for 3D printing to fill up your AMS 3P.

This print of Carl and Donut from the Dungeon Crawler Carl book series took 15 hours and 35 minutes to print using a .16mm layer height for smooth detail. I used the default settings, but slowed the outer walls to 65mm/s. The print is exceptionally good, though the supports left a few marks under horizontal areas like the arms and shin. Normally I’d tilt a model this back 45 degrees to save time and support, but the fin on this particular model is vexing and would have added an hour to the print time and another 10g of support.

This is printed in seven colors of PLA, maxing out the P3C slots. Polymaker HT-PLA Pro White, Prusament Galaxy Black and Galaxy Silver PLA, Elegoo PLA Brown and Silk Red, Inland Bone White, and Matterhacker’s Purple MH Build Series PLA.

Printing with seven colors allows you more creative freedom when printing complex models. Though the brown did a lot of heavy lifting on this print, I think the P3C really captured Carl nicely.

I tried some of Bambu Lab’s matte PETG on a Cute Blue Jay from Makerworld. Since the model prints best straight up and down, and requires pretty extensive supports, I used a scrap of Prusa Lipstick Red PLA as an interface layer.

Using a .16mm layer height, the print took 8 hours and 55 minutes running at a little under 100mm/s on average. The matte PETG makes the model look like it was painted. This is printed in black, white and baby blue matte PETG and the supports came off with no residue what so ever.

Though the P3C doesn’t get hot enough to print PPS, it does print most PA, PC, and PET filaments very well. I printed a spool and a half of Sunlu Easy PA, making all of the mechanical parts for two radio-controlled Death Racers. Initially, I made just a few items per plate, but with just a little glue stick, the bed adhesion was so good that I felt comfortable loading up the plates as full as possible and had zero failures. All were printed with a .2mm layer height and slowed to 100mm/s by me in the slicer. Other than a tiny bit of stringing, the parts are perfect mechanically.

The Bambu Lab P3C’s HEPA filter does a good job filtering the stinkier filaments like ABS and ASA. I printed this wire holding vice using Fusion ABS matte ionized cobalt black and Prusa lipstick red ASA.

This is printed with a .2 mm layer height and an average print speed of 120mm/s. The entire print took a little less than 2 hours and 3 minutes, and the parts fit together perfectly.

With the H3C and now the P3C, I have seen the future of printing with TPU. The AMS 3P bypass ports are just the ticket for printing soft materials.

I printed several plates of treads for my RC Death Racer project. Normally, I print these a few at a time to limit the fallout from print failures, but this proved unnecessary. I ran 48 treads in Bambu Lab Cyan 2.85mm TPU 75A and another 48 using Bambu Lab 90A TPU. Both prints used a .2mm layer height and were limited to 100 mm/s in the slicer. The 75A print finished a little quicker at 19 hours 10 minutes, with the 90A filament taking 19 hours and 40 minutes.

Both are terrific prints with no stringing at all and zero failures.

The P3C can print almost any filament I own beautifully. It lacks a few creature comforts like auto-loading, filament drying (which can be upgraded), and a heated chamber. It’s also tall enough to require a step stool to load those back filament slots.

The Bambu Lab P3C is best for anyone who wants poop-free multicolor, multi-material printing without paying H3C money. I highly recommend it for anyone who plans to print a lot of TPU and doesn’t need that larger build plate. If you can live without the automatic feeding you normally get from a Bambu printer, then the money you’ll save in filament is worth the hassle.

This machine gives you most of the advantages of the Bambu Lab H3C at roughly half the $2,499 price tag. The open-source four-color Snapmaker U1 is still worth it right now while it’s on sale for $699. Another option is the Creality K3, which is another four-color tool changer with a similar build size for $849.
