TDP-43 nuclear depletion is a defining pathological feature of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and frontotemporal dementia that induces widespread cryptic splicing defects in hundreds of transcripts1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8. Despite this, whether TDP-43 loss affects synaptic function has remained unclear. Here we show that TDP-43 loss induces a predegenerative state in which neurons remain structurally intact but suffer a substantial breakdown in synaptic communication. Genetic correction of a single cryptic splicing event in UNC13A was sufficient to rescue these deficits. We developed splice-switching antisense oligonucleotides that correct UNC13A cryptic splicing in human cortical and spinal motor neurons and in a humanized mouse model. In human neurons this intervention restored spontaneous and evoked synaptic vesicle release, release probability at individual synapses and wider network synchrony. Together, this establishes UNC13A splice correction as a mechanistically defined and genetically validated therapeutic strategy relevant to roughly 97% of patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and roughly 50% of patients with frontotemporal dementia with TDP-43 pathology.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a rapidly progressive and fatal neurodegenerative condition. Recently, antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting SOD1 and FUS have shown early promise in clinical trials9,10, but these approaches only benefit patients with SOD1 and FUS mutations—less than 3% of patients with ALS—leaving an urgent need for therapies for the remaining roughly 97% of patients with ALS. A common feature of all non-FUS and non-SOD1 ALS is the aggregation of TAR DNA binding protein-43 (TDP-43) in neurons1,2, a finding also observed in roughly 50% of both frontotemporal dementia (FTD) and Alzheimer’s disease2,11. However, no therapies that directly target TDP-43 are available, leaving the underlying nuclear loss of function (LoF) unaddressed. TDP-43 is an RNA-binding protein that is normally located in the nucleus and has crucial roles in the regulation of RNA splicing12,13. Cytoplasmic mislocalization of TDP-43 induces a nuclear LoF resulting in cryptic exon inclusion, cryptic alternative polyadenylation and/or exon skipping in some mature messenger RNA (mRNA) transcripts3. These events occur in hundreds of transcripts, including genes with crucial neuronal functions, such as STMN2, UNC13A and KCNQ2 (refs. 4,5,6,7,8). Whereas the impact of STMN2 mis-splicing on axonal regeneration and KCNQ2 exon skipping on intrinsic excitability has been studied, the direct impact of TDP-43 LoF on synaptic function has not been addressed.

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have shown that single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) located within and proximal to a cryptic exon in UNC13A increase the risk of developing ALS and accelerate disease progression14,15,16. Under normal physiological conditions, these risk SNPs do not induce inclusion of the UNC13A cryptic exon, but after TDP-43 LoF, risk SNPs exacerbate cryptic exon inclusion leading to increased loss of functional RNA transcripts and protein6,7. UNC13A is a presynaptic protein that is required at the active zone to properly dock and prime synaptic vesicles for fusion with the plasma membrane17. Genetic deletion of Unc13a in mice leads to a roughly 90% reduction in spontaneous and evoked glutamatergic excitatory synaptic transmission18,19,20. Although the UNC13A genetic association strongly implicates this event in ALS pathogenesis, the contribution of TDP-43 LoF to synaptic dysfunction and the role of UNC13A cryptic splicing—particularly in the context of many mis-splicing events in other synaptic transcripts—is unaddressed. Resolving these outstanding questions is crucial for establishing whether correcting UNC13A splicing represents a viable therapeutic approach for restoring normal neuronal function.

Here we report that TDP-43 loss in mature human induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cell-derived neurons induces loss of presynaptic UNC13A and a notable reduction in AMPA (α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazole propionic acid) receptor-mediated synaptic transmission, leading to a loss of coordinated network activity. Despite the widespread roles of TDP-43 in RNA regulation, isolated genomic deletion of the UNC13A cryptic exon rescues these deficits, demonstrating that this single event is a key driver of synapse dysfunction in ALS. To this end, we developed splice-switching ASOs that can rescue UNC13A mRNA splicing, UNC13A protein levels and UNC13A presynaptic localization, and restore both spontaneous and evoked synaptic transmission. Furthermore, we demonstrate that ASO treatment rescues UNC13A cryptic exon inclusion in a humanized mouse model that recreates this human-specific mis-splicing event. Thus, ASOs targeting the UNC13A cryptic exon represent an innovative therapeutic strategy for treating ALS and FTD, and their use for treating sporadic ALS is supported by genetic evidence.

TDP-43 plays important roles in development and therefore, to avoid interfering with neuronal maturation by depleting TDP-43 from the iPS cell stage, we used CRISPR–Cas12 gene editing to generate a cell line in which both endogenous copies of TDP-43 harbour a HaloTag to allow for proteolysis targeting chimera (PROTAC)-mediated21 knockdown of TDP-43 in mature neurons (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 1a–d). TDP-43 is degraded by HaloPROTAC in a dose-dependent manner and in a process blocked by proteasome inhibition or the NEDD8 E1 inhibitor (Extended Data Fig. 1e–g). HaloTag-TDP-43 iPS cells were differentiated into mature excitatory cortical-like neurons (HaloTDP iNeurons) that expressed the layer V excitatory cortical marker CTIP2 and lacked expression of the inhibitory marker NKX2.1 or the neuronal progenitor marker PAX6 (Extended Data Fig. 1h). TDP-43 was also found to be correctly localized to the nucleus (Fig. 1b). Tagging of TDP-43 can alter its function and, whereas the HaloTag partially affected TDP-43 function (Extended Data Fig. 1i), UNC13A cryptic splicing and mRNA downregulation were robustly detected only on HaloPROTAC knockdown of TDP-43 (Fig. 1c,d and Extended Data Fig. 2a), similar to CRISPR interference (CRISPRi)-mediated knockdown (Extended Data Fig. 2b–f). Loss of UNC13A mRNA led to a time-dependent decrease in UNC13A protein whereby less than 5% of protein remained after 10 days of HaloPROTAC treatment (Fig. 1e,f). Immunofluorescence staining revealed a loss of synaptic UNC13A staining at synapsin-positive puncta (Fig. 1g,h). HaloPROTAC-mediated knockdown of TDP-43 in mature neurons did not lead to alterations in the RNA levels of N-methyl-d-aspartate and AMPA receptor subunits as assessed by reverse transcription–quantitative PCR (RT–qPCR) (Extended Data Fig. 2g).

Previous investigations of the physiological functions of UNC13A have been mainly conducted using primary rodent neurons with limited studies in human neurons18,19,20,22. We therefore sought to assess the impact of UNC13A loss in human iPS cell-derived neurons (Extended Data Fig. 2h). Whole-cell patch clamp recordings showed that depletion of UNC13A through CRISPRi impaired synaptic transmission, as evidenced by a reduction in the frequency of miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) (Fig. 1i), consistent with a presynaptic deficit and in line with previously published work19,20. HaloPROTAC-mediated TDP-43 depletion also reduced mEPSC frequency (Fig. 1j), mirroring the presynaptic deficit observed on UNC13A loss. Treatment with 6-cyano-7-nitroquinoxaline-2,3-dione (CNQX) blocked mEPSCs, confirming that synaptic events were mediated primarily by means of fast AMPA receptor-mediated glutamatergic transmission (Extended Data Fig. 3a). Whereas UNC13A depletion had no effect on mEPSC amplitude, consistent with a purely presynaptic deficit (Extended Data Fig. 3b), TDP-43 depletion led to a modest reduction in amplitude (Extended Data Fig. 3c), suggesting extra postsynaptic deficits independent of UNC13A depletion. Passive membrane properties remained comparable between control and UNC13A–TDP-43 depleted neurons (Extended Data Fig. 3d,e).

To investigate the effect of UNC13A and TDP-43 loss on network activity, we carried out multi-electrode array (MEA) recordings. We found that either UNC13A or TDP-43 depletion induced a disordered and asynchronous pattern of network activity, measured by a reduction in the normalized electrode cross-correlations (Fig. 1k–m). This result indicates that UNC13A–TDP-43 depleted neurons fail to support network-level activity. The number of active electrodes remained consistent (Extended Data Fig. 3g) and phase-contrast images showed comparable cell coverage between conditions (Extended Data Fig. 3h), ruling out issues with electrode coverage as a confounding factor. CNQX treatment of control neurons confirmed network synchrony was highly dependent on fast AMPA receptor-mediated synaptic transmission (Extended Data Fig. 3f). Whereas extra MEA metrics were also affected by both TDP-43 and UNC13A loss, the network synchrony deficit was the largest and most consistently observed change (Extended Data Fig. 3g). Thus, we show that TDP-43 depletion substantially impairs fast glutamatergic synaptic transmission and synchronized network activity in human neurons, mirroring a comparable loss of synaptic function and network activity induced by direct UNC13A loss.

Knockdown of TDP-43 leads to cryptic exon inclusion within UNC13A intron 20 along with an independent downstream intron retention event at intron 31 and other lower-frequency splicing abnormalities throughout the UNC13A mRNA transcript6,7. To determine the degree to which protein loss is dependent on the UNC13A intron 20 cryptic exon, we performed CRISPR–Cas9 gene editing to remove the cryptic exon from the genome of SH-SY5Y cells (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b). Using two single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) we made a homozygous deletion of 233 nucleotides (nt) that contain both splice acceptors and the common donor splice site of the cryptic exon. In wild-type (WT) cells containing the full UNC13A gene there was robust induction of UNC13A mis-splicing after TDP-43 depletion as assessed by reverse transcription–PCR (RT–PCR) (Extended Data Fig. 4c,d), whereas in cells with genomic deletion of the UNC13A cryptic exon, no cryptic splicing was detected (Extended Data Fig. 4c,d). Cryptic exon deletion rescued correctly spliced levels of UNC13A mRNA, as determined by RT–qPCR, and led to roughly 75% rescue of UNC13A protein following TDP-43 knockdown (Extended Data Fig. 4e–g). We subsequently deleted the UNC13A cryptic exon in iPS cells (Extended Data Fig. 5a–e) and detected a similar rescue in UNC13A RNA and protein following CRISPRi-mediated knockdown of TDP-43, confirming the relevance of this event in iPS cell-derived neurons (Extended Data Fig. 5f–k). In the UNC13A cryptic exon deletion cell line, we performed further CRISPR–Cas12 gene editing to add a HaloTag in the TARDBP gene to efficiently induce TDP-43 loss after differentiation (Extended Data Fig. 6). Similar to results in SH-SY5Y cells, genomic deletion of the UNC13A cryptic exon in HaloTDP iNeurons rescued UNC13A splicing at the exon 20–21 junction and restored mRNA and presynaptic protein levels (Fig. 2a–h), demonstrating this splicing event is the main driver of TDP-43-dependent UNC13A loss.

TDP-43 knockdown yields synaptic transmission defects similar to those induced by direct depletion of UNC13A. However, TDP-43 has hundreds of validated RNA targets, regulating splicing, stability and transport of transcripts involved in virtually every aspect of neuronal function. Therefore, the reduction in synaptic transmission could reflect a multi-gene insult affecting pathways including synaptic vesicle trafficking, Ca2+ signalling and active zone organization. To determine the extent to which UNC13A cryptic exon inclusion contributes to impaired synaptic function following loss of TDP-43, we performed whole-cell patch clamping experiments in WT and cryptic exon deletion lines. Rescue of this single cryptic exon largely restored synaptic transmission following TDP-43 depletion with an 80% recovery in mEPSC frequency (Fig. 2i,j). mEPSC amplitude was still marginally (roughly 10%) reduced, supporting the conclusion that UNC13A cryptic exon deletion rescues specifically presynaptic function (Extended Data Fig. 7a). Passive membrane properties were similar between conditions (Extended Data Fig. 7b). As mEPSC frequency is influenced by synapse number, we measured excitatory synapse density along the dendrites of iNeurons. We found that TDP-43-knockdown neurons had fewer synapses than controls, a feature that persisted even when the UNC13A cryptic exon was deleted (Extended Data Fig. 7c,d), suggesting a strong recovery in synapse function in the remaining synapses.

Despite the small reduction in synapse number, MEA recordings showed that deletion of the UNC13A cryptic exon in TDP-43-depleted neurons restored synchronous network activity, indicating effective neurotransmitter release and propagation of signalling across the network (Fig. 2k,l and Extended Data Fig. 7e). This represented the most robustly rescued MEA metric (Extended Data Fig. 7f). Taken together, these findings show that correction of a single cryptic exon splicing event (intron 20 UNC13A cryptic exon) is sufficient to restore synaptic transmission and rescue the asynchronous patterns of network activity caused by TDP-43 loss. Active electrodes and cell coverage were comparable across conditions (Extended Data Fig. 7f,g). Likewise, analysis of neuronal survival confirmed that network-level changes were not attributable to changes in viability due to TDP-43 loss (Extended Data Fig. 7h) demonstrating that TDP-43 driven UNC13A loss drives a functional synaptic deficit in otherwise structurally intact, viable neurons.

The rescue of UNC13A levels and restoration of synaptic signalling conferred by deletion of the UNC13A cryptic exon in the context of TDP-43 knockdown highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting this event in patients. We therefore sought to identify ASOs that can induce splice switching similar to those successful in targeting splicing events in SMN2 and STMN2 (refs. 23,24). We first designed locked nucleic acid-modified ASOs that targeted regions covering the cryptic exon acceptor and donor splice sites (Extended Data Fig. 8a). We screened the ASOs by transfection in SK-N-BE(2) cells and assessed their ability to restore UNC13A splicing at the exon 20–21 junction by RT–PCR (Extended Data Fig. 8b) and found that 21-nt ASOs targeting the donor splice site could restore UNC13A splicing. We confirmed splicing rescue and ability to restore UNC13A RNA and UNC13A protein levels in SH-SY5Y cells (Extended Data Fig. 8c–g). To improve therapeutic tolerance, we adapted the best performing ASOs to have full 2′-O-methoxyethyl (MOE) ribose-modified bases and tested their ability to rescue UNC13A splicing and restore protein levels in HaloTDP iNeurons (Fig. 3a–c and Extended Data Fig. 8h). We found these could restore splicing to more than 90% and protein levels to 30–50% of normal levels. Treatment with the M21-4 MOE ASO led to increased presynaptic UNC13A protein expression at synapsin-positive puncta following TDP-43 depletion compared with treatment with the control scrambled ASO (Fig. 3d). To identify ASOs that could increase protein rescue above 50% of normal levels, we carried out a secondary screen by performing an ASO walk along the cryptic donor site with 18-nt MOE-based ASOs (Extended Data Fig. 9a). We evaluated the ability of the 18-nt ASOs to rescue splicing in SH-SY5Y cells by RT–PCR (Extended Data Fig. 9b) and confirmed that the best performing 18-nt ASOs could rescue correct UNC13A mRNA splicing to more than 90%, and protein to more than 50% in SH-SY5Y cells and HaloTDP iNeurons (Fig. 3e,f and Extended Data Fig. 9c–h). In addition, we performed amplicon sequencing, which confirmed the ability of ASOs to rescue both reference and risk alleles in our HaloTDP iNeurons (Extended Data Fig. 9i).

To validate the efficacy of UNC13A ASOs to rescue cryptic splicing in an in vivo model, we generated humanized UNC13A (hUNC13A) reporter mice, in which mouse intron 20 was replaced with human UNC13A intron 20 containing the cryptic exon. TDP-43 knockdown was performed by tail vein injection of an adeno-associated virus encoding a synthetic microRNA targeting the mouse Tardbp gene (Fig. 3g). On the same day, intracerebroventricular injections were performed in hUNC13A reporter mice with the M21-4 MOE ASO or artificial cerebrospinal fluid. Three weeks post-injection, spinal cords were dissected from the mice. RT–PCR analysis from spinal cord tissue following knockdown of TDP-43 showed no cryptic exon inclusion in WT mice and robust cryptic exon inclusion in hUNC13A reporter mice, which was partially rescued by administration of UNC13A splice-correcting ASO (Fig. 3h–k). Overall, these results show that ASOs can reduce inclusion of the UNC13A cryptic exon on TDP-43 knockdown both in vitro and in vivo, and can rescue UNC13A protein expression to above 50% in cultured neurons.

Unc13a heterozygous knockout mice and individuals with heterozygous LoF UNC13A mutations are viable and do not present with abnormal neurophysiology18,25. Recovery of UNC13A expression to WT levels after TDP-43 LoF is probably not required to restore synapse function, in agreement with previous work in primary mouse cultures that showed that clear deficits in synapse function are only observed when expression of Unc13a falls below 30% of normal physiological levels20,25. Indeed, treatment with M18-17 ASO, which rescues UNC13A levels to more than 50% of control, was able to rescue synaptic transmission as measured by an increase in mEPSC frequency (Fig. 4a). We then tested our M21-4 ASO, which rescues UNC13A protein levels to roughly 30% of normal levels. Despite the lower level of protein rescue, treatment with M21-4 ASO also rescued synaptic transmission following TDP-43 depletion as measured by an increase in mEPSC frequency (Fig. 4b). mEPSC amplitude and passive membrane properties were unaffected by treatment with ASOs (Extended Data Fig. 10a–d), suggesting that ASO treatment specifically rescues presynaptic deficits caused by the cryptic exon in UNC13A.

Whereas mEPSC recordings allowed us to detect deficits in spontaneous vesicle release, we also sought to determine whether TDP-43 depletion affected evoked synaptic transmission, the physiological mode that drives circuit function, and whether UNC13A ASOs could be beneficial. We conducted whole-cell voltage clamp recordings to examine evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents (eEPSCs) in autaptic micro-island cultures in which single neurons are grown on astrocytes and form synaptic connections onto themselves26,27. We performed experiments in two different ALS-affected neuronal subtypes: cortical-like excitatory iNeurons expressing the layer V marker CTIP2 and human iPS cell-derived spinal motor neurons expressing Islet-1 and the marker VAChT, performed in two laboratories with different methods for TDP-43 depletion (days in vitro (DIV) 14 PROTAC or DIV45 gapmer ASO knockdown). In both neuronal subtypes, TDP-43 depletion caused a substantial reduction in eEPSC amplitude, which was partially rescued by UNC13A splice-correcting ASOs (Fig. 4c,d and Extended Data Fig. 10e,f). Furthermore, the spontaneous vesicle release phenotype (decrease in mEPSC frequency) and ASO-mediated rescue were also confirmed in the separate spinal motor neuron model (Extended Data Fig. 10g–k). The amplitude of eEPSCs depends on several factors, including presynaptic probability of neurotransmitter release, postsynaptic strength (quantal amplitude) and synapse density. Our previous observations have already described UNC13A-independent decreases in both postsynaptic strength (Extended Data Fig. 7a) and synapse density (Extended Data Fig. 7c,d). Therefore, we decided to directly measure evoked presynaptic release probability at the level of single synapses in conventional iNeuron cultures (not autaptic), using live imaging of a genetically encoded reporter of glutamate release, iGluSNFR3 (ref. 28). TDP-43 depletion led to both a reduction in the number of active synapses as well as a reduction in the release probability of those synapses (Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 11a). UNC13A splice-correcting ASOs did not rescue the number of active synapses (Extended Data Fig. 11a), consistent with the morphological analysis showing UNC13A splice correction also failed to rescue synapse density along dendrites following TDP-43 loss (Extended Data Fig. 7c,d and Extended Data Fig. 11b). However, UNC13A splice correction was associated with improved release probability at the remaining synapses, although this effect narrowly missed statistical significance (P = 0.052) (Fig. 4e).

Next, we tested the effectiveness of UNC13A splice-switching ASOs in rescuing network synchrony in neurons in which we induced TDP-43 depletion at DIV14. MEA recordings showed a small but statistically significant rescue in network synchrony following treatment with M18-17 ASO compared with a scrambled control (Fig. 4f,g and Extended Data Fig. 11c). To better mimic the disease, in which TDP-43 loss occurs in adult neurons, we induced TDP-43 depletion at DIV33, when neurons have already developed mature network activity (Extended Data Fig. 11d,e). As with earlier knockdowns, synchrony was the metric most affected by TDP-43 depletion (Extended Data Fig. 11f). In this case, ASO rescue experiments showed a greater recovery of network synchrony (Fig. 4f,g and Extended Data Fig. 11g). Across all MEA metrics, network synchrony showed the most consistent and pronounced rescue (Extended Data Fig. 11h).

Together, these data demonstrate that therapeutic targeting of the UNC13A cryptic exon using an antisense oligonucleotide can rescue synaptic function: from neurotransmitter release at individual synapses through to emergent network-level function.

In this study, we show that TDP-43 LoF leads to a drastic loss of spontaneous and evoked synaptic transmission, leading to a disordered and asynchronous pattern of network activity in human neurons. Of the many hundreds of widespread cryptic mis-splicing events driven by TDP-43 LoF, we demonstrate that a single cryptic exon in the disease progression modifier gene UNC13A is a main driver of this synaptic breakdown. By designing splice-correcting ASOs targeting the UNC13A cryptic exon, we demonstrate how synaptic function can be restored, supporting UNC13A splice correction as a genetically anchored therapeutic strategy amenable to most patients with ALS and patients with FTD with TDP-43 pathology.

Out of the numerous cryptic splicing events induced by loss of nuclear TDP-43 that affect proteins that are predicted to affect neuronal health and function3,4,5,6,7,8,29, the cryptic exon in intron 20 of the UNC13A gene has attracted considerable interest. Risk SNPs accounting for the second strongest ALS and frontotemporal lobar degeneration with TDP-43 aggregation GWAS signal are located within this intron and enhance cryptic splicing, exacerbating transcript and protein loss6,7,14,15,16,30,31,32. Among patients with ALS, homozygous carriers of UNC13A risk SNPs (rs12608932 and rs12973192) show a 5–12-month reduction in median survival15,16,33,34,35,36. Therefore, genetic data strongly support targeting this event to modify disease progression.

The functional implications of UNC13A cryptic splicing in ALS and/or FTD remain unknown. UNC13A protein is crucial for synaptic vesicle priming; Unc13a-null mice die at birth from respiratory failure, and primary cortical cultures from these mice develop normally but have substantially reduced excitatory synaptic transmission18,19,20. Although ALS-linked mutations in TDP-43 have been shown to cause excitability and synaptic deficits in human neurons37,38,39, these studies did not establish a mechanistic role for cryptic exon inclusion or altered UNC13A protein levels. Therefore, we sought to determine whether direct depletion of TDP-43, modelling TDP-43 LoF in patients with sporadic ALS and associated alterations in RNA splicing also drive impaired synaptic function. Here we demonstrate TDP-43 depletion in iPS cell-derived neurons drives a reduction in both spontaneous and evoked synaptic vesicle release, leading to a failure of network-level signal propagation and asynchronous network activity. These results mirror the LoF we observed following direct UNC13A knockdown, as well as the synaptic defects described in previously published reports of Unc13a depletion in primary rodent neurons18,19,20.

TDP-43 LoF causes widespread mis-splicing in hundreds of transcripts, many of which are involved in synaptic transmission. Therefore, to specifically assess the contribution of the UNC13A cryptic exon to the decreased synaptic activity observed in TDP-43 depleted neurons, we selectively deleted the UNC13A intron 20 cryptic exon in human neurons. We found that rescuing this single mis-splicing event could restore spontaneous and evoked synaptic vesicle release in two independent models (cortical-like iNeurons and spinal motor neurons), and re-establish network synchrony, demonstrating that loss of UNC13A protein is a main driver of synaptic breakdown caused by TDP-43 LoF. Other synaptic genes are also likely to contribute to the broader range of observed phenotypes. For example, we observed changes in mEPSC amplitude on TDP-43 loss in iNeurons that are not recapitulated by direct UNC13A loss, nor rescued by deletion of the UNC13A cryptic exon. Similarly, we observed a reduction in synapse density following TDP-43 knockdown that does not appear to be driven by altered UNC13A protein levels. Further studies, focusing on mis-spliced transcripts whose products either localize to the postsynapse or are involved in synapse turnover40, will explain the functional mechanisms underpinning these changes. Despite this strong deficit in synapse density that is independent of UNC13A, we found that rescuing UNC13A led to a robust recovery in both spontaneous and evoked synaptic transmission as measured across two independent model systems and cell types relevant to ALS and/or FTD (cortical-like iNeurons and spinal motor neurons). Live imaging of individual synapses revealed that, although the number of active synapses was not restored, there was a trend towards higher release probability of these remaining synapses after splice correction, highlighting the importance of vesicle release kinetics at individual synapses in contributing to emergent network-level function.

Data assessing UNC13A protein levels in patient neurons are not available, but transcriptomic data on fluorescence-activated nuclei-sorted postmortem cortex from patients with ALS and/or FTD with TDP-43 pathology show that the UNC13A cryptic exon can be included in more than 90% of UNC13A transcripts within the cell population lacking nuclear TDP-43, which is thus predicted to greatly inhibit production of functional UNC13A protein6,41. These findings support the validity of using iPS cell-derived neuronal models in which TDP-43 loss induces a near-total loss of UNC13A, although models that recapitulate both nuclear loss and cytoplasmic aggregation will be beneficial in future work. Previous electrophysiology studies have demonstrated that the level or activity of UNC13A needs to be reduced below roughly 30% before strong synaptic deficits appear20,25. This agrees with studies using Unc13a-heterozygous mice, which showed no defects in synaptic activity. Our results show that restoring roughly 30% or more of UNC13A protein levels can rescue synaptic transmission. Altogether, this suggests that splice-switching therapeutic rescue of UNC13A to more than 30% in patients with ALS could be beneficial for the recovery of synaptic function.

Although loss of UNC13A effectively leads to synaptic silencing, transcranial magnetic stimulation studies have detected cortical hyperexcitability at an early stage in patients with ALS that worsens with disease progression42 and is associated with poorer survival outcomes43. Although other factors, such as KCNQ2 mis-splicing, may contribute to this hyperexcitability, it is possible that UNC13A loss also plays a role in this. Rare patients with inherited biallelic UNC13A mutations can present with seizures at a young age25,44,45, and one individual with a biallelic truncating mutation in UNC13A developed myasthenia and had cortical hyperexcitability on electroencephalography analysis44. Cortical hyperexcitability has also been shown in individuals with variants in other presynaptic proteins that reduce synaptic vesicle release through LoF or dominant-negative mechanisms, such as STX1B (syntaxin-1B)46, STXBP1 (Munc18-1)47 and SNAP25B (ref. 48). The impaired excitatory synaptic output driven by UNC13A mis-splicing could drive hyperexcitability through more complex circuit alterations, such as the reduction in microcircuit feedforward inhibition and an overall skew of the excitatory-to-inhibitory ratio49. Our experimental system consisted exclusively of excitatory neurons, in which impaired synaptic transmission manifested as a loss of network synchrony, rather than hyperactivity. Future studies should incorporate inhibitory interneurons to more precisely assess how TDP-43 loss and UNC13A dysfunction affect network dynamics. The humanized mouse model described here could provide a valuable platform for assessing network changes in an in vivo setting.

Our experiments were carried out in populations of neurons that had a homogeneous loss of TDP-43 and in which its presynaptic and postsynaptic effects are inevitably compounded. As such, our experimental setup represents a more extreme condition than the mosaic pattern of TDP-43 loss seen in patients with ALS, in which affected neurons can still project onto unaffected neurons. It is conceivable that UNC13A-mediated presynaptic release rescue could be sufficient to restore normal synaptic signalling in connections in which the postsynaptic neuron remains unaffected, and future studies in more complex models will help resolve how circuits are affected by mosaic UNC13A mis-splicing.

The cellular events that lead to TDP-43 aggregation in ALS are unknown and it is therefore challenging to correct this directly, but aggregation is associated with a loss of TDP-43 nuclear function that produces numerous cryptic splicing events, which may represent more tractable therapeutic targets than targeting mislocalization directly. Splice-modifying ASOs have been successfully developed to treat spinal muscular atrophy by correcting SMN2 splicing23,50. This same strategy can be used to correct cryptic splicing in ALS and has the advantage of being able to restore physiological transcript levels, without the risk of overexpression. Yet there is still a need to identify splicing events that have the strongest impact on neuronal function and hold the most promise of improving disease symptoms. Our findings, along with results from GWAS suggesting that modulation of UNC13A cryptic exon inclusion affects disease progression15, strongly support UNC13A as a key candidate for splice-modifying therapy development in ALS. TDP-43 LoF drives sufficient UNC13A reduction to impair synaptic transmission and network synchrony.

We show prevention of UNC13A mis-splicing by means of CRISPR–Cas9 gene editing or application of ASOs can rescue UNC13A protein expression and restore defects at many layers of complexity: from single-synapse release probability to spontaneous and evoked synaptic transmission and emergent network synchrony. This points to a stage of TDP-43-linked neurodegeneration in which neurons lose the ability to contribute to meaningful synaptic communication before overt neuronal death. This functional death provides a unique therapeutic opportunity: a pool of structurally intact neurons unable to contribute to normal network function that are recoverable through splice-correcting ASOs targeting a single TDP-43 dependent cryptic exon in UNC13A. Notably, therapies targeting UNC13A splicing would be amenable to most patients with ALS and/or FTD and TDP-43 pathology that is supported by clear genetic evidence and a clearly defined molecular and functional mechanism.
