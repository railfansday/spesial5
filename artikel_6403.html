A new telescope in Chile found two more stable Neptune trojans in just its first three weeks

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Rubin Observatory's test survey spotted 2025 MH348 and 2025 NN80, doubling the known stable Neptune trojans in the trailing cloud from two to four.

On June 21, 2025, the camera of the Vera C. Rubin Observatory picked up two faint moving objects. A new test survey on Cerro Pachón in Chile had begun just one day earlier.

One of them got the designation 2025 MH348. It orbits the Sun at about 30 times Earth’s distance. It also belongs to the trailing group of Neptune trojans, small icy bodies that ride behind the planet.

Meg Schwamb of Queen’s University Belfast and 14 co-authors now report two confirmed members of that group and one candidate.

Before this, only two long-lived trailing trojans were on the books. Still, the team says its sample is too small to estimate how many exist.

Neptune trojans move on Neptune’s orbit in two swarms. One sits about 60 degrees ahead of the planet, at a Lagrange point called L4, and the other sits 60 degrees behind it, at L5.

By the time the authors wrote the paper, surveys had found over 30 Neptune trojans. However, most of what astronomers know about them comes from L4.

Seen from Earth, L5 sat on or close to the Galactic center for more than 15 years. Fields less than 10 degrees from the Galactic plane teem with stars. The paper says that high stellar density “significantly hindered” the discovery of trailing trojans.

By 2025 a large swath of the L5 cloud had come out of the Galactic plane. Then Rubin’s test fields covered part of it by chance, even though their designers had not aimed them at Neptune trojans.

According to the paper, trojans on orbits stable for billions of years are primordial, not recent arrivals. So their sizes, colors, spin rates and orbits carry information about the disk of planetesimals Neptune migrated through.

For every known Neptune trojan, the Minor Planet Center lists about 450 trojans of Jupiter, even though earlier surveys estimate the Neptune population is 4 to 10 times larger.

Rubin’s Science Validation survey started on June 20, 2025. It took 30-second exposures in six filters and imaged its fields every night, instead of every three or four nights as in the main survey, and covered about 3,000 square degrees near the ecliptic over the commissioning window.

The Simonyi Survey Telescope has an 8.4-meter (27.6-foot) mirror and a 3.2-gigapixel camera with a 9.6-square-degree field of view. The authors wrote that this makes Rubin “an ideal discovery machine” for the region.

2025 MH348 and the candidate 2025 MD138 turned up on UT June 21, 2025, and 2025 NN80 on UT July 7.

The searches used Rubin’s Solar System Processing pipeline with two codes, an expanded version of HelioLinC and a newer program called pointdexter, developed by Matt Holman and Kevin Napier to look for distant moving objects.

The detections were reported to the Minor Planet Center in February 2026. Users of the Minor Planet Mailing List, an online group of amateur and professional asteroid observers, then flagged the three as possible L5 trojans, and the paper credits those discussions with helping inspire the work.

Afterward, astronomers linked older detections by the Pan-STARRS1 telescope in Hawaii to 2025 MH348 and 2025 NN80. That allowed orbit fits over several oppositions. The data arc for 2025 MH348 grew to 2,595 days and for 2025 NN80 to 1,547 days.

The team took orbits from NASA JPL’s Small-Body Database, dated June 5, 2026. It ran them in the Small Body Dynamics Tool, with the Sun and the four giant planets pulling on each object.

For each body it also drew 100 clones from the JPL covariance matrix, which gives orbits nudged within the measurement errors, and ran high-resolution 10-million-year integrations before extending them to 1 billion years.

All 100 clones of 2025 MH348 and all 100 of 2025 NN80 were still in step with Neptune at 1 billion years. JPL lists their orbital periods at about 164 years, the same as Neptune’s.

The two older stable L5 trojans are 2008 LC18 and 2011 HM102. Scott Sheppard and Chad Trujillo reported 2008 LC18 in 2010. With the Rubin pair added, the list of trailing trojans on gigayear-stable orbits has four names.

Each of the four has an inclination above 25 degrees (28.7 and 29.7 degrees for the new pair), which fits earlier models in which trojan stability peaks near 30 degrees and again below 10 degrees.

Rubin measured the objects in the g, r, i and z filters. 2025 NN80 had 32 measurements, 2025 MH348 had 11 and 2025 MD138 had 15.

The new three are bluer than the two L5 trojans measured in a 2023 study. Put together with the earlier pair, the L5 colors fall in the same locus as the L4 swarm. The authors take that as further support for a single parent population.

A spectrum-reconstruction model matched all three to a water-ice spectral type that is common among Neptune trojans. The paper calls this a probabilistic assignment and says it does not replace near-infrared spectra.

Co-authors work in São Paulo, Tucson, Seattle, Ann Arbor and Belfast. The authors thank Breakthrough Listen and Queen’s University Belfast for supporting the LSST solar system First Data Sprint.

2025 MD138 remains a candidate because its 38 days of Rubin observations leave large error bars on its distance and orbit shape.

Its best-fit orbit stays stable for a billion years. Of its 100 clones, 36 behaved like trojans over 10 million years and 23 lasted to 1 billion.

Its inclination is about 6 degrees, and all 23 surviving clones kept that low tilt. If follow-up confirms it, it would be the first known long-lived L5 trojan on a low-tilt orbit.

There is no population estimate in the paper either. Rubin has not yet published how efficient its early moving-object search was. So the authors did not compare their haul with predicted numbers.

Rubin started its 10-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time at the end of June 2026. Rain and snow on Cerro Pachón halted it in mid-July. Science work was due to resume in early southern spring, after a planned engineering shutdown.

The observatory released its first images of the sky in June 2025, and by April 2026 its early data had produced more than 11,000 newly discovered asteroids. The decade-long survey of the southern sky covers nearly all the area where L5 trojans should be.

Co-author Joseph Murtagh, of the University of Washington, led a 2026 study. It predicts that the survey will quadruple the known Neptune trojans. It expects tens to hundreds in the trailing swarm, most of them in the first two years.

Other bodies have been found moving in step with Neptune farther out, and a second trojan sharing Earth’s orbit was confirmed in 2022.

The authors describe the three objects as “the tip of the iceberg” for the Neptune trojan science Rubin will enable. Sixteen years after astronomers reported the first L5 trojan, the count of long-lived ones stands at four. Two of them came from Rubin’s first weeks of testing.

The full study was published in the journal The Astrophysical Journal Letters.
