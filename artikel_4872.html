The Man Behind Fashion’s Most Dazzling Spectacles Turns His Attention to the Art of Light

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

For as long as he can remember, Alexandre de Betak has been entranced by light. As a 10-year-old, he rewired his Paris bedroom so that visitors had to be buzzed in, greeted by shifting colored lights. He went on to found Bureau Betak, a set design agency that transformed the runway with its megawatt spectacles for clients like Dior, Gucci, Jacquemus, and Fendi. De Betak estimates that he produced “thousands” of fashion shows—including Saint Laurent’s spring-summer 2020 ready-to-wear extravaganza, for which he bathed the Eiffel Tower in golden light, and installations for Rodarte featuring fluorescent and neon tubes.

A new truss light sculpture by Alexandre de Betak, seen here in a 17th-century Swedish castle

Now, de Betak, who sold his company in 2021, is turning the spotlight on his own creative practice. On November 5, he opened his first solo exhibition of artworks at Salon 94 in New York. The electronically programmed Truss Light Sculptures are inspired by the scaffolding and fixtures of his early runway shows and in some cases literally made from them. “That equipment is old-fashioned now, but I wanted to honor it,” de Betak says. “And I liked the irony that I spent so much energy over the years trying to hide it.”

On view at Salon 94’s Upper East Side town house through December 19, the exhibit presents two series. In the gallery’s contemporary space, the first is composed of freestanding and wall-mounted aluminum structures fitted with LED bulbs. The upstairs Stone Room, a historic space with Caen stone plaster walls and arched windows, is the backdrop for a second series of versions in bronze. To make them, de Betak had a French foundry recast each part down to the smallest screw by hand, a process that sometimes required more than 20 molds.

A pair of wall-mounted sculptures in aluminum

The light sculptures, which vary from sconce-like creations to hulking constructions out of a sci-fi movie, have a sly sense of humor and a human quality. This is especially true when they come to life, thanks to a remote-control-operated system that activates light sequences that glow, sparkle, thrum, or react to ambient noise, “whether a whisper,” de Betak says, “or extremely loud music.” For these effects, he drew on his stage work, where he was renowned for cueing, or choreographing, light and sound. “I’m interested in the ephemeral nature of what this equipment was made to show—whether a fashion show or a ballet,” he says. “At a moment when everything is virtual, I think it’s more important than ever to create things that produce a live emotion.”

Salon 94’s founder Jeanne Greenberg Rohatyn observes that de Betak’s sculptures are equally powerful switched on or off. Unlit they remind her of David Smith artworks or Russian constructivism. Lit, and with synced sound, they evoke the theatricality of stage performers. “Alex has spent his career building structures that disappear into someone else’s spectacle,” she says. “Here, the structure is the whole point.”
