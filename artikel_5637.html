Why European Households Are Rushing to Buy Solar Panels Ahead of a Dark, Cold Winter

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Changes to solar incentives and long installation processes mean European homeowners could benefit from planning now.

Winter is cold, dark, and grey. Yet for many Europeans, it’s a great time to switch to solar power.

While many are focused on the holiday season, industry experts are urging homeowners considering a switch to solar to contact suppliers before Christmas — although not to take advantage of the winter sun.

Several European countries are making changes to solar energy subsidies and tax breaks in 2027, meaning homeowners might want to fast-track their plans to enjoy the financial benefits.

But even without changes to government incentives, there are good reasons to get ahead of the spring sunshine.

Although a typical rooftop solar installation takes just one or two days, getting building permits and, in some cases, approval to connect to electricity grids can take weeks. Sometimes months.

Last year, an OECD report citing earlier research found that permit times for rooftop solar projects ranged from six weeks in Malta to as many as 10 months in Bulgaria.

The country has since made it easier for homeowners, scrapping permit requirements for solar systems up to 20 kilowatts intended for personal use, but delays are still common across Europe.

In Germany, a consumer watchdog warned last year that homeowners in Saxony were waiting months to have their solar panels connected to the grid — and, in extreme cases, more than a year.

And Edward Gurney, the founding director of the UK-based Solar and Battery Company, says getting approval from electricity providers can take four to five weeks even under normal circumstances.

“A lot of solar equipment is manufactured in China, and getting replacement stock delivered can take around six weeks,” he says.

Winter weather can also disrupt installation schedules, as icy roofs make working conditions unsafe.

“When you’ve already got a busy installation schedule, losing even a few days to bad weather can push subsequent installations back,” Gurney says.

Waiting until the weather warms up could mean missing out on a prime period for solar production, too.

Solar power generated less than five per cent of the EU’s electricity in January last year, but by May, it was supplying more than 20 percent, according to the European Environment Agency.

In Germany alone, 74 per cent of the solar electricity fed into the grid last year was generated between April and September.

The sooner homeowners have their panels installed, the sooner they can start benefitting from this springtime bump.

In the meantime, Gurney recommends using winter to assess how much electricity you might use and compare quotations from different installers.

“The number of panels, battery capacity and how much electricity your household uses will all affect the long-term value of the installation,” he says.

Homeowners in several European countries could face changes to the cost of installing solar panels in 2027. Since April 2022, homeowners in England, Scotland and Wales have been able to have solar panels installed without paying value-added tax. From 1 April 2027, the tax rate will return to five per cent.

In Germany, people who install solar panels from January onward could miss out on government support unless new energy laws are approved before the end of the year, the German Solar Industry Association warns.

Although German lawmakers have tentatively agreed to continue paying homeowners for surplus electricity they sell to the grid from solar systems up to 25 kilowatts, they have yet to strike a final deal.

And in Austria, the government plans to stop offering grants for solar panels in 2027 and instead help homeowners buy batteries to store the electricity they generate.
