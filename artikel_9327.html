Are You Making This Common Mistake With Grief?

In recent years, the language of therapy has moved beyond the clinic and into everyday conversation, giving us new ways to name our inner lives and, at times, new ways to misread them. Nowhere is this more evident than in how we respond to grief.

In grief, we learn quickly which words help and which ones wound. “At least they lived a long life.” “They wouldn’t want you to be sad.” Most of us recognize these as unhelpful sentiments.

Not all harmful responses to grief sound like clichés, though. Sometimes they arrive dressed in compassion, wrapped in the language of awareness. A friend might say, “This seems to be activating your abandonment wound,” “Maybe this pain is really about your father,” or “Maybe this is more about your unhappiness in your marriage.” The words may be well-intentioned, even psychologically accurate, yet the effect can be deeply unsettling. Instead of feeling seen, we feel studied; instead of comforted, diagnosed.

This dynamic can feel like a kind of gaslighting because the tools and language of healing are used to explain away your emotions instead of tending to them. Unlike gaslighting in the usual sense, it isn’t malicious. It’s the experience of being told what your pain really means instead of having that pain simply met. It is a byproduct of a therapy-literate culture that often privileges interpretation over presence.

For the past several years, I’ve taught death and dying at the university level and led grief retreats. Again and again, I’ve watched grief ease, not when someone explained it, but when someone stayed with it. In what follows, I’ll look at how analysis can crowd out presence, and offer ways to stay with grief, someone else’s or your own, without rushing to interpret it.

When we’re grieving, people want to help, but few things wound more than having sorrow dissected while it’s still unfolding. Well-meaning insight can become a form of distance. A loved one may believe they’re offering understanding when they say, “This loss must be reminding you of when you lost your job,” or “You seem to struggle with change, don’t you?,” yet the grieving heart hears something different: that the pain belongs to something older and is therefore less legitimate.

Both can be true. A new loss often awakens older ones, but truth without warmth is just analysis. The body doesn’t sort its ache into past and present; it simply calls for accompaniment. In one well-known study, holding the hand of a trusted person calmed the brain’s response to threat, no words required. Seeing where a present loss touches earlier wounds can be deeply healing, eventually. But categorizing pain does not lessen the need to feel it.

We can also engage in this kind of gaslighting internally. Once we have absorbed enough psychological language, we can turn the same clinical lens on ourselves. We catch the echo of a past wound and then scold our grief for being disproportionate. “I’m overreacting,” we tell ourselves. “This is just my old stuff.” “Why can’t I just be happy?” Yet grief does not care how insightfully we label it. It still needs space to breathe. Awareness can be a form of care, but only when it does not rush ahead of the feeling itself. If it becomes a strategy to bypass feeling, our own or another’s, it defeats itself.

Many guests arrive at our retreats saying some version of “I should be over this by now.” Over the days that follow, that urgency to be finished often quiets, not through argument, but because the grief is finally given room. On the last day, they complete a ritual deep in the forest, at a fountain carved with the outline of a family. Around it, they leave rocks painted with the names of the people they’ve lost. The oldest rocks have since faded almost past recognition, the paint softening into a weathered imprint.

I’ve come to see this not as loss, but as integration: the sharp color of a fresh wound, still seeking an explanation, settling into something that no longer needs to announce itself. No one stood over the rocks interpreting them. They simply bore witness. Grief often becomes more endurable in exactly this way: when it is allowed to change shape in the space of time, care, and presence.

These suggestions come from years of sitting with grieving people, and from my own experience of loss. They are less techniques than reminders of what the moment is asking for.

Resist the urge to explain. The interpretive reflex—reaching for meaning, context, or psychological framing—is often a response to our own helplessness in the face of grief’s rawness. Notice when you’re doing it, pause, and return to the simpler question: Can I be here with this person, without needing it to mean something right now?

Let the silence be full rather than empty. Many people fill silence because it feels like abandonment. A silence in which you remain present, your body in the room, your attention undivided, can be the most companioning thing you offer. “I’m here,” said once and then honored, is worth more than 10 minutes of careful insight.

Follow their lead on meaning. If the grieving person begins to find meaning themselves, to name connections, to speak about what this loss is touching in their past, that is theirs to do. Your role is to witness it, not to initiate it. There is a world of difference between meaning that emerges from the griever and meaning that is handed to them. When you catch yourself doing it, try asking a different question: What does this need? Usually the answer is simpler than any interpretation: company, time, permission to be exactly this intense.

If you feel the pull to theorize about someone else’s grief, a few questions can help you track it in time: Am I trying to ease their pain, or my own discomfort with it? Did they ask for insight, or am I offering it uninvited? Have I acknowledged the loss itself, fully, before reaching for what it might mean?

When you’re unsure what to say, three phrases rarely go wrong: “Tell me about them.” “What do you miss most today?” “I’m here.” Each one keeps the person grieving at the center.

Grief has a way of making us distrust our own experience. We begin measuring whether we’re grieving correctly, whether we’ve stayed sad too long or not long enough, or whether the intensity means something beyond the loss itself.

Catch the self-diagnosis. This one is not easy, because the narrating voice sounds so reasonable: “This is really about my mother,” “I’m being too sensitive,” “I’m being disproportionate.” It rarely announces itself as unkind.

Notice when you’re applying the same analytical approach to yourself that you’d resist from someone else. Then let the intensity of the feeling stand without demanding it justify itself first.

Ask your grief what it needs, not what it means. Meaning can come later, if it comes at all. In the acute moment, the more honest question is practical: You might need rest, someone to call, permission to cancel a plan. Let the meaning-making wait.

Choose your witnesses. Some people simply cannot stay in the uncertainty that grief creates. Their discomfort shows up as advice, reassurance, or psychological evaluation. It doesn’t make them uncaring. It simply means they may not be the people to rely on in the rawest moments of loss. Notice who can sit in the room without needing to fix or explain, and lean on them more.

Insight has a place in grief. In time, many people do come to see how one loss is woven through the others, and that understanding can bring relief. But it tends to arrive on its own schedule, after the pain has been felt and met. Until then, what we can offer, to someone else or to ourselves, is company. The rocks at the fountain didn’t fade because anyone explained them. They faded because they were left where they were, held by time and by the people who stood beside them. Even when a wound is old, the tenderness it requires is always immediate.
