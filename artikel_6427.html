Mussels that live in total darkness have cells that still keep a daily rhythm

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Deep-sea mussels live in total darkness, yet their cells kept a roughly 24-hour rhythm in the lab, offering clues to how life keeps time without sunlight.

Deep in the Atlantic, a mussel called Bathymodiolus azoricus lives where no daylight reaches, and the tides, not the sun, change its surroundings on a schedule.

Even so, the mussel’s cells kept a daily rhythm when researchers grew them in a lab refrigerator, far from the seafloor.

The finding suggests that genes that run daily clocks in flies and people also help keep time in animals that never see sunlight.

Audrey Mat, a researcher at the Alfred Wegener Institute in Germany and the University of Vienna, grew the cells from mussels collected on the Atlantic seafloor.

Her team has kept two of those cultures alive for more than three and a half years.

The mussels come from the Lucky Strike vent field on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, south of the Azores, about 5,600 feet (1,700 meters) down. The water pressure there is about 170 times what it is at sea level.

At hydrothermal vents, seawater seeps into the seafloor, moves through the rock below and flows back out as hot, metal-rich fluid. Some of it reaches about 660°F (350°C).

The mussels settle in cooler water nearby, between 39 and 48°F (4 and 9°C). The direction of the vent flow shifts with the tides, so the water around the mussels switches between vent fluid and ordinary seawater.

In a 2020 study, Mat and her colleagues reported that on the seafloor, the mussels’ behavior and gene activity followed mainly a tidal rhythm of about 12.4 hours.

A weaker daily rhythm showed up as well, though no clear day-night cycle reaches the vents.

Mussels from a shallower vent switched to a mostly daily pattern in a lab with lights on for 12 hours and off for 12 hours.

How an animal with no brain produces either rhythm has been unclear.

Lab cells are usually kept warm, close to body temperature. Mat tried something different.

“While cells in the laboratory usually thrive at 25 to 37 °C, our mussel cells grow in the fridge, at deep-sea temperatures,” said Mat, who first proposed the experiments.

“This technical advance is unique in the world and allows novel insights into the biology of these animals.”

The team tried cells from six tissues at three temperatures. At 77°F (25°C), fungi and other contaminants quickly took over.

Out of 63 cultures, cells from the mussel’s foot survived best. The foot is the muscular part a mussel extends from its shell.

Keeping whole mussels alive took work too.

About half of the animals collected in 2023 died within a month, likely from the stress of the trip to the surface and the flight to Vienna.

The remaining mussels lived for 13 months in an aquarium shielded from white light.

To look for a clock, the team put foot tissue from five mussels in culture and kept it in constant darkness at 39°F (4°C). Then they sampled the cells every four hours for two days.

A pattern of gene activity that repeats about every 24 hours is called a circadian rhythm. Finding one is how scientists spot a clock at work.

Of 5,616 genes the team could track, 157 showed a circadian pattern, about 2.8%. That’s close to the 2.5% the team found earlier in mussels on the seafloor.

So the cells kept time on their own, with no ocean and no daylight around them.

Across 10,000 reshuffles of the sampling times, none yielded at least 157 daily cycles while also recovering the same 66 transcripts that earlier research had identified as rhythmic.

The team also found 109 genes with a tidal pattern. That number was no higher than chance alone would produce.

In fruit flies and mammals, CLOCK teams up with a partner protein to turn on clock genes such as period.

The proteins those genes produce then dial back CLOCK and its partner, and the cycle starts again.

No genome exists yet for this mussel. So the team compared three related deep-sea mussels and found two stretches of DNA, just upstream of period, that were nearly identical across species.

The researchers copied those stretches from B. azoricus and tested them in fruit fly cells that lack their own clock parts.

The mussel’s CLOCK and BMAL proteins increased the activity of a test gene controlled by one of those DNA segments.

A protein called CRY2 shut the signal down, and more CRY2 meant a stronger effect. The mussel’s PER protein had no effect on its own.

Added alongside CRY2, though, PER made the shut-off stronger and helped CRY2 build up inside the cell’s nucleus. A third protein, TIM, canceled PER’s effect.

The PER result matters because the period gene in these mussels rises and falls on a roughly tidal schedule.

The researchers suggest the balance between PER and CRY2 could switch the clock between tidal and daily timing.

The deep sea is one of the least explored ecosystems on Earth, and plans for deep-sea mining are putting it under pressure.

Kristin Tessmar-Raible, who studies biological clocks at the University of Oldenburg and the University of Vienna, led the study with Marjolaine Matabos of Ifremer, France’s national ocean research institute.

“The fact that these ecosystems are mechanistically highly complex and possess timing systems as sophisticated as those at the surface underlines how worthy of protection they are,” Tessmar-Raible and Matabos said.

“On land, we know that human activities such as light or vibrations disrupt circadian clocks. Deep-sea clocks are very likely just as vulnerable, with significant consequences for organisms and ecosystems.”

The team noted that mining could affect the timing of deep-sea animals even from a great distance.

A dish of cells doesn’t capture everything happening inside a whole mussel at a vent.

The team also detected RNA from the period gene in mussel tissue, while levels in the cultured cells were below what the test could detect.

Either the cells with a tidal rhythm didn’t survive in culture, or conditions in the dish turned off whatever drives the tidal pattern of period, the researchers wrote.

The next question is whether a mussel runs one clock that switches between tides and days, or keeps separate groups of cells that each follow one rhythm. The researchers wrote that their results fit either idea.

Genome studies and work locating the clock proteins inside mussel tissue could help tell those two ideas apart, the team wrote.

The full study was published in the journal EMBO Reports.
