There’s a lot of debate and heated discussions when it comes to AI. One one hand, it has been an effective assistant when used right, cutting down time when it comes to delegation and distribution of tasks. It has the ability to make more menial tasks that little bit less tedious, and can speed up workflow. Again, this is if the technology is used properly.

But with any new technology, it can also be used with malicious and criminal intent. The auto industry isn’t spared from that, and fraudsters can explore new possibilities to continue their activities with it.

In a report by Car Dealership Guy, organized networks are turning to auto financing fraud by exploiting gaps in security in digital automotive retail operations. Granted, it’s not a new thing, but AI has made it easier for criminal elements to do such schemes.

Per the National Insurance Crime Bureau (NICB), more synthetic identities are being flagged, and there’s now a higher incidence of credit washing and false theft claims. Other schemes include the submission of inflated or AI-fabricated pay stubs, and automated bot attacks on dealers and retailers. Dealers have reported a 77% increase in fraudulent activity over the past two years, while 24% of default cases have some links to fraud. As for credit washing, it skyrocketed to 228% from early 2024 to mid-2025.

The NICB says it estimates that $10.4 billion has been lost through these fraudulent lending schemes, and adds that number will be even higher this year. The agency also notes that nearly 70% of its vehicle finance fraud cases inflated or synthetic identities. It’s been estimated that each case costs aggrieved parties between $22,000 and $50,000. There have also been more elaborate schemes, with one such case involving an AI-generated cloned dealership scamming a buyer out of $77,300.

Financial fraud, as a whole, lowers economic productivity as it siphons cash flow from legitimate businesses to unsavory enterpises instead. It also erodes trust in an industry, and forces industries to put up more more rules to weed out criminal elements. But, in doing so, the cost of putting up more safeguards is passed on to the consumer via operational and administrative costs.

For dealers, this may lead them asking for even more requirements for the sake of identity verification, among other things. For consumers trying to fight fairly in life, it’s going through more hoops just to be able to buy a car. In its press release, the NICB “advocates for the modernization of state and federal statutory frameworks to close gaps exploited by synthetic identity rings,” with NICB COO Timothy R. Slater saying “Vehicle finance fraud has evolved from isolated credit misrepresentations into an organized, digital criminal industry that touches dealerships, lenders and insurers. Stopping these networks requires shifting away from reactive, siloed defenses toward standardized and synchronized cross-industry reporting.”
