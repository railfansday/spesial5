Does the Space Where We Deliver Therapy Matter?

Picture yourself trying to discuss your deepest anxieties with your therapist while a conversation about last night’s TV can be heard outside the door. How about attempting a therapeutic exercise in a room barely large enough for two chairs?

A number of therapists have raised concerns to me about the spaces in which they are expected to work.

Some report being expected to conduct telephone or online sessions in settings where conversations could be overheard by colleagues or others nearby. Others describe working in noisy buildings, sharing spaces with services that have very different needs, or using communal waiting areas, which can unsettle clients and disrupt sessions.

Changes to room layout, size, availability and accessibility have also raised concerns. These situations can arise when therapy services face growing demand for appointments and staff must share limited facilities for face-to-face, telephone and online sessions.

The space in which therapy takes place really does matter.

Protecting a client’s confidentiality is a foundational principle of psychological therapy.

The ethical principles of acting in the client’s best interests, doing good, avoiding harm and acting fairly may all be compromised if private conversations can be overheard.

Telephone appointments carried out in call-center style settings can increase the good done for a larger number of people by widening access to psychological therapies, particularly in high-volume, low-intensity services—but this must not come at the expense of confidentiality.

Clients are assured that their conversations will remain private but hearing background noises during a call could undermine that assurance. Even if a client is unaware that others are within earshot, therapists still have a duty to protect their confidentiality. If conversations can be overheard, this professional responsibility could be compromised.

There is also the potential for impact on those who inadvertently overhear confidential, sensitive, or distressing information.

In a face-to-face session, hearing footsteps passing along the corridor outside may not be particularly disruptive. But loud noises, conversations outside the door, or interruptions can break the focus of both the therapist and client, affect their relationship, and interfere with the session’s progress.

Room size and accessibility are not just administrative matters. They can also raise ethical concerns.

A cramped consulting room may be inaccessible or uncomfortable for a client or therapist who needs additional space, uses mobility aids, or requires another person to be with them in the room.

Is there enough room for an interpreter, family member, advocate, or even clinical supervisor for the duration of the session if necessary?

The nature of the issues being addressed may influence how comfortable the client may feel sitting close to someone else.

For those who have experienced a trauma or have cultural expectations about maintaining a respectful distance, being forced to sit very close to someone else, even if that person is their therapist, will be hard to accommodate in a small room and may affect their ability to fully engage in the session.

Even seemingly small details can have an impact. For example, are there windows and natural light? Is the space well-ventilated and kept at a comfortable temperature? An enclosed space may be uncomfortable for someone experiencing high levels of anxiety, while poor ventilation could increase the risk of transmitting infection. Small or unsuitable rooms could have a disproportionate and potentially discriminatory effect on clients who are traumatised, have disabilities, are neurodivergent or need additional support.

They may also create barriers for therapists with protected characteristics.

Therapy is not always just two people sitting opposite each other and talking. Depending on the treatment, therapist and client may need to move around, manoeuvre a wheelchair, take pacing breaks, carry out behavioural experiments or exposure exercises, or use techniques such as the empty chair exercise. The space should accommodate the required activities rather than dictate them.

Therapists may also need recording equipment or access to online or written materials.

Furniture matters too: A desk for being able to take notes, different types of seating, a coffee table, or a whiteboard may all play a part in delivering effective therapy.

Although perhaps less common than cramped rooms, a very large space could also present challenges. Unless the extra space is needed for the work being carried out, it may make it harder to create a comfortable, supportive ambiance in a clinical session.

Then there is room availability. Limited access to suitable rooms, or competition to book them at key times, can affect appointment availability and, therefore, clients’ access to therapy and the effective delivery of treatment. The knock-on effect of this is to add to pressures on practitioners and affect their wellbeing.

The space in which therapy is delivered matters, as it can affect the quality and safety of care, equity, client choice, and practitioner well-being.

To meet professional standards of conduct, performance and ethics, therapy services have a responsibility to provide accommodation that supports safe, effective, equitable and sustainable psychological therapies, as well as meeting legal obligations.

Practitioners and service leaders can draw on organisational and professional standards, best practice guidelines and NHS guidance on suitable accommodation when considering how to address or avoid any concerns.

Ensuring workplaces provide suitable spaces can support practitioners’ wellbeing and fitness to practice, reduce the risk of burnout and enhance the service provided to clients.

Helen Macdonald is a BABCP-accredited Cognitive Behavioural Psychotherapist Supervisor and Trainer, a consultant EMDR Therapist, and Senior Clinical Advisor to the BABCP. As a prominent voice within the organisation, she frequently hosts the “Members Matters” podcast.
