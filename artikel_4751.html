Circumcision is controversial, sexually and psychologically.

It involves surgical removal of the foreskin, the skin that covers the head (glans) of the penis. The foreskin contains touch-sensitive nerves. By removing them, circumcision opponents insist that it must reduce the penis’ sexual sensitivity and impair men’s pleasure. On Reddit and other Internet forums, opponents have denounced circumcision as barbarous, unfair to male infants, and physically and psychologically harmful.

Supporters counter that the vast majority of erotically sensitive nerve endings are located in the glans, not the foreskin, and that the procedure does not compromise men’s sexual function or pleasure. Recently, Turkish researchers analyzed 15 studies of circumcision and found that far from impairing men sexually, it enhances men’s sexual function.

The investigators conducted a meta-analysis of 15 previously published studies. Meta-analysis is a standard statistical technique that allows the results of small studies to be combined as if they’d all been one large trial. Study size is important. The larger the study population, the more likely the results are to reflect reality. The 15 studies involved 14,737 men, a number that lends credibility to the findings. The meta-analysis showed that, compared with men with intact foreskins, those who were circumcised reported greater sexual satisfaction (p < 0.001), improved erection function (p = 0.002), less pain during intercourse (p = 0.03), and fewer orgasm difficulties (p = 0.01) (Karaahmet and Lalaeh, 2025).

The researchers evaluated 15 studies, but many more have been conducted. How did they select those 15? They used the World Health Organization’s (WHO) guidelines for grading research rigor and chose only the most credible studies, which is important as many studies of circumcision’s sexual impact have been biased either for or against the procedure.

These reports don’t inspire much confidence.

But these studies are also problematic. When they were conducted, AIDS was epidemic in Africa, and many participants feared for their lives. Researchers reassured them that circumcision had no sexual downsides. Critics charged that circumcised subjects felt so relieved to have their AIDS risk reduced that they parroted back what the researchers had told them—that circumcision has no sexual impact.

Using WHO guidelines, the authors of the new report excluded many studies, both pro and con, such as those just mentioned, and included only the most methodologically rigorous studies, which showed that circumcision causes no discernible sexual impairment.

Worldwide, one-third of men are circumcised. Judaism and Islam require it, so in Israel and across the Muslim world, the practice is virtually universal. Circumcision is also popular in the U.S. and Asia, but less so in Europe, Latin America, and non-Muslim Africa.

Jews and Muslims have circumcised boys for centuries. During the 19th century, the practice spread to Christians who believed—mistakenly—that it prevented masturbation. Circumcision is not medically necessary, but it offers health benefits in several areas:

These findings have prompted some Jews and Muslims to claim their faiths were medically prescient and required circumcision for health reasons. But theologians of both religions insist that the practice has always been an act of faith and so its medical benefits, while welcome, are coincidental.

For the vast majority of men, the controversy over circumcision and sexual function is moot. They’re either circumcised or not, and if so, the vast majority have lived that way since infancy and have no way to compare their sexual sensitivity before and after the procedure.

If you’re intact and want to believe you have superior erotic sensitivity, you’re free to feel smug. But the weight of the evidence shows that circumcision does not impair sexual function, and that circumcised men have no reason to fear they’re missing out on erotic pleasure and satisfaction.
