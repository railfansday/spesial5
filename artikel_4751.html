Downsizing Isn’t for Everyone

As we grow older, we face considerable pressure to reduce our possessions and our living space. Such downsizing can have practical benefits and be personally liberating, but it can also lead to unnecessary stress and guilt.

This post does not criticize the act of downsizing, but it does push back against the pressure to do so, especially for the sake of our children. We can declutter and live more simply, but we should not feel obligated or guilted into discarding what is valuable to us.1

Downsizing is often promoted as a virtuous gift and a way of showing love to our heirs. As middle-aged and elderly adults, we are counseled to reduce clutter so our children won’t face the arduous task of clearing out and cleaning after we die. We are also advised not to accumulate any new things because of the additional burden this will add.2

In an enlightening post on Swedish Death Cleaning, Psychology Today blogger Dr. Donelle Dreese offers a different perspective, saying that such cleaning “is an act of love for all those who will be in the position to clean out our ‘stuff’ after we are gone.”

Disciplined downsizing is also presented as a path to wisdom. By going through all of our possessions, seriously considering what matters to us and discarding the rest, we may move toward a deeper understanding of our place on Earth, “growing downward, smaller, one among the grasses,” in the words of poet Wendell Berry. Such downsizing conveys a moral imperative to acquire wisdom through un-acquiring possessions.

Considering the Children. For many of us, when we die, our children will be in middle age. The ideas in Swedish death cleaning and in KonMari about being a good ancestor are thoughtful and inspiring, but we are not in the best position to examine each of our possessions and determine its future personal value for others or its potential to give joy. Our middle-aged children will be quite capable of assessing what is or is not valuable to them.

In fact, grown children may discover interesting artifacts that tell them more about their parents. My father, for example, kept a file of published cartoons that my sister and I had never seen, left over from his young adulthood as an amateur cartoonist.

Considering Ourselves. If the things we own give us joy and pleasure, why not keep them? Our possessions are not disposable content.

I have small, unnecessary collections of first editions of poetry and memoir, which I enjoy seeing as I walk past them on the bookshelf, occasionally taking down one of the books and slowly reading.

And if something new comes along that delights us, then why not consider getting it?

Nurturing Future Generations. For many older adults, extra living space bestows hope that our grandchildren and grown children will visit and need guest rooms. Two of my grandchildren are now old enough to visit on their own—which they have done—and my extra bedrooms set up a more inviting environment.

In general, as we grow older, we want autonomy with our belongings and living spaces. According to the AARP, nearly 90 percent of adults over 65 want to age in place whenever possible, in their current homes and with their own belongings.

Preserving Our Past. A pioneering study by Margaret Boschetti documented that cherished possessions contribute to our self concepts about who we are and what we’ve done. They help us remember important experiences, relationships, and identities of the past.

Looking Around and Looking Ahead. In a 2020 study, Tara Coleman and Janine Wiles found connections with belongings that transcend the past. The majority of participants in this study interacted with cherished possessions not only to connect with their past selves but also to cope with times of challenge and change in the present and to cultivate a sense of aging in place in the future.

Individual Differences. Research conducted by Mary Dozier and Catherine Ayers in 2021 showed that object attachment exists across our life span, with fluctuations in levels of attachment. For healthy management of possessions, there isn’t one fixed strategy across individuals or even within an individual.

Easily accessed possessions can provide comfort, affirming our preferred self concepts and improving our quality of life. Or they can disrupt our sense of self. The unplayed upright piano in our living room may reinforce our self concept of being musical. Or it may regularly remind us of what we’ve lost. There are no general rules about such possessions. We need to assess their personal value in our own way.

Most of us want to downsize as we get older, even if we find it difficult. How should we approach the management of our possessions?

1) Focus on what to keep, instead of what to throw away. Ask what items matter most or what stories lie behind cherished objects.

2) Begin by clearing out the more obvious, low-emotion areas first, such as the basement, attic, or garage. When considering the living room or bedrooms, we should give ourselves time and many iterations.

3) Before substantially downsizing, seek information and support from peers who have gone through the same thing.

4) Avoid an “Antiques Roadshow” perspective. The potential value of our possessions is not a sound reason for keeping them—unless they are appraised works of art or age-worthy bottles of wine. Chances are, they are not objectively valuable or not easily sold as individual items.

5) Research the cost of companies in the area that manage the clearing out, donating, and selling of belongings. Include that amount in a will for that particular purpose.3

Downsizing decisions may be easier than anticipated. My sump pump has broken twice in the past eight years during heavy rain, resulting in two basement floods and two separate clear outs. The first clear out was easy because only my low priority possessions were sitting on the basement floor and damaged. After that, I wanted my basement straightened out as quickly as possible, so I placed my surviving (and more treasured) possessions on the basement floor—which were then damaged eight years later during the second flooding. With the second clear out, I learned quickly I could live without these more treasured items. I don’t recommend flooding, but it could be imagined to allow more ruthless triage on our mostly unused belongings.

1. This post focuses on partially curated and organized collections of important belongings that do not interfere with our life or our living space. It does not address hoarding, which is an indiscriminate accumulation of possessions that impairs movement and functioning in the cluttered spaces.

2. Swedish Death Cleaning or “döstädning” encourages reflection about our possessions and our relationship to these possessions. It also asserts that shedding these possessions benefits our family’s well-being when we are gone. With KonMari, Marie Kondo takes a different approach to the same end, advising people to keep only what sparks joy.

3. Companies that help families sort through and clear out a deceased parent’s home go by several labels: estate cleanout services, estate liquidation services, bereavement cleanout services, or move managers. They offer a range of services, including hosting estate sales, selling valuable items, coordinating charitable donations, cleaning, and hauling away trash.
