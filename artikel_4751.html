What Is the Difference Between a Comforter and a Duvet Insert?

A comforter is a single, often quilted, piece of bedding with a fabric cover encasing the doughy fill in between the two fabric layers. A duvet, on the other hand, refers to both the insert and fabric cover. A comforter can be colorful or white, and made in a variety of fabrics. The fabric cover of a comforter is stitched directly onto the fill (down, feathers, or polyester are most common). A duvet’s cover is removable, and often tied to the corners via loops attached to the insert.

Not necessarily. Many duvet inserts are designed to be used on their own, as a comforter, or with a cover. I recommend a cover to help protect your duvet insert, ensure it lasts as long as possible, and make laundry day as easy as possible. Duvet covers act as a protective layer, keeping sweat, body oils, and normal wear from affecting your duvet insert. Plus, duvet covers allow you to add personality to your bedscape, whether you prefer an all-white minimalist look or a maximalist ruffle.

What Is the Best Type of Filling for a Duvet?

Similar to quality, the “best” filling is pretty subjective. People often choose silk, lyocell, down, or down-alternative fills. Down comforters tend to be firmer and heavier, while down-alternatives are usually considered more lightweight and airy. Down-alternatives derive from recycled and allergen-friendly materials, so they’re ideal for people who prefer or require those types of fillings. The fill power will also determine the ultimate comfort level, and most should hover around 650 for a down, cotton candy-like feel.

And again, those care instructions come into play. If you’d rather throw your comforter in the wash, that’s most down comforters in our list can handle it. Meanwhile, some specialty down-alternatives (like bamboo viscose options) need to take a trip to the dry cleaners, which can make them higher maintenance and more expensive long-term.

Does the Quality of a Duvet Insert Matter?

In terms of “quality,” you’ll see the effects in two main areas: the price point and longevity of your duvet insert. For instance, a down comforter with 750 fill power like Brooklinen’s heavyweight comforter is specifically designed for keeping you warm during colder winter months (or for anyone who spends most nights shivering). The fill power makes it lofty and fluffy, but the construction can also keep it lofty and fluffy.

Baffle-box construction, as mentioned above, is also considered a higher quality design option, which holds fill in separate chambers to keep the fill even. All of this said, the most expensive, technically high-quality comforters aren’t necessarily the best for every type of sleeper. And while we love comforters and duvet inserts that run as high as $500, we’ve also scoured the internet and tested alternatives you can shop for less than $100 on Amazon (we’re looking at you, Bedsure Waffle Comforter Set).

It’s also important to keep in mind that your duvet insert’s quality depends on how you care for it. Make sure to carefully read the care instructions, and if needed, take it to a trustworthy dry cleaner for a gentle clean (experts say about two to three times per year).

How Often Should You Wash Your Comforter?

Comforters and duvet inserts accumulate “dust, sweat, and oils, which can cause allergies and wear down the fabric,” says Camden Olivero, COO and cofounder of Clotheslyne, an on-demand pick-up and delivery laundry service. With this in mind, I recommend washing your comforter two to three times a year to help keep allergens at bay and the covering in its best condition (double check the label first, though).

Like your other linens, it’s best to keep your comforter in a cool, dry place to avoid mold or moisture buildup. Keep the comforter out of plastic and cedar too, as these materials can cause yellowing.

If your old comforter is still in good condition, we recommend donating it to charities in your area. However, if you’re looking to purely dispose of old comforters, organizations like Trashie ethically recycle textiles for you to keep them out of landfills—so long as they’re clean and free of soil like motor oil or paint.

How Do I Choose the Right Duvet Insert for Different Seasons?

There are duvet inserts and comforters with heavy fill powers (think 800 and above) for harsh winter months or cold sleepers, lightweight options (400 or below) for summer months or hot sleepers, and medium weights (around 400 to 600) for year-round comfort. We’ve also learned that the fluffiest duvet inserts often give sleepers that Goldilocks-level sleep. “A fluffy, high-loft duvet delivers the most consistent sleep experience across seasons,” Anthony Loiacono, director of rooms at L’Auberge de Sedona, previously told AD. “It provides warmth without weight, and most importantly, adapts to day-to-night temperature shifts.” Frette’s Cortina Medium Down Duvet Filler has all-season appeal, and you can always layer with an extra blanket if you want more coverage on colder nights.

Not all duvet inserts are created equal. This is a lesson you learn early on, when you wake up either sweating or freezing in the middle of the night. The fact is that this annoying experience is also completely avoidable. Reading up on the duvet insert you're considering buying—and actually understanding what all the terms mean—will tell you everything you need to know, including how hot it’s going to be and whether or not the down is going to end up smooshed in one corner come morning. So, I put this guide together with the help of Danielle Trotter, the textiles technical designer at West Elm, to break down what to consider in the confusing world that is duvet insert fillings, fill power, and the different types of construction.

Fill Power: This is the measurement of how much down occupies a cubic inch of space in the insert, a.k.a. how “lofty” it feels. You’ll usually see numbers in the 500s and up. The higher the fill power, the fluffier and warmer it will feel. I typically like fill powers that hover around 800 during the colder months. This fill power is typically on the marshmallowy side of the spectrum and feels cozier and insulating when the temperature outside starts to drop. For the summertime, 600 fill power or so is usually a sweet spot for me since it’s more airy and breathable, but individual preferences may vary.

Corner Loops: If there are corner loops or anchor loops, that means you can use the comforter as a duvet insert. If there are no loops, the comforter might be prone to clumping and bunching within the duvet, so we wouldn’t recommend it unless you have a lot of patience. Some of the loops in this list reach up to 12, which are super helpful to keep the fill even.

Fill Content: Fill material is usually classified as either down or down alternative options.

Outer Shell: The material on the outer shell of your comforter or duvet insert matters. Look at the thread count. I find that fabrics with thread counts around 300 are typically spongy and airy. Consider your preferred sleeping temperature and whether a cotton sateen shell that’s smooth but slightly thicker is a better option than one made of crisp percale cotton. If you’re a hot sleeper, you’ll probably want a cooling material with breathability, like eucalyptus.

Size and Color: The size of your insert is dictated by the size of your bed. Companies usually offer all standard sizes from twin to king, with some brands offering California king as well. In terms of color, white is most popular for the duvet insert, but you can also find ivory relatively easily. Comforter color ranges are more expansive, and you can likely find a hue that fits your decor. I’ve called out what’s available in each of the product picks.

Thread Count: This is the number of horizontal and vertical threads per square inch of the duvet insert's outer fabric. Expert tip: “In theory, people think that the higher the thread count, the better the quality because more yarns are being used,” Trotter says. “However, it’s the quality of the yarns or even the type of weave that really matters!”

Baffle-Box Construction: This design means the comforter or duvet insert has sewn-in square chambers with side walls. “Each chamber has an equal amount of fill, and the side walls prevent the fill from shifting,” says Trotter. “This construction is considered to be of a better quality because warmth is evenly distributed.”

Sewn-Through Construction: This type of construction is when the duvet insert is made of two pieces of fabric sewn around the perimeter, then filled and quilted into squares. This means the top and bottom of the comforter are sewn together. In turn, the fill is shift- and bunch-resistant. While this is a great option for those who like low-maintenance laundry days since you can typically toss these comforter types in the washing machine, the tighter construction usually makes the fill less lofty. Plus, cold air can seep through the seams, so it’s more ideal for a summer bedscape.
