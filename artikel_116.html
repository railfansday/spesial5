What is at stake at the EU-UK summit in Brussels?

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Leaders will wrestle with easing Brexit red tape, restoring emissions trading and opening up youth mobility

The EU and UK will hold a long-awaited summit in Brussels on Friday – the second since Brexit – to hammer out the UK’s longer-term relationship with the bloc.

It will be attended by the prime minister, Andy Burnham, the head of the European Commission, Ursula von der Leyen, and the president of the European Council, António Costa.

The gathering, which follows up last year’s inaugural EU-UK summit in London, was scheduled for July but postponed when Keir Starmer resigned.

The two sides hope to announce three deals, which were initiated under Starmer’s government.

First, a food and drink agreement that will remove a lot of Brexit red tape on animal and plant products, making it easier to trade meats, cheese, wine and flowers. To secure the deal, which is not expected to take effect until 2028, the UK will align with EU standards, meaning the government, for example, must abandon a planned ban on foie gras imports.

A second agreement will link the British emissions trading system with its bigger EU cousin, restoring a link severed by Brexit. It is a significant step for industry because EU and UK businesses will be exempt from each other’s charges on high-carbon imports such as steel, cement and electricity. This agreement also requires alignment with relevant EU rules.

The third deal has proved the most contentious: a youth mobility scheme that will give those aged 18-31 the right to live, work, travel and study on both sides of the Channel for up to three years. The scheme may not be up and running until 2030 – which is bad news for anyone in their late 20s.

With less than 24 hours before the summit, the two sides were closing in on a deal. EU negotiators gave up a demand that EU students pay UK rates of university tuition, although they will get a discount on steep international rates. The UK also made a concession, agreeing to allow in 100,000 young EU citizens a year, having previously wanted fewer to keep the numbers down.

To maximise its leverage on tuition fees, the EU is linking the three agreements, warning they must come into force together.

Not on Friday. The UK has been pushing for inclusion in the EU’s Made in Europe plan, a protectionist scheme to favour EU companies when public money is being spent on goods such as electric cars, renewable energy and nuclear technology. Inclusion in Made in Europe is a big priority for the Burnham government, which has dangled major concessions on tuition fees to make sure British firms are not shut out.

The problem for London is that the EU lawmakers have not finalised the Made in Europe law, known as the Industrial Accelerator Act. Many EU member states want to include the UK, but France and the European parliament take a stricter position.

The EU law is not expected to be agreed until spring 2027. To help the British government, which has linked a generous offer on youth mobility with Made in Europe, the European Commission proposed delaying agreement on tuition fees by six months. But member states want precise guarantees now about equal treatment for EU students.

Another problem is that British inclusion in Made in Europe requires the UK to sign up to EU trade-defence policies. The EU, for instance, has imposed high tariffs on “unfairly subsidised” Chinese electric cars and fears the UK, which has not followed suit, could become an alternative route into the EU if Chinese companies set up in Britain and sell into the bloc. The EU is calling on the UK to align with its trade-defence policies, but the UK insists it can guard against circumvention without copying the EU.

In a sign of possible tensions, an earlier summit text proposing a trusted partnership on economic security has been downgraded to a partnership.

Beyond the headline deals, the EU-UK draft summit statement opens the door to British integration in European policies on energy, crime-fighting, educational exchange, tech and foreign policy cooperation.

The next major agreement, targeted for next year, is bringing the UK back into the EU’s internal electricity market. This is a priority for five EU member states: Germany, Belgium, the Netherlands, Denmark and Luxembourg. They argue that Britain’s inclusion in the EU electricity market is essential to take advantage of the nascent green power boom in the North Sea. In return for market access, the UK will be required to contribute to the EU budget to aid the development of poorer EU regions.

Beyond that, the two sides will “explore opportunities” for the UK to join the Erasmus student exchange programme after 2027; the current arrangement applies only for the 2027-8 academic year.

In a win for the UK, the EU appears ready to expand talks on the mutual recognition of professional qualifications, beyond existing negotiations on architects. The sides will sign a digital partnership, which includes joint work on AI safety, and examine how to share data on law enforcement and border management.

Far from it. A lot of diplomatic plates are spinning, but the UK remains outside the EU’s biggest economic clubs: the single market and the customs union. It also has no decision-making powers inside the EU.

Burnham says he will set out the long-term options for the future relationship after this week’s summit. “The hard Brexit we were given is not good enough for Britain,” he said on Monday in a major speech. But amid concerns among some cabinet ministers about reopening Brexit divides, the prime minister appears to be taking a more cautious approach. He said the process would take time and insisted that “at every stage the British people will be the boss”.

The EU has welcomed Britain’s interest in rejoining but is wary of a protracted negotiation that could be reversed by an anti-EU British government.

In an echo of the Brexit years, EU insiders say, once again, that it is up to Britain to define what it wants.
