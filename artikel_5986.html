Trump administration ups ante on aviation funds after ground stop

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Top administration officials doubled down on calls for Congress to supply additional funds for an ongoing air traffic modernization effort after they said old equipment contributed to the need for a nationwide ground stop of flights on Friday.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin increased their ask to $50 billion on Friday night, a day after Duffy and White House budget chief Russ Vought formally requested $30.1 billion.

The increased allotment, which the administration officials didn’t fully flesh out, comes after the nation’s air traffic control system experienced “instability” in its radar and communications at certain facilities earlier in the day, Duffy told reporters.

He said “inconsistency in the data” being issued by those systems led to implementation of the nationwide ground stop, saying the order was given out of “an abundance of caution” before being lifted about four hours later.

During a Friday night White House briefing, Duffy and Mullin explained the issues were driven by “an incredibly old air traffic control system.”

Earlier in the day, the stoppage had caused mass confusion and raised questions about potential terror attacks, or artificial intelligence breakouts resulting from new software the agency is rolling out. While Mullin said “we’re not ruling out anything,” he explained that “the public was never at danger through this process.”

Still, Duffy called the incident “an emergency” while calling for additional modernization funds.

“This is an emergency,” he said. “I want $50 billion. President Trump is a builder. He’s going to build this network and deliver for the American people. But the president can’t deliver for America unless the Congress delivers the money to make it happen.”

The latest figure is $19.1 billion higher than the figure Duffy and Office of Management and Budget Director Russ Vought called on congressional appropriators to approve in a Thursday letter.

Mullin said the new $50 billion figure was an administration estimate for a “complete overhaul of the system” which has been going through a “patchwork” upgrade process that may have overwhelmed the older equipment still being used.

“We need someone to come in and build the whole system,” Mullin said. “Nickel and diming it together does not work.”

Thursday’s call for $30.1 billion echoes a White House-backed bill that Sen. Eric Schmitt, R-Mo., introduced in September, which would provide $10.1 billion for air traffic control upgrades, including $2 billion for “cloud computing and national airspace system telecommunications.”

Those funds would come after $12.5 billion allocated to the effort through the July 2025 reconciliation law that Duffy and Vought called a “down-payment.”

Senate Commerce, Science and Transportation Committee Republicans touted those already-appropriated funds in a social media post. They pointed to $1.9 billion provided in the reconciliation law that would go toward upgrading air route traffic control centers, which guide planes across long distances after departure and which the FAA had cited as the cause of the problem.

The 22 air route traffic control centers across the country guide aircraft using instrument-dependent flight plans. Under such plans, pilots use instruments, electronic navigation and air traffic control assistance to guide them and maintain separation from other aircraft in certain airspaces.

The committee’s majority said Friday’s outage highlighted the need to prioritize modernization of the air route traffic control centers, arguing that President Lyndon B. Johnson was in office the last time a new one was built.

Commerce ranking member Maria Cantwell, D-Wash., said in a statement Friday night that the panel is looking into FAA and airspace telecommunications issues.

“Technology issues related to how planes communicate through primary frequencies, backup frequencies, and other issues will be something the Commerce Committee will be reviewing,” Cantwell said.

Cantwell is poised to take the committee’s gavel come January as Democrats assume the majority in the Senate.

In a joint statement Friday night, House Transportation and Infrastructure Chairman Sam Graves, R-Mo., and ranking member Rick Larsen, D-Wash., called the communication failures “concerning” and said their committee would “thoroughly review the issues that led to this nationwide ground stop.”

Both committees have also been in monthslong negotiations over dueling aviation safety bills responding to the January 2025 midair collision near Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport that killed 67 people.

At issue in those negotiations is which location transmission technology should be mandated to aid in air traffic control operations and allow aircraft to maintain appropriate distance. Leaders have said they hope to reach a deal in the lame-duck session.
