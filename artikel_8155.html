The Best Ice Climbing Gear of 2027: Boots, Tools, Crampons, and More

Overview: The best ice climbing gear of 2027 includes Petzl Dart crampons, La Sportiva Olympus Mons Pro boots, Black Diamond Viper ice tool, the Blue Ice Choucas Pro harness, and the Mountain Equipment Xenith jacket, plus proven picks like the La Sportiva G-Summit boots. Our testers climbed up to WI5+ and M7 from the Canadian Rockies to the Patagonian Andes, in temperatures down to -30°F.

Ice climbing is a high-stakes sport. Do it right, and you’re in for a meditative cruise with just the right level of spice. Do it wrong, and you’ll be left spooked, shivering—or worse. The difference often boils down to your experience level. But right after that comes your ice climbing gear. When you’re 100 feet off the ground, pinned to a silvery ribbon of ice by nothing but four blades of steel, you need absolute trust in your equipment. But how can you tell which products to rely on? That’s our job.

This season, our team of testers tried out 15 pieces of ice climbing gear that are either new for 2026-’27 or came out partway through last winter. Some turned out to be overengineered, overpriced, or poorly built. Others proved thoughtfully designed and dependable in a range of winter conditions. Over thousands of vertical feet of testing everywhere from the Patagonian Andes to the Canadian Rockies, we sorted out the bad from the good. Below: nothing but the best ice climbing gear available this season.

Weight: 11.3 oz (men’s medium) / 9.9 oz (women’s medium)

Pros and Cons + Novel and effective baffle design + Two-way zipper with bottom hem snap + Low-profile, elasticated cuffs + Impressive warmth-to-weight ratio – No way to tighten hood – Gimmicky and impractical hexagonal stuff sack

Mountain Equipment’s all-new Xenith is the big experiment in winter insulation this year. After a research paper suggested hexagons might be the most thermally efficient shape, the brand built the jacket with hexagonal baffles. (These weirdly shaped baffles also seem to limit down migration.)

Our testers found it extremely warm for 11.3 ounces (with 4.3 ounces of 800-fill hydrophobic goose down)—making it the ideal midlayer on -30°F days and nearly unnoticeable clipped to a harness on warmer ones. The Xenith’s low weight comes from its spare design. It has two hand-warming pockets, elasticated cuffs, a two-way zipper, internal drop pockets, a hem-cinch cord, and a hood. Downside: The hood can’t be cinched at all. Without a helmet to fill it, you’ll feel like a kid wearing your parent’s oversized clothes.

Weight: 8.5 oz Sizes: men’s XXS-XXL (the women’s counterpart is the M10 Storm Bib)

Pros and Cons + Surprising stretch and durability + Superior range of motion – No women’s-specific version

The M10 Storm Hardshell offers hands-down the best range of motion we’ve ever experienced in an alpine climbing pant. Thanks to a roomy fit, gusseted crotch, and slightly stretchy fabric—a mix of ripstop nylon, proprietary waterproof laminate, and a slick jersey backer—we were able to step high and wide without feeling constricted—a refreshing departure from the Euro-style, slim-fit trousers that have taken over the market in recent years.

“While transitioning from a panel of limestone to a hanging ice dagger, I stemmed my legs as far as I could between the two mediums and felt zero resistance,” reported Climbing senior editor Anthony Walsh. Some testers reported doing a full split just to test out the mobility. No torn crotches yet.

The rest of the layout is smart but simple. The thigh pocket, big enough to fit a smartphone, and fly are both positioned to remain accessible even with a harness on. “The zipper is on a diagonal rather than vertically-oriented like most other pants, so you can relieve yourself without a harness’s tie-in points or belay loop getting in the way,” Walsh reports. Elasticized cuffs keep moisture out—something we appreciated when spring conditions left our ice pitches streaming with water.

We loved how waterproof the M10s were, too. “I tested the M10s on some of the wettest pitches of my entire season,” said Walsh. “My ‘waterproof’ gloves soon wetted out, and my face had a steady drip of water coming off my nose, but under the M10 pants, I stayed 100 percent dry.” Credit goes to a lightweight, breathable, proprietary membrane and a PFCs-free DWR (durable water repellent) that boasted best-in-test longevity. After eight straight days of fighting up scrappy mixed lines in the Canadian Rockies, both the waterproofing and the fabric remained intact.

Pros and Cons + Legitimately waterproof + Decently breathable + Cuff closure keeps out loose snow – Not very warm

These insulated rubber gloves were originally designed for fishers, but nowadays you’re equally likely to see them on ice climbers, especially on steep water ice in the Pacific Northwest. The Temres 282-02 is the only fully waterproof glove we’ve ever worn, and they climb decently well thanks to a supple, dexterous rubber, and a wool liner that doesn’t migrate much within.

The Temres is not exceedingly warm, but for temps hovering around freezing (or those with excellent circulation) these are a fantastic, cheap option. Otherwise, buy a larger size and add a thin liner glove for deep-winter leads. A note on sizing: Showa gloves run small, so size one up from your typical ice glove.

Pros and Cons + Warmest technical mountaineering boot made + Easy adjustment with BOA system + Surprisingly nimble and lightweight – Expensive – Unnecessarily tall gaiter

Knee-height mountain boots are no longer just for guided Himalayan climbers. La Sportiva’s new Olympus Mons Pro—a 4-pound-4-ounce pair of “8,000-meter boots”—is a game changer for ice and mixed climbers who need serious warmth without sacrificing performance.

Our tester wore them on Field of Dreams (WI5; 250ft), Mixed Master (WI4 M5; 1,000ft), and M7 cragging. “I have generally cold feet yet I was sweating standing around in -5°F in these boots,” he said. “Despite all the insulation, these are plenty nimble for WI5 and M5.” The Olympus Mons Pro might be a couple ounces heavier than La Sportiva’s G-Summit boot, but it is drastically warmer and just as comfortable. Unbelievably, it’s also 7 ounces lighter per pair than the brand’s flagship leather single boot, the Nepal EVO, thanks to Primaloft Gold Aerogel insulation.

The one gripe: an unnecessarily tall gaiter. “With such a long zipper to get in and out of the boot, I’m less likely to adjust the Velcro power strap when I need to,” said one tester. Thankfully the external BOA allows a climber to adjust some of the fit on the fly.

Weight: 1 lb 13 oz (per boot) Sizes: 37-48 (half sizes)

Pros and Cons + Easy to adjust with gloves on + Highly versatile + Ideal fit for small and low-volume feet – Not as durable as they should be for the price

The G-Summit climbing boot accomplishes a small but significant miracle: It’s a double boot that climbs like a single boot. The insulated foam liner—combined with the wrap-around inner shell and external gaiter—creates an envelope that’s warm enough for high-altitude mountaineering, and yet low-profile and precise enough for hard technical climbing up to WI6 and M7. The rubber rand was sticky enough to provide purchase in foot jams on B.C.’s Stanley Headwall, and the Vibram sole gave testers enough grip to cross boulder fields and icy scruff in Norway, Patagonia, and beyond.

“I loved the BOA system for quick tightening mid-pitch and for equally quick loosening when I arrived at the next belay and needed blood to rush into my feet,” said Climbing Senior Editor Anthony Walsh, who was able to operate the dial easily with gloves on. The system eliminated heel-lift, both on-route and off. “I’ve front-pointed 10,000 feet in these boots and walked many horizontal miles. I haven’t received one blister, and yet I can still climb WI6 and M7 in them,” Walsh said.

Our female testers raved about the fit, calling it a serious game-changer in a world dominated by men’s boots. “Not only does this accommodate lower-volume feet, but the BOA ratchets tight enough to keep my heel down,” said one woman. “That allows me to climb a lot harder, for a lot longer.”

The boot isn’t totally waterproof, but its TPU coating, weather-resistant zipper, and water-repellant finishes kept our feet dry after dozens of days on ice and snow. “Lots of approaches in the Rockies have semi-frozen rivers at the base,” said one tester. “I punched through multiple, and never felt humidity or wetness.”

But while the weather resistance easily survives hard use, some of the plastic parts may not. One tester noted a sticky zipper and a peeling toe cap. Another reported that the toe welt quickly shows signs of wear and had to order a replacement after just a few weeks of testing. The toe welts on her second pair have held up fine. Still, if you’re hoping for a boot that will last you for years, you might want to get some reassurances from La Sportiva’s warranty department first.

Pros and Cons + Top-tier durability + Technical aptitude + Out-of-the-box comfort – Not quite warm enough for extreme-altitude objectives

Scarpa’s fan-favorite Phantom Tech HD offers the same climbing precision as the old Phantom Tech, with more warmth, durability, and comfort at just a 2.5-ounce penalty. Body-mapped insulation—micropile, aerogel, and PrimaLoft Gold Eco—kept our feet frostbite-free at -22°F (with vapor barrier socks) near Field, British Columbia, but we wouldn’t quite trust it on Denali. “The rockered outsole has a trail-runner-like feel,” said one tester. “I’ve hiked and skied many miles in ice climbing boots over the years, and the Phantom Tech HD is without a doubt the most comfortable I’ve used.” After a full season, our pair shows no wear or delamination.

Pros and Cons + Packable + Superlight + Five gear loops – Uncomfortable to hang in – Ice clipper locations reduce usability of gear loops

For multi-pitch ice, where hanging belays are rare, our testers gravitate to a minimalist harness like the updated Blue Ice Choucas Pro. Its UHMWPE construction is surprisingly durable for 5.1 ounces, and it’s fully featured: gear loops, a haul loop, four ice clipper slots, and leg loops that open for skis or crampons.

Unfortunately, Blue Ice sewed the clipper slots over the gear loops, so loop real estate feels a bit scarce. Nevertheless, it easily held a full rack of screws on Murchison Falls (WI4+; 600ft), but our tester noticed the unpadded leg loops on the free-hanging 100-foot rappel.

Skip it for cragging or steep terrain; it thrives on big days in the alpine—where a harness’s weight and packability make a real difference.

Pros and Cons + IP56 water resistance + Low operating limit (-20°F) + Invaluable for anchors and belay-station communication – Expensive

An IP56 water-resistance rating and a -20°F lower operating limit make the Rocky Talkies as well suited to ice as to the mountains they’re named for. We found them invaluable for setting up anchors in Ouray Ice Park and for communicating between multi-pitch belay stations. The only knock is the price, though most testers found the durability worth it.

Pros and Cons + Long-lasting dry treatment + Easy handling – No middle mark

If you’re the kind of person who prefers the safety and flexibility of a half/twin rope setup, you’d be hard-pressed to do better than the DYAD 7.7. Our testers used these ropes on everything from Bridal Veil Falls to ice park laps over a full season in Ouray, Colorado, and not even the wettest pitches iced them up or wetted them out. Cons: There’s no middle mark, and the ropes are pricey.

Pros and Cons + Many accessories for specific customization + High-performance ice pick – Shaft shape and handle is not suited for sustained vertical or overhanging pitches

Gently curved ice tools have fallen out of favor for the flashier, radically shaped Black Diamond Hydra, but the updated Viper still has a home in our quiver. Its 2.5-millimeter pick is incredible in ice, but the tool’s standout feature is its customization: it comes with modular headweights, a long alpine spike for snow plunging, a micro spike, a full-size alpine hammer, a micro hammer, and an adze. One tester, an ACMG apprentice alpine guide, found the mellow shape natural for sniffing out thin hooks on slabby, traddy mixed up to M7, versus an aggressive tool like the C.A.M.P. X-Dream. The knuckle-bashing shape isn’t ideal for overhanging mixed or WI5, but it’s a great option for moderate and big-mountain routes.

Weight: 1 lb 9 oz (per pair, with monopoint setup) Sizes: 34-44; 38-49 with extension bar (sold separately)

+ Extremely light + Solid climbing performance + Surprisingly durable + Wide range of adjustability – Aluminum heel doesn’t penetrate dense ice well

With razor-sharp points and a modular design, the Petzl Dart has been our favorite technical crampon for years. Its new aluminum rear section and linking bar save 4 ounces per pair and eliminate the need to jerry-rig fit with Petzl’s Cord-Tec Kit.

Testers climbed up to WI5+ and M7 with zero flex or shifting. Like the all-steel Dart, it’s simple to switch between mono- and dual-point layouts, and interchangeable toe bails customize the Dart for any boot, welts or not. Testers used these ‘pons on everything from Bridalveil Falls (WI5+) in Telluride and hard mixed cragging to big Canadian Rockies north faces, including Mount Robson and Mount Temple.

The geometry of the front point is just downturned enough to hook securely into ice, but not so aggressive that you have to adjust your technique to get a solid stick. In fact, most testers found they hardly had to double-kick at all to get a quick, reliable bite.

“The secondary points are far enough back that they don’t get in your way when you just want to use your front point, but forward enough to engage when you want them to,” said one tester, who wore them on more than 100 pitches this winter. He also found them blessedly intuitive to walk in: “Even when hiking up and down the Ouray Ice Park trails or navigating a mixture of rocks and ice between backcountry pitches, the Darts kept me secure and balanced.”

Cons: Honestly, not many. One climber found the newly slimmed-down heel lever tough to operate with thick gloves on. Another found the metal buckle fussy—though Petzl’s sold-separately Elastic Strap ($26) fixes that issue with a surprisingly durable plastic buckle.

Pros and Cons + Triple-grip handle + Flexibility and reach in steep terrain – Too aggressive for pure ice – Handle isn’t as ergonomically contoured as other triple-grip models

One climbing coach and U.S. Ice Climbing Team member used the X-Dream Total Dry on hard mixed routes in Ouray and D14 king lines in the Italian Dolomites. The first widely available commercial tool with a triple-grip handle, it gives you tons of flexibility and reach in steep terrain. But it’s too aggressive for pure ice, and the handle isn’t as ergonomically contoured as on other triple-grip models. Thankfully, the X-Dream’s all-around handle is available separately.

Pros and Cons + Modular head weights + Ultra-sharp picks + Adjustable handle size – Geometry isn’t aggressive enough for seriously overhung terrain

An impeccably balanced swing, ultra-precise pick, and a high degree of modularity make the Black Diamond Hydra our tool of choice for moderate and vertical ice. Interchangeable screw-on head weights let us dial in balance for any terrain, and the razor-thin picks—2.5 millimeters at the tip versus a 3- to 3.5-millimeter industry average—place like a dream. “I certainly appreciated being able to adjust the handle size to accommodate the difference,” said one tester, who swapped between thin dry-tooling gloves and thick, three-finger mitts. The geometry isn’t aggressive enough for overhung mixed, though, like the crux ice-dagger transfer on Dark Nature (WI5+ M5/6).

Pros and Cons + Lightweight + Durable steel + Seamless rotation + Easy to place – Spring-loaded crank knobs are sometimes overeager

We occasionally steer clear of aluminum screws because we find they bind up in plasticky ice. But the featherweight, all-steel Blue Ice Aeros are easily the next best thing. The lightest steel screw on the market, the Aeros are easy to start, easy to clean, and rack neatly on a harness. The three-tooth design seems to bite more reliably than four-toothed models, and we found the Aeros never bound up, even in warm, snowy conditions in Colorado’s Ouray Ice Park.

Ease of racking was another fan-favorite perk. You can fit six screws on a single clipper as opposed to Black Diamond or Petzl’s four to five. Credit goes to the low-profile aluminum hangers, which Blue Ice managed to trim down without sacrificing the size of the crank knob or the length of the hanger.

“The hangers are long enough to provide plenty of torque to easily turn the screw, and they’re simple to manipulate with gloves,” said one Colorado-based tester after a season of backcountry ascents in the San Juans.

The Aeros also offer a larger barrel diameter than any other screw we’ve tried, which means you can thread them into old screw holes in a pinch and still get a tight, reliable placement. The larger diameter also came in handy while setting up rappels.

“V-threading was a breeze with the Aero, providing a wide window of ice to thread the rope and get it back out with my V-thread tool,” reported one tester.

The only thing we didn’t like was the spring-loaded crank knobs. While some testers loved that they snap back into the screw automatically, others found that the snapping sometimes occurred prematurely, pinching their fingers or gloves in the process.

Buy in this order: boots, layers, gloves, then a big belay parka. Crampons, ice tools, and screws are easy to rent or borrow while you’re learning. Boots and apparel aren’t, and they matter most to your comfort on the ice.

Start with boots, because fit is everything.

Buy safety essentials new. That means ropes, harnesses, and helmets. It’s hard to tell if used gear has been compromised, and trustworthy safety gear is the cheapest insurance policy you’ll ever buy.

Gear reviews are highly subjective, but we do our best to combat that by taking a slightly more scientific approach to testing ice climbing gear. At the beginning of each season, we wade through sheaves of PR requests, consider the specs and promise of each one, and then carefully select the gear we deem fit to test. Then, we do our utmost to get each piece in the hands of as many testers as we can—and in as varied conditions as the team can manage.

This year, testers picked and kicked their way up everything from steep ice in Colorado’s San Juans to long mixed lines on Mount Robson. Samples traveled from Denali to the Italian Dolomites to the Patagonian Andes and back again. We belayed through blizzards, swung into bullet-hard ice, whooped over perfect sticks, and cursed through some of the wettest pitches of our lives. At the end of the year, we compared notes and selected only the best ice climbing gear to review. Those are the products you’ll see here.
