Alterations in the microbial composition of the human gut have been associated with a wide range of chronic diseases1,2. Yet species-level composition alone can obscure extensive within-species genetic diversity and functional variation. This has driven a shift towards strain-resolved, genetic-level analysis of the microbiome, leveraging microbial genetic variation to identify disease-relevant genes and pathways3,4,5,6. Here we conducted a phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) analysing 12.8 million gut microbial single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) with 244 host health phenotypes and environmental exposures in 10,781 individuals from 6 independent cohorts. We identify 940 independent associations across 69 host factors, including age, sex and cardiovascular and metabolic parameters. Structural analyses identified host-phenotype-associated SNVs in functionally important regions of proteins, findings that inform mechanistic hypotheses about how microbial genetic changes relate to environmental and host physiological factors. For example, we identified an age-associated SNV in Faecalibacterium prausnitzii that affects an amino acid within the protein pocket that binds glutathione. To support future efforts, all study summary statistics are provided in an online catalogue (https://fulab.web.rug.nl/SNV-MWAS/). Together, these results identify new biological connections between the human host and microbial genetic variants at base pair resolution and provide a resource for hypothesis generation that will aid future understanding of the strain-level functionality of the gut microbiota.

The gut microbiome exhibits large variations in its composition and genetic landscape. Within-species genetic variation underlies strain differences in fitness, metabolic capacities, pathogenicity and other biological properties. Thus, the field of microbiome research is moving towards strain-level or genetic-level dissection of the microbial contributions to human health (Supplementary Information). Structural variations in the gut microbiome have been linked to human body mass index (BMI), bile acid concentrations, the human plasma metabolome and host genetics, thereby identifying bacterial genes involved in glucose metabolism, bile acid biosynthesis and biotransformation, and N-acetylgalactosamine utilization3,4,5,6.

However, gut microbial genomes also contain abundant SNVs that can modulate microbial function and host interactions. For example, a SNV in the blc gene of Escherichia coli disrupts the intestinal barrier by decreasing the amount of lysophosphatidylethanolamine, a metabolite that aids remission of inflammatory bowel disease7. In the stomach, a SNV in Helicobacter pylori increases gastric cancer risk by enhancing the proteolytic activity of the serine protease HtrA (ref. 8). In Bacteroides coprocola, SNVs in glycosyl hydrolase genes have been associated with type 2 diabetes9. A SNV-based metagenome-wide association study (SNV-MWAS) in an Israeli population identified BMI-associated SNVs that were replicated in a geographically independent Dutch cohort10. Despite these examples, systematic explorations of the relationships between gut microbial SNVs and host phenotypes have not, to our knowledge, been carried out at phenome-wide scale. Such analyses require large-scale human cohort data with both metagenomic and deep phenotypic data, and they need to overcome a number of technical challenges. For example, microbial species exhibit complex population genetic structures, and these strong lineage effects may lead to false discoveries in association studies if population structure is not properly controlled.

In this study, we compiled shotgun metagenomic sequencing data of 11,831 faecal samples from 5 independent cohorts from the Netherlands and a cohort from Tanzania. Gut microbial genotypes were characterized using GT-Pro11, yielding 12.8 million SNVs in core genomic regions. We further developed a linear mixed-model (LMM)-based analysis workflow that controls for within-species population genetic structure to ensure that SNV–phenome associations detected are independent from the cumulative effect of strain lineage. We then conducted a PheWAS to identify associations between common metagenomic SNVs and 244 host phenotypic and exposure factors, using LMM in each cohort, followed by meta-analysis. In total, we detected 940 independent association signals supported by multiple cohorts that link 39,663 SNVs across 101 species to 69 host phenotypes or exposures, and the full set of results are available in an online catalogue. We also performed protein structural analyses to interpret the biological relevance of these associations.

We compiled faecal metagenomic sequencing data for 11,831 samples from 10,781 individuals from 6 cohorts (Fig. 1). Five cohorts are from the Netherlands—the Dutch Microbiome Project (DMP; n = 7,955)12, Lifelines-DEEP (LLD; n = 1,135, plus 340 followed up after an average of 4 years (designated LLD1 for baseline samples and LLD2 for follow-up samples))3,13, the 500-Functional Genomics Project (500FG; n = 529)14,15, the 1000 Inflammatory Bowel Disease Project (IBD; n = 544)16 and 300-Obesity (300OB; n = 298)15,17—and one is from Tanzania (300-Tanzania Functional Genomics Project (300TZFG), n = 320)18 (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). For LLD2 and 500FG, we have additional datasets from some of the same individuals (nLLD2 = 251, nFG500 = 459) with samples processed using both DNA extraction methods19 that allowed us to assess the effect of extraction protocol on SNV profiling and the robustness of microbial genotyping (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Note 1). The extensive phenotypic information for the corresponding samples was also curated and harmonized. We integrated 244 phenotypic and exposure factors (92 binary and 152 continuous or ordinal traits) available in the DMP or in at least 2 other cohorts for analysis (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 2). Among these, 131 phenotypes were available for at least 2 cohorts, whereas 113 were only available for the DMP. Across the whole dataset, the sample size per phenotype ranged from 593 to 11,706 (Supplementary Table 2).

We genotyped 50,945,657 biallelic SNVs in the core genome of 881 species using GT-Pro11, which conducts reference-panel-based SNV detection using k-mer searching and exact matching. The number of SNVs detected in the DMP was higher than in other cohorts, probably owing to its larger sample size (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). The total number of SNVs detected in at least 1 sample for each species ranged from 1 to 342,230 (mean = 58,131) (Extended Data Fig. 1c). The average density of SNVs in the core genomic region of gut bacteria was 32 SNVs per kilobase pair (Extended Data Fig. 1d), which is close to previous reports20. To keep only informative species for downstream analysis, we selected ‘common’ SNVs that were present in more than 10% of samples and had a minor allele frequency (MAF) of more than 5% in at least one cohort. Among the 50.95 million SNVs that were detected, 12,761,497 SNVs were common (Supplementary Table 3). As the distribution of common SNV counts per species was bimodal, with two peaks separated by approximately 1,000 SNVs (Extended Data Fig. 1e), we selected species with at least 1,000 common SNVs, resulting in 433 species with 12,706,564 common SNVs (Fig. 2b, Extended Data Fig. 1f,g and Supplementary Table 4). These 433 species collectively accounted for, on average, 84.3% (s.d. = 9.4%) of the total abundance of the gut microbial community. After filtering, 300TZFG had the highest number of species for analysis (327) and the most common SNVs (7,369,266) (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 1f–h). The average density of common SNV sites in the core genomic region of gut bacteria was 16 SNVs per kilobase pair (Extended Data Fig. 1i). The within-species population structure of the 433 species and the effect of technical factors are described extensively in Supplementary Notes 2 and 3 (Supplementary Figs. 1–4 and Supplementary Tables 5–8). We also pre-calculated the Bonferroni-controlled threshold for study-wide significance by dividing 0.05 by the total number of phenotype–SNV pairs tested (922,497,753), yielding a threshold of 5.42 × 10−11 (Methods).

To understand the functional consequences of SNVs, we annotated genes from reference genomes and predicted the potential functional effect of SNVs on bacterial gene products (synonymous, missense, start or stop codon, and non-protein-coding)21 (Fig. 2c, Extended Data Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 9). Most SNVs (11,819,660, 93.02%) were located in protein-coding regions, including 9,579,907 (75.39%) synonymous, 2,216,969 (17.45%) missense and 22,784 (0.18%) start or stop codon variants (Fig. 2c). The distribution of the proportion of codon-altering SNVs (missense or start or stop codon SNVs) varied across different bacterial species (Extended Data Fig. 2), reflecting the genetic diversity and evolutionary pressures experienced by these species.

We next performed an SNV-MWAS of 244 factors by linking them to 12 million common SNVs using LMMs fitted separately for each species, phenotype and cohort. These LMMs controlled for population structure by including the species-level between-sample genetic linkage matrix as a random effect. Age, sex, species abundance and clean read count were included as fixed-effect covariates, except in the analyses for age and sex themselves. Given that reference and alternative alleles may coexist within a sample with differing abundances, we encoded gut microbial genotypes as a dosage value ranging from 0 to 1 to represent the frequency of the alternative allele.

Following the per-cohort SNV-MWAS, we conducted a meta-analysis weighted by sample size for the SNV–phenotype pairs available in at least two cohorts to integrate the SNV-MWAS results derived from different cohorts (Fig. 1). This included only the independent datasets—that is, excluding LLD2 and 500FG-APK. This analysis identified 49,346 significant and robust associations that passed the study-wide significance cut-off (meta-analysis P < 5.42 × 10−11; Extended Data Fig. 3 and Methods) and were supported by at least 2 cohorts based on a permutation-based robustness test (cohort permutation P < 0.05 with 999 permutations), with the same association direction. These associations involved 39,663 SNVs of 101 species and 69 phenotypes.

As a resource for further validation and hypothesis generation, we have constructed a catalogue (https://fulab.web.rug.nl/SNV-MWAS/) that includes all 49,346 robust associations as well as the suggestive signals that passed the species-wide significance level (species-specific, defined by Bonferroni correction based on the number of tested SNVs within each species; Methods) in the meta-analysis and the largest cohort DMP (see examples in Supplementary Note 4 and Supplementary Fig. 5).

Most SNV associations (85.76%) were supported by two cohorts, and 14.24% were supported by three or more cohorts (Fig. 3a). Although all associated SNVs showed a similar ratio of start or stop codon or missense SNVs (17.49%) compared with the background (all common SNVs), six phenotypes were significantly enriched for start or stop codon or missense SNVs (χ2 test P < 0.05): Lifelines protein diet score, on-a-diet behaviour during the past month, thrombocyte count, waist circumference, β-blocker intake and sex (Extended Data Fig. 4). SNVs in the non-coding region may affect gene expression through upstream regulators. For example, variants located approximately 10 bp upstream of the start codon may affect the ribosome-binding site or other elements involved in translation initiation, thereby altering bacterial protein expression22. We further examined the distribution patterns of common and phenotype-associated SNVs in gene upstream regions. The common SNV count showed a drop in −10 bp regions (Fig. 3b), and the ratio of SNVs with at least one association showed an uneven distribution, with a drop at −11 bp and two peaks at −55 bp and −83 bp (Fig. 3c).

As SNVs can be correlated due to linkage disequilibrium in the genomes, we conducted a grouping analysis to select the most significant SNVs within each linkage disequilibrium block as representative tags of the independent signals (between-SNV r2 < 0.01; Methods). We then identified 940 independent SNV-MWAS signals (Fig. 3d,e and Supplementary Table 10) and 13,198 SNVs with a high probability of contribution to phenotypic variation (posterior inclusion probability > 0.8) (Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Fig. 6). Power assessment found that 70.5% of signals have more than 0.80 power to be detected given the observed effect size, sample size, and MAF (Supplementary Note 6 and Supplementary Fig. 7) and showed a negative correlation between MAF and effect size (Fig. 3f, R = −0.29, P = 6.06 × 10−20). We also assessed replication across cohorts of different ancestries. Among the 940 signals, 215 had both SNVs and phenotypes available in the Dutch and Tanzanian cohorts, with 161 (74.9%) signals involving 8 different phenotypes supported by both cohorts. For example, 48 association signals of 23 species with age are supported by Dutch and Tanzanian cohorts (Extended Data Fig. 5).

Notably, among the 940 SNV–phenotype association signals, 65.74% showed no significant associations at the abundance level (linear regression, Bonferroni-corrected meta-analysis P > 0.05; Fig. 3d,e). To illustrate the added value of SNV associations, we systematically assessed the phenotypic associations with the gut microbiome at the species abundance, SNV and structural variant levels. Among the 62,464 species–phenotype pairs (Methods), 533 showed associations at SNV level, 460 (86.30%) showed associations only at SNV level, and just 5 (0.9%) showed associations at all 3 levels (Extended Data Fig. 6 and Supplementary Tables 11–13). These results suggest that SNV-based analyses provide complementary information to structural variant- and abundance-based analyses when investigating host–microbiome associations4,5,23.

Among 940 signals, 810 were linked with intrinsic factors and 130 with exposure factors (Supplementary Note 7). We found 152 pairwise signal combinations involving 26 intrinsic phenotypes (excluding age, sex and height), in which the same tag SNVs were associated with 1 or more exposure factors (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Table 14). To assess whether exposure factors confounded the association signals of the intrinsic factors, we included the exposure factors as covariates in a robust association test using LMM-based permutation tests (999 permutations) for the association signals shared between intrinsic phenotypes and exposure factors. Owing to data completeness requirements (phenotype, exposure and SNV all had to be observed in at least 100 samples), we could evaluate 66 signals. Of these, 54 (81.8%) remained significant in at least 2 cohorts after controlling for the exposure factors (permutation P < 0.05), suggesting that the exposure factors did not confound the majority of the SNV associations with intrinsic phenotypes.

Age showed the highest number of association signals (99 signals) involving 8,117 SNVs across 42 species (meta-analysis P < 5.42 × 10−11) (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Table 10). We next assessed how well microbial SNVs could predict host age (Methods). To this end, we trained a machine learning model in the DMP cohort and evaluated its predictive performance in the other cohorts. The model explained 12.8–42.0% of the variation in age (R2) in the test datasets (Supplementary Notes 8 and 9, Supplementary Figs. 8–10 and Supplementary Tables 15 and 16), suggesting the predictive potential of gut microbial genetics for host age.

The most robust signal was supported by four independent cohorts (meta P < 5.42 × 10−11; Fig. 4a, Extended Data Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 10). This signal contains five SNVs in the genome of F. prausnitzii_I (species ID: 102274), and their associations were not confounded by other microbial features, including bacterial lineage, species abundance and structural variants (Extended Data Fig. 8 and Supplementary Note 10). Notably, all five age-associated SNVs are missense variants located in the sarA_2 gene (gene ID: 102274_01471). The most significant SNV was 102274_1617247 (effect allele: T, effect allele frequency = 17.38%, β = 4.93, meta-analysis P = 1.00 × 10−17), showing that individuals carrying the T allele were 4.9 ± 0.6 years older (mean ± s.d.) than those without the T allele (Supplementary Table 10). The linkage disequilibrium analysis showed that the lead SNV 102274_1617247 is linked with the other four missense SNVs (R2 > 0.2; Fig. 4a,b) in sarA_2, and significant signals were detected in all cohorts except 300OB (Fig. 4c,d). Notably, all 300OB participants are older than 50 years and show a higher frequency of age-associated alleles compared with the other four Dutch cohorts, which have younger average ages (Extended Data Fig. 9). In other words, the age-associated allele in the sarA_2 gene of F. prausnitzii_I was enriched in an older population. Moreover, replication of the association across the Dutch and Tanzanian cohorts (Fig. 4d) suggests convergent genetic changes during host ageing in different ethnic groups.

The sarA_2 gene is a homologue of the oppA gene, which encodes an oligopeptide-binding lipoprotein with a Tat pathway signal sequence in its N terminus. The protein product of sarA_2 also forms an outer-membrane unit of an ATP-binding cassette (ABC)-type transporter system responsible for the uptake of oligopeptides—the oligopeptide permease (OPP) complex (Fig. 4b). We thus hypothesized that the age-associated missense SNVs may influence the function of the SarA_2 protein (also known as OppA). To test this, we assembled the OPP complex based on the AlphaFold3 prediction, including the five subunits encoded by sarA, oppB_4, oppC_4, oppD_4 and oppF_4 (Fig. 4e). This structural comparative analysis revealed that the predicted OPP structure is similar to the substrate-binding protein-dependent ABC transporter systems of Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37Rv (Protein Data Bank (PDB): 8JA7)24 and E. coli (PDB: 6CVL)25, supporting the plausibility of the predicted ABC transporter architecture. The sectional view of the predicted OPP complex structure revealed a tunnel-like cavity, with the OppA subunit acting as a gate for the transportation tunnel (Fig. 4e). Notably, the age-associated SNVs we identify are located in the cavity of OppA that relates to the tunnel (Fig. 4f), which suggests that these SNVs may affect the transportation function of the protein complex.

To infer the potential ligand of the OPP complex and better understand the underlying age-related changes in transport function, we examined the functional annotations of each subunit of the complex in detail. The OppF_4 subunit, encoded by the gene 102274_01467, was annotated as a GsiA subunit of the transporter for glutathione, an antioxidant that can prevent the damage caused by oxidative stress for both eukaryotes and prokaryotes. We then compared the protein sequences of OppA, OppB_4, OppC_4, OppD_4 and OppF_4 with 3,239 existing protein sequences of the ABC-type glutathione transport system and found that all 5 subunits match existing protein sequences (E-value < 1 × 10−10; Methods), indicating that the OPP complex may function as a glutathione transport system. In silico molecular docking analysis further revealed that glutathione can bind to the pocket region of the OppA protein (Fig. 4g and Extended Data Fig. 10). This suggests that age-associated mutations may affect glutathione uptake efficiency and help F. prausnitzii_I better resist ageing-related oxidative stress in the intestinal environment.

Motivated by the structural characterization of age-associated SNVs in sarA_2, we further systematically depicted the effect of associated SNVs in relation to protein structures. To this end, we focused on 6,799 associated missense SNVs, which are related to 3,724 proteins. We retrieved high-confidence three-dimensional structures of 2,141 (57.49%) proteins with 3,705 associated missense SNVs from the ESM Metagenomic Atlas26 and AlphaFoldDB27. We then systematically characterized these SNVs in relation to structural properties, including relative solvent accessibility (RSA, how exposed a residue is to a solvent), distance to ligand (DTL, distance from the residue to the nearest predicted pocket centre) and distance to protein–protein interaction interface (DTPPI, distance to the closest residue within a predicted protein–protein interface region) (Methods). We designated SNVs in low-DTPPI regions (DTPPI < 10 Å) or low-DTL regions (DTL < 15 Å) as structurally important28,29,30,31,32. Out of 3,705 SNVs, 752 (20.3%) were structurally important by these criteria, including 246 and 528 SNVs affecting amino acid residues in low-DTL and low-DTPPI regions, respectively (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table 17). These SNVs were associated with 40 different phenotypes (Fig. 5b,c).

Age was associated with 165 structurally important SNVs in 122 genes (Fig. 5d,e). Among these, 54 were identified to be low-DTL SNVs, including 3 of the 5 missense SNVs in the OPP subunit encoded by sarA_2. In addition, seven low-DTL SNVs were found to be located near a pocket region of the serpin domain-containing protein (gene ID: 104463_01487) in Vescimonas sp900548615 (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 18). Previous evidence suggested that microbial serpins can inhibit host serine proteases, thereby affecting many physiological processes, such as inflammatory responses33,34. Moreover, 114 age-associated SNVs were identified as low-DTPPI SNVs, including 7 SNVs in the Vescimonas sp003539495 gene (gene ID: 102612_00172) encoding an HTH marR-type domain-containing protein located in the protein–protein interaction region (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Table 18). The bacterial marR homologues are transcriptional regulators that can regulate the expression of many genes related to oxidative stress response35. The marR protein functions as a homodimer, and the age-associated mutations with zero DTPPI we identified were located in the dimerization interface where the two marR monomers directly interact (Fig. 5f,g). To assess how these SNVs may influence marR dimerization, we performed an in silico alanine scanning mutagenesis analysis, systematically replacing each amino acid residue with alanine and quantifying the changes in binding free energy. The age-associated SNVs were clustered in regions showing substantial shifts in binding affinity in the alanine scan (Fig. 5h,i), indicating that they may alter marR dimerization and consequently its regulatory activity. Collectively, this structure-informed analysis highlights how integrating protein-level information can pinpoint potential functional consequences of SNVs, thereby facilitating prioritization of variants for experimental validation.

Over the past decade, genome-wide association studies (GWASs) and metagenomics-based microbiome studies have enhanced our understanding of the roles of host genetics and the symbiotic microbiome in host health. However, the application of GWASs to metagenomic data remains limited. In this study, we took advantage of the growing number of large-scale, deeply phenotyped cohorts with gut microbial metagenomic sequencing data to conduct a PheWAS on 244 phenotype and exposure factors through a gut microbial SNV-MWAS. This identified 940 independent meta-analysis association signals between SNVs of 101 gut microbial species and 69 human phenotypes and exposures. All associations are detailed in an online catalogue (https://fulab.web.rug.nl/SNV-MWAS/).

To carry out this study, we had to resolve a few technical challenges. First, metagenomic sequencing can result in a high genotype missing rate for low-abundance species when sequencing coverage is insufficient. Second, construction of a population genetic structure based on metagenomic data, rather than on isolate assemblies, is problematic owing to the possibility of within-species strain mixture within the host individual20,36,37. We thus adapted several solutions to make our work feasible and plausible. We first filtered out species with insufficient genotype information. We then retained sample pairs with sufficient overlap in SNVs to permit construction of population genetic structure based on kinship. Further, to assess whether a sample was very likely to be dominated by a single main strain within the corresponding species, we adapted the idea of phaseability37,38 to estimate the phased haplotypes on the basis of the distribution of allele frequencies (quasi-phaseability). We then retained only quasi-phaseable samples that were dominated by a single main strain for the association analysis.

Out of 940 study-wide significant signals, 482 (51.3%) carry at least one missense change. It is notable, but perhaps not surprising, that a large proportion of associated bacterial SNVs are located in the coding region but do not alter the protein sequence. It is already known from human GWASs that most single-nucleotide polymorphisms associated with complex traits and diseases lie in non-coding regions or mark synonymous substitutions39,40,41. These single-nucleotide polymorphisms have been found to be more likely to have regulatory roles in gene expression or to serve as proxies for causal variants. Although functional exploration of synonymous substitutions in bacterial genomes remains challenging, previous evidence suggests that synonymous changes can themselves affect gene expression or protein folding. Synonymous substitutions have been shown to alter mRNA structures and protein levels42, to perturb co-translational protein folding in E. coli43, and to influence translational efficiency by altering the secondary structure of mRNA in Salmonella enterica44. Future studies may explore the effect of synonymous SNVs on expression or folding using metatranscriptome or ribosome profiling readouts, along with targeted perturbations in representative isolates.

This study provides a catalogue of microbial SNVs associated with various human phenotypes, offering a valuable resource for validation and hypothesis generation. However, our resource primarily includes samples from the Netherlands, with the DMP cohort supplying nearly three-quarters of samples. This imbalance amplifies the discovery power of the DMP and raises the spectre of winner’s curse inflation in the effect sizes inferred from that cohort alone45. Although we included the Tanzanian cohort to test for replication of associations in a genetically and geographically distant population, its smaller sample size limits the power of the replication analysis. In future, more large-scale cohorts with diverse genetic and lifestyle backgrounds should be integrated to enhance gut microbial SNV-MWAS in a multi-ancestry context, especially including non-European populations46. Moreover, the biological interpretation of these signals currently remains limited. Although we applied structural biology to assess the functional importance of SNVs, we need to take several further key steps to move from association signals to hypotheses. These include identification of causal variants; molecular readouts through metatranscriptomics, metaproteomics and metabolomics; and phenotypic readouts of microorganisms (such as growth rate, biofilm formation and energy utilization). Looking further ahead, experimental validation will be required to confirm the underlying mechanisms, including approaches such as culturing bacterial isolates with different genetic variants on human induced pluripotent stem cell-based organ-on-a-chip models to assess the effect of bacterial SNVs on human cells1,47.

The levels of 15 bile acids and C4 concentrations in fasting plasma were quantified by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) procedures, as previously described52,53. In brief, for sample preparation, 25 µl plasma was mixed with 250 µl internal standard solution to precipitate proteins. Samples were centrifuged at 15,800g, and the supernatant poured into a clean glass tube. The fluid was evaporated under nitrogen at 40 °C. Before measuring, samples were reconstituted in 200 µl 50% methanol in water. For the quantitative determination of bile acids, we used a Nexera X2 Ultra High-Performance Liquid Chromatography system (Shimadzu), coupled to a SCIEX QTRAP 4500 MD triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (SCIEX) (UHPLC–MS/MS). The LC–MS/MS system is controlled by Analyst MD 1.6.2 software. Bile acids were separated with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 Column (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 VanGuard Pre-Column (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm × 5 mm) (Waters). The proportions of 15 bile acids (with suffix ‘_p’) were calculated by dividing by total bile acid concentration. Additionally, eight different total bile acid concentrations and ratios were calculated: (1) total bile acid (Total_BAs) = sum of all bile acid concentrations; (2) total primary bile acid (Total_primary_BAs) = sum of all primary bile acid concentrations; (3) total secondary bile acid (Total_secondary_BAs) = sum of all secondary bile acid concentrations; (4) ratio of secondary bile acids to primary bile acids (Secondary_primary_ratio) = Total_secondary_BAs/Total_primary_BAs; (5) ratio of CA to CDCA concentrations (CA_CDCA_ratio) = (CA + TCA + GCA)/(CDCA + TCDCA + GCDCA); (6) ratio of unconjugated bile acid to conjugated bile acid concentrations = (CA + CDCA + DCA + LCA)/(TCA + GCA + TCDCA + GCDCA + TDCA + GDCA + TLCA + GLCA); (7) ratio of dehydroxylated CA to deconjugated CA concentrations (CA_dehydro_deconju_ratio) = (DCA + TDCA + GDCA)/(CA + TCA + GCA); and (8) ratio of taurine-conjugated bile acid to glycine-conjugated bile acid concentrations (Taurine_glycine_ratio) = (TCA + TCDCA + TDCA + TLCA)/(GCA + GCDCA + GDCA + GLCA).

Telomere length was assessed using an automated multicolour flow–fluorescence in situ hybridization (Flow-FISH) technique as previously described62,63. In brief, white blood cells were obtained by lysing erythrocytes in whole blood with NH4Cl. These white blood cells were then combined with bovine thymocytes, which have a known telomere length and serve as an internal reference. Signal joint excision circles produced during T cell development (sjTREC) expression was quantified using quantitative PCR (qPCR) as previously described63. In brief, DNA was extracted from whole blood using proteinase K digestion, phenol extraction, and isopropanol precipitation. We then used TaqMan qPCR on a ViiA 7 system (Life Technologies) to quantify sjTREC expression. The qPCR was performed on 75–100 ng of DNA in a 12 μl reaction mix with 18 µM of sjTREC primers (5′-TCGTGAGAACGGTGAATGAAG-3′ and 5′-CCATGCTGACACCTCTGGTT-3′) and albumin primers (5′-TGAACAGGCGACCATGCTT-3′ and 5′-CTCTCCTTCTCAGAAAGTGTGCATAT-3′) and 5 µM probes for sjTREC (5′-(FAM) CACGGTGATGCATAGGCACCTGC-3′ (TAMRA)) and albumin (5′-(FAM) TGCTGAAACATTCACCTTCCATGCAGA -3′ (TAMRA)). The PCR protocol was 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Reactions were done in duplicate. Fluorescence from probe cleavage was measured at the threshold cycle (CT). Valid results had a duplicate s.d. ≤ 1. Normalized sjTREC expression was calculated from the difference between average CT values of albumin and sjTREC.

We removed host genome-contaminated reads and low-quality reads from the raw metagenomic sequencing data using KneadData (v0.7.4), Bowtie2 (v2.3.4.3)64 and Trimmomatic (v0.39)65. In brief, the data-cleaning procedure includes two main steps: (1) filtering out human genome-contaminated reads by aligning raw reads to the human reference genome (GRCh37–hg19) and (2) removing adaptor sequences and low-quality reads using Trimmomatic with default settings (SLIDINGWINDOW:4:20 MINLEN:50).

We estimated the abundances of reference genomes in metagenomic samples using CoverM (v0.7.0)66 based on the identical reference genomes used in metagenomic SNV detection.

Structural variants were defined as within-species highly variable genomic fragments (>1 kb) across microbial genomes. Structural variants of the gut microbiome were detected from cleaned metagenomic reads using SGVFinder (v2.0)4 based on the identical reference genomes used by metagenomic SNV detection.

We characterized the SNVs for metagenomic samples using GT-Pro (v1.0.1) based on the pre-constructed sck-mer database11. In brief, a catalogue of 104 million biallelic SNVs in the core genomic region of 909 gut microbial species was initially constructed based on Unified Human Gastrointestinal Genome (UHGG) assemblies, the genotypes of the gut microbial SNVs were tagged by species-specific k-mers, and a k-mer-based gut microbiome metagenomic SNV panel was constructed. GT-Pro directly conducted genotyping for gut microbiota from metagenomic sequencing data by k-mer-searching and matching the genotype k-mers of the metagenomic SNV panel. GT-Pro output was further converted to SNV dosage format, with genotype indicating the frequency of the alternative allele.

We calculated allele frequencies based explicitly on the average reference or alternative allele proportions across samples:

Ri = relative abundance (proportion) of the reference allele in sample i (for example, 0.7)

Ai = relative abundance (proportion) of the alternative allele in sample i (for example, 0.3)

These values typically sum to 1 per sample if only two alleles are considered.

We then performed quality control in each cohort separately, filtering out SNVs with a high missing rate (>90%) or low MAF (<0.05) to get the cohort-specific common SNVs. We next counted the number of SNVs retained for each species in each cohort and removed species with a low common SNV number (<1,000). The manipulation and quality control of genotype datasets were conducted using PLINK2 (v2.0.0-a.6.9LM)67.

The genes of gut microbial reference genomes were predicted and annotated using Bakta68 (v1.10.1, database v5.1). The functional effects of the common SNVs were annotated using SnpEff (v5.1f)21.

For each species, we calculated the species-specific between-sample genetic dissimilarity Di,j measured by a Manhattan distance scaled by the number of shared SNVs within each sample pair using the parallelDist() function in R package parallelDist (v0.2.6). Sample pairs sharing <1,000 SNVs were skipped for the genetic dissimilarity calculation, and the corresponding Di,j were marked as missing values. To maximize the size of the retained sample with no missing value, we removed the sample with the highest number of missing values from the matrix in each round until there are no missing values in the matrix. We further calculated the between-sample genetic relatedness, Ri,j = 1 – Di,j, and the matrix MR containing Ri,j was used as the representative of population genetic structure. We performed principal coordinate analysis on the genetic dissimilarity matrices MD using the pcoa() function from R package ape (v5.7-1) with the parameter correction = “cailliez”.

To depict the linkage disequilibrium (LD) structure overview of each species, we randomly extracted 1,000 SNVs from the cleaned genotype dataset using plink2 (v2.0.0-a.6.9LM) with the parameters -thin-count 1000. We then calculated the LD as a squared correlation for all pairs of extracted SNVs using plink2 with the parameters -r2 inter-chr yes-really -ld-window-r2 0. We visualized the whole-genome LD structure using LDBlockShow (v1.40)69 with the parameters -SeleVar 2 -NoShowLDist 100000000 -OutPdf -OutPng.

The co-existence of multiple strains of a species within the host may bias the position of the sample in the population genetic structure and decrease the power of association analysis. For a given species, samples in which > 80% of the SNVs have the same allele in > 90% of the reads were considered quasi-phaseable, meaning the sample is very likely to be dominated by one strain of the corresponding species. The criterion and concept of quasi-phaseability were described before38,70.

We first manually integrated and curated the phenotypes from different cohorts and datasets to ensure the consistency of units. Each phenotype was analysed with a LMM that contained: (1) fixed effects for corresponding species abundance, age, sex and sequencing depth, and (2) a random-effect term capturing within-species population structure, with the variance–covariance of this random effect specified by the sample-by-sample kinship matrix (MR). When sex or age was the trait analysed, that variable was omitted from the covariate list. The LMM-based analysis was conducted using EMMAX (version beta-07Mar2010)71 with the parameters emmax -d 10 -t genotype.file -p phenotype.file -c covariate.file -k kinship.file -o output -Z. The association analyses were performed for each cohort and each species separately, with only the quasi-phaseable samples of the corresponding species included for the association analysis. For the SNV–phenotype pairs available in two or more independent cohorts (DMP, LLD1, 500FG, 300OB, IBD and 300TZFG), a follow-up meta-analysis was performed in the independent datasets (excluding the LLD2 and 500FG-APK datasets) using the Metal software (v2020-05-05)72 with default options (weighting cohort-based P values according to sample size). To ensure robustness and further reduce the chances of false discovery of identified associations, we conducted a permutational LMM with 999 times permutation following the Monte Carlo procedure within each cohort to assess the robustness of significant associations. Regional association plots showing LD structures on the gene regions covering the association signals were generated using LDBlockShow (v1.40)69.

The threshold of study-wide significance was determined by the total number of tested phenotype–SNV pairs (922,497,753) using the Bonferroni method: 0.05/no. of phenotype–SNV pairs = 5.42 × 10−11. Similarly, suggestive significance was determined using Bonferroni correction based on the total number of tested SNVs in each species and for each phenotype. Thus the suggestive significance did not control for the number of species and phenotypes involved in the study.

The SNVs could be correlated due to LD in the genomes. To count the number of independent association signals, we further conducted a grouping analysis that grouped interrelated SNVs by LD and picked the most significant SNVs within each LD block as representatives or tags of the independent signals (between-SNV r2 > 0.01, window size = 10,000 kbp) using PLINK2 (v2.0.0-a.6.9LM)67. The primary and secondary significance cut-offs used in PLINK2 were study-wide significance (5.42 × 10−11) and species-wide significance (species-specific) cut-offs. We visualized the local LD structure using LDBlockShow (v1.40)69 with the parameters -SeleVar 2 -NoShowLDist 100000000 -OutPdf -OutPng.

We performed a post-hoc approximate power analysis for the tag SNVs of significant associations using the genpwr package (v1.0.4) in R. The proportion of phenotypic variance explained (R2) was estimated from the observed effect size, standard error, observed sample size and MAF using the pheno_var_explained() function. Power was then calculated with power.calc.linear() under an additive genetic model and a study-wide significance threshold of α = 5.42 × 10−11. To evaluate the effect of sample size, power was also computed across fixed sample sizes (n = 100, 500, 1,000, 5,000 and 10,000) for the tag SNVs of significant associations.

As AlphaFold3 supports structure prediction for protein complexes, the three-dimensional (3D) structures of the OPP complex were modelled using AlphaFold3 (ref. 73) and visualized in ChimeraX (v1.10.1)74 and PyMOL (v2.5.7). However, according to the terms of use of the AlphaFold3 server, its output cannot be used in docking or screening tools (https://alphafoldserver.com/about). We therefore used local AlphaFold2 for monomer protein structure prediction and conducted in silico docking and other downstream analyses. To explore interactions between OppA/SarA_2 and glutathione, the protein structure of OppA/SarA_2 was first predicted using AlphaFold2 (v2.3.1)75 for both the wild-type and mutated protein sequences under monomer settings. The default reference databases and a template cut-off date of 14 May 2020 were used to guide the structure generation. For each experiment, five models were produced and further refined using graphics processing unit-based AMBER relaxation. The best model, based on pLDDT confidence scores (mean ≥ 90), was selected for downstream analysis, excluding the low-confidence N-terminal residues. The glutathione structure was downloaded from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and converted into PDB format. Ligand-binding pockets in the OppA/SarA_2 structure were predicted using P2Rank (v2.5)76, focusing on residues with high ligand-binding probabilities (>0.6) from top-scoring pockets (probability > 0.85). These residues were then used as active sites in HADDOCK (v2.4)77,78, a docking platform that generated 200 structural complexes for each variant. The top four docked poses from the best-scoring cluster were selected and energy-minimized in water. Binding free energies (ΔG) were estimated using PRODIGY-LIG (v1.1.4)79,80, and PLIP (v2025)81 was used to identify non-covalent interactions. As a docking benchmark, a literature-derived82,83 tripeptide panel representing basic (KAK, KWK, KKK), neutral (AAA, GFG, GAG) and acidic (EEE, DED) chemistries was docked to the same OppA binding site using the same set of active residues defined for the glutathione docking in HPEPDOCK (v2.0)84. All the resulting complexes were refined through water minimization in HADDOCK (v2.4)77, and binding free energies were estimated with PRODIGY-LIG (v1.1.4)79.

The protein structural property analysis was conducted using an internal pipeline (https://github.com/GArcho44/snvs-annotation-pipeline). In brief, to interpret effect of SNVs at the protein structure level, only missense variants in coding regions were retained. Functional gene annotations from Bakta (v1.10.1)68 and UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/)85,86 enabled linkage of genes to protein sequences within UniRef100 and consequently to structure entries in the AlphaFold Database (AlphaFoldDB, v2022)27. Recognizing that many genes lacked direct matches in UniRef100, we applied a homology-based approach to expand structural coverage. Gene coding sequences were extracted using GffRead (v0.12.7)87 and translated to proteins with EMBOSS transeq (v6.6.0)88. The resulting sequences were queried against AlphaFoldDB and ESM Metagenomic Atlas (version 1 November 2022, https://esmatlas.com/)26 using DIAMOND BLASTP (v2.0.15)89, retaining only full-length matches with ≥98% identity. When multiple matches occurred, the best match was selected by identity score, preferring AlphaFoldDB entries when there was a tie. Protein accessions (UniProtKB or MGnify) were then used to retrieve high-confidence structural models (mean pLDDT ≥80) via custom application programming interfaces. Multiple per-residue properties were measured on these curated protein models to functionally annotate the structural properties of SNVs. Relative solvent accessibility (RSA) was calculated using DSSP (v3.0.0)90,91, normalizing surface exposure per residue against empirical maxima. Ligand-binding pockets were detected using fpocket (v4.2.1)92 and rescored with fpocketPRANK to boost detection sensitivity. Binding residues were defined from pockets with druggability score > 0.5. Each residue’s distance to ligand (DTL) was computed based on the minimal Euclidean distance to the closest pocket centre. Protein–protein interaction interfaces were predicted using PeSTo (model version i_v4_1)93. Residues with interface confidence scores > 0.5 were grouped into clusters based on spatial proximity (Cα–Cα < 10 Å), and only high-confidence clusters (average score > 0.8, pLDDT > 0.7 and ≥6 residues) were retained. Each residue’s distance to protein-protein interface (DTPPI) was calculated based on Euclidean distance to closest residue of predicted interface.

The 3D structure of the marR homologue dimer was modelled using AlphaFold3 (ref. 73) and visualized with ChimeraX (v1.10.1)74. Protein-protein interface analysis, specifically in silico alanine scanning, was performed using the mCSM-PPI2 web server94.

The original gene annotation was conducted using Prokka (v1.13.3)95, and the age-association signal was identified in the gene annotated as sarA (UniProtKB: P31306), which is located next to oppB, oppC, oppD and oppF. The sarA gene encodes a periplasmic oligopeptide-binding protein, SarA, and contains an oppA gene family domain. According to the literature96,97, OppABCDF is a typical oligopeptide permease (OPP) or ABC transport system complex. Therefore, sarA, oppB, oppC, oppD, and oppF could form a gene cluster whose products can assemble into an oligopeptide ABC-transport system complex.

To confirm whether the gene organization of these five genes is conserved as a gene cluster across different bacterial genomes within the F. prausnitzii taxonomic clade, we conducted a gene cluster search using cblaster (v1.3.20)98 in 17,496 assemblies of F. prausnitzii from publicly available databases such as UHGG (v2.0.2)99, SPIRE100, and our in-house F. prausnitzii MAG and isolate genomes6. We found that all 5 genes co-occur in 2,185 F. prausnitzii genomes, situated close to each other (maximum distance between any 2 hits in a cluster <20 kb). The F. prausnitzii species with the complete OPP operon are Faecalibacterium butyricigenerans (223), Faecalibacterium duncaniae (3), Faecalibacterium longum (1,568), F. prausnitzii _I (372), Faecalibacterium sp900539945 (1), Faecalibacterium sp934514285 (15) and 3 unclassified genomes. Therefore, the SarA gene and the other four genes (oppB, oppC, oppD and oppF) do form a gene cluster or operon that is conserved in its gene organization within the F. prausnitzii genome. We also conducted 3D structure prediction using AlphaFold3 for the protein complex, compared it to the experimentally derived structures, and found that the predicted structure is similar to experimentally validated ABC transporter structures in M. tuberculosis (PDB: 8JA7)24 and E. coli MetNI (PDB: 6CVL)25. Therefore, the SarA with the OppA domain can function as OppA and can assemble with the other four subunits to form an OPP complex.

To further identify the potential ligand of this OPP complex, we conducted gene function annotation with Bakta (v1.11.0)68. We found that one of the five genes in the OPP gene cluster was annotated as an ABC-type glutathione transport system ATPase component (gsiA). We then compared the protein sequences of SarA (OppA), OppB, OppC, OppD and OppF to 3,239 existing protein sequences of the ABC-type glutathione transport system using blastp (v2.16.0 + ). We found that all five proteins match existing protein sequences of the ABC-type glutathione transport system (E-value < 1.00 × 10−10). The OPP complex may therefore function as a glutathione transport system.

To identify the genes from reference genomes potentially related to nicotine degradation, we obtained sequences of 92 proteins involved in two nicotine-degradation pathways from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (KEGG entries: M00810 and M00811) and searched for them in the genes of 909 reference genomes using tblastn (v2.14.1-gompi-2023a)101. In total, we identified 2,434 genes similar to known nicotine-degradation genes (E-value < 1.00 × 10−10).

We constructed a host age prediction model based on light gradient-boosting machine implemented in the R package lightgbm (v3.3.5). To ensure generalizability of the model, we used tidymodels (v1.1.0) to conduct nested cross-validation with a five-times-fivefold split for the outer loop and a fivefold split for the inner loop to assess the predictive power of gut microbial SNVs on host age. To avoid information leakage for the samples collected from individuals with close microbiome kinship, samples from the same lineage group were assigned to the same split dataset (either internal train dataset or internal test dataset) using the group_vfold_cv() function during the nested cross-validation procedure. We defined lineage groups using all SNVs used for age model training, pooled across species. Pairwise sample distances (scaled Manhattan distance) D were computed, and kinship was then defined as K = 1 – D. From D = 1 – K, we built a neighbour-joining tree and rooted it to orient the cut. Lineage groups were assigned by a single tree-height cut at h = (1 – c)/2 with kinship cut-off c = 0.9 (thus h = 0.05, equivalent to distance cut-off 0.1). Each descendant clade crossing this cut was then treated as one lineage group. In all cross-validation runs, we used a set of predetermined hyperparameters: trees = 2000, tree_depth = 15, min_n = 30, sample_size = 0.6, mtry = round(0.2 * total_feature_number), and learning_rate = 0.01. We estimated the importance of features using Shapley additive explanation, which assigns each feature an importance value for a particular prediction. After 5 × 5 rounds of fivefold cross-validation, we calculated the median importance value of each SNV feature, sorted the SNV features from high to low median importance values, cumulatively summed the median importance values until we reached 95% of the total sum of importance values, and took the counted SNV features as the predictive features for the final model. Because lineage groups were defined using SNVs pooled across species, this approach captures overall microbiome kinship but may not fully prevent information leakage for individual species whose species-specific kinship structure differs from the overall pattern.

The partial correlation test was conducted using the pcor() function of R package ppcor (v1.1). For the partial correlations between gut microbial SNV age and other biological age indices and between gut microbial SNV age and host intrinsic phenotypes, we used partial Spearman’s correlation, adjusting for chronological age. For the partial correlations between gut microbial SNV age and untargeted blood metabolome, we used a partial Pearson correlation, adjusting for chronological age, sex and blood creatinine level.
