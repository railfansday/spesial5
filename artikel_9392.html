Can you be friends with your ex? What to consider first

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Your phone buzzes. It’s your ex, sending a meme you would’ve laughed at together six months ago. You smile, then wonder why you smiled.

Moments like this are why staying friends with an ex can feel so confusing. For some people, staying connected feels natural because the habit, and maybe the care, is still there. For others, it makes healing feel harder. Every situation is different, and you’re allowed to take your time figuring out what works for you.

Whether you’re the one hoping to stay in touch or the one being asked to, here’s how to tell if staying friends would help you move on or make it harder.

In some cases, you may be able to. Post-breakup friendships can be healthy and meaningful when the breakup was mutual, the relationship had little conflict, and both people have had time to grieve.

That said, every friendship between exes is different. Researchers who study friendships after breakups describe two kinds. In one, both people have moved on and value the connection. In the other, one or both people stay in touch because of lingering attraction or reluctance to end things. The first kind can be rewarding. The second can make it harder to get over the breakup.

The dynamic you had with your ex matters, too. A study found that relationships shaped by high conflict, emotional dependency, or attachment anxiety may be harder to turn into a friendship afterward.

Before you decide anything, it may help to reflect on why you want to stay in touch. A few questions to sit with:

What am I hoping this friendship gives me?

Am I trying to stay close to them or avoid the discomfort of letting go?

How would I feel if they started dating someone new?

If the last question stings, it may just mean that you need more time.

If you were the one who ended things, you might want friendship out of guilt or a wish to soften the loss. If you were the one who was left, you might view friendship as a potential path to getting back together.

If the answers don’t come right away, try a mindfulness practice. A few minutes of reflection or a short breathing exercise can help you sort through what you’re thinking and feeling.

If you’ve thought it through and still want to stay in touch, a few signs suggest a friendship may work. People who are securely attached often find it easier to stay friends after a breakup, but the list below can apply to anyone:

You both see the breakup as mutual, with no ongoing hurt, resentment, or blame.

You’ve both accepted that the romantic relationship is over, with no unresolved romantic feelings.

There’s a reason for the friendship beyond comfort or familiarity, such as shared values, kids together, or a work connection.

In certain cases, staying friends can make healing harder or keep you from pouring energy into new parts of your life:

You’re still in love with your ex and hoping the friendship leads somewhere.

The relationship involved emotional manipulation, control, or abuse.

The friendship gets in the way of new relationships, like when a new partner feels uneasy about your connection.

You have different expectations for the friendship, which could leave one of you hurt.

It can be, especially since staying in touch can make it harder to let go. One study found that regular contact with an ex may slow emotional recovery. When you’re still part of each other’s routines, it can be harder to mourn the end of the relationship.

By contrast, a low-contact friendship, where you talk occasionally and keep to limits you’ve agreed on, is less likely to hold either of you back.

How you feel after you talk is worth paying attention to. If you often feel sad, anxious, or unsettled, or you keep replaying the conversation, it may help to take some space.

It may be appropriate to be friends with your ex, depending on your situation. It tends to work best when you’ve both moved on, the breakup didn’t involve harm, and the friendship fits both of your lives. If it also feels honest and comfortable to you, that’s a good sign.

Being friends with an ex isn’t automatically a red flag. If your partner is friends with an ex, how they handle it matters more than the friendship itself. A few questions to consider:

Transparency: Do they talk about it openly, or does it feel secretive?

Priorities: Do they make time for you and the relationship?

Boundaries: Are there clear limits on the friendship, or does contact feel constant and undefined?

Responsiveness: When you raise a concern, does your partner listen or dismiss it?

If something doesn’t sit right, you could bring it up and see whether they listen, get defensive, or brush you off. If you’re the one staying friends with an ex, your new partner’s concerns are worth hearing out, even if nothing is wrong.

You don’t have to keep in touch with your ex after a breakup. Taking space is often healthy and necessary, and you can change your mind later. You can choose a full break, a time-limited break (“let’s not talk for a few months”), or a low-contact friendship.

If you do stay in touch, some boundaries may protect your well-being. A few to consider:

Set a pace. Daily texting can pull you back into old routines, so try starting with occasional check-ins instead.

Decide what’s off limits. That might mean dating lives, old conflicts, and late-night “remember when” talks.

Give yourself space online. Mute or unfollow for a while to adjust without ending the connection.

Plan a review and a way out. After a few months, ask whether the friendship still feels good. If it doesn’t, have a line ready, like “I think I need to take a step back for now.”

If you share children, a workplace, or a friend group, you may need to stay in touch. It’s often best to stick to logistics, like pickup times for the kids or project deadlines at work.

There’s no set amount of time to wait before trying to be friends with an ex. Some people feel ready within months, while others take years. Some never feel ready or choose not to be friends, and that’s okay.

A question to ask yourself is whether you can feel okay about the thought of your ex moving on. If that still hurts, you probably need more time.

Yes, being friends with an ex can sometimes turn into getting back together. Whether that’s a good thing depends on why your relationship ended and whether those reasons have changed.

Problems tied to circumstances, like bad timing or distance, may be easier to work through than core differences, like values, long-term goals, or how you communicate. If you get back together and those core differences haven’t changed, the same patterns can resurface.

It’s painful when one of you wants to stay friends after a breakup and the other doesn’t, and what helps depends on which side you’re on.

If you’re the one being asked, you’re allowed to say “not yet” or “no.” If you’re unsure, you don’t have to answer right away. You might say:

“I care about you, and I need some time before I know what this looks like.”

“I’m not able to be friends right now, and I hope you can respect that.”

If you’re the one who wants friendship, the kindest move is to respect the space your ex has asked for. If you push for contact, it can get harder for you both to move on, even if giving them room hurts.
