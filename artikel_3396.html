8 Strongest Villains In The Lord Of The Rings & The Hobbit Movies, Ranked By Power (& Sauron Isn't #1)

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

J.R.R. Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings features some of the best villains in fantasy history. Modern fantasy often plays on humanized villains, ensuring we can empathize with the bad guy's motivation. Tolkien cared little for that; his grandest villain, Sauron, became the Dark Lord who set the pattern for every Dark Lord afterward. Smartly, though, so many of the other villains were tied to the corruptive power of the One Ring; it gave the story a wonderful thematic strength.

It's actually surprisingly difficult to rank the villains of The Lord of the Rings and The Hobbit. That's largely because there are so many different forms of "power" in this saga; physical strength, magical ability, military skill, and even political influence. Still, playing all those variables against one another, here are the eight most powerful villains of this saga.

Let's start with a character who possesses absolutely none of the traditional Tolkien powers: Grima Wormtongue. Saruman's agent, he became the "power behind the throne" for King Théoden; spinning a web of lies that ensnared the monarch and brought the entire kingdom of Rohan to the brink of destruction. It's easy to underestimate Grima, but he was a ruthless, manipulative man skilled in all the arts of manipulation. Words are his weapon, and he knows how to cut deep with them.

Interestingly, Peter Jackson's The Lord of the Rings weakens Wormtongue by suggesting he's drawing on Saruman's power. That's explicitly visible when Gandalf frees Théoden from the lies binding him, and Saruman himself experiences the backlash. Tolkien's books make Grima the mastermind in his own right, stressing how dangerous (and powerful) a manipulator he is.

Now let's move from political and manipulative power to something a little more physical: Gothmog, Sauron's senior military commander. The Orcs are far stronger than any human, and can endure a lot more punishment too. Given Orc society, we can assume he was considered one of the Orcs' greatest fighters, although we sadly never see him going up against powerful Elves like Legolos or Dwarves like Gimli.

Viewers tend to underestimate the Orcs, treating them as mindless brutes. But Gothmog's power is demonstrated by his military skills, and he is a proven master tactician. He recognizes that the capture of Minas Tirith requires more than brute force, and he adapts effectively. As formidable as the Orcs may be, they would be far less effective and dangerous without Gothmog.

The Witch-King of Angmar is probably the most powerful agent of Sauron, a mortal Man who received one of the Rings of Power and was corrupted by Sauron. He became the most dangerous of the Nazgûl, transforming him into a near-immortal spirit whose existence is tied to the One Ring. A powerful sorcerer, his very presence weakens the resolve of his opponents, and he inspires overwhelming fear in normal soldiers.

The Witch-King of Angmar is a skilled warrior, and he goes head-to-head with some of The Lord of the Rings' most skilled fighters. He is, however, not truly invincible; his eventual defeat proves there's a limit to his power and his endurance. The Witch-King remains one of Tolkien's most sophisticated villains—a blend of sorcerer, spirit, and monster—but he can be defeated.

Saruman is one of the most powerful wizards in The Lord of the Rings, an immortal Maia sent to Middle-earth by Eru (Tolkien's version of God). He's basically a fallen angel, and indeed was easily the most powerful of all the wizards until Gandalf too ascended to become a White. Aragorn gives a sense of Saruman's raw power in the books:

"Once he was as great as his fame made him. His knowledge was deep, his thought was subtle, and his hands marvelously skilled; and he had a power over the minds of others. The wise he could persuade, and the smaller folk he could daunt. That power he certainly still keeps. There are not many in Middle-earth that I should say were safe, if they were left alone to talk with him, even now when he has suffered a defeat. Gandalf, Elrond, and Galadriel, perhaps, now that his wickedness has been laid bare, but very few others."

As powerful as Saruman may be, though, note that Gandalf implies Saruman's corruption—his turn to wickedness—has diminished him. Although Saruman's ego would reject this idea, he is far less powerful than he was originally.

This next placement is actually pretty tricky, because the comparison between Saruman and the Balrog is somewhat imprecise. The Balrog is one of several Maiar who turned from the Valar and followed Morgoth, Sauron's own master. Little is known about the process by which a Maiar became a Balrog; it's unknown whether Morgoth turned all his Maiar into them, or whether he only did it with his most devoted followers. Regardless, the Balrogs were absolute powerhouses.

The Balrog possesses immense physical strength, extraordinary combat abilities, and mastery of fire and shadow. The creature's duel with Gandalf is a stunning one, pushing both to their limits (and beyond). It's difficult to say how the Balrog compares with Saruman, but I'm choosing to place it higher, given it has even more experience working with its dark magic than the fallen White.

Smaug is a Great Dragon, descended from the dragons bred by Morgoth during the First Age. He demonstrates a very different kind of power to some of the other entries on this list; he's another physical powerhouse, a being of phenomenal destructive potential. In Tolkien's lore, dragons aren't simply gigantic beasts; they are incredibly intelligent, and this allows them to use their powers—flight and breathing fire—with tremendous skill.

Smaug is large enough to destroy buildings and to crush most enemies, and he's arguably more dangerous than any other villain in the fantasy saga. I'd tentatively place Smaug above the Balrog, given his intelligence and aerial agility. But it would certainly be a fight to behold; it's just a shame Tolkien never really gave us a sense of how it would play out.

The Dark Lord is undoubtedly the most powerful villain of the Third Age, the one whose malevolent evil inspires the entire saga. Sauron is another Maia, one of their greatest, but he was corrupted by service to Morgoth back in the First Age. Sauron is not simply a powerful wizard or monster; he's a cosmic being in his own right, one even the other wizards could not stand against. He's a phenomenal sorcerer, a skilled manipulator, and (in earlier ages) was a potent shapeshifter.

It doesn't matter what form of power you're talking about; Sauron excels in it. Most significant of all, though, is the fact he is bound to the One Ring. Using this, he can exercise control over the will of others, and could easily have conquered all Middle-earth had he managed to acquire it. Sauron appears in many forms throughout this saga, each more dangerous than the last. But, surprisingly, he isn't the most dangerous character. That distinction lies with a villain who I have already mentioned, and who is discussed a great deal in The Lord of the Rings, but who was never seen.

Morgoth, originally called Melkor, was the most powerful of the Valar; the godlike beings who created the entire world. He's essentially a class above Sauron in power, which explains why Sauron became his servant after Morgoth corrupted him. "Morgoth" means "the Black Foe of the World," which gives a sense of just how monstrous he really was.

In truth, there is nothing in all of Lord of the Rings to compare with Morgoth. He's as far beyond Sauron as Sauron is beyond Wormtongue; although his powers diminished during the First Age due to grievous injuries, he'd still have far outclassed any of the other characters and creatures in the list. Sauron represents domination, but Morgoth stands for corruption; Sauron could conquer, but Morgoth could transform. He's easily the greatest villain in all Tolkien's lore.
