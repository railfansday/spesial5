Sotheby’s Sold the World’s Most Expensive Van Gogh for $190 Million

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

This is the November 20, 2026 edition of True Colors, a look inside the global deals and colorful critiques that make the art world go round, with a weekly guide to the must-see openings, auctions, and fairs.

It’s True Colors, from New York, where over the course of a few days billions of dollars have been spent at auction houses. Nvidia has lost more than $2 trillion in market cap since early October, but on Thursday, Anthropic’s IPO minted a class of new billionaires. It’s whiplash. As one dazed art dealer mused aloud on Monday night in the elevator at Sotheby’s: “It seems like money’s lost all its value.” Maybe the money, but not the art. Amid a surreal era of wealth creation and artificial intelligence, once-in-a-century oil-on-canvas masterpieces only get more covetable. Two Van Goghs from the same collectors have now sold for $190 million apiece, both in the span of a year.

One thing remains constant: Everybody has to eat dinner. And increasingly, they’re doing it at Borgo, perhaps the best all-around restaurant in New York—where the owner is a semisecret acclaimed painter. I talked to Andrew Tarlow about maintaining a practice as a serious, boundary-pushing painter while running a Manhattan restaurant.

Speaking of restaurateurs: Michael Chow opened his first Mr. Chow in London nearly 60 years ago, and the spot is still as good as ever. A few works from Chow’s collection, some made specifically for the restaurant, are on view at Gagosian on Madison Avenue. I recently spent some time with him (and, briefly, Larry Gagosian) going through works by Ed Ruscha, Julian Schnabel, Jean-Michel Basquiat, and Andy Warhol, as well as many portraits of Chow himself.

We’ve also got reports from the Baby2Baby Gala in LA, the Ballroom Marfa Gala in Harlem, and a special dinner in Tribeca. And all the gossip from the auctions.

Mentioned in this issue: William Acquavella, Steve Wynn, Steve Cohen, Patrick Drahi, Charles Stewart, Jackie Wachter, Anthony Haden-Guest, Leonardo DiCaprio, Vittoria Ceretti, Jeff Koons, Frances Outred, Andrew Tarlow, Walter Isaacson, Matthew Schneier, Ross Bleckner, David Salle, Valentina Akerman, Joe Bradley, Kate Huling, Alex Tieghi-Walker, Alex Levy, Albert Oehlen, Ignacio Mattos, Louis Shannon, Victoire Bourgois, Nate Lowman, Julian Schnabel, Dustin Yellin, Meredith Darrow, Annie Morris, Michael Chow, Lady Gaga, Jim Dine, and Larry Gagosian

Van Gogh’s Châtaigniers en fleurs sold for $190.4 million, including fees, Wednesday night at Sotheby’s Breuer building headquarters on Madison. It’s the most expensive work sold at auction this year, and easily among the top 10 priciest things to ever be publicly auctioned. It’s a bit insane to say this, especially about a single canvas that sold for more than the total value of the art set to be on offer at Art Basel Miami Beach next month, but that price feels a bit like a…disappointment? The on-request estimate for the work was $180 million, and the hammer price fell well short of that, at $165 million. Bidding in the room was thin, and the lot was guaranteed by Sotheby’s. Though it’s unclear where the exact guarantee price landed, it was probably around the estimate.

Honestly, it’s a relief to the house and the seller—the family of the Argentinian collectors Carlos Pedro Blaquier and Nelly Arrieta de Blaquier—that this painting sold at all. A Jewish collector bought the work in 1909 from a Paris gallery. His heirs claimed, just days before the auction, that the work was later sold under duress due to Nazi persecution, entitling them to receive restitution. The parties came together over an eleventh hour deal, terms undisclosed, to allow the sale to go through.

So who bought it? The winning bidder in the room was William Acquavella, the patriarch of one of New York’s great art-dealing families, who has an august gallery on the Upper East Side. It’s unclear for whom he bought it. In the past he’s been known to broker art deals on behalf of former Las Vegas casino tycoon Steve Wynn. In 2013, Acquavella was the winning bidder on a Francis Bacon tryptic for $142 million—that time on behalf of Wynn’s ex-wife, the late Elaine Wynn. (The piece is currently on view at LACMA, where Elaine Wynn was a co-chair of the board.) Steve Wynn has been more of a seller than a buyer recently, so the jury’s still out on whom Acquavella was on the phone with Wednesday.

The $190 million price tag is an almost exact match for the amount someone else paid for another Van Gogh from the Blaquier-Arrieta family. Reports say that, in 2025, Christie’s conducted a private sale where Le Zouave, a Van Gogh portrait of a soldier, sold for $190 million. While there’s no indication that the most recent buyer was someone connected to the AI gold rush, it’s surely indicative of the times that a Van Gogh has sold for 9 figures right around the moment that the seven cofounders of Anthropic have become worth about $20 billion—and around 600 Anthropic employees have become worth more than $100 million. When reached for comment through a rep, Acquavella didn’t get back to me.

— As I walked into Sotheby’s Monday for the first sales of the week, there were clients going downstairs to get their tables at Marcel, where diners can bid tableside while cocktails are served from a bar cart. Other clients went to a sumptuous overflow room with an open bar and a large spread of Marcel goodies. Those lucky enough to snag seats moseyed up to the sales room, where telecom billionaire Patrick Drahi, the owner of Sotheby’s, was standing in the back, near CEO Charles Stewart.

The 6 p.m. start time meant some collectors were still trickling in when Jackie Wachter snagged a Gerhard Richter work for a hammer price of $5 million. In the back of the room, white-jacketed waiters wheeled around with flutes of Champagne for those standing in the back. The writer Anthony Haden-Guest, a longtime Vanity Fair scribe whose book True Colors gave this column its name, walked in and took a seat in the back. One woman started taking selfies. As the contemporary sale got underway, Leonardo DiCaprio and his girlfriend, Vittoria Ceretti, walked down from the skybox and watched as an Agnes Martin hammered at $9.2 million. DiCaprio, a very serious collector, didn’t appear to be bidding, but no matter; he was still there at Sotheby’s. Auction week had officially begun.

— Over at Christie’s, the house sold a Georgia O’Keeffe that had been on view in an apartment in my neighborhood for more than 60 years. Suffice to say, there aren’t many expensive works of art that get discovered in apartments in the vicinity of Tompkins Square Park. I had to savor the lot’s beautifully poetic provenance, a phrasing that I don’t believe has ever been printed in an auction catalog previously: “Property from an East Village Private Collection.” It sold for $4.9 million.

— The week’s hottest lot? Maybe a sculpture of a dog by Jeff Koons, Bob-Tail, that was first on view at the artist’s still-controversial “Made in Heaven” show. Estimated to sell for an ambitious high estimate of $3.5 million, it hammered at $9 million, or $9.7 million with fees. Former auction chairman Frances Outred said the sculpture was consigned by a college friend of his who bought it from Anthony d’Offay for a five-figure sum in 1995, “when the art market was in the doldrums and the Jeff Koons market was at its nadir.”

— For a dinner on Tuesday, Galerie Sardine installed a long table where every seat had a great view of one of the Joe Bradley paintings that had been installed in a space on 9 White Street. It wasn’t exactly a tough sell to get the works at the dinner—Bradley is married to Galerie Sardine founder Valentina Akerman. The always exquisite Ignacio Mattos whipped up some Estela-level pinchos ahead of serving an unctuously rich rice-and-chicken dish.

— Ballroom Marfa held its annual gala Thursday at the Cathedral of St. John the Divine, which, as honoree Anne-Cecilie Engell Speyer reminded attendees, is the largest Gothic cathedral in the world. For context: The cathedral would take up nearly four whole blocks of downtown Marfa, Texas. In addition to Speyer, the honorees included photojournalist Lynsey Addario and musician Lyle Lovett, who was introduced by VF contributing photographer Mark Seliger. The evening raised $1.5 million for Ballroom Marfa.

— After the auction on Wednesday, the gallery Entrance, a space opened by dealer Louis Shannon on Ludlow Street nearly a decade ago, hosted an opening for the painter Victoire Bourgois. Lots of artists were there to support her—Nate Lowman, Julian Schnabel, Dustin Yellin—and the event was followed by a dinner at the ultimate Dimes Square redoubt. That’s right: Dimes itself. Can you believe Dimes has been open for—checks age—more than 13 years? And guess what? After all the Dimes Square discourse, the actual restaurant itself still slaps.

— The annual Baby2Baby Gala went down in LA last weekend. I wasn’t able to make it out West, but luckily for Vanity Fair, Tallulah Willis was on the scene and wrote a fabulous party report that you can read here. I also want to note that the live auction of artworks, curated, as always, by Darrow Contemporary founder Meredith Darrow, included an Annie Morris sculpture that achieved $410,000.

On a Friday in November, I walk into Gagosian on Madison Avenue for a meeting with Michael Chow, the artist, collector, filmmaker, designer, actor, and founder of the restaurant Mr. Chow. There’s a portrait of Chow by Jean-Michel Basquiat, a portrait by Julian Schnabel, a portrait by Andy Warhol. There’s a drawing of Chow by David Hockney, made soon after Mr. Chow opened in London in 1968.

Michael Chow: So I opened that restaurant. You’ve got Hockney, you’ve got fashion, you’ve got every single thing. It’s almost like a Dada movement on the First World War thing. This one is in London—all happening.

He starts talking about duopolys: Christie’s versus Sotheby’s, Coke versus Pepsi. There was an Italian restaurant in London’s Soho, so he opened a Chinese restaurant in Soho with Italian waiters. He has a theory about name-dropping.

Chow: I hate people who drop names, and Lady Gaga agrees with me.

Why is name-dropping important? Because the minute I say “Napoleon,” you will go there immediately. So it saves a lot of time. Okay, so why am I saying that? Do you know who Robert Fraser is?

The art dealer from the ’60s. Groovy Bob.

I was looking forward to meeting him, blah, blah, blah. He was sitting there. Name-dropping. Sitting in front is Jim Dine. Name-dropping again. I say, “I would like to borrow some paintings.” I said, “Just like”—do you know the Kronenhalle restaurant?

We walk over to the Hockney portrait of Michael Chow sitting in his restaurant.

Chow: The first person I went to is David. He drew one and I said, “Boy.” You know what he did? Tore it all up.

Simultaneously, I went to Peter Blake. I say, “Give me a painting that’s the antithesis of racism,” which he did. At that time he’s into the circus, he’s into all that shit. All right? So I asked him, “Why did you do that?” He did a portrait. So now Peter Blake is fucking crazy. He loves the circus, he loves wrestling and all that shit.

Larry Gagosian walks in and gives Chow a fist bump. Gagosian takes a long look at the Schnabel and then flips through a book of drawings made by artists for 57th Street’s Mr. Chow when it was the art world’s clubhouse. We walk up to the Basquiat.

True Colors: To have it as this sort of landscape, you have the horizon line here, but it’s almost like a Western. It’s almost cinematic.

Shane. The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance. High Noon.

I’m going to act out the first scene of each. Okay, High Noon.

He uses his hand to pull a finger gun on me, then gets distracted by the Ed Ruscha and the Warhol double portrait.

Chow: Andy said, “Oh, what do you want?” I said, “Hold the color and add lots of diamonds.”

Gagosian brings up Anselm Kiefer, and then Richard Serra.

Chow: If you look at the year 2000, Charlie Rose and Richard Serra, their conversation on the culture of China, and Richard Serra identifies very clearly what is fine art: poetry, music, sculpture, and painting.

The works in the show are for sale. I ask Chow if he’s emotional about parting with them. He seems very calm.

Chow: I’m only a temporary steward of the works.

“Friends and Legends: The Collection of Michael Chow” is up at Gagosian’s 980 Madison space until December 22.
