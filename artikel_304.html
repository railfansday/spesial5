Thuma Dupes: Japanese Joinery Bed Frames That Stack Up (2026)

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Silk & Snow (queen, with solid headboard)

Japanese joinery bed frames are bolt-free beds made from notched wood pieces that slide and click together to make a stable frame. Think Jenga, but with really nice, sleek wooden furniture. The advantages are many, starting with easy assembly that takes just a few minutes and requires no tools.

Thuma is the leading brand in this space, and we love its product lineup—several members of the WIRED Reviews team have tested Thuma beds, and all rave about them. However, they’re not cheap. A Thuma bed frame starts at $895 for a twin frame with no headboard, and the king-sized Thuma signature bed our testers loved costs $2,500.

Japanese joinery is a traditional carpentry method that uses only wooden components. Instead of nailing boards together, each piece of wood is carved so it snugly fits into openings carved into neighboring pieces—the “joints,” if you will. The tension and strong hold of the interconnected pieces create structural integrity.

It’s a pretty easy way to channel your inner Bob the Builder, and the finished bed frame looks stunning in its simplicity. However, we are always on the lookout for products at the intersection of high quality and low price—curious to see if any contenders could give Thuma a run for its money—so we researched and tested, and these are our findings.

Be sure also to check out our guides on the best mattresses we’ve tested, as well as the best pillows and bedsheets. You’ll need something to put on your new frame, after all!

DreamCloud (queen, with bamboo headboard)

I’m a longtime fan of DreamCloud mattresses, so it came as no surprise that the brand’s Japanese joinery bed frame is excellent. Like the mattress, this frame has numerous thoughtful details that stood out immediately.

DreamCloud made setup seamless. Each piece is labeled, and when it comes time to put it all together, take a gander at the endpoints of the long pieces. If you look closely at the joints where they intersect at the ends, you will see holes drilled into the top and bottom pieces. Small rods attach to the boards and fit into them, serving as a secondary mechanism to ensure stability. I find this impressive because the beams stack neatly into the grooves carved into the ends. In a word, it’s crisp. It’s a tiny detail, but the frame stayed virtually silent when I put a mattress on it or moved around in my sleep. I didn’t notice any shaking, either.

Another excellent feature is the frame’s center stability beam (crucial for preventing long-term sagging), which slides in last. This lets you avoid nesting it before closing the frame, unlike some other Japanese joinery models from different brands. It’s far more convenient.

Lining up the slats is the most challenging part of the process; you have to make sure everything is aligned perfectly. To help the process go smoothly, you’ll find wooden rods—similar to those on the ends of the boards—in the center of the bed, where you insert the slatted frames. They’re about the width of a pencil. Pop those slats in, unroll, Velcro to keep in place, and go.

You don’t need a headboard with this frame, but DreamCloud provided one for testing. Two square openings are located inside the top beam of the frame, right where your head would be. The headboard slides into these internal slots, which hold the end pieces and keep it upright. Foam block stickers are included to be attached last, so your wall doesn’t get scuffed up. All that, and still no tools necessary. Down to the details, people! And this detail you don’t want to skip—it’s around $449 for a queen. The headboard costs $299 extra, but even with that, it’s still far cheaper than luxury alternatives. You’ll also have two stain options: natural or a very pretty walnut.

That said, you’ll have to consider how many sleepers will use this frame and the weight of the mattress you’ll place on it. When I was testing new mattresses every week, two of the frame’s center support legs split right down the middle. Make sure you don’t screw them on too tightly, as I suspect the tension is what made the wood look like I had been chopping it with a small axe. Regardless, I was shocked to see this happen, and it is another notch on Thuma’s belt for heightened durability. —Julia Forbes

The Bamboo Bed Frame by DreamCloud ranges from $299 for a twin to $549 for a California king (frame only).

Silk & Snow (queen, with solid headboard)

Silk & Snow is another brand that I hold in high regard. Every time I’ve tested a mattress from the brand, it consistently packs quality for the price. This platform bed frame was no exception. There are two options: natural rubberwood and acacia wood. I opted for the latter, which is a medium-brown color. I thought that the stain was stunning, with a richness akin to a lovely latte. However, I really appreciate that they offer options not just in wood stain color, but also in the actual hardwood itself.

This frame arrived in four separate boxes. I liked this approach because it protected the wood stain’s integrity during shipping. Every other frame in this guide was packed into one box—not unlike a mattress-in-a-box, but heavier and longer. I actually preferred this route, as every box was also labeled for easy setup.

Like the DreamCloud frame, Silk & Snow’s doesn’t require tools or hardware to set up—it’s made entirely of wood. One thing I learned the hard way: Make sure the center beam is within the frame perimeter before fully closing it off. Then place the slats into the internal notches on the side rails, and it’s ready for a mattress. I had no issues with noise, shaking, or feeling unstable. It supported a mattress just fine, and I am still truly enamored with how the wood stain looked amidst my studio’s decor.

However, I do a lot of testing, so I’m constantly setting up and taking down mattresses, frames, and bedding. This is where having two people will be crucial, as I decided to do it all by myself for the sake of time, to the detriment of the frame. The frame got badly scratched up around the slatted portions where the corners of the boards stack together, due to my awkward solo assembly motions. I doubt you’ll be doing this as much as I do, but it was still a bit disappointing to see. Despite this, it’s worth recommending because I loved how it looked and performed. —Julia Forbes

The Silk & Snow Wooden Platform bed frame ranges from $550 for a twin to $900 for a king (frame only).

The Avocado City Bed Frame is a minimalist wood (blonde or walnut) platform frame with a completely tool-free Japanese-joinery assembly process. The same natural, nontoxic, and sustainable materials practices that guide the company’s mattresses are at play here as well.

The City Bed is regularly $1,299 for a queen, and it is the most affordable of Avocado’s bed options (though you have to factor in a shipping fee). It’s made from very nice, solid wood (I tested the walnut version). There’s an optional headboard that I did not test. The bed itself is attractive, fairly subtle, and unobtrusive. In fact, if your duvet is reasonably large, you may not even see much of the bedframe.

The frame fits together using interlocking joints with metal reinforcements. In my testing, it’s been solid and sturdy, with no squeaking or any signs of weak joints. It is a tight fit at times, especially at the four corners, and I had to tap it with a soft mallet to get it fully flush (which sort of defeats the no-tool promise, but you probably could have done the same with a copy of Infinite Jest or something equally solid). —Scott Gilbertson

The Avocado City bed frame ranges from $750 for a twin to $1,499 for a California king (frame only).

The Birch frame was the most basic of the Thuma dupes I tested. I was really excited about this one because the wood is sourced from the land of the pines, otherwise known as my home state of North Carolina. Poplar is a durable hardwood, and southern yellow pine is one of the harder pine woods. (I can attest to this because, for an eighth-grade science project, I tested the durability of regional woods.) The manufacturing partner is a certified member of the Forest Stewardship Council (FSC). The legs are made of a completely different wood, Appalachian maple. It also uses a soy-based resin to remain completely formaldehyde-free.

It arrived in two boxes, and setup was quick and easy—the step-by-step instructions were the most thorough of the bunch. With written descriptions and pictures, it’s hard to mess this up. Plus, you don’t need any tools to get this frame up and running. The outer boards were polished to maintain the gorgeous hue of the pine wood. However, be cautious when handling the inner slatted-board sections, as this wood is not finished. I got a small splinter and was not a happy camper. Next, I set up the bed and slept on it for a week—the standard amount of time I test mattresses. The first few days, this thing creaked and moaned like crazy. It didn’t feel stable, and I was shaken if I moved even slightly. By the end of the week, it seemed to fall into place and settle, so I no longer noticed the noise or motion.

I don’t want to discount this as a possible option, though, because the wood quality was what I wanted it to be, and so was the overall price. If you’re looking for a cheaper option for a guest room, upgrading your teen’s room, or furnishing a first apartment on a budget, this could work well for you. —Julia Forbes

The Birch Madison bed frame ranges from $749 for a twin to $978 for a California king.

Nectar Sleep (queen, with bamboo headboard)

I had never set up or slept on a Japanese joinery-style bed frame before testing this model from Nectar, and now I’m a true believer. The frame is made from bamboo, and assembly took just about 20 minutes with no tools required.

Putting it together was super simple: the four feet had cut-out notches where the frame slid into place, and you just needed to push down to make the pieces lock together—it’s essentially like putting together a giant set of Lincoln Logs. After sliding the footboard into the feet’s notches, you finish both sides the same way by pushing down into the notches. The middle section is springy and has a floating foot to adjust for the bed’s pressure, so no cumbersome box spring is needed. The slats are connected with Velcro and just need to be unspooled and adhered to the sides. I opted for a headboard that slides in from above and stays in place because of the cutouts on the head portion of the frame below.

I’m physically disabled, so putting together furniture with tools and nails or screws is nearly impossible. I recommend that anyone who’s in a similar position check out a Japanese-style bed frame, as it’s lightweight, requires no additional tools, and requires very little physical strength. Plus, this frame comes in two colors, has optional headboard attachments, a 60-night home trial, a two-year warranty, and free shipping. —Molly Higgins

The Nectar Bamboo bed frame ranges from $299 for a twin to $549 for a California king (frame only).

Here’s the thing: Thuma is still a Japanese joinery bed frame. Therefore, I’d be remiss not to include it on this list. While I can vouch that the options above are credible dupes, many of my teammates feel nothing beats the original in this case.

Thuma’s Classic Bed Frame is made from upcycled, sturdy rubberwood that comes in four finishes: walnut, natural, espresso, and gray. You can also choose different headboard variations, including all-wood, a pillow board (Italian wool and foam), or a hybrid of the two. The design’s not only beautiful, but it’s also backed by certifications such as UL GreenGuard Gold and CertiPur-US (if you opt for the pillow board).

WIRED Gear Editor Julian Chokkattu has had his Thuma frame for several years, and he notes it takes next to no time to set up. He also loves that it doesn’t creak or slide—just a strong sense of security while sleeping on it.

The Thuma Classic bed frame ranges from $895 for a twin to $1,495 for a California king (frame only).

Just like with the best mattresses we regularly test, each frame required a week of testing (not including naps) to evaluate night-over-night performance. This also allowed time for the frames to settle into place and perform alongside the bed each was paired with.

How Do I Set Up a Japanese Joinery Bed Frame?

Your bed frame should come with installation instructions, whether it features Japanese joinery construction or not. In the same vein, I recommend having someone assist you with the setup. As mentioned in our Silk & Snow review, this helps mitigate the risk of bumping and scratching your new furniture.

Are These Bed Frames Better Than Thuma’s?

We wouldn’t say better, just different. Each brand offers its own approach to creating a bed frame that adheres to the Japanese joinery method. This could include the type of wood used, the stains offered, the types of headboards you can pair it with, where it was manufactured, and, of course, the price.
