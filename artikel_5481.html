The pattern behind the Washington gap has a name: the urban heat island effect. A city can reach its first freeze up to a month after nearby suburbs, according to The Weather Company. Several features of built-up areas drive it. Asphalt and concrete tend to be dark, so they absorb heat rather than reflect it. Those surfaces are also impervious, meaning water cannot pass through them. With no water moving through or evaporating from them, those surfaces lose a source of cooling. Tall buildings and narrow streets keep heat from rising away. Cities also have fewer plants, so they get less of the cooling that comes from transpiration.

The effect lasts all year. It shapes spring frost dates as well as fall ones. In summer, urban areas run a bit hotter than suburban ones.
