If your travel plans were disrupted, here's how you can get refunded or rebooked.

An estimated 1.5 million travelers had their flight plans upended on Friday, Nov. 13, 2026, after the Federal Aviation Administration announced a rare nationwide ground stop. That number comes from the flight data firm Cirium, which tracked passengers scheduled to depart U.S. airports that day after the stop was declared. The ground stop lasted more than four hours.

That ground stop lifted at 8:15 p.m. ET on Friday, but the ripple effects could be massive. Planes are out of position for the weekend, and some travelers are still trying to reach their destinations or get refunds on flights and other expenses.

NerdWallet travel writer Craig Joseph was among those affected. He was flying out of Asheville, North Carolina, for a one-night trip to a college football game, and learned his flight was delayed by three hours. Because it was a quick trip, and he had booked with frequent flyer miles that could be redeposited into his account, he canceled. Moments later, his flight — and every other one on the departures board — was canceled.

He canceled his hotel before the deadline, too, so his only sunk cost was the football ticket. But not all travelers will be so lucky.

If your travel plans were impacted, here's how you can get your money back.

Don’t count on calling customer service. Major airlines have been overwhelmed with customers asking for help.

As of Friday night, Southwest’s automated phone number said it would take several hours to get a callback from a human. American Airlines’ automated line said it would take at least two hours to reach a human. Frontier Airlines wouldn’t even let us speak to a human, and instead sent us to a chatbot.

Skip the chatbots and long waits. Instead, do this:

Check the airline’s app or website, and look for any emails or other alerts. Most airlines are proactively notifying affected passengers with rebooking options, and you may be automatically rebooked.

Get a refund or rebook online or through the airline's app. Every major airline has issued a travel waiver due to the ground stop, which gives you more flexibility to rebook without a change fee, often without charging for fare differences. More on that below.

Check your travel insurance. Because the ground stop was outside the airlines' control, they may not offer vouchers for hotels or food. If you paid these costs out of pocket, you might be able to get reimbursed from your travel insurance policy, if you purchased one, or your card's coverage. More on that below, too.

Under U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) rules, you are entitled to a full refund if your flight is canceled or significantly delayed — no matter the reason — and you decide not to travel and turn down other forms of compensation like travel credits. The money must be delivered to your original payment method, even if you bought nonrefundable tickets.

If your flight disruption meets the DOT criteria, make sure you're getting a full refund. Some airline waivers offer a travel credit as a default option. Credits typically expire, and you won't get value from them until you redeem them. With a refund, you can get your money back within days.

If you booked with points or miles, you'll generally get those redeposited to your loyalty account, plus a refund of any taxes and fees you paid.

But if you still want to forge on with your travels, most airlines are allowing affected passengers to rebook new flights without owing a change fee or fare difference, which is more generous than usual. If your original travel dates were Nov. 13 or 14, and you purchased your tickets on or before Nov. 12 or 13, you're likely eligible for these flexible change policies.

Depending on the airline, you might have even more flexibility. For example, Southwest’s more generous rebooking policy applies to flights scheduled through Monday, Nov. 16.

Right now, you and everyone else who was supposed to be on that Friday afternoon flight are vying for limited seats on aircraft flying the same route over the next few days. Get creative if the flight you need is booked up. Think about whether a nearby airport might be possible. You might also opt for a train or rental car.

If your flight disruption also cost you a hotel night, a rental car, a concert or sports ticket, or some other prepaid piece of your trip, here's your best chance at getting that money back, too:

Call the hotel or rental property directly. Although not guaranteed, your host may waive a cancellation penalty or no-show fee for a documented flight disruption like this one. Have your canceled or delayed flight confirmation handy, and ask kindly.

For nonrefundable event tickets like concerts or sports games, you’re unlikely to get a direct refund. Check the specific event's refund policy first (and it never hurts to ask!), but your best bet in most cases is reselling the ticket through a platform like StubHub, SeatGeek or Vivid Seats rather than counting on a refund from the original seller.

Keep every receipt and email confirmation. Whether you're asking a hotel for an exception or filing an insurance claim, having your original flight confirmation, the cancellation or delay notice and your other prepaid reservations documented in one place will make every one of these conversations faster.

If you have a standalone travel insurance policy: Travel delay and missed connection benefits may cover you for a disruption like this one, according to the insurance marketplace Squaremouth. If you already hold a policy, contact your provider's 24-hour emergency assistance line directly rather than waiting to file a claim after the fact.

If you don't think you bought travel insurance, check your card terms. Many premium travel credit cards include travel insurance for covered events as a card benefit, assuming you paid for the trip with your card. But in this case, trip cancellation and interruption coverage likely wouldn't apply, because covered events are limited to things like illness, injury and inclement weather.

But trip delay coverage (which typically applies after a delay of 6 to 12 hours) often covers "equipment failure," so it's more likely to apply here — if the ground stop is deemed to be an equipment failure. This coverage would reimburse "reasonable" expenses during a delay, like meals and lodging. Save your receipts so you're ready to file a claim, but keep in mind that card terms vary and the claim might be denied.

The backlog from Friday's ground stop is expected to keep disrupting flights through the weekend. If you have a flight in the coming days, recheck your specific flight status before heading to the airport even if things looked fine an hour ago.

And whatever you were counting on — a refund, a rebooking or an insurance payout — make sure you move fast, document everything and don't assume you only have one option. With airline waivers, DOT refund rules and travel insurance, you might have more ways to get your money back than you think.

As for Joseph, he’s still trying to resell his football ticket.
