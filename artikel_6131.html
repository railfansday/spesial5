Understanding the conflict four years on.

U.S. President Donald Trump’s eleventh-hour pivot back toward Moscow in a desperate bid to lower fuel prices ahead of next week’s midterm elections is not going great. But neither is his war on Iran, with oil prices climbing toward $120 a barrel and one U.S. lawmaker describing the administration’s plans for a renewed war on Iran as “insane.”

Three weeks ago, in a telephone call with Russian President Vladimir Putin, Trump made a deal to purchase Russian diesel, which is forbidden under U.S. law. The global benchmark price of oil, which was about $70 a barrel before Trump started the Iran war in February, rose to over $117 a barrel on Friday. U.S. nationwide average gasoline prices are $4.20 a gallon, more than a dollar higher than the year before; no president ever has kept gasoline above $4 more than Trump. Diesel, which powers the entire economy, is worse: At $6.32 a gallon, it is about 70 percent higher than one year ago.

Another of Trump’s pro-Russia moves to keep energy prices in check was a pressure campaign on Kyiv to refrain from targeting Russian oil refineries with drones and missiles. U.S. negotiators including Steve Witkoff said that Kyiv should show “gratitude” toward Washington by curtailing strikes.

Ukraine complied for a spell in October, before remembering that what Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky calls “long-range sanctions” are the country’s best way of severing the sinews of Russia’s war machine. Overnight Thursday, Ukraine resumed strikes on Russian oil targets, with a devastating attack on a large Rosneft refinery that makes jet fuel for the military.

U.S. leverage over Ukraine is less than it used to be, since the Trump administration has not delivered any new military or financial assistance since Trump took office for a second time, and on several occasions, including this past month, has suspended what assistance there is, including some access to Starlink’s military-grade satellite internet service and intelligence sharing about inbound missile strikes. (While Ukraine has pleaded in vain for additional missile interceptors, the United States has pleaded poverty. This week, the United States sent a Patriot air defense system from Texas to Israel ahead of an anticipated restart to the fighting in the region.)

The U.S. pivot to Russia isn’t limited to diesel deals and strong-arming Ukraine, though. In addition to stopping future aid, U.S. officials have reportedly been cautioning countries on the front line of Russia’s shadow war to tone down their warnings about Moscow’s hostile intentions. And on Friday, the U.S. welcomed Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov to the G-20 foreign ministers’ meeting in Atlanta, to the irritation of European allies. The abandonment of Ukraine has lately raised bristles in the European Parliament, where some politicians are redoubling their support for Ukraine even as Trump retreats.

“This is not one of those instances of many different shades of gray. There’s clearly an aggressor who is in the wrong, and that’s Russia, and so I think, for me, that stands clearly,” said Reinier van Lanschot, a Dutch member of the European Parliament from the Volt Europa party and a vocal critic of Russia’s invasion.

Kyiv’s energy war on Russia may hurt on the margins, but the real culprit is Trump’s war on Iran.

It won’t help consumers, won’t help Kyiv, and won’t mean riches for Moscow.

But Trump’s travails aren’t limited to his limited leverage over Ukraine. The war in Iran, which is now in its eighth month and likely to escalate in coming weeks, has kept oil prices high as shippers struggle to navigate a strait strewn with high costs, Iranian drones and missiles, and, for the first time, limpet mines.

It may get uglier after the midterms. The Trump administration is reportedly considering a sharp military attack to try to once and for all break the back of a regime that has withstood war and sanctions for nearly half a century. Democratic Sen. Chris Murphy, who was briefed on defense planning, described the war plan as “insane.”

None of Trump’s moves to placate Putin will help him in the midterms, but they could haunt him in the two years remaining in his presidential term. Winter is coming for Ukrainians who endure daily and undefended assaults from Russian missiles, but it may be coming as well for an administration that has aided and abetted.
