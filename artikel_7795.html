An air humidifier and purifier puts two indoor-air appliances together into one space-saving everyday essential, filtering airborne pollutants while adding moisture when it gets too dry. We hands-on tested six combination models for two months, using them throughout bedrooms, offices, kitchens, and living spaces to compare purification, humidification, noise, controls, cleaning, and everyday usability.

Its clean interface, polished app, hidden water reservoir, and understated styling also make it unusually pleasant to live with.

Living with dry and unclean air can negatively affect comfort and health. Dry indoor air can cause an uncomfortable feeling at best and cracked skin at worst. Even “clean” indoor air can benefit from an air purifier, which can remove common pollutants such as pollen, pet hair, and smoke. Air purifying is even more important for allergy and asthma sufferers, who may notice a reduction in symptoms with a good air purifier.

There is a difference between air purifier and humidifier units: Purifiers remove pollutants from air, whereas humidifiers add moisture to the air. Combination units do both. Consider the following factors when shopping for the best air humidifier and purifier: room size, filtration system, reservoir capacity, and additional user-friendly features.

Air humidifier and purifier combo units are intended for use in one room. (Whole-house humidifier and air purifier options are separate products and are usually professionally installed into the home’s climate control system.) For optimal performance, a unit should most often be used in a room that is the size of the manufacturer’s recommendation.

It’s possible to use a unit rated for a larger room in a smaller room, however, as long as a unit has a humidistat and auto-shutoff. The humidistat measures the ideal humidity, usually between 30 and 50 percent, and triggers the unit to shut off when ideal humidity is reached. Just know that if there’s no auto shut-off feature, a large humidifier can make a small room too humid.

While it’s important to take room size into account, the recommended room size can sometimes be misleading, so consider the CADR rating as well, explained ahead.

Air humidifiers and purifiers can have a few filter types, and many have a multistage filtration system. These systems can include activated carbon filters to remove odors, large particle filters, dust and pet hair filters, and the “gold standard,” HEPA (high-efficiency particulate air) filters. HEPA filters remove over 99.97 percent of dust mites, mold, pollen, bacteria, and other airborne particles measuring 0.3 microns (and actually trap smaller and larger particles even more effectively).

These extremely effective humidifiers with filters are shown to improve air quality. Shoppers who are interested in an air purifier and humidifier for allergies will want to look for a unit with a HEPA filter.

Air humidifiers and purifiers have a CADR (clean air delivery rate) score. This rating is used to measure an air purifier’s efficiency. It indicates the amount of filtered air a unit can deliver per minute and is often broken down into separate ratings for tobacco smoke, dust, and pollen. Products certified by the AHAM (Association of Home Appliance Manufacturers) are proven to live up to their CADR rating.

The higher the CADR score, the faster the filtration system. CADR is often used to calculate the appropriate square footage for a unit. The AHAM recommends that the CADR score of a unit should be a minimum of two-thirds of the room’s square footage.

Some units don’t display a CADR score and only display the room size the unit is suitable for. Knowing the CADR score is more reliable, as some manufacturers will recommend a room size that does not align with the AHAM’s recommendations. According to the AHAM, the recommended room size should be based on 4.8 air changes per hour (how many times the purifier cleans and circulates the air in a room). Many manufacturers will share a recommended room size based on only one or two air changes per hour to boost their square footage recommendation.

Air humidifier purifier units have a reservoir to hold water, which the machine turns into moisture to release into the air. The unit’s overall size (and therefore reservoir size) often corresponds to its suited room size. Small units are usually ideal for small or medium-size rooms, but they may need more frequent refilling if they also have a small reservoir tank. Larger units will need less frequent topping up, but they’ll also take up more space.

Keep in mind that usage habits will also affect how often the water tank needs refilling. Many air humidifiers and purifiers have a water tank between a half gallon and 1 gallon, but extra-large options are also available—we’ve included one with a 3-gallon-plus capacity on this list.

In our testing, reservoir design mattered almost as much as capacity. A slightly smaller tank that’s easy to remove, carry, and clean can be less frustrating than a larger reservoir with awkward access.

Daily cleaning and maintenance can ensure that your air cleaner humidifier operates correctly and does not emit any harmful particles into a space. Users are encouraged to use distilled water in their humidifiers and purifiers to reduce general maintenance and germ buildup. Wash and dry the humidifier after each use to remove any potential bacteria, mold, minerals, and chemicals.

The Environmental Protection Agency advises using a 3 percent hydrogen peroxide solution to clean these machines every few days, clearing out any potential toxins. Finally, filters should be replaced as recommended by the manufacturer to ensure all contaminants are being removed.

A good air humidifier and purifier should have a built-in humidistat that measures room humidity and adjusts operation accordingly. At minimum, the humidifier should shut off once the set humidity is reached to prevent the room from becoming overly damp.

Digital onboard controls are also useful, especially when they make it easy to adjust fan speed, humidity targets, and operating modes. Some models include a remote control for making changes from across the room.

Smart models can connect to Wi-Fi and a smartphone app for monitoring air quality, setting schedules, and changing settings remotely. In our testing, however, we often found the physical controls faster for everyday adjustments. Apps were most useful for checking air-quality data and receiving maintenance alerts.

Filter-change and refill reminders are particularly helpful, whether they appear in an app or through indicator lights on the unit itself.
