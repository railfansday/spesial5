Why Medicare’s 2027 physician pay changes worry some specialists

Podiatrist Heather McGuire always wanted to serve in a clinic in her hometown of Ventura, Calif. Fifteen years after she opened her practice, she is realizing her dream in the community that raised her, treating suburbanites and agricultural workers with diabetes on Medicare.

But McGuire believes that dream may be ending.

The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services’ recent update to how Medicare pays physicians cut the payment rate for certain services provided on the same day by 20% when it’s provided by the same physician or practice. McGuire’s practice already operates on extremely thin margins, and she recently shelved a plan to hire another doctor to improve the practice’s exceedingly long wait times.

It could worsen Medicare recipients’ access to specialist care in Ventura, too. Last week, the podiatrist saw a new patient, a woman in her 80s, who waited two and a half months for an appointment to get her heel pain checked out. When the patient arrived, McGuire found that it wasn’t just heel pain. The woman had also developed an ulcer on her other foot during her long appointment wait. Left unchecked, an ulcer in someone with diabetes can lead to amputations.

“I would be paid 20% less for providing potentially lifesaving or limb-saving treatment care for that wound because she also had another complaint at the same visit,” McGuire said.

Podiatrists, dermatologists, and otolaryngologists are among the biggest losers with this change, according to an analysis by the American Medical Association, an advocacy group for physicians. Opponents of the policy, including the AMA, say it will force patients to return for separate appointments, raise out-of-pocket costs, and increase wait times, especially for patients in rural areas who have less access to specialists.

“If you’re running your own small group practice [accepting Medicare patients], can you still financially do that?” said Miranda Franco, a senior policy adviser at the law firm Holland & Knight. “Or are you going to start thinking about, you know, maybe consolidating into a hospital system or private equity to defray some of that financial loss?”

These billing code changes are an attempt to modernize Medicare and reduce administrative burden, a CMS spokesperson said. It is part of the agency’s broader shift away from specialist care and the fee-for-service model, and one of many reimbursement challenges that specialists are contending with.

Not everyone is against the change in payment rates for services rendered on the same day. The Medicare Payment Advisory Commission, a nonpartisan group that advises Congress on Medicare, supports it, saying it will remove redundancies and duplicative payments. The Justice Department has also found multiple instances of providers committing fraud through this billing code.

The cuts to payment rates for same-day services were slated to be 50% when first proposed in 2018, before backlash from physicians and patients softened the blow. Under the final rule, effective Jan. 1, 2027, Medicare will pay for the highest-valued same-day service at 100%, then reduce payment for each additional service rendered on the same day by 20% when physicians perform evaluations and other routine outpatient services. CMS argues the lowered rate is justified because providers use less resources — for patient registration, scheduling, and support, for example — when patients are seen and treated on the same day at the same practice.

Amy Hunsberger, a lawyer with Manatt, a legal advisory and consultancy, said the cuts are partly motivated by the statutory requirement that physician payments cannot increase without Congressional approval, forcing Medicare to cut back on expenses as the agency spends more on health care costs.

“You have a balloon that has so much air in it, and when you reduce payment in one area of services, you’re squeezing the balloon on one side, and it pushes payments out to the rest of the balloon,” said Hunsberger. “This balloon is not updated for inflation and relies on continual, almost annual congressional bailouts to get clinicians to some semblance of reasonable payment.”

Because these policy changes apply to the number of claims relating to health care services rendered on the same day and to any specific specialty, it’s unclear how they will impact Medicare beneficiaries. The American Parkinson Disease Association said patients with Parkinson’s disease are worried that it could impact their ability to get outpatient Botox injections that are critical for relieving pain.

McGuire estimates that the policy change will decrease her profits by 6%. The policy goes into effect on Jan. 1. She doesn’t know how much longer her practice will last.

“There are times where I ask myself if I should just close up shop and call it a day, and go find a job at Trader Joe’s or Starbucks, where I can have a reliable income,” she said. “If it wasn’t for the fact that medicine has always been a calling for me and the joy it brings me to take care of patients, I probably would have done that already. But I’m also getting to the point where I don’t think this is financially viable and a long-term solution.”
