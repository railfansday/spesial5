Hottest years on record: 10 topics, from 2016 to 2024 and what 2027 would need

The hottest years on record, including 2016, 2023 and 2024, explained in 10 topics, plus why 2027 could join them if long-term warming and El Niño line up

For the first time in the Copernicus Climate Change Service record, a full calendar year ran more than 1.5°C warmer than the pre-industrial era. Copernicus put 2024 at 1.55°C above its 1850–1900 baseline. The World Meteorological Organization also reported 2024 as the warmest year in its records.

That milestone came only a year after the previous record. Copernicus put 2023 at about 1.48°C above the same baseline. Before that, 2016 held the title, helped along by a strong El Niño. The record has now changed hands twice since 2016.

The pattern raises a question for anyone tracking climate, energy markets or long-range business risk. Could another year soon join the hottest years on record? The answer depends on two forces working together. The first is long-term warming from greenhouse gases, which sets the base trend. The second is short-term natural variation, such as El Niño. El Niño adds warmth to the global average, and its effect shows up in the year after it peaks.

Both forces mattered in 2023 and 2024. El Niño helped lift those years, but it does not explain the full rise across several years. The longer climb comes from greenhouse gas warming.

The numbers also need context. The Paris Agreement's 1.5°C goal refers to warming averaged over decades, not to a single year. One year above the line, as 2024 was, does not by itself mean the goal has been breached. That distinction matters when reading each new annual figure.

A single calendar year above 1.5°C does not mean the world has missed the Paris Agreement target. The 1.5°C goal refers to long-term warming measured over decades, not the temperature of any one year.

That distinction now applies to a real case. The Copernicus Climate Change Service described 2024 as the first calendar year above 1.5°C.

The record year before it sat on the other side of the line. In the Copernicus record, 2023 came in just under 1.5°C. Two record years in a row straddled the threshold, one below and one above. That shows how close individual years now run to that line.

The Paris goal is built around a sustained level of warming. A long-term measure averages many years together. Averaging smooths out the swings that push any one year up or down.

Single years carry short-term noise. El Niño is the clearest example of that noise, and it helped lift both 2023 and 2024. A year boosted that way can sit above the long-term level.

The reverse also holds. A year without that boost can land below the long-term level while the underlying trend keeps rising. Judging the goal on one year would mean judging it partly on natural swings rather than on greenhouse gas warming.

Greenhouse gas warming is the base trend beneath those swings. That base trend is what a decades-long measure is designed to track.

For anyone reading annual climate reports, a record year and the Paris goal measure different things. A record year is a statement about one calendar year. The Paris measure is a statement about sustained warming.

A year above 1.5°C shows that annual temperatures can now reach the threshold. It cannot show, on its own, whether the long-term average has crossed it. That question can only be answered by looking at warming across decades.

El Niño adds warmth to the global average temperature in the year after it peaks, not only while the event is under way. It helped lift both 2023 and 2024, two consecutive years that each set a new high in the Copernicus record.

El Niño is the warm phase of a natural climate pattern in the tropical Pacific Ocean. During an El Niño, surface waters across a wide stretch of that ocean run warmer than usual. The pattern recurs on its own rather than being caused by greenhouse gas emissions.

The timing matters for annual temperature rankings. Records are kept by calendar year, but an El Niño event does not start in January and end in December. Its effect on a given year's average depends on when the event peaked.

That lag explains why a record-setting year can arrive after an El Niño has already passed its peak. A reader looking only at when an El Niño was strongest would miss part of the effect.

For anyone reading a list of record years, this means a year's rank reflects timing as well as the underlying climate. Two years with similar long-term warming can land in different places, depending on where an El Niño peak falls.

El Niño is a short-term boost, not the main driver of rising temperatures. It does not explain the full rise across 2023, 2024 and the years before them. El Niño adds to the long-term warming trend for a period, then fades.

That makes the El Niño share of a record year temporary and tied to a single event. The warming that remains after it fades comes from greenhouse gases.

Long-term warming from greenhouse gases is the base trend beneath the hottest years on record. El Niño can lift a single year above that trend, but it does not explain the full multi-year rise in global temperatures.

One way to picture the relationship is a rising slope with bumps on it. The slope is the greenhouse gas trend. The bumps are short-term natural variation, and El Niño is the best-known source of them. A bump can push one year above the line. Once it passes, the slope is still there.

The record years fit that shape. A strong El Niño helped drive the heat of 2016, the record holder until 2023. El Niño also helped lift 2023 and 2024, the years that took the title after it. Each of those boosts was temporary. A temporary ocean pattern cannot explain a climb that has continued across several years. That part of the rise comes from the base trend.

The distinction shapes how any new record should be read. A record year reflects both forces at once. Separating them shows which part fades when El Niño ends and which part stays in place. Short-term boosts come and go, while the level they push off from keeps rising.

The 1850–1900 baseline is the reference period Copernicus used to measure how warm recent record years were. Each annual warming figure shows how far a year's global average temperature sat above the average for those five decades.

Every warming figure needs a starting point. Without one, a number in degrees Celsius would have nothing to be measured against. The baseline supplies that reference. The period stands in for pre-industrial conditions, which gives the baseline its name.

Because it is an average across five decades, the reference is not tied to any single year. A warm or cool year inside the 1850–1900 window has little pull on the overall average.

The numbers built on it are differences, not raw temperatures. Climate reporting calls such a difference an anomaly. An anomaly shows how much warmer or cooler a year was than the reference period. It does not show what a thermometer read in any one place.

A shared baseline makes years comparable. Copernicus reports different years against the same 1850–1900 average. The gap between their figures therefore reflects a real difference in warmth, not a change in the yardstick.

The baseline stays fixed as the climate warms. Each new record is set against the same pre-industrial average, not against the year it replaced. So a rising series of anomalies tracks warming built up since the late 1800s.

The choice of baseline also shapes the size of the number. Measuring the same year against a later, warmer reference period would produce a smaller anomaly. A reading quoted against one period cannot be set beside a reading quoted against another without adjustment.

The same reference period gives the 1.5°C threshold its meaning. That line is measured from 1850–1900 conditions, not from a recent decade. A year counted as above 1.5°C sits above it relative to the pre-industrial average.

The Copernicus Climate Change Service and the World Meteorological Organization each name a record year by comparing it with their own temperature records. Both reported 2024 as the warmest calendar year in those records.

The unit being ranked is the calendar year. A record covers January through December as a whole. A single hot month or season does not earn the title.

The wording of each finding also sets its limits. The phrase "warmest in its records" refers to the period each organization's data covers. It is a statement about that record, not about every year in Earth's history.

Copernicus and the WMO reached the same conclusion about 2024. The record was reported by two organizations rather than one. When both bodies name the same year, the record does not hinge on a single organization's report.

For 2027 to join the hottest years on record, it would need two things working together. One is the long-term warming trend driven by greenhouse gases. The other is a short-term boost such as El Niño. That pairing explains how a record year forms. It is not a forecast for 2027.

Greenhouse gas warming sets the floor each new year starts from. Because that floor has kept rising, a short-term boost of a given size lands on a warmer starting point than it would have in earlier decades. El Niño on its own cannot account for the climb across several years.

The timing of El Niño is what makes it relevant to any specific year. Its added warmth shows up in the global average in the year after it peaks. Applied to 2027, the question is whether an El Niño peaks during 2026. If one does, 2027 could receive that boost. If none does, 2027 would be leaning on the base trend alone.

The record years show the pairing at work. El Niño helped lift 2023 and 2024. A strong El Niño also helped drive the heat of 2016, the record holder before them. In each case, the short-term push arrived on top of the long-term trend rather than replacing it.

Setting a new record outright and joining the hottest years are different tests. A new record would mean beating 2024, the warmest calendar year in the records of both Copernicus and the World Meteorological Organization. Ranking among the warmest years is a lower bar, and a year can clear it without passing 2024. How high 2027 might land depends partly on the size of any boost it receives.

Specific numbers for 2027 belong to named forecasters and their dated releases. The U.K. Met Office publishes an annual global temperature forecast. NOAA issues outlooks on El Niño and La Niña conditions. Any temperature or probability for 2027 should come from one of those releases, read alongside its publication date.
