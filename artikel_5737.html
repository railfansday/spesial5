Doctor's Mass Shooting Stuns Medical Community

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

— Colleagues never perceived Matt Lambert as a danger to himself or others

Doctors who knew Matt Lambert, MD, the emergency physician at the center of last week's mass shooting in Washington, D.C., were shocked by what happened, and said they never expected him to do something so dire.

Those who had interacted with him described a collegial, friendly person who showed no signs of any distress or any mental health conditions. Physicians spoke with MedPage Today about Lambert on the condition of anonymity.

One said Lambert spoke about problems with his condo unit, but it was "no different than if I said I was frustrated with a building problem. There was never any dark humor or anything that seemed off."

Nor did Lambert ever talk about guns, they said -- despite the fact he had at least three of them in his apartment the day of the shootings. On Nov. 12, he killed the building's property manager, then shot and wounded four other people before killing himself.

Joel Braithwaite, an attorney who represented Lambert, said at a press conference that he had spoken with Lambert not long before the shooting and said he was in "great spirits. ... I'm at a loss for how things took any sort of turn that would have us here today."

Braithwaite described Lambert as a "humanitarian" who helped people with scholarships for medical school. "He was also a great thinker, an author, someone who's very calm and collected."

Michael Brice-Saddler, a reporter for the Washington Sun, spoke with Lambert about a year ago about his ongoing issues with his condo unit. In a video posted with his report, Brice-Saddler said there were "no indications to me that he was dangerous or capable of violence."

"I went back and visited him multiple times," he said. "If I thought my safety was in question, I would have pulled the plug."

Last December, Lambert said this to Brice-Saddler about his ongoing condo unit fight: "It's affected every part of my life -- my sleep, dietary habits. I pride myself on my resilience, and I've been kind of surprised at how much it has been affected."

Brice-Saddler wrote that that Lambert did sometimes use "intense, combative language -- and at one point, his frustration veered into what at the time felt like dark humor." He made a reference to Luigi Mangione, the suspect in the 2024 shooting death of UnitedHealthcare's CEO who is accused of inscribing "delay, deny, depose" on his ammunition. Lambert said those words described the condo association's legal strategy, according to Brice-Saddler's report.

Lambert also posted something similar last month on the X account he created for his unit: "After months of 'Luigi' tactics (delay, deny, defend) by the HOA, we were finally able to submit an independent engineering report into the record," it stated in part.

Brice-Saddler uncovered something else about Lambert in his reporting -- the sad fact that he was raised in Appalachia by a single mother who died of cancer during his first year of medical school at Marshall University in Huntington, West Virginia.

Physicians who knew Lambert told MedPage Today it was unclear if he had a partner.

It's well established that physicians with mental health conditions don't often seek help, which is particularly concerning given that doctors experience elevated rates of burnout, depression, anxiety, and suicide compared with the general population, according to a special communication published last year in JAMA.

Jesse Ehrenfeld, MD, MPH, a past president of the American Medical Association and a co-author of that communication, said that much work stands to be done on reducing barriers to mental healthcare for clinicians. That includes expanding confidential care options and removing intrusive questions on licensure questionnaires.

"We need to make that a best practice everywhere instead of just in a handful of states," Ehrenfeld told MedPage Today.

N. Adam Brown, MD, MBA, an emergency physician and healthcare executive who writes about clinician mental health, noted that "having letters behind our names as physicians does not grant us any special privilege of not having an underlying mental health condition," and that the U.S. needs to "take very seriously the health and wellness of clinicians."

"We also need to recognize the role that gun violence plays in our society," Brown told MedPage Today. "We need to take a look at both sides of the coin -- the mental health aspect, as well as the accessibility of guns -- to prevent acts like this in the future."
