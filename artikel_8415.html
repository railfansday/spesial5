The Not So Spiritual Camino Variant

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Today marked Stage 2 of the Variante Espiritual, which we decided to hike in reverse. After days of walking against the main flow of pilgrims, yesterday was the first time we had the trail mostly to ourselves—aside from the crowds packing onto the upriver boats.

I woke up early to enjoy the solitude of breakfast on the patio with front-row views of the water. Watching the sun light up the morning sky with a plate of boiled quail eggs and a couple of strong cups of espresso was a wonderfully peaceful start. Today was billed as one of the most scenic and spiritual sections of the entire Camino.

At 8:00 AM, we stepped out into a cold breeze blowing across the water. The sun was just over the horizon, casting warm rays across the long footbridge as we crossed a small inlet bay to hike along the coast.

After walking along the coast for a bit, the trail angled inland. Following a slight climb up to a small church, we made our first wrong turn of the day.

Once we realized we were off the official Camino, we decided to forge our own path using Google Maps rather than backtrack.

The route crossed a high bridge over a busy highway before diving into a peaceful forest. Climbing up, we looked back and were amazed to see how far away the coast already looked. We could see the bay, distant coastal cities, and mountains on the horizon—all after just an hour and a half of walking.

As we exited the trees, we stumbled upon a massive vineyard stretching out across the hills. Unfortunately, the winery wasn’t open for another 20 minutes, so we kept moving. Walking through endless fields of grapes was incredible, and skipping the wine stop turned out to be a blessing given what the trail had in store for us later.

Walking against the grain of the Camino created endless confusion for everyone we passed:

They became genuinely concerned for us, which we thought was endearing. Every time we ran into a local, we used a mix of butchered Spanish and sign language to explain we were hiking toward Porto. Other times, we just smiled, waved, and kept moving.

We were deep in the countryside, passing beautiful solid-stone homes with immaculate gardens and tiny backyard vineyards.

By mid-morning, we had already knocked out 9 miles in under three hours.

We spotted the only café we’d seen all morning, grabbed a coffee and black tea, and relaxed on the porch overlooking the river. With such a fast pace early on, we assumed the rest of the day would be a breeze. We were very wrong.

The path ran right alongside the river for the next mile. The water was teeming with huge, two-foot-long fish—an invasive species, I assumed, since the river was practically overflowing with them. As the shade disappeared, the heat began to rise quickly.

At the junction of two busy multilane highways, we tried crossing via an elevated pedestrian bridge. As we climbed, thick briers started crowding the walkway—a red flag I should have noticed sooner. Reaching the other side, I realized we were totally off-track. We backtracked over the highway and ended up at a massive roundabout packed with nonstop traffic. Fortunately, we spotted a pedestrian tunnel to get us safely across.

On the other side of the highway, we stopped at a small roadside restaurant. This was a crucial stop: there were no grocery stores, cafés, or towns along our next stretch of the route.

Before leaving, I refilled our water bottles from the bathroom sink, knowing we were heading into a dry, rural stretch.

The next 5.5 km section was advertised as a tranquil forest trail running along a bubbling river with numerous waterfalls, climbing gently up to the Mosteiro de Santa María da Armenteira.

Since we couldn’t get a room at the monastery, our plan was to split off the Camino early and head to our accommodation in the tiny village of Silva. Armed with Google Maps, I figured it would be an easy shortcut.

Hot, tired, and out of water, we took a brief breather in a shaded cove when a cool breeze finally offered a moment of relief.

After climbing along the creek for 5 km, we left the Camino to forge our own path to Silva.

What started as a narrow, paved road quickly turned into a steep, unpaved logging trail climbing straight up through the forest. On Google Maps, it looked like a legitimate road; in reality, it was an overgrown, barely used dirt track.

I started to question: were we climbing all this way just to hit a dead end and be forced to hike all the way back down?

Knockerz was melting, I was completely exhausted, and we were both soaked in sweat and desperately thirsty. I hit that dazed, survival-mode state where you just keep putting one foot in front of the other, hoping the hill ends soon.

We kept pushing up the incline until, suddenly, the dirt track broke through the trees and met a paved road leading right to our door. Google Maps had come through.

Our lodging was a small, unmanned albergue with eight rooms and a shared kitchen.

Upon arrival our first stop was to hydrate. We went straight into the shared kitchen and drank glass after glass of cold water.

Next stop was to shower. We each took showers with our clothes on to rinse the caked-on sweat and dirt off both our bodies and gear at the same time.

Laying in the bed we were glad this day was done.
