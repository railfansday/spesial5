Can potential clients find you using AI?

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

A few months ago, I got a DM on LinkedIn from someone wanting to interview me for a feature she was writing. At the end of the interview, I asked her how she found me. She replied, “ChatGPT.” 

If you’re running a solo business, you’ve probably thought about how people find you online. Maybe you even track the information by asking or through a “How did you find me?” question on your website.

Many people now ask an AI chatbot for a recommendation, the same way they’ve typed questions into Google for years. No matter how you feel about using AI tools personally, AI search is something that may have more and more of an impact on your business over time.

AI as the new recommendation engine

For a long time, getting found online meant search engine optimization (SEO), or showing up in Google results by publishing content like a blog. The newer version is AEO (answer engine optimization) or GEO (generative engine optimization). The idea is that AI tools might “find” you the way Google once did.

AEO and SEO share the same fundamentals. AI tools pull from publicly available information on the Internet, so the more you put out there, the easier you are to find. 

In BrightLocal’s 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey, 45% of consumers said they used ChatGPT or another AI tool to get recommendations for local businesses. A year earlier, it was 6%. That’s a big jump in a single year. The survey looked at local businesses, so it may not line up exactly with the work you do, but directionally it suggests that more and more people are turning to AI search to find what they’re looking for.

Make sure you have a consistent online presence

Like Google, AI tools look for an authoritative answer to whatever question someone asks. SEO has long focused on ranking in Google by following a set of “rules.” To me, AEO feels more like a wide-open, unclaimed field. No one quite understands it yet, though many people are trying to influence the AI search results.

You can’t control the algorithms or LLMs, but you can control some of the narrative when Google Search or AI search finds you. AI tools piece together a description of you from all of these sources. 

Your website

Your website is an obvious source for who you are and what you do. Your About and Products/ Services pages are good places to start. Make sure they’re up to date with the information you’d want someone to know or find.

From there, you might add content that answers the questions potential clients are likely to ask. My website includes an FAQs section to help the algorithms and LLMs understand me better.

Content also increases the likelihood that you’ll appear in search results, AI or otherwise. A blog works, and so does a publicly available newsletter. Case studies and testimonials help too, especially when they include client names and outcomes (with permission, of course).

Your content on other sites

Content on other sites gives AI tools something to cite when it answers someone’s question. It might be a podcast episode where you were a guest, or an article you wrote for someone else’s website. YouTube videos and social media posts count as well.

Claim your business profile

Google Business Profile is the original way to claim your business and correct its information. It’s worth setting up if you serve a local area, but for many online service providers (like me) it wasn’t necessary.

In November 2026, OpenAI launched a beta of Businesses in ChatGPT, which lets a business claim and manage its listing. It’s in beta and open to eligible ecommerce and online service businesses in the U.S. Once your business is verified, you can update its details. You’ll even be able to see how often ChatGPT mentions your business in its answers and how many people click through to your website.

A LinkedIn Company Page works for a one-person business, too. I have one, and it shows up when people search on LinkedIn.

AI is one more way people ask for information

I’ll admit: I immediately claimed my business profile in ChatGPT when I heard it was available. It only took a few minutes to verify my website and identity, and then I could see what ChatGPT “knows” about me. I also plan to track how many times ChatGPT mentions me (currently sitting at 19).

Word of mouth still keeps my pipeline full, because people know the type of work I do. But I can’t ignore that people are also turning to AI chatbots for recommendations.
