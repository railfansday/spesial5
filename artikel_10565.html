"It stayed alive for so long" - Behind the wheel with the Last Riders saying goodbye to GTA Online, amidst GTA 6's world-changing launch

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

In the decade or so since Grand Theft Auto Online's arrival, its roads have become a kind of home; well-loved by its racers, its gear heads, and speed freaks. It's a game that allows players to lose themselves in the roleplay, the heists, and the crime sprees. But for me, an occasional dipper into its major updates, the car folk have always been the engine driving its appeal.

So when the GTA Online community decided to host Last Ride events this week - a sort of mass in-game road trip and a communal farewell before attention inevitably turned to GTA 6 - I was keen to join in. The Last Drive was scheduled to begin an hour before GTA 6's launch, and would see numerous groups embarking on their own journey across GTA Online's servers, all taking the same route: starting at Los Santos' Del Perro Pier and following the highway around Los Santos' entire perimeter before finally rolling up to its airport. Players would then fly off into the sunset, close the game, and move on. Some just for a while, others perhaps forever.

Kirsty is one of the many long-time players who chose to embark on their own last drive as part of the event. She runs the Fast Crew racing community in GTA Online and has been playing since the beginning in 2013, even meeting her fiance through the game. Unlike some, however, she plans to stick around in GTA Online after GTA 6's release, at least until the new game's equivalent comes along.

It's the social dynamic of GTA Online that makes it special, Kirsty tells me when we speak online, and she calls it a community of players "that thrives from supporting each other, racing together and just having fun". She points to GTA Online's 30-player sessions as one reason for its enduring appeal "That's important for people in communities like ours," she explains. "Gaming is an escape to many people, a distraction or a coping method. GTA is special because it stayed alive for so long, giving people that escape."

Then there's Brittany, who leads the GTA Online Ascent racing community. Brittany has a real-life kart racing background, and built race tracks across various Xbox 360 games before arriving in GTA Online. As with many of the players I spoke with, the in-game connections Brittany made have blossomed into real-life friendships, all built around racing.

"GTA Online introduced me to many lifelong friends," they explain. "Hell, I even met my girlfriend Ari through the GTA Racing community. Every year, five-to-six of us go to the Indy 500 and all of us met through my [GTA Online] PC racing community BRITCAR." Brittany was also planning their own Last Drive, using old cars from the game's earlier years.

It's clear GTA Online has become more than just a game to many people, and I was eager to see how players might choose to say goodbye. So as GTA 6 loomed, I booted up GTA Online and prepared for my own Last Drive, waiting near the pier in a random online lobby. Slowly, other people started to arrive - all seemingly strangers - and at the allotted time, we slowly began making our way around the map as one.

What I saw was brilliant. A snaking line of customised cars, custom paint jobs, and underlighting, slowly navigating the island; a peaceful, reverential convoy of players who weren't blasting away at each other with weapons for once. The chaos of the typical GTA Online lobby never broke out. Instead, there was a sort of unspoken armistice between those eager to enjoy one last moment in a game they'd spent so much time in. Gradually, more people joined our conga line.

Besides being a lovely way to close a chapter for many, it allowed for a cheeky bit of peacocking too. Many players pulled up in truly exceptional rides: there were supercars, bulky muscle cars, and street-racer lightshows, all as much an expression of their owners' personalities and tastes as any human avatar.

At one point, the leader of this snaking pack made a wrong turn, taking us into a dockyard and an unfortunate dead-end. We then all had to slowly reverse out - a time-consuming, if amusing, process which allowed one opportunistic spectator to pull up and take a few of us out with a well-placed RPG. But after we'd all reconvened nearby, the respectful atmosphere resumed. No flying Oppressor Mk II rained missiles on us, and there weren't any kamikaze crashes interrupting our final drive.

When we did finally reach the airport, people left their vehicles and things took a more knockabout turn. Together we huddled for a few moments, letting off gunfire and fireworks into the night. Then the cops came, and the group rushed to steal planes and helicopters before things got wilder still.

Slowly, eventually our group began to disperse. Players either went on to cause some final havoc before GTA 6's arrival, or they did as I did, flying over the ocean into the sunset before leaving the server once and for all. It sounds sappy but it was moving. Here, a game perhaps best known for its player-versus-player hijinks had - thanks to a close-knit community consisting of people like Kirsty, Brittany, and all their friends - became a wholesome drive along. A send off to the game as it was, before ushering in a new dawn.

It speaks to what is special about GTA Online, really. A playground people have spent years in, clearly still important to them, and a chance to say a farewell of sorts from the place they feel most comfortable: behind the wheel.
