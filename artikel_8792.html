Turn Your Pool Workout Into Playtime With These Game-Inspired Exercises

We know all the excuses to skip a swim because we’ve used many of them ourselves. Either it’s too cold outside or it’s too beautiful a day to pass up a bike ride. Or perhaps there’s a storm 100 miles away that might suddenly decide to blow right over the aquatics center. But the truth is, if your workout is interesting enough, you’ll have all the excuses you need to get yourself into the pool, not out of it.

And we’re not just talking about adding in a random drill here and there. Gamifying your swim workout, whether through solo exercises or team activities, helps make a workout enticing enough that you’ll want to do it over and over again. Adding an element of play to your swim can make it more challenging, productive, and fun all at once.

Before we get into a handful of our favorite games and drills, we asked Sage Hopkins, head coach of Women’s Swimming & Diving at San José State University, for his tips on how to make swimming more enjoyable. With these tools in your arsenal, you might not skip your pool workouts anymore, but actually look forward to them instead.

First, Hopkins stresses how crucial it is to hear laughter echoing across the pool deck. This might not be possible if you’re training alone, but if you have a training buddy who always keeps you smiling, make it a priority to meet with them more regularly. Hopkins also encourages using music to establish an upbeat atmosphere; that might look like playing a pump-up song on your way to the pool, or investing in a pair of swimming headphones to keep the tunes going lap after lap.

Once you know your purpose, Hopkins says, it’s easier to figure out what kinds of activities will make swimming more meaningful and enjoyable. Those who enjoy the camaraderie aspect of swimming and triathlon will enjoy team games like sharks and minnows.

Even simple details like naming a set, Hopkins says, can add a thrill to swimming. If there’s a test set you’re always dreading, give it a quirky or amusing name to make it seem less intimidating. You could use the most challenging triathlon you’ve ever lined up for as inspiration (The Wales One Hundreds, for instance, or the Norseman No Breathers).

Now, without further ado, let the games begin!

You don’t need any fancy clubs to play a round of swim golf — just enough glycogen in your head to add numbers. Hopkins suggests this drill for data-driven triathletes because it’s easy to keep track of your progression over time. Essentially, you add the number of strokes with the number of seconds that it takes you to complete a certain distance (such as 50 yards) to get a “score.” You’ll then repeat that distance several times within a single set, trying to keep your score as low as possible. This game helps improve your distance per stroke and ultimately conserve energy.

Healthy suspense invites bursts of dopamine and curiosity into your workout, and it also means that there’s no one to blame but fate for back-to-back sprint sets. Make sure to write the following rules down on a whiteboard or protected piece of paper to reduce confusion.

Start by grabbing a set of waterproof playing cards, and assign a technique to each suit; for example, spades might call for kicking, hearts for drills, diamonds for swimming, and clubs for pulling. Next, assign a distance to groups of numbers (for instance, 2s and 3s might mean 50s, while 4s and 5s might mean 100s, and so on). Keep your jacks, queens, kings, and aces in a separate pile because they’ll dictate the number of repeats (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5, respectively).

So, for example, pulling a 5 of clubs alongside a queen would require you to grab your pull buoy for 2×100. Pulling a 2 of hearts alongside an ace would mean a 5×50 drill.

Another way to bring a measure of unpredictability to your workout is to roll a single die four times to determine your next set. The first roll determines your technique (5s could require kicking), the second your effort level (perhaps 4s always mean sprints), the third your distance (1s could mean 50s), and the fourth your number of reps (anywhere from 1 to 6).

If you were on a summer swim team as a kid, chances are high you played many games of sharks and minnows. The game starts out with one “shark” who tries to tag all the other “minnows” as they swim from one side of the pool to the other. Each swimmer who gets tagged turns into a shark for the next round. You can complicate this game by instituting an underwater safety zone.

If you have an area of the pool to yourself, form teams, set up goals on opposite walls with pull buoys, grab a ball, and play water polo. This is an excellent way to make vertical kicking, sprinting, and treading water more exciting. It also facilitates team bonding.

Head to the deep end for this game to work on endurance as you tread water and practice vertical kicking. If the lifeguards allow it, you can play water volleyball over the flags with an inflatable beach ball. Your pool might even have a water volleyball net, and some versions float so that you can move them as required.

Hopkins recommends “call it sets,” inspired by Bob Steele, to work on pacing and fine-tuning your internal clock. Essentially, you and your training partners will swim a set of 100s, and before each repeat, each one of you will predict the time you expect to hit. Whoever is closest to their estimates wins the set.

Chase sets are a fun way for training groups with seemingly disparate ability levels to unite, says Hopkins. Slower swimmers are allowed a head start, while faster swimmers are tasked with trying to catch them. Chase sets are ideally employed for speedwork, not drills.

Casino night is extraordinarily fun for large teams and helps highlight everyone’s unique talents. First, two or three swimmers who are roughly equivalent in speed line up at the blocks. Then, the other teammates line up behind the swimmer they predict will win.

From there, you’ll use cards or dice to determine the “event” that the swimmers will be competing in (see general rules above in the solo workouts). Whoever has picked the winning swimmer gets a rest break while the rest of the team completes a penalty swim (a 50 sprint, perhaps). If you have one seemingly unbeatable swimmer on the team, you can assign them a permanent handicap.
