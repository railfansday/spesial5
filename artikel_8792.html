The 7 Best Garmin Watches for Cyclists: Editor-Tested Picks for Training, Recovery, and Everyday Wear

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Skip the bike computer? These Garmin watches can track rides, workouts, sleep, and recovery.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

Cyclists, myself included, are often data driven. Even for cyclists that aren’t particularly motivated to optimize their performance there’s a belief that if a ride, run, hike, or any activity isn’t on Strava, it didn’t happen. Tracking your rides is easy to do with a head unit, but the tracking is limited to just the time spent on your bike.

Many riders and multisport athletes just don’t need a dedicated bike computer—especially with how smart smartwatches have become over the last few years. Smartwatches offer many of the same features that you find on bike computers, but smartwatches can also track sleep, heart rate, and activities you do off the bike. I have a few friends who even track mowing their lawn to make sure they don’t lose out on the activity for the day. With so many smartwatches out there, it can be hard to pick which one to choose, but time and again we go back to Garmin as the benchmark for tracking tech.

Tracking three back-to-back activities can be an annoyance for data-hungry triathletes. However, the Forerunner 970’s “Triathlon” mode gives multisport athletes the freedom to change between workouts without ending their activity. You only need to tap the lap button at the start and end of each transition. However, the 970 is a great option for folks who aren’t also swimming and running on their bike rides.

While I’m not a triathlete, I’ve been testing the 970 for a couple of weeks during road rides, commutes, and my daily routine; my overall impression is quite positive. This is a performance-optimized smartwatch for performance-minded athletes. It can track and measure seemingly every data point across an enormous range of sports and activities. Specifically related to cycling, the Forerunner 970 has preset profiles for: Road, mountain biking, gravel, commuting, touring, e-biking, eMTB riding, indoor, cyclocross, and BMX. If none of those options meet your niche, you can also create your own.

The variety and specificity of data you can track with the Forerunner 970 is mind-boggling. If you want to measure or see something related to riding, you probably can—although finding it in the array of options is sometimes the hardest part. And like Garmin’s head units, that’s the watch’s biggest downside. Garmin packs so many options into its devices that it can be difficult to navigate within the screens. Luckily, the Garmin Connect app is (mostly) easy to use, and changes made in the app instantly push to the phone.

A feature I like on the 970 is the ability to “Lap by Location”. With this turned on, your watch will automatically start a new lap each time you pass through that GPS location again. That means no more forgetting to tap “Lap” when you’re deep in the red zone with one lap to go in an event. This makes the 970 perfect for cyclocross, criterium, and XC mountain bike racers seeking to track in-race performance data at a granular level.

The Venu 4’s sleek, not-too-techy profile allows you to maximize your smartwatch mileage, both during workouts and in daily life. It prioritizes general smartwatch functionality, but there’s still plenty of processing power. That makes the Venu great for all-day wear, both on and off the bike.

Senior Art Director, Colin McSherry, has tested the Venu 4 against his trusty Forerunner. He notes the Venu‘s solid 12-day battery life is closer to 8 to 10 days when heavily using route navigation for cycling, running, and hiking. He also said, “I found the navigation a bit simpler and easier to use compared to the Garmin Forerunner 970 due to fewer physical buttons.”

On the bike, starting and tracking a workout is easy. McSherry noted that, “GPS does take a few seconds to register, but usually picks up as you are taking your first few pedal strokes.” Compared to his Wahoo Elemnt, he found the GPS data was very close. “So if I forgot my computer or wanted to get away from staring at a screen while on a ride, it’s a great way to track basic metrics,” he said.

Garmin released the Fēnix 9 this fall, but we still love the Fēnix 8—and there are some great deals on the watch right now. The 8 Pro is Garmin’s flagship model in its class-leading Fēnix lineup. This watch is packed with virtually every feature you could want to measure and manage your outdoor adventures. The Fēnix 8 Pro includes it all in a rugged design featuring dive-rated buttons, a crisp AMOLED display (or MicroLED for an extra $400), and a titanium bezel. The device connects via ANT+ or Bluetooth to third-party sensors and supports a wide range of activity profiles.

However, the Fēnix 8 Pro’s biggest upgrade is the included inReach satellite/LTE connectivity. (You previously needed a separate Garmin inReach device for that.) For backcountry adventurers, the ability to message, call, share location, or trigger SOS from your wrist is a must-have feature. All that capability comes at a cost—in both price and battery life. Garmin claims up to 15 days of battery life (in smartwatch mode) for the 47mm model and 27 days for the 51mm version. However, those numbers drop when LTE or satellite features are active.

Jason Griffin, Senior Health and Fitness Editor, is currently testing the 8 Pro. He said, “It’s smarter than I expected a watch could be,” and notes that “answering calls through the watch is super easy, and tracking both rides and runs is intuitive.” He added, “The flashlight was great to find things in the middle of the night while I was holding my sleeping toddler.”

While the Forerunner 265 is an older model, we find it strikes a good balance between Garmin’s budget-minded watches and its thousand-dollar devices. The 265’s accuracy is top-tier for its price, and this model has a far more readable display than its predecessor, thanks to a bright OLED screen—a near-critical feature if you don’t want to take your hands off your grips for too long to check your mileage.

If you have smaller wrists (or prefer a more compact watch), the Forerunner 265S is a great choice. It’s lighter, slimmer, and even offers longer battery life.

Garmin’s new Forerunner 70 isn’t really an update of its long-running Forerunner 55 entry-level model: the 70 is an entirely new device. The new model has a larger display, tons more features, and (most importantly for cyclists) many more bicycle profile options.

The Forerunner 70 is designed to be a great running watch, and its features and data logging abilities are best for off-bike activities. However, the 70 is still a good option for fitness-minded riders or cyclists needing to log their rides and basic training data. So, while the 70 cannot connect to your power meter or smart trainer, it still features solid GPS tracking, a heart rate monitor, and it can follow pre-planned courses.

Buying Tip: If you need to track power, spend the extra $50 and upgrade to the Forerunner 170 model.

Garmin released the Enduro 4 this fall and we are currently testing it to see how it compares to the Enduro 3. The Enduro 3 is still a great option from Garmin and is on sale right now. The Enduro 3 is what happens when you take the top-shelf performance of the Fēnix and optimize it for maximum battery life. It’s a high-caliber solar watch that can last a staggering 36 days in smartwatch mode and up to 150 hours in GPS mode. It’s perfect for long bikepacking outings to places lacking spots to recharge.

At 51mm, the watch face is large but surprisingly unobtrusive. It feels light (63 grams) and comfortable on the wrist, even during long rides. The Enduro also has a sleeker profile than many premium smartwatches, with fewer sharp edges and oversized buttons. A feature our tester didn’t know they needed is the built-in flashlight. It works very well and is unexpectedly useful in everyday situations.

While the Velcro strap may not win style points off the bike, it’s incredibly functional. It allows for a perfectly snug fit, unlike traditional watch bands (frequently, one loop is too tight and the next is too loose).

Garmin markets the Forerunner 170 Music as a running watch, but that label doesn’t do it justice. It’s a true multi-sport smartwatch, built to support a wide range of activities—including multiple cycling disciplines—and it connects to ANT+ and Bluetooth sensors for more detailed ride data and notifications.

As the name suggests, it includes 4GB of onboard storage for music. This means you can leave your phone behind and still have your playlist. It also features an altimeter, barometer, compass (ABC sensors), a pulse oximeter, and advanced tracking tools, making it capable in almost any scenario.

If you participate in various outdoor activities, you may prefer to own a single device to track everything. Whether cycling, running, hiking, paddling, walking the dog, or playing pickleball, a Garmin watch offers powerful, versatile functionality in a streamlined profile that’s always with you.

If you regularly track multiple types of workouts, while many road and gravel riders prefer cycling head units for instant access to maps, routing details, power metrics, and other data, some riders prefer smartwatches for logging ride data.

Not needing to learn or own multiple overlapping devices simplifies and declutters your life. And in his excellent story, “The Problem with Modern Cycling Computers Isn’t the Huge Screens,” Test Editor Dan Chabanov argues that many head units are bloated with unnecessary functions.

Mountain bikers who often ride technical trails (bike park riders and enduro racers, for example) are increasingly turning to watches to document rides. With some cycling computers costing upwards of $1,000, you don’t want it dangling off the front of your bar or attached to your top tube in the event of a crash. Additionally, a computer is sometimes an unwanted distraction when trying to concentrate on a challenging descent.

Triathletes and duathlon racers are another group of riders who often prefer smartwatches. Watches allow athletes to transition between segments without needing to change devices or combine files after an event. Similarly, since the discipline involves swapping out muddy bikes in the pits during a race, cyclocross racers often use smartwatches.

A quick spin through Garmin’s website reveals a dizzying lineup of watches—sleek, feature-packed, and often overwhelming to sort through. While nearly all of them include the core functions you’d expect from a sports smartwatch, four series stand out as the best options for cyclists: Forerunner, Enduro, Fēnix, and Venu.

The Forerunner series is Garmin’s core watch for cyclists and multisport athletes. It includes five models, starting with the $250 Forerunner 70, which covers the basics, and going up to the $750 Forerunner 970, which combines high-end smartwatch features with advanced training and navigation tools.

The Enduro is designed for endurance athletes and offers the longest battery life of any Garmin watch. It includes the premium features of a cycling computer and smartwatch, along with extras such as solar charging and a built-in flashlight—making it a great option for long adventures.

The Fēnix series shares many advanced features with the Enduro line—activity profiles, multisport tracking, and smartwatch tools—but adds specialized options for adventurers and divers. Fēnix has a slightly shorter battery life than the Enduro, and the watch faces are smaller.

Finally, the Venu is another option. It blends performance training, GPS, and data tracking features with fashion-forward styling. So, it looks good on and off the bike.
