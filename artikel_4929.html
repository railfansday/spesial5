The experience and expression of human attachment is complex, to say the least. As humans, we may share a universal ingrained wiring for connection, but how we navigate this interpersonal world is not the same from person to person.

Are you someone who obsesses about others? Maybe you understand infatuation in the early stages of love, but then know how to transition to a mutual relationship or let go? Forming steady and stable connections is easy for some people; others are tortured with intense and uncertain attachments. New research provides insight into limerence, a specific form of intense attachment that is starting to gain more attention.

Imagine someone fixating on another person. This isn't hard to do. We hear about it on podcasts, watch it unfold in fantastic crime dramas, and, of course, some of us experience it in real life. From infatuation to unrequited love, examples of preoccupations with another person, outside of established relationships, color the romantic landscape. Limerence, however, is different.

Limerence is an intense and highly disruptive cognitive and emotional experience (Marshall et al., 2025; Tennov, 1979). Individuals in the throes of limerence are overwhelmed by the desire to be viewed positively by a specific someone else (i.e., the limerence object). This obsession manifests as uncontrollable rumination to the point that it interferes with day-to-day life.

Limerence is typically expressed toward someone with whom the experiencer is highly unlikely to form a real relationship. On the surface, this might seem like unrequited love, but it is far more intense and generally less realistic.

Different dimensions of limerence are predictable by key psychological constructs. Recent research suggests limerence is best understood as a two-dimensional construct, comprised of both an intense need for attachment and neglect of self or others (Marshall et al., 2025).

Attachment anxiety, imaginative fantasy (i.e., the tendency to daydream), and the need to belong are linked to the need-for-attachment dimension of limerence for those experiencing it. This dimension is also predicted by psychological inflexibility and emotional dependence, obsessive preoccupation, self-conscious anxiety, and idealization (Marshall et al., 2025). The neglect dimension operates similarly, except it is not predicted by attachment orientation or idealization, and is associated with more social anxiety and lower self-esteem.

Whereas scholars have noted parallels between limerence and addiction and noted the potential path from limerence to stalking (e.g., Bradbury et al., 2025), there's reason to suggest that limerence can also serve as a coping mechanism in an unfulfilling relational context. Experiencing limerence can be a one-time experience or repeated, short-lived or long (Tennov, 1979), yet the context in which it occurs offers the suggestion that it is the mind's way of addressing an undermet need for belonging and intimacy. The field has much more to learn about limerence as an unusual manifestation of the human attachment system.
