EXCLUSIVE: California officials urged to examine Zillow Preview, referral model

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Consumer and advocacy groups asked California’s housing regulators to investigate Zillow’s pre-market Zillow Preview program and its agent referral model. The letter also alleges steering toward Zillow Home Loans and argues renters faced higher costs after the Redfin rentals deal.  AI Summary

A coalition of consumer and advocacy groups is urging California housing regulators to investigate Zillow Group’s listing, agent referral and mortgage practices, arguing the company is distorting the state’s housing market and overcharging buyers and renters.

Demand Progress, Revolving Door Project, Americans for Financial Reform, Children’s Advocacy Alliance and the Consumer Federation of America sent a letter to Californian officials alleging that Zillow has leveraged its position in listings, buyer representation and mortgage lending to “soak homebuyers” and renters, and call for enforcement under multiple state laws.

The letter, addressed to Rohit Chopra, secretary of the California Business and Consumer Services Agency, and Chika Sunquist, commissioner of the California Department of Real Estate, contends that “the California housing market is broken” and that Zillow is “one of the most aggressive actors in distorting the market, to its own profit and to the detriment of the public.”

The groups focus heavily on Zillow Preview, the firm’s pre-marketing program launched in March 2026 that allows major brokerages to promote listings on Zillow before they hit the broader market. Keller Williams, REMAX, HomeServices of America, Side and United Real Estate were among some of the first firms to sign on to the program. More than 400 brokerages have entered exclusive agreements with Zillow, the letter states, arguing that the arrangement locks “freshest inventory” on Zillow and, more recently, Realtor.com, while sidelining competing platforms. The coalition urges California regulators to scrutinize these vertical agreements and what it characterizes as “monopsonistic capture of pre-market listings.”

The coalition also targets Zillow’s long-running agent referral model. The letter alleges that when consumers click “Contact agent” or “Request a tour” on many listings, they are often routed to Zillow-affiliated buyer agents rather than the listing agent, citing prior academic research and litigation.

A Wharton School study found most consumers believed those buttons would connect them to an agent associated with the property and that “almost none” could correctly identify who would contact them based on Zillow’s interface, according to the letter.

The groups highlight Zillow’s Flex program (now known as Zillow Preferred), which charges participating buyer agents a “success fee” reportedly as high as 40% of the buy-side commission. They argue that this structure reduces incentives for agents to compete on price and aligns agent incentives more closely with Zillow than with consumers.

The letter notes that Zillow has faced class action litigation over these practices, including claims that its referral and commission structures constitute a racketeering “enterprise.” The case, filed in federal court in Washington state, challenges both the referral flows and undisclosed fees as deceptive under federal and state law.

On the listing side, the group says Zillow offers listing agents a 10% share of the buy-side commission on deals that close from Preview leads, including when the buyer ultimately purchases a different property. They argue that this unilateral setting of referral compensation could conflict with the spirit or letter of the National Association of Realtors’ (NAR) commission lawsuit settlement, which sought to decouple listing and buyer-broker compensation.

On the financing side, the letter accuses Zillow of using its agent network to steer borrowers toward Zillow Home Loans. It cites allegations in the amended complaint in the renewed Taylor lawsuit that affiliated buyer agents risk losing leads or expulsion from Flex if they do not prioritize Zillow’s lending arm.

Most buyers do not shop widely for financing. Zillow’s own research has found that roughly 72% of buyers submit just one mortgage application, according to the letter. Separately, Zillow research has shown that rate shopping can save borrowers thousands of dollars over the life of a loan.

The coalition points to an empirical analysis by Georgetown University Law Center professor Steven Salop, which concluded that Zillow Home Loans borrowers paid about $4,600 more on average than comparable borrowers at other lenders between 2025 and 2027. Salop’s work estimates $186 million in projected overcharges over that period, with even higher average costs for military veterans and Black and Asian borrowers.

The letter compares the alleged steering to the in-house mortgage referral arrangements that drew a 2024 lawsuit from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) against Rocket Homes. That case, brought under the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act, focused on alleged kickback and referral schemes between affiliated real estate and mortgage businesses and has since been dismissed.

The advocacy groups also raise concerns about Zillow’s expansion in rentals. They note that renters tend to have lower incomes and less bargaining power than buyers, and argue that Zillow has long tried to prioritize its own rental listings while limiting rivals’ visibility.

The letter references Zillow’s $100 million multifamily rental syndication deal with Redfin, claiming that it paid Redfin to shutter its multifamily rental advertising business and transfer clients to Zillow. That agreement later triggered an antitrust complaint by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and five states, which alleged Zillow illegally paid a competitor to exit the rental listings market.

FTC staff estimated that landlords using Zillow paid an average 14.5% more per listing after the deal, according to public statements cited in the letter. The coalition says renters ultimately bore those higher marketing costs.

Zillow and Redfin settled with the FTC in August 2026 without admitting wrongdoing. The order requires changes to certain agreements but allows the companies to maintain some form of partnership through at least 2030.

The coalition urges California officials to deploy what it calls the state’s “strongest toolkit in the nation” against Zillow’s business model. Specifically, the letter asks Chopra and Sunquist to use the Department of Real Estate’s licensing and rulemaking authority to investigate alleged steering by licensees that may violate fiduciary duties, and to consider rules requiring privately marketed properties to be listed concurrently on public platforms, drawing on legislation passed in Connecticut and New York.

Additionally, they ask officials to investigate and, where warranted, bring actions under the state’s Cartwright Act and Unfair Competition Law against Zillow Preview’s exclusive agreements, Zillow’s arrangement with Realtor.com and any “re-coupling” of commissions after the NAR settlement.

They also want officials to pursue enforcement under the Consumers Legal Remedies Act and Unfair Competition Law targeting Zillow’s “Contact agent” and “Request a tour” interfaces, alleged nondisclosure of Preferred referral fees and failure to disclose that agent recommendations can be driven by Zillow’s financial interests and to coordinate with the multistate coalition that brought the Redfin rental action, to avoid jurisdictional gaps that could allow similar conduct to persist in California.

The groups argue that while the FTC settlement with Zillow over its Redfin agreement “closed one case,” it did not address what they describe as a broader pattern of conduct spanning purchase, financing and rental markets. They frame California, the nation’s largest housing market, as a critical venue for testing new state-level responses.

If regulators accept the coalition’s framing, California could move to restrict or reshape referral fee structures and pre-market listing programs, directly affecting how agents source leads and are paid. In addition, a California investigation into Zillow’s referral flows could set new guardrails on when and how real estate professionals can recommend affiliate lenders, particularly around disclosure and independence requirements.

Zillow did not immediately respond to HousingWire’s request for comment on the letter.
