Jim Jordan and the 2027 House speaker vote: 10 things to know

Jim Jordan and the 2027 House speaker vote, explained through his record, his 2023 bid and the rules that decide who holds the gavel after the midterms

The next speaker of the House will be chosen by a chamber that has not been elected yet. Voters set its membership in the November 2026 midterm elections. The members they send to Washington will then pick a speaker on the opening day of the new Congress in January 2027. Until those two events happen, no one can say who will hold the gavel.

That uncertainty frames any question about Jim Jordan and the House speaker vote. Jordan is a Republican who has represented Ohio's 4th Congressional District since January 2007. He has chaired the House Judiciary Committee since January 2023. In 2015 he helped found the House Freedom Caucus and served as its first chairman. He wrestled at the University of Wisconsin. Before he reached Congress, he served in the Ohio legislature and worked as an assistant wrestling coach at Ohio State University.

Jordan has also been a speaker candidate before. In October 2023, after Kevin McCarthy was removed from the post, the Republican conference chose Jordan as its nominee. He lost three successive floor votes that month, and the conference then dropped his bid. Mike Johnson of Louisiana was elected speaker on October 25, 2023.

The job carries weight beyond the House floor. Under federal succession law, the speaker is second in line for the presidency, after the vice president.

The Constitution says only that the House chooses its speaker. It does not require the speaker to be a House member, though every speaker so far has been one. The winning threshold is a majority of the votes cast for a named person. Absences and "present" votes lower that threshold.

The 10 slides that follow do not predict an outcome. They set out Jordan's record, the events of 2023, the mechanics of electing a speaker and the calendar that leads to January 2027. Each one explains a single piece of how the next leader of the House will be chosen, using facts already on the record.

Jim Jordan, an Ohio Republican, has held the U.S. House seat for Ohio's 4th Congressional District since January 2007. His route to Washington ran through college wrestling, a coaching job at Ohio State University and a stretch in the Ohio legislature.

Jordan competed on the wrestling team at the University of Wisconsin. The university's flagship campus sits in Madison, the state capital. Wisconsin and Ohio State both compete in the Big Ten Conference. His years as an athlete and his later coaching work took place inside the same college league.

After his own competitive career, he worked as an assistant wrestling coach at Ohio State. The university's main campus is in Columbus, which is also Ohio's capital. The Ohio Statehouse, where the state legislature meets, stands in the same city.

Jordan went on to serve in that legislature, formally called the Ohio General Assembly. It has two chambers. The Ohio House of Representatives has 99 members elected to two-year terms. The Ohio Senate has 33 members elected to four-year terms. Jordan served in both chambers. Together they write state law and pass the state budget.

His move to Congress came in the 2006 election. He won the 4th District seat after Mike Oxley, the Republican who had held it, retired. Jordan took office with the 110th Congress, which convened in January 2007.

U.S. House members serve two-year terms. Every one of the 435 seats is on the ballot in each even-numbered year. Holding the 4th District without a break since 2007 means Jordan has won it in every general election from 2006 onward. The 119th Congress, which runs through early January 2027, is his 10th in a row.

His own seat is part of the next turnover. The 4th District is on the ballot again in November 2026. Its voters will choose who represents it in the 120th Congress, the body that elects a speaker on its opening day.

Jim Jordan has chaired the House Judiciary Committee since January 2023. The Ohio Republican leads one of the oldest standing committees in the House, created in 1813.

He took the gavel at the start of the 118th Congress, after Republicans won the House majority in the 2022 midterm elections. Committee chairs in the House come from the majority party. When control of the chamber changes hands, the leadership of each committee usually changes with it.

The Judiciary Committee's jurisdiction covers a wide stretch of federal law. It handles bills on the federal courts, criminal law, immigration, antitrust, intellectual property and civil liberties. Proposed amendments to the U.S. Constitution also pass through it before reaching the House floor.

The committee has a central role in impeachment. Articles of impeachment against presidents have usually been drafted and approved there. The committee approved articles against Richard Nixon in 1974, Bill Clinton in 1998 and Donald Trump in 2019.

The House panel does not vote on judicial nominees. The Senate confirms federal judges. That work belongs to the Senate Judiciary Committee. The House committee shapes the courts through legislation and oversight instead.

Much of a chairman's power lies in setting the agenda. The chair decides which bills get hearings and markups, schedules witnesses and directs the majority's committee staff. Legislation that the chair declines to take up rarely advances.

Oversight is the other major lever. House committees can investigate executive branch agencies within their jurisdiction. For Judiciary, that includes the Justice Department and the FBI. Hearings, document requests and subpoenas are the standard tools.

The gavel does not carry over automatically. Each new Congress reorganizes its committees, and the full House approves committee assignments by resolution. Who chairs Judiciary in the Congress that begins in January 2027 depends first on which party holds the majority after the November 2026 midterms.

Jim Jordan was one of the founding members of the House Freedom Caucus in 2015, and he served as the group's first chairman. The caucus is an organized bloc of conservative Republicans inside the House.

A caucus in the House is an informal group of lawmakers who organize around a shared interest, region or ideology. It is not a formal House body. It cannot hold hearings, report bills or assign committee seats. Its influence depends on members voting together. That leverage grows on floor votes where a party's margin is narrow.

A caucus also differs from a party conference. The Republican conference includes every Republican in the House, and it chooses the party's leaders. The Freedom Caucus is a smaller group within that conference. Its members can press their own leadership from inside the party without leaving it.

The Freedom Caucus was announced in January 2015, at the start of the 114th Congress. It had nine founders. Alongside Jordan, they included Scott Garrett of New Jersey, Raúl Labrador of Idaho, Mick Mulvaney of South Carolina and Ron DeSantis of Florida. The other four were Justin Amash of Michigan, Matt Salmon of Arizona, John Fleming of Louisiana and Mark Meadows of North Carolina.

Several founders later held posts outside the House. DeSantis became governor of Florida, and Labrador became Idaho's attorney general. Mulvaney served as acting White House chief of staff. Meadows later held that job outright. Amash left the Republican Party in 2019.

Membership in the caucus is by invitation. The group does not publish a full roster of its members.

Jordan came to the chairmanship with experience leading House conservatives. He chaired the Republican Study Committee, a larger conservative group, during the 112th Congress. He led the Freedom Caucus through its first two years. Meadows succeeded him as chairman in 2017.

The new caucus moved against its own party's leadership within months. In July 2015, Meadows filed a resolution to remove John Boehner as speaker. Boehner announced his resignation that September. He left Congress at the end of October 2015.

Jim Jordan won the House Republican conference's nomination for speaker in October 2023, after Kevin McCarthy was removed from the post. He then lost three successive floor votes that month, and the conference dropped his bid.

The vacancy that opened the door was itself without precedent. On October 3, 2023, the House voted to remove McCarthy through a motion to vacate the chair. No speaker had been ousted that way before.

Patrick McHenry of North Carolina became speaker pro tempore while the chamber searched for a replacement. The temporary role kept the post filled in name. The House still had to elect a permanent speaker.

Jordan was not the conference's first choice. Republicans initially nominated Steve Scalise of Louisiana, the House majority leader. Scalise withdrew from the race a day later. The conference then held another internal vote and selected Jordan.

The nomination settled only the party's internal choice. It did not make Jordan speaker. That decision belongs to the full House, in a public roll call on the floor.

The first floor vote took place on October 17, 2023. Jordan did not win. The House voted again the next day, October 18, and he fell short a second time. A third ballot on October 20 produced the same result.

After the third loss, House Republicans met privately and voted by secret ballot to drop Jordan as their nominee. His candidacy ended without his taking the gavel.

The contest then reopened inside the conference. Tom Emmer of Minnesota, the majority whip, won the next nomination on October 24 but withdrew the same day. The speakership stayed vacant until the full House elected Mike Johnson of Louisiana later that month.

For anyone following the 2027 contest, the 2023 bid is a record, not a forecast. It shows that Jordan won his conference's nomination and did not win on the floor. It does not settle whom the next Congress will choose when it elects a speaker in January 2027.

The House elects its speaker by roll call on the first day of a new Congress. The winner needs a majority of the votes cast for a named person. That vote comes before nearly all other business. The chamber cannot fully organize itself until someone holds the gavel.

The authority comes from Article I, Section 2 of the Constitution, which says the House chooses its speaker and other officers. The text sets no further qualifications. It does not require the speaker to be a member of the House. In practice, every speaker in the chamber's history has been a sitting member.

On opening day, the clerk of the House from the previous Congress presides over the chamber. At that point, members-elect have not yet taken the oath of office. The clerk calls the roll, and each member-elect answers aloud with the name of a candidate. Each party usually puts forward a nominee before the vote. Members are still free to name anyone they choose.

The math turns on who actually votes for a person. That is not the same as a majority of the full chamber. If all 435 seats are filled and every member names a candidate, the threshold is 218. Members who are absent drop out of the count. So do members who answer "present." Each of those lowers the number a candidate needs to win.

When no one reaches a majority, the clerk calls the roll again. The House repeats the process until a candidate clears the threshold. In January 2023, Kevin McCarthy needed 15 ballots before he won the gavel at the start of the 118th Congress.

Once a speaker is elected, the new speaker takes the oath of office. The longest-serving member, known as the dean of the House, typically administers it. The speaker then swears in the rest of the members as a group. Only after that can the House adopt its rules and turn to legislative business.

Mike Johnson, a Republican from Louisiana, was elected speaker of the House on October 25, 2023. He won the post after Jim Jordan's bid ended. He was re-elected speaker on January 3, 2025, when the 119th Congress convened.

Johnson represents Louisiana's 4th Congressional District, which covers the northwest part of the state and includes Shreveport. He won the seat in 2016 and took office in January 2017. Before Congress, he served in the Louisiana House of Representatives. He earned his law degree at Louisiana State University's Paul M. Hebert Law Center and practiced as an attorney focused on constitutional cases.

His rise inside the House Republican conference came through internal party posts rather than a committee gavel. From 2019 to 2021 he chaired the Republican Study Committee, a large caucus of conservative House members. He then became vice chair of the Republican conference, a lower-ranking leadership job he still held when the 118th Congress began in 2023.

The October 2023 contest changed his position. The House removed Kevin McCarthy as speaker on October 3, 2023. Patrick McHenry of North Carolina then served as speaker pro tempore. The House did not take up regular legislation while the post sat empty. After Republicans dropped Jordan's candidacy, the conference turned to other contenders. Johnson won its nomination and was then elected on the House floor.

His route to the chair bypassed the top rungs of party leadership. Before the vote, he held none of the three highest House Republican leadership posts: speaker, majority leader or majority whip. He had never chaired a standing committee. Jordan's candidacy also came before his that month.

The January 2025 vote reflected a basic rule of the chamber. A speaker's election lasts only as long as the Congress that chose him. The 119th Congress runs through early January 2027.

Johnson's current term as speaker ends with the 119th Congress. The members who take their seats in January 2027 will hold a new vote. No House procedure carries the gavel from one Congress to the next. A sitting speaker who wants to keep the job must win it again on the floor.

The November 2026 midterm elections decide who gets a vote for speaker in January 2027. Every voting seat in the House is on the ballot. The members who win those races choose the speaker on the opening day of the next Congress.

House members serve two-year terms. All 435 voting seats come up for election at the same time. That includes the seat of the sitting speaker and the seat of every committee chair. Jim Jordan's seat in Ohio's 4th Congressional District is on the same ballot.

Federal law sets congressional elections for the Tuesday after the first Monday in November. In 2026, that date is November 3. The vote is called a midterm because it falls midway through a presidential term.

The results settle two questions that matter for the speaker vote. The first is which party holds a majority of seats. The second is how large that majority is. Neither will be known until the votes are counted.

Margin matters on the floor. With all 435 seats filled and every member voting for a named candidate, the winning number is 218. A party with a narrow edge has little room to spare. If a handful of its own members back someone else, its nominee can fall short even though the party holds more seats than the other side.

The House may not have all 435 members on opening day. A death, a resignation or an unresolved race can leave a seat empty when the new Congress convenes. The Constitution directs the governor of the affected state to call an election to fill a House vacancy. That process can take months.

The House also has six nonvoting members. They represent the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico and four other U.S. territories. They do not take part in the speaker election.

The vote happens before the new members take their oath. On opening day, the clerk of the House presides while members-elect call out their votes. Once a candidate wins, the new speaker is sworn in and then administers the oath to the rest of the chamber.

The midterms fix who sits in that room. Each party's choice of a nominee comes after that, followed by the floor vote.

The speaker of the House is second in the line of presidential succession, behind only the vice president. Whoever wins the speaker vote in January 2027 will hold that place for as long as they keep the gavel.

The order comes from the Presidential Succession Act of 1947. After the vice president, the law lists the speaker, then the president pro tempore of the Senate, then members of the Cabinet. Cabinet officers follow in the order their departments were created, starting with the secretary of state.

The line has not always looked this way. An 1886 law left congressional officers out and ran succession through the Cabinet. Harry Truman became president in April 1945, following the death of Franklin D. Roosevelt. He then served nearly four years with no vice president. Truman pushed for a change. He favored putting an elected official next in line rather than a Cabinet secretary appointed by the president.

A speaker does not step into the presidency automatically. The 1947 act requires a speaker who acts as president to resign both the speakership and the House seat. That person must also meet the Constitution's eligibility rules for the presidency. Those rules require a natural-born citizen who is at least 35 years old and has lived in the U.S. for 14 years.

The 25th Amendment, ratified in 1967, made long vacancies in the vice presidency less likely. It lets the president nominate a new vice president, who takes office after majority votes in both the House and the Senate. Before the amendment, the office could stay empty until the next presidential term began.

Short gaps still occur while a nominee awaits confirmation. Spiro Agnew resigned as vice president in October 1973. Speaker Carl Albert stood first in line for the presidency until Gerald Ford $F was confirmed as vice president that December.

No speaker has ever become president through the line of succession. James K. Polk, a former speaker, is the only person to have held both offices. He reached the White House by winning the 1844 election.

The speaker's place in the line ties a House leadership contest to the executive branch. The speaker is chosen by members of one chamber, not by a national electorate. The winner still becomes the first person in line after the vice president. That link applies to whoever the House elects when the next Congress convenes in January 2027.

A party's speaker nominee is chosen inside that party before the full House votes, but the nomination itself decides nothing. The binding choice comes only on the House floor, where the whole chamber takes part.

The Constitution gives the power to choose a speaker to the House as a whole. It does not mention political parties, and it sets out no nominating process. The internal party step operates outside the constitutional text.

In the House, Republicans organize as the House Republican Conference. Democrats organize as the House Democratic Caucus. Each group settles on its own candidate for speaker. Its members then carry that choice to the floor.

The gap between those two stages is where a nomination can come apart. A candidate can win the party's internal backing and still fall short once the full chamber votes. Only the floor result decides who holds the gavel.

Jim Jordan's experience in October 2023 put that distinction on the record. After Kevin McCarthy was removed as speaker, the Republican conference made Jordan its nominee. Jordan then lost three successive floor votes that month. The conference then dropped his bid. Mike Johnson of Louisiana won the speakership later that month.

The episode showed that a conference nomination opens the way to a floor vote but does not secure the result.

A nomination does not bind individual members once the roll call begins. Each member answers by naming a candidate aloud, and any member can name someone other than the party's choice. A vote for any other person does not count toward the nominee's total.

The minority party also nominates a candidate of its own. Its members' votes count toward the total even when its nominee cannot win. When the majority is narrow, that leaves the majority party's nominee little room to lose votes from within the conference.

The same two-step sequence applies when the next Congress convenes in January 2027. Each party's internal choice comes first. The vote that decides the speaker follows on the floor on opening day.

The next House speaker will be chosen on the opening day of a new Congress in January 2027. Two milestones come before that vote: the November 2026 midterm elections and the close of the 119th Congress in early January 2027.

Election Day falls on November 3, 2026. Federal law sets congressional elections on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November of even-numbered years. All 435 House seats are on the ballot. House members serve two-year terms, so the entire chamber faces voters every cycle.

The votes cast that night are not the final word. Each state counts and certifies its own results on its own schedule. Close races can take weeks to resolve, and recounts can stretch the process further. The membership that will vote for speaker is not settled until those counts are complete.

The weeks between Election Day and the new year belong to the outgoing 119th Congress. Mike Johnson of Louisiana was re-elected speaker when that Congress convened in January 2025. He holds the post until its term formally ends in early January 2027. Members who lost or retired keep their votes until then. A session held in this stretch is known as a lame-duck session.

The Twentieth Amendment to the Constitution sets the timing of the handover. It ends House members' terms at noon on January 3. It also directs Congress to meet on that date unless it passes a law naming a different day.

Electing a speaker is the first order of business on opening day. The members-elect have not yet taken the oath of office when they vote. The clerk of the House presides over the chamber until a speaker is chosen. Members call out their choice by name as the clerk reads the roll.

The chamber can do little else until that vote is settled. Members cannot be sworn in, and the House cannot adopt its rules for the new Congress. If no candidate wins on the first ballot, the House keeps voting. In January 2023, it took 15 ballots over four days to elect Kevin McCarthy.

After a candidate wins, the longest-serving member, known as the dean of the House, administers the oath of office to the new speaker. The speaker swears in the rest of the chamber as a group. The House can then take up its rules package, which governs how it operates for the next two years.
