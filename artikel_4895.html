What Running the New York City Marathon Topless After Breast Cancer Was Actually Like

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Following her historic NYC Marathon run, Lauren Spiezia opens up to Women’s Health in an exclusive essay about the thing that surprised her the most.

On Sunday, I ran the 26.2 miles of the New York City Marathon completely topless to raise awareness for breast cancer and show people what survival really looks like. Despite all the training, the experience still surprised me.

My legs hurt. My quads cramped at mile 16. The wind chapped my chest. I thought about my form. I thought of my husband Elio, who was running beside me, and whether he was okay. I thought about how grateful I was to run with him and to have a family and support system waiting for me at the finish line and throughout the course. I thought about what a privilege it is to wake up and move my body. And I thought, repeatedly, about not stopping.

Before my run, I wrote that I wanted to do this so I could inspire other women to advocate for themselves and feel okay in their bodies. I ran with a QR code temporarily tattooed on my back that led to a free Tyrer-Cuzick breast cancer risk assessment in hopes that the attention could highlight one of the tools to help women understand their risk. When I finally crossed the finish line, my heart was so full. We had raised over $50,000 for cancer research, helping the very hospital where I had received my own treatment just one year prior.

What I was not thinking about was the fact that I was topless. That was the strangest part. Some people have wondered how I ran without a sports bra. The answer is that because my surgeries had severed the nerves in my chest, I didn’t feel anything. By the time I was at the starting line with the gun minutes away, that decision had already been made a year ago. All that was left was to run.

Mid-race, I passed a group of girls holding a sign that read “This is Women in Sports.” I locked eyes with them. They pointed at me, jumping up and down. Their sign was a nod to a recent sports betting ad that had people talking about women, sexuality, and the representation of female athletes. These girls were making their own statement. This—the marathon and its record 28,942 women running it—was women in sports. I almost backtracked against the current of runners to hug them, but the race carried me on.

There were, of course, plenty of jaws that dropped. That was expected. But I didn't anticipate how many people would cheer me on—from the women on the sidelines who put their hands on their chests in solidarity when I passed them, to fellow runners encouraging me as we ran side-by-side even though I could barely hear them over the crowd, to the volunteers who handed out water cups, telling me they had the same scars.

That was the biggest surprise. It wasn’t the hecklers or the attention, but the love from strangers on the best day in New York City. The response since has been overwhelming.

My inbox is full of women and men I’ve never met. Some are people in treatment. Some just finished treatment. Some whose mothers, daughters, sisters, and aunts were just diagnosed. Women I have never met who want to ask me whether they can see my reconstruction so they can make the decision for themselves. The answer is always yes.

I have also received the opposite. Comments calling me inappropriate. Comments about my size, my choice to reconstruct, my decision to use implants. Comments that I was doing this for attention or for the camera.

Most of the shaming has come after, not during. On the course, people either cheered or stared. But online, people typed.

I made a conscious decision to read them. Not because they do not sting. They do. But because I know that the women I most want to reach—the ones sitting in a chemo chair, the ones who just got a biopsy result, the ones bracing to look at their chests in their bathroom mirror for the first time—are also reading the comments on my page right now. If they see me stay quiet or disappear, they will learn that is what women with bodies like ours should do.

To the people shaming me, I would say this. When you shame a woman’s body online or in person, you do more than silence her. You keep us from talking clinically about our bodies. You keep us from making informed decisions. You keep us from going to the doctor. You keep us from advocating for ourselves. You keep us quiet.

Breast cancer does not care about your opinion on my implants. It does not care whether you think my reconstruction counts. Breast cancer comes for 1 in 8 U.S. women in their lifetimes, and can affect men too. If breast cancer does one day impact someone you love, you will have wanted the women who went through this before them to have spoken loudly.

In 2024, Louise Butcher ran the London Marathon topless with her own mastectomy scars uncovered. She paved the way for women like me. After I first had the idea of running the New York City Marathon topless, I reached out to her to ask about her experience. We spoke again after marathon day. She checked in on the race and on my mental health. She understood, in a way almost no one else can, what the commentary around this kind of run can do to a person. Her message to me was simple. There will always be noise. There will always be criticism. Remember your why.

Louise and I also both noted how outlets have either blurred or strategically covered my chest. A mastectomy removes the breast tissue, and in my case, it removed the nipples too. Nothing on my chest needs censoring or blurring. There is just my chest—a body with prosthetics. Any blurring suggests there is something explicit worth hiding, when there isn’t.

There is also a thread of criticism that suggests some reconstruction choices are more “legitimate” than others. Louise chose flat closure. I chose implants. Neither of us is right or wrong. These are deeply personal choices, and every woman deserves the space to make hers without being audited for authenticity.

I understand the marathon is a family event and that not everyone will agree with the choice I made. Cancer happens to families too. But I hope my run started some important conversations.

Every photo and video I have seen of myself from race day shows the same thing. A young woman who built herself back up, brick by brick. I’m smiling and full of joy. The light that cancer had dimmed in me is now back.

If my run reminded even one woman to go for her annual check, to investigate a suspicious lump, to advocate for herself when a doctor dismisses her, the run was worth it. The scrutiny does not change that.
