Judge Acquits Brothers in $25 Million Ethereum MEV Case After Finding No Proven Wire Fraud

A Manhattan federal judge has acquitted Anton and James Peraire-Bueno of criminal charges stemming from a 2023 Ethereum trading maneuver that prosecutors said extracted about $25 million. The decision is important, but the takeaway needs precision. It does not amount to judicial approval of every aggressive trading technique on public blockchains. Instead, the judge found that prosecutors failed to prove the material misrepresentations necessary for wire fraud and related convictions in this particular case.

The case concerned maximal extractable value, or MEV, the ability to affect the ordering or inclusion of blockchain transactions for financial gain. Prosecutors said the brothers engineered a bait-and-switch involving Ethereum validator infrastructure and trading bots in April 2023. Their alleged manipulation diverted value that the bots otherwise expected to capture. The defense contested the fraud theory. The unusual technical setting made the case a test of how existing criminal statutes apply to blockchain transactions that may be opportunistic or adversarial but do not necessarily resemble ordinary deceptive financial communications.

US District Judge Jessica Clarke focused on whether the conduct, as charged and proved, met the legal requirements for fraud. According to the ruling reported by Reuters and The Block, the relevant transactions did not involve material false representations of the kind prosecutors needed to establish. The judge distinguished that legal test from the question of whether the behavior was fair, ethical or objectionable. She also noted the absence of a regulated-market rule prohibiting the charged conduct in that setting. An acquittal under particular charges does not pronounce on every possible theory of liability.

The brothers were arrested in May 2024, and a jury failed to reach a verdict in November 2025. That sequence matters when interpreting Monday’s ruling: the result followed substantial litigation rather than a quick dismissal immediately after an indictment. The court’s reasoning offers a specific limit on a criminal fraud case, not a complete set of rules for MEV markets. Other facts, jurisdictions or statutory claims could produce different outcomes. Describing the ruling as the legalization of MEV attacks would overstate what the court decided.

MEV remains a practical concern for users who trade on-chain. Transaction ordering, block building and the design of trading bots can affect execution prices and who captures value. Better routing, slippage controls and protocol-level improvements may reduce some forms of extraction, but they do not eliminate competition over transaction priority. Legal certainty matters for developers and traders, while consumer protection remains a separate operational issue. The industry will be watching how prosecutors and policymakers respond to the limits the judge identified.

A ruling on criminal liability also leaves open the technical question of how MEV infrastructure should be designed. Relays, builders and validators can change their software or operational policies, but those changes have different trade-offs for permissionlessness, censorship resistance and execution fairness. Developers can study the facts of this case without treating the acquittal as a blanket endorsement of every form of transaction manipulation. The legal finding is narrower: the government did not prove the fraudulent representation required for the charged offences on the trial record.

The headline is an acquittal in a closely watched federal case, not a blanket safe harbour for exploiting counterparties. For Ethereum users, it reinforces the need to distinguish legal allegations, proven misrepresentations and the technical safeguards available during on-chain execution.
