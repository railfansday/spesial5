Has the Most Wonderful Moment of Your Life Already Happened?

In a recent newsletter, Jack Maden, the founder and writer of Philosophy Break, was asked about ideas that changed how he sees the world. He cited the Vietnamese Buddhist philosopher Thich Nhat Hanh, who posed the question that is in the title: Has the most wonderful moment of your life already happened?

For Hanh, this is not about looking back on our lives through a new lens that highlights what we may not have appreciated then. No, for Hanh, the most wonderful moment of our lives may already have happened, because for him, every moment has the capacity baked into it to be that wonderful moment, but it eludes us because we’re too preoccupied to notice it. What most of us do is put our hope in the future: the “when we’s—graduate, get married, have a child—or the “only if’s—make a million dollars, get that promotion—etc. By not being mindful in the present and instead dwelling mentally on the past or future, both the opportunity and the awareness of the wonderfulness of now slip right by.

The psychology of mindfulness is, of course, well known, thanks not only to Buddhism but also to writers like Eckhart Tolle and his book The Power of Now. While Tolle does a good job, in my mind, of helping us realize that the now is all we have, all we can live in, I agree with Maden that Hanh’s question offers a different, positive, and challenging spin: that wonderfulness is always available to us, ready to be found if only we learn to pay attention to it.

Easier said than done, of course. Here, meditation and breathing exercises come to the fore as ways to build that attention muscle. This is where, 20 years ago, Carlson and Bailey, in their book Slowing Down to the Speed of Life, advocated doing things at half speed to help us take in the moment better. Something relatively easy to do and a good starting point. But learning to notice and see the wonderfulness of the now through our senses and a clarified mind is different, I believe, from proactively, more robustly creating it. Are we capable of doing that?

Yes, we can. Modern philosophers such as Hume, Nietzsche, and Kierkegaard, as well as ancients like Epicurus, advocated not dismissing logic but setting it aside, putting passion and desire on the front burner, and using them as a divining rod to guide our lives. But we can also combine them with mindfulness and the power of now: If we make present decisions based on our preferences, excitement, and passions, and support them with reason rather than impulse, we have a winning combination. We keep our ear close to ourselves, identify what we need, and act to create it, which in turn translates not only into a wonderful moment but ultimately into a wonderful life.

So, are you ready to slow down, let the present seep more fully into your life, and pay attention? And maybe, taking a step further, actively practice making these moments the best they can be?
