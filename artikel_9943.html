European Commission confirms personal data stolen in AI-assisted cyberattack

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

EU Academy names and email addresses exposed, with users warned via website notice rather than contacted directly

The European Commission has confirmed that hackers made off with personal data in the AI-assisted cyberattack disclosed last week, but has relied on a website notice rather than contacting affected users directly.

Speaking at a press briefing on Monday, Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier confirmed that EU Academy, an online learning platform offering courses on EU policies and initiatives, was among the systems compromised during the cyber campaign targeting the bloc's executive arm.

The stolen data includes the names and email addresses of people outside the EU institutions, though the Commission has yet to figure out how many users are affected or whether other personal information was compromised. Public access to the platform has been disabled as a precaution.

Despite confirming the theft, the Commission hasn't contacted affected individuals directly. Instead, it has posted a notice on the EU Academy website, a decision Regnier defended when quizzed by reporters on Monday.

"There was no reach out to individual users, but rather a general message to all users on the website out of transparency," Regnier said.

The notice warns users that they could find themselves on the receiving end of spearphishing attacks, impersonation attempts, and spam as a result of the breach. The Commission is also advising users not to hand over personal information to anyone who contacts them claiming to be involved in EU Academy activities.

The trouble with notifying users via the EU Academy website is that they've already been told not to come back until January. A separate notice says the platform will be offline from November 11 through January 14, 2027, while the Commission rebuilds it. When it reopens, users will have to register again, and any record of their previous coursework won't be transferred.

The Register has asked the Commission whether the downtime and the requirement to create new accounts are connected to the cyberattack.A spokesperson didn't answer those points.

The Commission has informed the European Data Protection Supervisor (EDPS), the watchdog responsible for policing EU institutions' handling of personal data.

The EDPS told The Register that the Commission had notified it of the incident in stages, but declined to say whether it considered the Commission's approach to notifying affected users sufficient.

"I can confirm that the EDPS has been informed and notified in phases by the European Commission," a spokesperson said. "Our colleagues remain in regular contact with EC services on the cyberattack and its implication. This is an ongoing case, so I cannot tell you more at this stage on the actions the EDPS might take as supervisory authority."

The Commission first publicly acknowledged the cyberattack last Thursday, almost a month after suspicious activity was detected on October 22. Several internal applications were temporarily taken offline, and while most had since been restored, some remained unavailable at the time of Thursday's briefing.

The Commission has confirmed the attackers used an AI-assisted hacking tool, and the incident comes amid mounting concerns over ARTEX, an AI-powered penetration testing framework that security researchers have linked to large-scale cyber espionage campaigns.

More than a month after the Commission first spotted the intrusion, Regnier was unwilling to say on Monday whether its security teams had finally seen off the hackers.

"As long as the investigation is ongoing, I will not make any conclusive statements at this stage on where we are," he said. ®
