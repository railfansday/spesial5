InfoQ Homepage News How LinkedIn, Netflix & DoorDash Run Agents, Evals & Inference in Production: QCon AI New York 2026

Nov 11, 2026 4 min read

QCon AI New York 2026 has published its full schedule for December 15–16. Engineers from LinkedIn, Netflix, DoorDash, Figma, and other organizations will examine the architecture and operational decisions behind production AI systems, including agent authorization, evaluation pipelines, model serving, and AI-assisted software delivery. With the conference a few weeks away, there is still time to join other practitioners before the full ticket price takes effect on December 9.

The program addresses questions facing teams moving beyond initial AI deployments: where to enforce agent permissions, how to connect offline evaluations to production behavior, and how to support growing inference workloads. It also examines how coding agents affect code review, engineering practices, and organizational adoption.

The following presentations explore these challenges through systems the speakers build and operate.

Nancy Wang, CTO at 1Password, who founded and scaled AWS Data Protection and previously worked at Rubrik and Google, opens day one with When Software Becomes a User: Identity and Authorization for Agents in Production. She argues that identity systems assume a human behind every credential, with a stable role, a bounded session, and someone accountable afterward. Agents have none of these.

Tara Sainath, distinguished research scientist at Google DeepMind, lead of the Gemini Audio area and modeling lead for Project Astra, opens day two with Audio Processing with Large Language Models. She covers how LLMs have changed audio processing, and the research and product impact of audio LLMs at Google.

Pratik Agarwal, distributed systems engineer on Figma's sandbox and caching platforms, explains how Figma provisions and secures production agents. Figma found that the trust boundary for agents extends beyond the sandbox. Warm pools cut provisioning latency by 90%, and availability rose from 99% to 99.95%.

Ronak Nathani, principal staff software engineer in Compute Infrastructure at LinkedIn and host of the Software Misadventures podcast, presents LinkedIn's Kubernetes ops agent, which works across 500,000+ nodes and 5M+ pods. Its guardrails sit on the server, including rate limits, delete protection, and peer approval for production changes.

Fabio Flores, software engineer at DoorDash who leads foundation development for its agentic ordering system, explains the eval loop behind it. Every change is checked before launch by an LLM judge calibrated against human reviewers. The same rubrics score simulated and real sessions, so offline gains carry into production.

Emrick Donadei, software engineer on the Google DeepMind infrastructure team who works on evaluation and safety for agentic harnesses, covers agent evals the whole organization can write and keep running. The talk starts from a common problem: an eval that passes once, then fails on the next run.

Fan Yang, staff software engineer building Netflix's AI serving platform, and Rajat Shah, staff software engineer who leads the technical architecture for ML model serving, explain how Netflix serves ML and GenAI from one platform. It handles about 1 million inference requests per second across 300+ models, after five years of consolidation. They cover what multi-tenancy costs to operate.

Salina Wu, software engineer building Pinterest's generative AI serving platform, and Cristian Lopez, software engineer on GenAI Serving at Pinterest, present Pinterest's vision-language model serving stack on NVIDIA Dynamo. It is built for multimodal workloads with heavy prefill computation and large KV cache pressure.

Min Chen, principal staff engineer at LinkedIn with more than 25 years of software development experience, presents LinkedIn's AI code review system. It reviews tens of thousands of pull requests a week across about 10,000 repositories. More than 60% of sampled suggestions are accepted, and a validation pipeline filters findings before developers see them.

Joey Wang, engineering lead at Harvey who created Spectre, Harvey's internal collaborative cloud agent platform, explains how Harvey shipped its agents internally first on Spectre. Spectre passed 7,000 merged pull requests across every repository before the agents reached lawyers.

Liz Fong-Jones, technical fellow at Honeycomb and site reliability engineer with more than two decades of experience, and Rebecca Murphey, principal for AI Ops at Honeycomb and author of Build: Elements of an Effective Software Organization, reflect on Honeycomb's 2x initiative. Peak weekday PR merges to Honeycomb's main monorepo rose from about 30 to about 74 between early 2025 and April 2026, and have held steady since.

Brian Yang, member of technical staff at OpenAI who builds AI-native advertising and monetization systems, covers making AI adoption work across an engineering team. He looks at what happens after every engineer has coding agents, when one person finds a way of working that teammates struggle to reproduce.

Other sessions come from engineers at Anthropic, Microsoft, BlackRock, Databricks, Datadog, Tessl, LangChain, Baseten, and Google Cloud’s DORA team. Topics include agentic standards, sandbox density, SRE agents, AgentOps for financial services, inference with vLLM, voice agents, software factories, and enterprise AI adoption.
