‘One of the Best Recording Experiences You Could Have’: Mike Mills on R.E.M.’s ‘Reveal’ at 25

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

The deluxe edition includes a previously unreleased live show from the era.

Twenty-five years ago, Reveal was a great sigh of relief for R.E.M.

Just three years before, the group had released Up, its 11th studio album but first as a trio after the amicable departure of drummer Bill Berry in 1997. Recorded with other drummers and even drum machines, it was an experimental set released to a skeptical alt-rock world in which R.E.M. had become elder statesmen. The pressure came from outside and within, and while it was the band’s fifth consecutive top 3 album on the Billboard 200, Up‘s sales paled compared to its platinum-and-better predecessors.

Reveal did not necessarily revive R.E.M.’s commercial fortunes — it reached No. 6 on the Billboard 200 and was certified gold, with “Imitation of Life” hitting No. 1 on the Adult Alternative Airplay chart for three weeks — but it did return a sense of equilibrium that would sustain the trio of Peter Buck, Mike Mills and Michael Stipe for another 10 years and three more albums. “I look back on it as a happy record,” Mills tells Billboard via Zoom from his home in Athens, Georgia, “because we had just come through a really rough two years, three years, whatever and we had all recommitted to each other as friends and as band members. One thing I really liked about it was the same thing I liked about Murmur and Automatic For the People; there’s a cohesion to it. There’s a feeling that runs throughout the record.

“That’s not necessary for a good record; (1991’s) Out of Time was a little all over the place, but I feel it’s a great record. But (on Reveal) there is a nice feeling of continuity when you listen to this record from beginning to end. It takes its journey from the start to the finish.”

R.E.M. is celebrating Reveal‘s 25th anniversary with a deluxe reissue due out Friday (Nov. 20), pairing the original 12-song set with a May 7, 2001, appearance on Radio France’s The Black Sessions – recorded a week before Reveal‘s release and one of a handful of live performances the group made in support of the album. The new set has no demos, alternative versions, unreleased songs or other archival extras, but Mills says that’s because “there really wasn’t a lot of it — a few things, but none interesting enough and not enough of it, to make it a part of the package.”

But he’s confident The Black Sessions Revealed disc adds perspective to the story of R.E.M. at that time.

“Because we weren’t doing all that much live work at that point, it’s really interesting to hear the new songs live,” Mills notes. “The Up songs, that was the first time we played them, really, live. (Reveal) was a very studio record in that it hadn’t been played live before we recorded it. For the first 10 years of R.E.M. we would go out and play the songs for six months, a year and go knock ’em out because you know how they go. With (Reveal), it’s kinda like what happened to the Beatles; rather than setting up a 10-song record for a tour, you’re creating an LP of music to stand alone. I think that gives you a certain amount of freedom that you may not have without that.”

Recording at studios in Vancouver, Athens, Miami and Dublin with producer Pat McCarthy, R.E.M.’s sense of self was also fortified after the “trauma” that accompanied Berry leaving, the band’s first personnel change in its two-decade history to that point.

“It was getting comfortable with where we were,” Mills acknowledges. “We’d put all of the readjustment behind us. We decided we were happy to continue. We were happy to have Joey (Waronker) on drums and to have Pat McCarthy on the board. It all felt right. One of my great memories is the last third of it was in Ireland. We recorded in a house in Dalkey, so you weren’t in this sterile studio in a business district or something. We were out in the beautiful Irish countryside, in a house with a big picture window that looked out over these green fields. That was one of the best recording experiences you could have.

“The commitment we had moving forward felt really good. Lyrically, it’s not necessarily a happy record — there is some doubt going on in those (songs) — but the feeling I get from it is one of the joy of summertime. There’s a wistfulness because summertime always comes to an end and you have to go back to school and the leaves start falling and all of that — even though the last song is ‘Beachball,’ which implies more summer. But it’s just a funny little journey, and that’s why I like it.”

Mills considers Reveal to be “more of a Peter record, songwriting-wise,” but doesn’t minimize his own contributions on bass and keyboards. “There are two songs on there where I walked in the studio and I had never heard the songs and they were playing, so I just jumped in,” he remembers. “If you listen to the chorus in ‘The Lifting,’ I don’t hit the downbeat a lot ’cause I don’t know what the next chord is going to be; I have to wait for the chord to play and then I can start playing and it actually made it a better bass part. That was the joy and confidence that we had, that I could just walk in there, a song I never heard, and lay down a good part.”

R.E.M. did tour to support Up but played a more limited run during 2001, including a variety of radio and television appearances in North America, Europe, Japan and Australia.

“You never want to feel like it’s obligatory to take a tour,” Mills explains “You want to play these songs and show them to people, but if your heart’s not 100% in it, it’s a bad idea. It’s the most fun thing in the world, but it’s also incredibly taxing and difficult and I think we were all OK without (touring). We just decided we would be choosy and picky about where and when we played these songs and save our energy for the next big tour, which was inevitable.”

R.E.M. is, of course, the rare band that has stuck to its guns after disbanding in 2011. The group did reunite to perform “Losing My Religion” for its Songwriters Hall of Fame induction in 2024, while all four members took the stage with Michael Shannon and Jason Narducy’s R.E.M.-celebrating band during a 2025 performance in Athens. And Mills insists that “it’s not even a temptation” to do anything more substantive.

“We had agreed never to (reunite), that R.E.M. was done,” he says. “The Songwriters Hall of Fame was one of the few awards we were all very, very pleased to get, the one that meant the most. We played our one song there, and that made us realize, ‘Good, we’re done. We don’t need to do this again.'” And yet he’s cognizant that despite its absence, R.E.M. remains present in the world, via periodic reissues and a legacy that has not diminished in the years since the dissolution.

“We were very diligent about not being faddish,” Mills says. “We always wanted to make record that, when you listen to them…it could’ve been yesterday, it could’ve been 20 years ago or whenever. So in that sense it can still sound fresh and contemporary, even if it isn’t. That was something we strived for, and I think we succeeded. We were really good and had something really special, and we wrote some really, really great songs. We’re all very talented musicians and good at what we do, but the fact that we put together a great catalog of that size, I’m really pleased about that.”

The group members remain active on their own. Stipe is working toward his first solo album, expected next spring, with a single to drop on New Year’s Eve. Buck recently released a new album with the all-star collective Drink the Sea and plays with Mills in the Baseball Project. And Mills is part of the Big Star Quintet and continues to present iterations of his Concerto for Violin, Rock Band and String Orchestra.

Last week, meanwhile, Mills’ other band — Howl Owl Howl with Darius Rucker and former Black Crowes drummer Steve Gorman — released a new single, “Everybody Wants Her,” and announced tour dates for February. A full album from the trio is expected in the new year.

More archival releases from R.E.M. would seem to be in the offing, too, though Mills is circumspect about that. “Peter and I used to talk about maybe going into the vaults and getting all this unreleased stuff,” he says, “but that sounded like a daunting undertaking and a lot of it’s just been coming out with these (reissues). But we don’t feel the pressing need to release a lot of these things; a lot of it was not meant to be released, either. If we get bored in the next couple of years and want to dig stuff out, maybe, but I don’t see it coming.”
