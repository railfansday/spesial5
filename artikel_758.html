‘Never Go Backwards, Just Make Things Bigger and Better’: How Paul O’Neill Chartered Trans-Siberian Orchestra’s Journey Ahead

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

"Paul left us a huge body of unfinished work we need to get to," says Al Pitrelli, TSO's musical director.

When Trans-Siberian Orchestra turned 20 back in 2016, the troupe’s enduring success was as surprising to founder Paul O’Neill as anyone in or outside of the camp.

“I would love to say that we planned for it to resonate like this, but it was just pure luck,” acknowledged O’Neill, who passed away the following year at the age of 61. And that it was an endeavor almost entirely based around the Christmas holiday made it seem even less likely. “Normally you have five or six other platinum albums, then you take on Christmas,” O’Neill noted. “Of course, we took it on first and the rest of the year on later. It’s all worked out.”

That’s certainly an understatement for TSO, which celebrates the 30th anniversary of its triple-platinum debut album, Christmas Eve and Other Stories, this year with its annual, two-company concert tour opening Nov. 19 in Green Bay, Wisc., and Council Bluffs, Iowa. The 97 performances through Dec. 30 will again feature the soundtrack and narration from The Ghosts of Christmas Eve, TSO’s 1999 made-for-TV film, and other material amidst the usual fusillade of arena-filling special effects and pyrotechnics, with two shows a day at most stops.

During the past three decades TSO has released six studio albums — four platinum or better, three Christmas-themed  — and an EP, along with the multi-platinum singles “Christmas Eve/Sarajevo 12/24” and “Christmas Cannon.” The 2005 track “Wizards in Winter” also hit platinum, and a fan’s synchronized home display soundtracked by the tune was turned into an ad campaign for Miller Lite.

Since 1999, meanwhile, the tours have sold more than 20 million tickets, grossing more than $1 billion. This year, too, TSO will continue its practice of donating $1 from each ticket sold to local charities.

“One of the first things Paul taught us was, ‘Listen, just try to make great art, try to make the best of this as we can, and with a little bit of luck everything will fall into place,'” Al Pitrelli, TSO’s musical director and guitarist with the West company, tells Billboard via phone. “Boy, those words come true every time we hit the stage. His integrity and work ethic carried us through 21 years of touring and 25 years of recording, and with his family at the helm and everybody’s dedication to this, there’s really no end in sight.”

“Christmas Eve/Sarajevo 12/24” was the opening shot for TSO. Inspired by the true story of cellist Vedran Smallovic, who would play Christmas carols atop rubble in war-torn Sarajevo even amidst attacks during the Bosnian War, it was written by O’Neill, Jon Oliva and Robert Kinkel and first recorded by Savatage — a band O’Neill began producing and writing for in 1987 — for its 1995 concept album Dead Winter Dead. TSO drummer Jeff Plate, who had recently joined Savatage, remembers that “there was a lot of conversation about whether this song should even be on the record. Was it too weird having a Christmas song on this rock/metal record? But it was awesome. There was really something special to it.”

It was released as the album’s second single and climbed both the Hot Mainstream Rock Tracks and Hot Adult Contemporary Track charts, with enough radio to play to give O’Neill an idea.

“It became an instant holiday hit,” Plate says. “This was the vehicle for Paul to create the Trans-Siberian Orchestra.”

Pitrelli, who was part of Savatage along with Plate and Oliva at the time, joined the “Sarajevo” brain trust in creating songs for Christmas Eve and Other Stories, based on a narrative created by O’Neill.

“We’d get together at Paul’s apartment in New York City,” he says, “and literally sit around what we called the kitchen table with four Korg Mi1s, whatever keyboards we had available, a couple guitars, a couple practice amps, an ashtray and a bunch of cassette recorders. Paul would say, ‘Here’s an idea, who’s got a musical thought?’ and we’d throw ideas around and Paul would go, ‘Ooh, I like that. Let’s run with that’ and press record on the tape recorder,’ get it down and then, ‘OK, who’s got something else?’ and move on to the next song.

“It was three months of having fun, because what did it matter? It had never been done before, so we weren’t up against it. We were just being artists from four different backgrounds, collaborating, throwing out our respective ideas. And Paul saw the big picture and picked and chose what he thought was appropriate.”

The formally trained Pitrelli says the quartet did share a fondness for progressive rock and its blending of classical and rock motifs that they felt was well-suited to the Orchestra component of the Trans-Siberian sound.

“I grew up listening to a lot of Yes, Emerson, Lake & Palmer, King Crimson and (Pink) Floyd, Queen,” he says. “So the orchestral piece made sense in the context of soundtrack and story; it wasn’t far-fetched at all. We just toughened it up and made it a little more aggressive…and then brought in the holiday component and wove those themes and songs into what we were doing.

“We put it all together and took it into the studio. Two or three months later (October 1996) we had a record out, and then it sold three million copies that first year. So it was like, ‘Alright, we’re a band. This is cool, and it’s working. People like it!'”

Adam Lind, who was managing Savatage with Krebs Communications and now works with both acts for Night Castle Management Inc., adds that, “The part that surprised me was Paul’s accompanying story and poetry. I didn’t know it was coming ’til it was dropped on our desks…The music, for me, was so out of the ordinary and so powerful that it shocked me when I heard it. I wasn’t at all surprised that people loved it…(but) we didn’t know how important and influential it would be.”

Lind notes, also, that, “Atlantic (Records) as a whole didn’t understand it, but they were great supporters like Jason Flom, who tirelessly worked every angle and pulled every favor to get it in front of people, and all of a sudden it had momentum.”

TSO released one more album, the double-platinum Christmas Attic in 1998, before taking the next step to become a road act. “We were making records, selling millions of copies, but I almost felt like I was in the Steely Dan of Christmas,” Pitrelli remembers. And there was certainly skepticism about the idea of TSO as a live band — though those were allayed when the seven initial theater shows in 1999 all sold out.

“I think Paul’s presentation and vision of the live show was the secret sauce,” Lind says. “Knowing Paul, it was in his brain the whole time. One of his many talents with being a producer with an ability to see greatness in other people. Over two years he put his friends from his years in the industry together, just as he did for the first record.”

Pitrelli remembers sitting with O’Neill in the dressing room of Philadelphia’s Tower Theatre on opening night, still not certain about what lay ahead. “It was like, ‘I don’t understand; who bought all these tickets, dude?’ Who is this audience,” the guitarist says. “When the houselights went down and the curtain dropped, I looked out at the audience and right in front of me was this older couple, grandparents wearing crocheted seaters, and right next to them was a dude wearing a Slayer hoodie. Welcome to the demographic of TSO; that was the weirdest thing that had ever happened to me, musically.”

Plate recalls being “so nervous” at the same show, and looking at the crowd before to see “metalhead kids, long hair and black T-shirts, and then there were families and children. And in the front row was this gentleman with a tuxedo on his wife had a beautiful red dress. I thought, ‘Omigod, these people are here to see an orchestra!’ But it was just magic; the band, the story, everything just clicked.

“And at the end of the show the band took a bow and the man in the tuxedo gave me a high five. It set the tone for all these years later.”

TSO quickly grew into the two-company, arena-filling endeavor it is now, hauling 325 tons of equipment around in 38 trucks, with a crew of 250. And rest assured it’s definitely a concern where size matters, and bigger is better.

“Every year we always say the same thing — ‘How the heck are we going to beat this?'” O’Neill, a self-confessed “pyro whore,” said during 2016. “It’s a good problem to be having, and it forces us to come up with something new, something different. We know all the special effects companies. They all know that if they invent great special effects that’s insanely expensive there is one band that is dumb enough to buy it, and that’s us. It’s just worth it.”

TSO tour director Elliot Saltzman says that “What would Paul do?” has remained a credo since O’Neill’s passing. “His theory was to never go backwards, just make things bigger and better,” Saltzman explains. Planning for each year’s tour begins in earnest during March, starting with drawings from longtime production designer Bryan Hartley, and continues with several meetings in Las Vegas to consider lighting, effects and other aspects of the show — an exacting, detailed dialogue that Saltzman says runs even through rehearsals that begin during October in Omaha, Neb.

“The idea is to really capture the audience and say, ‘Yes, maybe it’s a story you’ve heard before, but the way it’s portrayed this year is different, and that makes it fresh and new,” says Saltzman, who also oversees the logistics of transporting band crew and stage from an evening performance and having it ready for a matinee in another city the following day. “We have basically three or four different stories we use, so we’ve got to increase the excitement every year and give them a reason to come and hear them again.

“I talk to people and read comments — ‘This is my 28th show,’ ‘This is my 90th show…and this was the best one yet!’ So we seem to be doing it right.”

O’Neill considered the live edition of The Ghosts of Christmas Eve, which first aired on the Fox Family Channel, to be “a big experiment” when TSO presented it on stage in 2015. “We weren’t sure it was going to work,” he acknowledged the following year. “But it worked so well we decided to do it again this year.” Its combination of relative brevity and a “greatest hits” nature of songs from the first two TSO albums has turned it into a something of a staple; this year’s tour marks Ghosts’ eight performance, and fourth in the past five years.

“People love this show,” drummer Plate says. “It’s always worked whenever we’ve done it. It moves along swiftly. The TV special is on all sorts of channels during the holiday season and has become kind of a tradition, too, so it all works hand in hand.”

Pitrelli adds that, “The story is so universal and has such lovely, happy ending. I just think people love it, myself included. And we listen to the comments; I know our management team is constantly scouring the Internet for feedback, and people say the same thing — ‘We love the story, and they made it even better than the previous year.’ So it’s back by popular demand, and I’m OK with that.”

More surprises, meanwhile, come during the rest of the show, drawing from the other TSO albums, both Christmas and secular, and in recent years even including songs from Savatage, which itself began touring again last year with Pitrelli, Plate and other members who are also part of TSO.

“It’s not the same band, but they’re certainly related (because) the same people wrote (TSO)’ songs,” Pitrelli says. And Plate explains that, “If you listen to that band you can hear and see hints of what Paul O’Neill was thinking, and how it turned into (TSO). So doing some (Savatage) songs just fits.”

That TSO will go on for the foreseeable future is a given. But there are also hopes that the group’s catalog will expand at some point.

The last TSO album Letters From the Labyrinth, came out during 2015, and TSO did perform a new song, “Christmas Carousels” — an expansion of a track from 2004’s The Lost Christmas Eve — during 2022. Meanwhile, O’Neill left behind several unrecorded projects that he spoke freely about — among them: Romanov: When Kings Must Whisper, about the 1917 Russian Revolution; The Path of the Fairytale Moon; and an expanded, rewritten version of Savatage’s 1990 concept album Gutter Ballet.

“Paul left us a huge body of unfinished work we need to get to,” Pitrelli says, “but we got blind-sided when we lost him and we’re still kind of licking our wounds a little bit. It’s just extremely daunting. There’s always talk about that, but (touring) has become such a full-time job throughout the year that we’re just trying to move forward on this and make sure it’s great.”

Plate says he’d like to see one of those works finished and would also like to see TSO try another spring tour, which it did during 2010-2012 in North America and Europe. “It’s difficult in this day and age to go out on tour, no matter who you are,” he acknowledges, “and Trans-Siberian Orchestra is not a small group. If we don’t have the luxury of the holiday it changes things, so it may not be feasible. But it would be nice if we could.”
