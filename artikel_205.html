What's going on with the Riemann hypothesis?

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

A new AI-assisted proof seems to be solid, but we’re not sure what it tells us.

Last week, news broke that a mathematician had used AI to help crack one of the biggest outstanding problems in math: a proof of the Riemann hypothesis. The news follows several other major breakthroughs in math that have either been enabled by or managed entirely by large language model-based systems, a development that has left the entire field of mathematics in a state of upset.

It’s too early to say much about the proof itself—mathematicians are still in the process of digesting it. But we can say a little about how the proof came about, and what it tells us about the state of math.

The Riemann hypothesis dates from the mid-1800s and was formulated by (you will be surprised to learn) Bernhard Riemann. There’s not an obvious way to describe the details of the actual hypothesis simply, but it has implications for the distribution of prime numbers. This gives it significance for areas ranging from number theory to cryptography. As Wikipedia accurately states, “It is considered by pure mathematicians to be one of the most important problems in the field.”

The proof of the hypothesis was announced by a mathematician named Levent Alpöge, who has been working at Anthropic, and, not surprisingly, used Claude to generate it. His announcement is written in a somewhat florid style (example: “one of which was already new and shocking, making very simple one of the shadows of Riemann over which we mathematicians felt most triumph”), and he has apparently dropped some hints that some of it was meant in jest.

But it does make clear that this proof was a longstanding interest of Alpöge’s, and he had some insights into how to connect different areas of math to reach the final proof. The process seems to have involved getting the AI started with handling a simplified “toy” form of some of Alpöge’s insights and then directing it to expand what it found there to the full scope of the Riemann hypothesis.

The proof itself comes in two different forms, and the difference between them gets at the heart of why AI usage in math has been controversial. One is a rigorous description of the proof using a programming language named Lean that has allowed the rapid verification that the proof is valid. The second is a description of the logic behind it, which may ultimately be the key for mathematics as a field to benefit from this proof.

It’s important to emphasize that, so far at least, AI has been solving problems that humans previously recognized as significant. That significance is typically ascribed to mathematical ideas because of their implications or utility—they either tell us something about areas of math that people care about, or they keep popping up in problems that mathematicians are working on. So far, there are no indications that AI can recognize or formulate interesting and important unresolved problems on its own.

But AI has tackled many problems humans have considered significant with impressive ease. Trained on a body of material that included a lot of advanced math, the software has provided both formal proofs as well as counterexamples that have clearly demonstrated that some proposals are incorrect.

When it comes to proofs, AI might have some advantages over people. For starters, it’s not specialized in specific fields of math and can potentially pull together tools from a variety of disciplines. AI also pays no cost (either in terms of career or emotional soundness) from going down the wrong track, giving up, and starting over.

AI models have also had one of their biggest limitations removed via humanity’s development of Lean, a computer language that allows mathematical proofs to be expressed in a way that can be readily verified. Due to their tendency to hallucinate, a description of a proof generated by an AI would still require extensive human validation before it could be accepted, a task that might radically shift the effort/reward ratio of using AI models. But by instructing an AI to include the necessary Lean code as part of its output, verification has become straightforward.

The unfortunate aspect of this for mathematics as a field is that it’s often the process of working through the reasoning behind a proof, rather than the step-by-step logic, that advances the field as a whole.

Mathematicians have expressed their fears about this aspect of AI usage in a formal open letter. We’ll try to connect those fears to the recent announcement.

Before AI, proofs were typically worked through by people who recognized potential pathways connecting disparate fields of math and spent years finding ways to formalize those intuitions and validate that the math worked. It wasn’t necessarily an efficient use of time if the only output was the proof itself.

But that was rarely the case. By finding the connections between problems, this work helped many other people see new ways of tackling whatever issues they were wrestling with. Mathematicians often developed new tools, found shortcuts, or stumbled across related problems while working through their proofs, enriching math as a whole. In other words, the proof itself was often less important for math than the process of generating it.

By contrast, the internal workings of large neural networks are notoriously opaque. Asking an AI to explain its reasoning runs into the same problems that asking it anything else does: Since the software has only been selected for producing decent-sounding text, it’s likely to make something up that sounds good when asked about its own internal processes. So, an AI-generated proof has little to offer the field in terms of new ideas or unexpected advances, at least on its own.

Indeed, nobody can say much about the Riemann proof at the moment, simply because mathematicians have barely started the process of shifting through the logic as expressed in Lean. It’s possible it can eventually produce new insights and tools, but it may take years before humans can figure out what they are and how to express them in a way that other mathematicians can appreciate.

How mathematicians are expected to identify new challenges that can push their fields beyond the current body while seeing AI relieve them of the need to actually do math is anybody’s guess.

This is all new and progressing incredibly quickly; there has been no time for the field to find a new normal. It’s possible that, once Alpöge gets around to describing the approach he pushed Claude to use, mathematicians will start getting the same sorts of insights that they normally expect to receive from proofs. It could be that Alpöge’s approach, mixing human intuition with machine-generated detail and rigor, provides a model for what math will look like in a few years.

But it’s impossible to tell at this early stage in development.
