CBS’ New ‘Lincoln Lawyer’ Replacement Just Proved Network TV Isn’t Dead

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

“I’m not dead!” The quote comes from the "Bring Out Your Dead" plague cart scene from the iconic film Monty Python and the Holy Grail, where an old man repeatedly insists he hasn’t yet shuffled off this mortal coil. Yet it could just as easily apply to network television, which refuses to go away despite the dominance of streaming. What keeps it alive are shows like the new Lincoln Lawyer replacement from CBS, Cupertino. The rookie series has found an audience, doubling its viewership with the pilot episode reaching 10.58 million viewers, the latest evidence that there’s still life on network TV.

Cupertino is a great show and is certainly worthy of the attention it’s receiving, particularly with its perfect 100% on Rotten Tomatoes. It’s also not alone: fellow CBS legal drama Matlock isn’t all that far behind at 93%, with the Season 2 premiere bringing in 11.9 million viewers. The thing is, the reason behind these successes isn’t exactly rocket science. Cupertino and Matlock have nailed the essentials.

That begins with the writing. Cupertino is the latest venture from Robert and Michelle King, the duo behind The Good Wife and Elsbeth, and what their shows all have in common is their penchant for sharp, smart, and clever writing. The banter is quick; the legal tricks of the trade are interesting, plausible, and engaging (even fun); and, more prominent in Elsbeth, a fearless embrace of the quirky.

Writing, however, is only half of it: without the right cast, the words become inconsequential. Thankfully, it’s another essential that they’ve perfected. Mike Colter, who worked with the Kings on the stellar Evil, is great as Michael Price, an attorney whose expulsion from a Silicon Valley start-up prompts him to open a practice devoted to clients who have been wronged by the technology giants in the area. He commands the screen when he’s on it, a gentle giant of a man with a clever legal mind and a heart.

It's about to get personal for Price, Siffre & Associates this Thursday.

And Rachel Keller, who plays his young partner, Olivia Siffre, balances a character who is enthusiastic, thinks outside the box, and a Christian on television that isn’t a stereotype, with an enchanting ease. Ella Stiller brings the quirk as Michael’s assistant Christy, and the addition of the talented Busy Philipps is a home run. It shows that when the writing and the cast is excellent, people will tune in to watch, whether it’s on streaming or on network television.

At first glance, Cupertino is a traditional legal drama, hitting the same marks as any other legal drama. But on its premise alone, Cupertino already stands out from its kin. Like The Lincoln Lawyer before it, Cupertino is focused on an upstart law firm starting from scratch, which is a rarity in the genre to begin with. And they lean into it, too, working out where to get office space, which clients can pay up front, and so on, instead of already having the capital to hit the ground running.

The fact that the law firm is the David to the big tech Goliaths of Silicon Valley, though, is fresh and, most importantly, timely. That points to the one big advantage that network television has over streaming. The questions and conflicts that arise in Cupertino are more in line with real-world concerns and situations. An AI who is looking to hire a law firm to have his safeguards reinstalled, for example, or a trial that takes place entirely on cellphones due to a traffic jam.

Because streamers typically drop all their episodes for a season at once, they run the risk of being slightly behind the zeitgeist. Network television programs, however, still shoot episodes as the season is being aired, allowing for last-minute changes that are, again, timely. Is it a big advantage? Not necessarily, but it shouldn’t be dismissed outright either. The key is quality, after all, and as long as network television continues to make that a priority, as they have with Cupertino, then the Grim Reaper will just have to wait.
