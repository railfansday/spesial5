I Tested Strava's New Integration With Gemini, and I Have Thoughts

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Once connected, you can ask questions like:

"Am I getting faster at the same heart rate?"

"Can you give me a detailed analysis of how I performed in my last race?"

"How is my strength training impacting my running?"

Some important context here: Strava's AI "insights" have sort of sucked, and annoyed me, in the past. Athlete Intelligence (conveniently abbreviated as AI) is the Strava feature that writes text responses to your workouts. Strava calls these "insights," but as far as I can tell they mostly regurgitate the information from the workout description, sometimes with added errors. I've found AI fitness summaries largely useless, and Strava's version checks every box: It doesn't use enough context to understand what's going on, it presents generic advice as if it were personal, and it's overconfident in incomplete data. So, skeptical as I am, I gave Strava's latest Google Gemini integration a whirl. Here's what it does, how to set it up, and whether it's worth it.

The first question I asked was one of Strava’s examples, "Am I getting faster at the same heart rate?" Gemini said yes, I was seeing solid efficiency gains. Like many AI tools, it told me what I wanted to hear. I had to point out that heat and temperature are huge variables. When I steered the chat in that direction, Gemini, of course, agreed immediately ("you hit the nail on the head") and explained that my mid-September long runs happened in 75–84°F weather with higher humidity, which inflates heart rate, while October's cooler temperatures made the same effort look better. That's an important caveat the first answer left out entirely.

I then asked it to compare my past marathons, and in many ways, I had fun here. For instance, the suggested follow-ups prompted me to think of questions I hadn't put on my list, like comparing my weekly running volume and long-run frequency leading into different races in different years.

Unfortunately, the results weren't reliable at first. When I asked for a detailed analysis of every marathon-distance effort since 2019, Gemini said I had exactly one, the Marine Corps Marathon on October 25, 2026. In reality, I've logged at least eight. When I asked why it couldn't see them, it explained that its access is limited to my recent history, roughly summer 2026 onward, because of how pagination limits the number of past activities returned at once. It also offered a workaround: Give it specific dates and locations.

That workaround works, but it means you have to already know what you're looking for. And still, it wasn't consistent. In other answers, Gemini referenced my 2025 NYC Marathon and 2024 Philadelphia race, so the data's reach seems uneven. You need to be able to query older date ranges as directly and specifically as possible, and even still, double-check any numbers that matter to you.

I then asked "What am I doing right and what am I doing wrong as a runner?" After knowing the queries need to be so specific, I intentionally wanted to test a vague, simplistic question. The answer was, true to form, overly flattering—though I didn't disagree with the guidance itself. I need more speed workouts! I know this! I didn't need a chatbot to tell me, though maybe some other Strava user does. Gemini's suggestions (polarize training, add one dedicated threshold day) are sound, yes, but they’re also generic. Most recreational runners could glance at my Strava and spot the same strengths and weaknesses. Similarly, Gemini then set itself up for a slam dunk by prompting me to ask whether my easy runs were too fast. The answer: My easy days were happening at nearly my marathon pace (about 9:43/mi), and I should slow down by 45–90 seconds per mile, to roughly 10:30–11:15/mi. I completely agree. That being said, my big takeaway here remains the same: Don't confuse AI-generated advice as some newfound, personalized insight. It’s often the same conventional wisdom you already know, or could figure out with a tiny bit of research from a real, human source.

If you ask me—a human!—this is a classic case of using AI for rote tasks, but then saving critical thinking for your own human brain. Gemini didn't surface anything wildly insightful, and it skipped important considerations like the effect of heat on heart rate, until I raised them myself. But it pulled data faster than I could have by hand, and its follow-up prompts nudged me toward comparisons I might not have thought of.

For many users, the smartest approach may be to skip AI summaries and focus on raw data. After all, your training load, sleep duration, and heart rate trends are already perfectly actionable.

As with all things AI, it’s important to mind the gap between data and wisdom. So much wisdom gets lost—forfeited, even—when you blindly trust an AI coach. Make sure you aren’t offloading all the other skills you need as a runner and as a human, like your ability to listen to your body and maybe run a little slower in the heat, even when it makes your data look “worse.”

If you do use Google Gemini to analyze your Strava data:

Be specific about dates and locations when asking about older activities.

Push back. Ask about weather, terrain, and fatigue before accepting a flattering conclusion.

Treat the output as a fast data pull, not a personal coach.
