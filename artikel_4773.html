A Cheat Sheet for Coping With Postpartum Anxiety

Posted November 12, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

Postpartum anxiety (PPA) is not postpartum depression or psychosis, as the Lindsay Clancy trial brought to light. Rather, postpartum anxiety is one of the most common and misunderstood conditions following childbirth, yet we hardly talk about it.

New mothers experience PPA to varying degrees. You could have constant worry, experience intrusive and frightening thoughts about the baby's safety, or exhibit excessive behaviors like checking the baby monitor every other second.

PPA is an element of what’s called “matrescence"—the holistic, developmental process of becoming a mother which includes the hormonal, neurological, and emotional changes that you go through after your baby arrives. In some cases, this veritable metamorphosis can precipitate a sharp increase in anxiety that is underrecognized and has not been well explored in medicine.

Too many mothers have anxiety and feel misunderstood and, beyond that, feel isolated and alone. Katie Grant, a mother of one, had severe postpartum anxiety and wanted to make sure others didn’t have to suffer as she did.

She lived it and wanted to support new moms by relaying pointers and comforting reassurance in quick doses because, as she says, “you’ve only got 2 minutes and a baby on your hip!”

The beauty of The Postpartum Anxiety Cheat Sheet is that the pocket-sized book fits in your diaper bag or, yes, your pants pocket. I reached out to Grant to hear more about what she wants new moms to know.

Susan Newman: We are bombarded with images of the idyllic new mom, but reality can be so different. How can a new mom experiencing postpartum anxiety, or PPA, be realistic and not get swept up in the fantasy of new mom perfection?

Katie Grant: The first thing a new mom can do is acknowledge that she is experiencing PPA. Admit to yourself and your loved ones that you are having a hard time and need help, patience, and understanding. Then, stop comparing yourself to those photo-shopped, perfectly lit photos on your feeds. No one has it that easy, and everyone is struggling. They’re lying if they say they’re not.

I also recommend one of the “MOMtras” from my book. A MOMtra is a mantra to repeat to yourself or out loud to help shift your mindset and ease your worry in this very moment. One of my favorites is, “My thoughts are not me. I am not my thoughts. My brain is playing tricks on me, but I can win this game.”

SN: Who are the best people to talk to for the new mom experiencing anxiety?

KG: You need to talk to yourself and let it be OK that you are feeling anxious and off. Then, I would say your partner or best friend—there are cheat sheets for both of those relationships—since they are the ones spending the most time with you and can notice changes right away. You will find exactly what to text your best friend, for instance, so you don’t have to think about it too hard.

Professional help is always a solid option. Talk to a therapist, OB-GYN, or pediatrician, or call or text the PSI (Postpartum Support International) HelpLine at 1-800-944-4773. They can help you in the moment and direct you to the best resources for your situation.

SN: You explain what intrusive thoughts are in your book and how they are extremely common, yet most moms don’t admit having them for fear of being taken literally. What can we do to normalize that aspect of postpartum anxiety?

KG: Yes, intrusive thoughts are one of the most common symptoms of PPA, but because they appear only in our mind, we are afraid people might think we want to act on them. Intrusive thoughts usually involve picturing harm coming to your baby. For me, it was picturing knives coming at my baby’s bald head. And, turns out, that exact image is the most common. That helped me understand that I did indeed have PPA when I saw that on a list of screening questions.

What we can do to normalize this aspect is to make it widely known that PPA happens to most, or at least many, mothers. Those thoughts do not mean you want to or will act on them. They are your brain showing up in protective overdrive so you can and will shield your baby from harm. It is your mother instinct stuck in the "on" position. Admitting those thoughts to your doctor or a trusted loved one can help put that switch back into the "off" position.

SN: You have a dozen cheat sheets, short lists with directions, to help moms better understand their anxiety. In “Cheat Sheet #10,” you ask (and answer) how you know if the situation is really an emergency; “Cheat Sheet #11” addresses how to explain what seems like your odd behavior to your baby’s older sibling; and “Cheat Sheet #8” goes into how to ask for help when you need it, to name a few. Which ones do you feel are the most important for anxious mothers?

KG: I would say the most asked for would be “Cheat Sheet #1 For Your Partner.” It is imperative that your partner recognizes what you’re going through, how they can help you, and how to find professional help for you. Your partner doesn’t get to decide when you should be better. Partners need to be educated about this condition and be there for you, not against you.

“Cheat Sheet #5 For Your Pediatrician” flips the narrative about baby wellness checkups expressly to focus on your anxiety. Pediatricians are the doctors who see you most often after you have a baby, and they should be trained in what to look for, but most of them are not. They are not supposed to focus solely on the baby, even if that’s what their title says. If you know what to mention and the right questions to ask, you’ll be able to help your pediatrician help you.

Whether you call it a cheat sheet, checklist, or guide, The Postpartum Anxiety Cheat Sheet can get you through the times you doubt yourself and lead you to the support you need. It can also help you figure out if what you are feeling is “OK” as you pace the floor with a baby you can’t seem to put down.

When you feel overwhelmed by anxiety, heeding the factoids in this useful little book and urging loved ones to do the same can make a huge difference in the often rocky, anxiety-driven transition to motherhood that is PPA, postpartum anxiety.
