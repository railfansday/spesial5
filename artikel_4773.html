What If Nothing Is Wrong With You?

In my nearly two decades as a therapist, I’ve sat with hundreds of people of all ages and backgrounds, and one thing has become clear: By adulthood, feeling inherently lovable and worthy seems to be the exception rather than the rule.

The quiet belief that “something is fundamentally wrong with me” is a burden so many of us carry as we move through life, often without even realizing it. Psychologist and Buddhist meditation teacher Tara Brach describes this as living under “the trance of unworthiness.” It may sound like:

But we don’t start out this way. “Babies don't wonder whether they deserve to be seen, connected with, or held,” says Jackie Ourman, LMHC, couples and individual therapist at Values Aligned Therapy and founder of Social Connection Collective. “Our earliest relationships teach us about our value through the ways the people who are supposed to care for us respond to our needs.”

What we learned, we can begin to unlearn.

Consider these practices to help you awaken from the trance of unworthiness.

1. Recognize you're in the trance of unworthiness.

When we are caught in the trance, deeply rooted negative beliefs can feel like indisputable facts. Ourman encourages her clients to notice that what they are experiencing is a thought, not necessarily a fact.

“There’s a difference,” Ourman says, “between saying, ‘I’m not enough’ and saying, ‘I’m having the thought that I’m not enough.’ This creates a little distance from our thoughts so we don’t automatically believe everything our minds tell us.”

Simply noticing that we are in the midst of a self-critical inner monologue is a powerful act in itself. That is mindfulness: observing thoughts without judgment and without being swept away by them.

In her book, Radical Acceptance, Brach describes a practice she calls the “sacred pause,” drawing on Buddhist teachings. The next time you notice yourself spiraling, stop and take a breath. Notice what’s happening in your body and which thoughts have arrived. Resist the urge to fix, explain, or dismiss what you feel. Then ask yourself:

“What does my heart truly long for in this moment?”

Not “what would make me more impressive or more acceptable to others.” Simply, “What does your heart truly long for right now?” Ask again, and wait.

The pause gives us an opportunity to listen more deeply and reconnect with our soul voice, the one within us that knows what we truly need.

3. Send compassion to the little you who took on the burden of “not good enough.”

For many of us, it's easier to offer compassion to our child selves than to our present selves. If this resonates, try this exercise.

Find the most endearing photo you can of yourself as a child. One that evokes warmth or tenderness. Spend some time looking into the little one’s eyes. See their desire to belong, or their simple wish to be loved. Then ask yourself:

What did that little one need to hear? What did they need to know about themselves? Perhaps they needed to know that their needs weren’t too much, or that they weren’t responsible for the things that happened to them.

4. See yourself through the eyes of those who love you.

When we’re caught in the trance of unworthiness, our view of ourselves can become remarkably narrow. We fixate on our perceived flaws and overlook the qualities others see so clearly.

Think of a person who genuinely loves you; someone who sees you with affection. What might they say about you? What qualities would they notice that you routinely overlook? What would they remind you of when you are convinced that you have nothing worthwhile to offer?

This is not about depending on others’ approval to feel okay. It is about recognizing that our self-perception isn’t always reliable and never fully captures who we are.

5. Understand where the beliefs came from.

When we begin to understand how our negative beliefs originated, we can extend compassion to the younger version of ourselves who was simply trying to make sense of their experiences.

“I think it's important to acknowledge and validate those early experiences while also recognizing that the conclusions someone drew about themselves may not be true,” says Ourman. “For many people, the work is less about building worth from scratch and more about finding their way back to it.”

You don’t have to believe you are worthy overnight. These old beliefs may have been with you for a long time, but with patience, they can begin to loosen. Start with one small practice, whichever one speaks to you.

May you be kind to yourself and find your way back to the worth that was always there.
