Does Banning Phones in School Boost Test Scores?

You might know a child who can read every word in a paragraph but still not know what it means. Maybe she misunderstood why a character left their home, or why two people were talking in a certain conversation. She might keep reading until she stops understanding and realizes that she does. She might go back to find what she’s done wrong, or she might keep going. At the end of the page, she might have no idea what she’d actually read.

In my work as a speech pathologist, I’ve studied that type of breakdown for many years. When readers notice their mistakes, they might return to the earlier pages or rethink what they initially thought. But they have to hold the earlier information in mind. They have to have gaps where they can start to think about it and process. Those gaps take sustained attention and time. I’ve written about what happens in those gaps as productive uncertainty, the quality of sitting with and wrestling with questions.

I was thinking about those gaps in the context of two large studies on school phone bans that were released this year. The studies examined whether students score higher on tests when they have no phones available. One study suggested that they do, but only by a small margin. The other suggested that the scores hardly improve.

While this data are important, I’m more interested in how the process of learning shifts when students are less interrupted by phones. A phone within reach asks a child to keep deciding not to look at it, and each of these decisions draws on the attention she needs for the page.

Locking Phones Away Removes Repeated Distractions

A recent study by Thomas Kane and colleagues at the Harvard Center for Education Policy Research was revealing. They found schools that locked phones away during the day saw small gains in test scores compared with similar schools that did not.

In the Harvard Gazette, Kane pointed out that teachers differ in how much they enforced no-phone rules, and that with a strict ban, “you’re not relying on teachers to enforce it.” That may suggest an environmental reason. Imagine a student who has a phone in their pocket, and the teacher sometimes forgets or doesn’t want to argue. The child might check a message or think about checking a message and decide not to. But then their phone might ring or beep, and the same decision comes up again. Each time, the child’s attention leaves the paragraph they were trying to understand.

But when the environmental conditions are changed, that is when the phones are completely locked away, the rule no longer depends on each teacher's vigilance. This also removes the need to make the phones a battleground, as in many classrooms it will inevitably become that.

I recently came across a comment by David Figlio, an economist at the University of Rochester. As he told NPR, at 55, he still has to resist looking at a text when his phone buzzes in his pocket. “Now imagine you’re a 14-year-old.” This comment strikes me as well-put because it shows how much we’re asking of children when we expect them to somehow stand up to addictive technologies. We treat it as a question of will and morality, even though companies designed these phones to be as addictive as possible.

Changing your environment matters because learning takes sustained attention. As in the initial example, when a child loses their place on the page or misinterprets a sentence, sustained attention helps them backtrack and self-correct. She might have a chance to reflect on what went wrong and understand what she needs to do next.

Even without AI, if students have access to a phone, the simple distraction of looking at it or thinking to look at it can interrupt those moments of reflection. They might look up an answer to a comprehension question, without realizing the need to go back and reread the text. This is a very minor slip in the moment, but if you repeat these day in and day out, the changes in what’s being learned and what’s only being written down add up.

The same goes for adults. Productive uncertainty is easy to ignore or push aside, especially if we have ready answers. In addition, it can feel as if we’re being more efficient when we do so. That sense of efficiency can compound and make us more interested in getting the answers faster. Especially since we have no school bans for ourselves, we might find ourselves simply glancing at our phones in moments of difficult thought. Say we’re stuck on how to phrase a hard email, or unsure what we think about a decision. The pull to check the phone tends to arrive right at that moment of difficulty, which is also the moment when the thinking would have happened. We get an answer, but we have lost the chance to think for ourselves.

That’s why the environmental conditions matter far more than we think. Willpower is one thing, but the cognitive drain of needing that willpower all the time can make it harder to have deeper or more sustained thoughts.

The next time someone asks you to focus harder, or asks a child to, ask them what conditions are making it more difficult. Make one change, and then reassess. You might find that it has far less to do with willpower than you thought.
