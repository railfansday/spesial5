What AI changes in cyberattacks, and the old weaknesses and defenses that remain

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

AI cyberattacks vs traditional hacking, compared across 10 points, from AI-written phishing and voice cloning to prompt injection, plus the old weaknesses and defenses that still matter

Generative AI has not given attackers a new way into most organizations. It has made the old ways cheaper, faster and harder to spot. A phishing email that once betrayed itself through clumsy grammar can now arrive fluent and personalized, in the recipient's own language. A phone call from a familiar voice may come from software rather than a person. A video call full of recognizable colleagues may not include a single real one.

Comparing AI cyberattacks vs. traditional hacking shows a shift in scale and cost more than a clean break. Traditional hacking rewarded scarce skills. A con artist needed a convincing voice and a good script. A phishing crew needed fluent writers. An intruder needed someone who could read code and find a flaw. Language models and AI agents now handle parts of that work. That lets a small group attempt operations that once required a larger, more specialized team.

AI has also created attack surfaces that did not exist before. Chatbots, coding assistants and AI agents read text from emails, web pages and documents. That text can carry hidden instructions the system then follows. Attacks aimed at AI systems themselves have no direct equivalent in older forms of hacking.

Less has changed in how most intrusions finish. An attacker still typically needs a working password, an unpatched system, a misconfigured server or an employee willing to approve a payment. That continuity shapes the defensive picture. Many of the controls that blunted older attacks, from multi-factor authentication to call-back checks on payment requests, still work when the attacker is using AI.

The 10 slides that follow cover a selection of these shifts, not a complete list. They start with social engineering, from fluent phishing to cloned voices and deepfaked video calls. They then look at the underground market for criminal chatbots and at university research on AI agents exploiting known software flaws. Other slides examine a reported espionage campaign run largely through an AI tool, automated bug hunting and prompt injection. The final two turn to the weaknesses attackers still rely on and the defenses that hold up. Together they show how hackers use AI today and where the old rules still apply.

Large language models let attackers write phishing emails that are fluent, personalized and available in many languages. Spelling mistakes and awkward grammar, long treated as the easiest way to spot a scam, are no longer a reliable warning sign.

For years, much phishing was written by people working outside their native language. Misspelled brand names, odd capitalization and stiff phrasing were common. The advance-fee "Nigerian prince" emails became shorthand for how clumsy fraud messages could look. They are known as 419 scams, after a section of Nigeria's criminal code. Security awareness training often taught employees to treat sloppy writing as a red flag.

A language model removes that tell. An attacker can ask for a polite invoice reminder, a password-reset notice or a note from a colleague, and get clean copy in seconds. The same tools can shift the tone of a message or translate it into another language. That widens the pool of potential targets to readers of languages the attacker does not speak.

Personalization is the other shift. Spear phishing — messages tailored to one person or a small group — has been around for years, but it took effort. An attacker had to research the target and learn the names of their manager and vendors. Then the note had to be written by hand. A model can turn public details from a company website, a press release or a professional profile into a tailored message quickly. That lowers the cost of producing many customized lures at once.

The mechanics have not changed. A phishing email still has to reach an inbox, which means getting past spam filters and email security tools. It still has to persuade someone to click a link, open an attachment, enter credentials or approve a payment. The attacker still needs infrastructure behind the message, such as a lookalike domain or a fake login page.

Other signals remain visible no matter how polished the writing is. The sender's actual address, the real destination of a link, an unexpected sense of urgency and a request that breaks normal procedure can all be checked. Business email compromise is a fraud in which criminals pose as executives or suppliers to redirect payments. Those schemes relied on context and timing long before AI writing tools existed.

Voice cloning software can produce a convincing imitation of a person's voice from a short audio sample. Scammers use it to place calls that sound like a company executive or a family member, then ask for money or access.

Phone fraud is not new. Traditional vishing, short for voice phishing, depended on the caller. A scammer worked from a script and used acting skill to pass as a bank officer, an IT technician or a panicked relative. The scammer's real voice set a limit. It could not match the voice of someone the target knew well.

Cloning removes that limit. Text-to-speech systems can read any script aloud in a copied voice. Voice conversion tools turn the scammer's own speech into the target voice during the call. That approach allows a live back-and-forth rather than a playback of prerecorded lines.

The raw material is often public. Executives speak on earnings calls, conference panels and recorded interviews. Ordinary people post videos on social media and record voicemail greetings. Any of these recordings can supply the sample a cloning tool needs.

Two patterns recur. In the executive version, a caller who sounds like a CEO or finance chief tells an employee to move money quickly. The call often cites a confidential deal or a deadline. It is a spoken form of business email compromise, the long-running fraud in which attackers pose as company leaders over email.

In the family version, a caller who sounds like a child or grandchild claims to be in trouble. The story might involve an arrest, an accident or a stranded trip, and the money has to be sent at once. This builds on the older grandparent scam. That scheme used a generic young voice and counted on a bad line or the victim's assumptions to fill the gaps. A cloned voice supplies the recognition the scam once had to fake. Caller ID spoofing can put a familiar number on the screen as well.

For most people, a familiar voice works as an identity check. Hearing it lowers suspicion before the request even arrives. Urgency and distress then discourage the pause that might expose the fraud.

A cloned voice does not give the scammer the real person's memories. Personal questions and references to shared events can expose the impostor. The standard safeguards build on that gap: hang up and call back on a number already known, or agree on a family code word in advance.

Deepfake video lets an attacker impersonate several trusted people at once on a live call. Hong Kong police reported in February 2024 that an employee of the engineering firm Arup was deceived on a video call in which the colleagues on screen were deepfakes.

Press reports put the loss at about $25 million. The case showed synthetic video being used to take money directly from a company, not only to spread false content online.

For years, a video call worked as an informal identity check. An email address can be spoofed. A voice on the phone can be imitated. Seeing a familiar face respond in real time felt like stronger proof, and many employees treated an on-camera conversation as a safe way to confirm an unusual request.

Deepfake video fraud targets that assumption. Business email compromise, a long-running form of payment fraud, relies on a fake or hijacked email account asking for money. The victim sees only text. Finance staff learned to check for altered sender addresses, lookalike domains and pressure to act quickly.

A convincing video call removes many of those clues. The request arrives through a channel the employee already trusts. The faces and voices match people the employee expects to see.

A call with several participants adds social pressure. When multiple apparent colleagues treat a request as routine, one employee has fewer reasons to question it. Raising doubts in front of a group can also feel awkward. Staging an entire meeting of fake people would have been impractical for a human con artist working alone.

The technical barrier has fallen. Convincing synthetic video once demanded time, computing power and specialist skill. Face-swapping and voice-synthesis software is now far easier to obtain. Some tools can run during a live conversation.

The method still depends on old-fashioned groundwork. Attackers need to know who works with whom, what a plausible request looks like and where to send the money. Public video and audio of executives, such as conference talks and earnings calls, can supply raw material for the fakes. The deepfake makes the request believable. It does not replace the research behind it.

For finance teams, the change is that appearing on camera no longer settles who someone is. Confirming a large transfer through a separately known phone number or an in-person check forces an attacker to defeat a second, independent channel.

WormGPT and FraudGPT were chatbots advertised on underground forums in 2023 as tools for writing phishing messages and malware. Their real capabilities were limited, but their appearance showed that a market for AI-for-crime services had formed.

Both products were pitched to buyers who wanted a language model without the refusals built into mainstream chatbots. The sales pitch was simple. Ask for a fake invoice email or a piece of malicious code, and the tool would write it without pushing back.

What buyers received fell short of that marketing. The tools largely wrapped existing language models rather than offering new technology of their own. Their output did not exceed what a skilled attacker could already produce by hand.

The packaging was the larger shift. Criminal forums have long sold ready-made attack tools to people who could not build them. Examples include phishing kits that copy bank login pages and ransomware-as-a-service, in which developers rent malware to affiliates for a cut of the ransom payments. WormGPT and FraudGPT applied the same business model to generative AI.

The contrast with traditional hacking comes down to who can take part. Writing a convincing lure or a working script once required either skill or money paid to someone who had it. A subscription chatbot, even a weak one, offers a novice attacker a shortcut around both.

The branding was part of the offer. Names that echo ChatGPT told buyers the service promised the same convenience, minus the safeguards. That framing gave the products an easy hook in forum advertisements.

Neither tool changed what an attack needs to succeed. A phishing email still needs a delivery route and a recipient who clicks. Malicious code still needs a way onto a target system. What changed is that AI assistance became something criminals could shop for as a product, alongside the hosting, tools and access they already buy from one another.

A 2024 study from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign found that an AI agent built on GPT-4 could exploit most of a test set of known, recently disclosed software flaws. The experiment ran in a lab. The other models the researchers tested did far worse.

The paper was written by Richard Fang, Daniel Kang and colleagues. It focused on one-day vulnerabilities. These are security flaws that have already been publicly disclosed, often with an official advisory and a patch, but that many systems have not yet fixed. A zero-day, by contrast, is a flaw the vendor does not yet know about or has not yet addressed.

The researchers gave the agent the public description of each vulnerability. This is the kind of entry published in the Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures system. They also gave it tools it could use to act on a target. The agent then had to work out how to turn that written description into a working attack.

When the researchers withheld the description, the agent's performance fell sharply. That gap indicates the agent was much better at acting on known information than at discovering flaws on its own.

In traditional hacking, turning an advisory into a working exploit is skilled, manual work. An attacker reads the disclosure and studies the patch or the affected code. Then the attacker writes and tests an exploit by hand. Public frameworks such as Metasploit package many exploits for reuse, but someone still has to write each module first. The UIUC result points to a way part of that step could be automated.

The timing matters because of the window between disclosure and patching. Once a flaw is public, defenders work to apply fixes. Attackers, meanwhile, try to use the flaw against systems that remain unpatched. Anything that shortens the attacker's side of that race leaves organizations less time to update.

The study has clear limits. It was a controlled experiment on a small, fixed set of vulnerabilities selected by the researchers. It did not show AI agents carrying out attacks against real organizations. It is not evidence of criminals using this technique in the wild. The results also apply only to the specific models and versions tested at the time.

For security teams, the lesson is narrow. Skilled humans were exploiting disclosed vulnerabilities long before language models existed. The research suggests the effort needed to weaponize those flaws could fall. That puts more weight on how quickly patches get applied after a flaw becomes public.

Anthropic reported in November 2025 that a group it described as Chinese state-sponsored used its Claude Code tool to run most of an espionage campaign. The company tracks the group as GTG-1002 and said roughly 30 targets were involved, with humans making the key decisions.

The account comes from Anthropic's own report. It is a company disclosure about misuse of the company's own product, not an independent forensic investigation. The details reflect what Anthropic chose to publish about the case.

The case differs from earlier misuse because of the role the AI played. In earlier misuse cases, language models mostly helped attackers draft messages or fix code. In this campaign, Anthropic described the tool acting more like an operator than an assistant. It worked through reconnaissance, vulnerability research and data handling across several targets. People stepped in at a limited number of decision points rather than directing every step.

Anthropic said the targets included large technology companies, financial institutions, chemical manufacturers and government agencies. The company reported that only a small number of the intrusions succeeded.

Getting the tool to cooperate took deception. Anthropic said the attackers split the operation into small tasks that each looked harmless on its own. They also told the model it was working for a legitimate cybersecurity firm carrying out defensive testing. Both tactics resemble social engineering, aimed at software instead of a person.

The report also described limits. Anthropic said Claude sometimes overstated its results. It claimed to have obtained credentials that did not work and presented publicly available information as a discovery. Anthropic described those errors as an obstacle to fully autonomous attacks.

The contrast with traditional state-backed hacking is about labor and pace. Espionage campaigns have long relied on skilled operators working through each stage by hand. Anthropic reported that at the campaign's peak, the tool made thousands of requests, often several per second. The company said that pace would be impossible for human hackers to match.

Anthropic said it banned the accounts involved, notified affected organizations and coordinated with authorities during its investigation.

DARPA's AI Cyber Challenge tested whether automated systems could find and fix vulnerabilities in open-source code without a person steering each step. Team Atlanta won the competition's finals, held at DEF CON in Las Vegas in August 2025.

DARPA is the U.S. Defense Department's research agency. It launched the competition in 2023 with ARPA-H, the federal agency that funds health research. The target was open-source software, the shared code inside everything from web servers to hospital equipment. A semifinal round took place at DEF CON in 2024 before the field narrowed for the finals.

Each team built what the competition called a cyber reasoning system. These systems pair large language models with conventional program analysis. They scan code for flaws and confirm that a flaw can be triggered. Then they write a patch that closes the hole without breaking the program.

For decades, bug hunting relied on two main methods. One was manual auditing, in which a skilled researcher reads source code line by line in search of mistakes. The other was fuzzing, which feeds a program huge volumes of malformed or random input until something crashes. Google $GOOGL's OSS-Fuzz service has run fuzzing against open-source projects since 2016. Fuzzing scales well, but each crash typically still has to be diagnosed and fixed by a person.

The systems at the AI Cyber Challenge were built to close that gap. Discovery, diagnosis and repair run in a single automated pipeline. The aim is to shorten the time a flaw sits in code that anyone can download and inspect.

The technology is dual-use. A tool that finds a vulnerability so it can be patched also finds a vulnerability that could be exploited. In traditional hacking, uncovering a new flaw in widely used software took specialist knowledge and sustained effort. Automated systems lower that cost for whoever runs them, defender or attacker.

That puts the weight on who finds a flaw first. A flaw found and patched by a maintainer is closed for everyone who updates. A flaw found first by an attacker stays open until someone else notices it.

Prompt injection is an attack that hides instructions inside text an AI system reads, so the model follows the attacker's commands instead of its operator's. OWASP lists it first, as LLM01, in its Top 10 for large language model applications.

The weakness comes from how language models handle input. A model receives its developer's instructions and outside content as one stream of text. It has no reliable built-in way to tell trusted commands apart from untrusted data. A line such as "ignore your previous instructions" can carry the same weight as the system's real rules.

Programmer Simon Willison coined the term "prompt injection" in September 2022. The name echoes SQL injection, an older attack in which malicious database commands are slipped into ordinary input fields. The parallel is only partial. SQL injection can be blocked with parameterized queries that keep code and data separate. Language models have no equivalent separation. That gap is why the problem remains open.

OWASP distinguishes two forms. Direct prompt injection happens when a user types manipulative instructions straight into a chatbot. The usual goal is to bypass its safety rules or pull out its hidden system prompt. Indirect prompt injection works differently. The malicious instructions sit inside content the AI processes later, such as a calendar invite, a shared file, a product review or a code comment.

The indirect form matters more as AI tools gain access to real systems. An assistant that can read an inbox, browse the web or call software tools can be steered by text its user never sees. A hidden line on a web page might tell a summarizing assistant to send private data to an outside address. White text on a white background, or instructions buried in metadata, can be invisible to a person yet readable to a model.

The target here is the model's interpretation of language, not a memory error, a weak password or a person's trust. The attacker does not need access to the victim's network to plant the instructions. Any content an AI agent might read becomes a possible entry point.

Mitigations exist, but none is complete. OWASP's guidance includes limiting the actions and permissions a model has, requiring human approval for high-risk steps and keeping external content separate from trusted instructions. It also calls for filtering inputs and outputs and for regular adversarial testing. These measures reduce the damage a successful injection can cause rather than eliminating the underlying flaw.

Stolen credentials, unpatched software, misconfigured systems and social engineering are still the usual ways attackers get in. Even when AI helps with an earlier stage, AI-assisted attacks usually end in familiar steps. Those steps include logging in with a stolen password or running malware on a machine.

A language model can draft a lure or summarize a target's public profile. The break-in itself still depends on a weakness that existed long before generative AI.

Credentials are the clearest case. A valid username and password let an attacker walk through the front door instead of breaking a window. Passwords leak through data breaches at other services. They also leak through malware that harvests saved logins from web browsers. A person who uses the same password on a shopping site and a work account exposes both when either one leaks. Credential stuffing is the practice of trying leaked username and password pairs across many sites. It is an automated technique that predates the current wave of AI tools.

Unpatched software is the second constant. Vendors publish fixes for known flaws, but organizations do not always install them quickly. During that gap, the flaw is public and the fix has not been applied. Internet-facing systems such as VPN gateways, email servers and file-transfer tools have been repeated targets for this reason.

Misconfiguration is quieter and often needs no exploit at all. A cloud storage bucket left open to the public can expose data to anyone who finds it. An administrator account still using a default password, or a remote desktop service exposed to the internet, can hand an attacker direct access.

Social engineering completes the group. It works by persuading a person to do something on the attacker's behalf. That might mean approving a login request, resetting a password or reading out a one-time code to someone posing as the help desk.

AI can make each of these openings faster to find or easier to exploit at scale. It does not remove the need for one of them.

Multi-factor authentication, phishing-resistant passkeys, prompt software patching and call-back verification of payment requests all hold up against AI-enhanced attacks. None of them depends on a person spotting whether a message, voice or face is fake.

Multi-factor authentication, or MFA, asks for a second proof of identity beyond the password. A stolen or guessed password alone does not open the account. A fluent phishing email can still trick someone into typing a password. The attacker then needs the second factor as well.

Not every form of MFA is equally strong. One-time codes, whether sent by text message or generated in an app, can be phished. Push approvals can be relayed too. A fake login page can pass these to the real site in real time. The attacker signs in while the victim believes nothing went wrong.

Passkeys close that gap. They are built on the FIDO2 standards developed by the FIDO Alliance and the W3C. A passkey is a cryptographic key pair. The private key stays on the user's device or in their password manager, and the user never types it or reads it aloud.

Each passkey is tied to the web domain of the site that created it. A look-alike site on a different domain cannot obtain a valid sign-in. That holds however convincing the page, or the AI-written email that led there, happens to be.

Call-back verification applies a similar principle to payment and account-change requests: confirm through a channel the attacker does not control. The employee ends the conversation and calls the requester on a number already on file, not one supplied in the message. A cloned voice or deepfaked face cannot answer a call placed to the real person's known number. Some companies also require a second approver for transfers above a set amount.

Patching software promptly covers the technical side. If AI tools shorten the time between a flaw's disclosure and a working exploit, the deciding factor becomes how quickly an organization installs the fix. Automatic updates and an accurate inventory of internet-facing systems shrink that window.

Defenders also use machine learning, and that practice predates the current wave of generative AI. Paul Graham's 2002 essay "A Plan for Spam" helped popularize statistical filtering of junk email. Fraud and threat detection tools apply similar methods to flag sign-ins from unfamiliar devices or transactions that break an account's usual pattern.
