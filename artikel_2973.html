Eliminate Before You Automate: The Agentic AI Step Missing From 2027 Plans

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Murthy Malapaka builds enterprise intelligence for peak business performance, from autonomous IT operations to every workflow and data layer

As 2027 planning gets underway, most technology budgets I see include a line for agentic AI. Far fewer include a line for finding out which work should exist at all.

That gap is expensive. An agent placed on top of an unnecessary workflow does not remove the waste. It runs the waste faster, at scale, with a token bill and a new layer of oversight attached. The organization then reports automation gains on work that never needed doing.

I saw this with every customer we onboarded to our AI-native operations platform. Each arrived with plans to automate runbook tasks, password resets and routine end-user requests. When we ran historical intelligence on their ticket data, a large share of that work should never have been a ticket. Password resets belonged in self-service identity management. Runbook triggers belonged in existing monitoring and orchestration tools. Many end-user requests disappeared once their root causes were fixed. Those were one-time configuration changes, not candidates for an agent.

The better sequence is simple to state and hard to practice: Eliminate first, then decide what is left for AI.

Most enterprises are sitting on years of tickets, logs, alerts, change records and telemetry. This data is usually treated as an operational exhaust: kept for compliance, searched during incidents, then forgotten.

Read as a whole, it is a map of how work actually flows. It shows which requests recur every week, which alerts get closed without action, which approvals never get rejected and which handoffs exist only because two systems do not talk to each other.

No interviews or consulting workshops can match that record. It captures what people do, not what process documents say they do.

In my work building AI-native operations platforms, the approach that consistently delivers follows four steps.

1. Build intelligence on the current state. Mine operational data to see where work originates, how often it repeats and what business outcome, if any, it serves.

2. Diagnose the real constraint. Ask whether a given workload exists because of a process gap or a technology gap. The answer determines the fix.

3. Eliminate what should not exist. Retire redundant approvals, fix the root causes behind recurring requests and remove handoffs that add no judgment.

4. Then decide what is left for AI. Only the work that survives elimination earns an agent, and it should be work where speed, scale or consistency truly matters.

The same logic applies well beyond IT operations. In one software delivery engagement, we connected user stories, epics, test cases and years of historical bug reports. The pattern was clear. Many recurring defects traced back to requirements that were never written down. On a retail e-commerce site, for example, localization and currency rules were missing from the stories, so testers never covered them and the same bugs kept returning. Fixing the requirements gap eliminated whole classes of defects. Only then did we train a model on that history, giving the delivery pipeline context specific to the organization. Release times dropped, and code quality improved.

When teams follow this order, they are often surprised by how much smaller and more valuable the AI scope becomes.

The economic case is asymmetric. Work that is eliminated has no run cost, no model to maintain, no guardrails to govern and no incident surface. Work that is automated keeps all of those costs and adds new ones: inference spend, monitoring, exception handling and the human review agentic systems still require.

On a multi-tenant shared managed services platform I built over five years, with customers in insurance, manufacturing and wealth management, every onboarding started the same way. We ran historical intelligence across the customer’s in-scope ticket and request volume, often well over 200,000 records, and sorted each workload into one of three outcomes: eliminate, automate with agents or assist a human expert. Measured against that baseline, eliminating first removed 60% to 80% of the workload and cut operating costs by more than 35%. Most of that value came from the elimination step, not the automation step. It also let us onboard more customers each year while holding people and technology costs flat.

This matters for 2027 budgets because AI costs scale with usage. Automating a bloated process locks in a larger, recurring bill. Eliminating first shrinks the base that every future AI dollar is spent on.

Leaders do not need a new platform to start. They need a few deliberate commitments in this planning cycle.

• Fund a current-state diagnostic before any agent pilot. Scope it to one high-volume domain, such as IT service management or finance operations, and set a firm deadline.

• Set an elimination target alongside the automation target. Measure how much work was removed, not only how much was automated.

• Require a “why does this exist” review for every agent use case. If no one can name the business outcome the workflow serves, it is a candidate for elimination.

• Tie AI spend to the post-elimination baseline. Forecast inference and oversight costs on the work that remains, not on today’s volume.

The organizations that get the most from agentic AI in 2027 will be the ones that first had the discipline to stop doing work that does not serve the business.

The first question in any AI investment review should not be how fast an agent can perform a workload. It should be why that workload exists. Leaders who make that question the gate for every 2027 AI initiative will spend less, govern less and get more from the AI they deploy. Eliminate first. Then automate what is left.
