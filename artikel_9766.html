Users Don't Complain, They Just Quit: 7 Signals to Catch in Your React Native App

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Most people who quit your app never tell you why. They don't write a review or email support. They just close it and don't come back.

But right before they leave, they almost always do something you could have seen: tapped a button five times, waited for a screen that never loaded, closed the paywall after one second.

I learned this the annoying way. My analytics only knew about what I'd added a track() call for, and on every release I'd forget a few. Worse, I usually didn't know which signal mattered until after users had already left.

So here are the seven signals I'd track in any React Native or Expo app today, with the code for each. All of it works with whatever analytics tool you use. I've written it as analytics.track(...); swap in your own.

Every signal below is more useful when you know which screen it happened on. Don't add a screen call to every screen; listen to navigation once instead. With Expo Router:

useSegments() gives you the route file (/product/[id]) instead of the address (/product/8812), so all product pages count as one screen. Add the app version to every event too, so you can compare releases later.

Someone taps the same thing three or more times in a second. It almost always means "this isn't working": a button that's too slow to respond, a coupon field that does nothing, a spinner that never ends.

Call noteTap() from the onPress of your buttons (a small wrapper around Pressable saves you doing it by hand everywhere). Use the button's accessibilityLabel or text as the label.

The quieter cousin of rage taps: people tapping things that aren't buttons. A product image they expect to zoom, a banner that looks clickable, a price they think opens details.

These are some of the most useful signals, because they tell you what users expect your app to do. They're also the hardest to catch by hand: the thing being tapped has no onPress, so there's nothing to hook into. You'd have to listen to every touch at the root of the app and work out which ones nothing responded to. If you only take one idea from this section, take this: when you see many taps on something that isn't interactive, make it interactive.

When a request fails, the user usually sees a spinner, an empty list or nothing at all, and quietly gives up. Your backend logs might show it, but not which screen it broke or what the user did next.

Don't send full URLs: they often contain user IDs, emails or tokens. The status code and the screen are usually enough.

One catch: when the error is fatal, the app may close before the event is sent. Save events to storage first and send them on the next launch, otherwise you'll miss exactly the crashes that matter most.

People will wait a second or two. After that, many of them leave. Measure from the moment a screen opens to the moment its content is ready:

Look at the slowest 10% of visits, not the average. The average hides the people on bad connections, and they're the ones leaving.

Counting paywall views tells you little. How long people stay on the paywall tells you a lot: closing it in a second means they didn't read it, usually because it appeared too early.

If most closes happen in under two seconds, try showing the paywall later, after the user has done the thing your app is for.

Finally, the simplest and maybe the most telling one: which screen people are on when they leave the app.

Every app has screens where visits naturally end (a confirmation page, say). The interesting ones are screens where many visits end that shouldn't be the end: halfway through onboarding, the cart, the sign-up form. Compare them with the signals above. A screen with lots of exits and lots of rage taps is usually your most expensive bug.

That's seven pieces of code to write, test, and remember on every release. Dead taps are very hard to do by hand. And each new screen or button is one more place to forget.
