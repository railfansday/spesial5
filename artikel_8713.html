The third quarter brought a sharp rise in interest rates, the Fedâs first rate hike since 2023, and oil back above $100 per barrel. Many investors might have expected stocks to struggle against that backdrop. Instead, the S&P 500 reached record highs, supported by strong corporate earnings.

The economy stayed resilient. Real GDP grew at a 3.4% annualized rate in the third quarter, up from 2.2% in the second, according to the Bureau of Economic Analysisâs advance estimate. (2) The unemployment rate was 4.2% in September, little changed from 4.1% in August. (3) Job openings exceeded the number of unemployed workers for a fifth straight month, and layoffs were just 1.0% of the labor force. (4) One soft spot: average hourly earnings grew about 3.1% from a year earlier, slightly behind inflation. (5) Households also appeared less sensitive to higher borrowing costs than in past cycles, because many locked in low fixed-rate mortgages earlier in the decade.

Oil surged. Renewed fighting involving Iran disrupted shipments through the Strait of Hormuz, pushing Brent crude above $100 per barrel in September. (6) Commodities were the quarterâs strongest major asset class, with the S&P GSCI commodity index up 17.9%. (7)

Inflation persisted, and the Fed resumed tightening. Consumer prices rose 3.4% year over year in August, with gasoline up 27.4%, while core PCE inflation, the Fedâs preferred gauge, stood at 3.0%. (8) On September 16, the Fed raised its policy rate by a quarter point to 3.75%â4.00%, its first increase since July 2023, in a unanimous vote. (9) The reversal in expectations was striking: investors entered 2026 anticipating two rate cuts. (10) Inflation did not ease after quarter-end; September consumer prices rose 3.7% from a year earlier, and the Fed held rates steady at its October meeting. (11)

Bonds posted their worst quarter in four years. The 10-year Treasury yield climbed to its highest level since 2002, (12) propelled by strong growth, sticky inflation, a more hawkish Fed, concern over government debt, and heavy borrowing by companies financing AI infrastructure. (13) Because bond prices move inversely to yields, the broad U.S. bond market lost 3.51% for the quarter, its weakest showing in four years. (14) Maturity determined the damage: long-term Treasury bonds fell 7.65%, while short-term bonds lost less than 1%. (15) Municipal bonds had their worst quarterly loss in 45 years.(16)

There is a silver lining in higher starting yields. Vanguard estimated that, at late-September yield levels, U.S. Treasuries with maturities of up to 10 years would not lose money over the following year even if rates rose another 50 basis points, a stark contrast with 2022. (17)

Stocks advanced on earnings, not valuations. The S&P 500 gained 2.30% for the quarter and 12.75% year to date. Earnings did the heavy lifting: analysts expected third-quarter S&P 500 earnings to grow about 29% from a year earlier and, unusually, raised their estimates as the quarter progressed. (18) Since June 30, forward earnings estimates rose 8.9% while the index rose 2.7%, so the forward price-to-earnings ratio fell from 20.4 to 19.2. Stocks became less expensive relative to profits even as prices climbed. (19) In prior years, rapid earnings growth was typically accompanied by expanding valuations; this year the opposite occurred. (20)

Leadership was narrow. Only four of the 11 S&P 500 sectors advanced, led by energy (+16.5%) and technology (+7.1%), while utilities (â13.0%) and industrials (â9.9%) fell the most. (21) Small-cap stocks lost 7.23%, in part because smaller companies rely more on floating-rate and short-term debt and feel higher rates sooner. (22) Fewer stocks participating in gains can leave the market more vulnerable to setbacks.

The most consequential story of the quarter may not have been interest rates or oil, but the extent to which the financial system came to depend on a single theme: artificial intelligence. Concentration is hardly new, yet this quarter it surfaced in four places at once.

In U.S. stocks. Technology stocks made up nearly 40% of the S&P 500 (23), and the 10 largest companies accounted for nearly 40% of the index, well above the long-term average. (24)

In emerging market stocks. The pattern extended abroad. The 10 largest stocks in the MSCI Emerging Markets Index, all AI-related, made up 36% of the index, and technology represented more than one-third of the market. (25) An investor who bought emerging markets to diversify away from U.S. technology may well have ended up owning substantial AI exposure anyway.

In profits and the economy. Technology-sector revenue was expected to grow 40% in the third quarter, led by semiconductor companies at roughly 80%. (26) Amazon (AMZN), Alphabet (GOOGL), Meta (META), and Microsoft (MSFT) were expected to spend a combined $721 billion on capital investment in 2026, nearly double the $376 billion they spent in 2025. (27) Data-center construction surged, yet total private construction spending in September remained below its level at the end of 2023. (28) And AI had not yet produced meaningful gains in worker productivity, which grew just 1.4% in the second quarter. (29)

In the bond market. Large technology companies increasingly turned to debt to finance data centers. Goldman Sachs estimated that hyperscalers issued $229 billion of debt this year through the third quarter and that issuance could reach $420 billion in 2027; earlier in the year, AI-related debt accounted for about 15% of the corporate bond universe. (30) That borrowing competes with the U.S. Treasury for investorsâ capital and adds to upward pressure on interest rates.

Why it matters. There is a credible case that this concentration rests on real fundamentals: the largest AI spenders are among the most profitable companies in the world, and valuations declined this year even as profits soared, which is not the hallmark of a conventional bubble. The risk is that when the same small group of companies drives stock returns, earnings growth, business investment, and corporate borrowing, a setback in any one area can spread rapidly to the others. On a longer-term valuation measure, the Shiller P/E, which compares prices with 10 years of inflation-adjusted earnings, was above 40 in September, a level last seen in 1999â2000. (31) For investors, the practical question is how much of a portfolio depends on this one theme, sometimes in places they may not expect.

Since quarter-end, markets have been choppy without establishing a clear direction. Between October 29 and November 9 the S&P 500 fell about 2.2%, but that number hides the swings along the way: a 1.6% gain on November 4, a 4.4% drop on November 5, and a 2.3% rebound on November 9. (32) The VIX, a widely followed gauge of expected stock market volatility, jumped from 16.6 on November 4 to 28.2 on November 5 before settling back near 20. (33)

Through the first ten days of the month the major U.S. indexes were little changed on net, with the Dow Jones Industrial Average up 0.9%, the S&P 500 down 0.8%, and the Nasdaq Composite down 1.5%, the latter reflecting a 4.4% single-day decline on November 5. (34) Beneath the surface, the rotation was pronounced: communication services (+7.9%), consumer discretionary (+4.9%), real estate (+3.3%), and financials (+3.2%) led, while technology (â5.9%) and energy (â11.2%) fell sharply. (35)

This volatility arrived alongside the November 3 midterm elections, and it is natural to connect the two. History suggests caution in doing so: election years are not inherently more volatile, and markets have grown under a wide range of political outcomes; what has tended to matter for returns is the underlying economic backdrop, not which party prevails. (36) The sharper moves this month have been concentrated in technology and energy, the two sectors that led the market in the third quarter, which suggests the same themes discussed above â AI concentration, interest rates, and oil â remain the primary drivers. We will continue to monitor these developments.
