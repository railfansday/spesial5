India turned its population bomb into a demographic dividend; surely its polluted air deserves the same rebranding.

A few mornings ago, I landed in Delhi and caught the city’s signature scent outside the airport. In the Uber to my hotel, people were out on their morning walks, exercising in thick smog. I barely saw a mask. The tourism ministry should have been filming.

Those of us who grew up in India and moved abroad remember misty winter mornings fondly. Delhi has thoughtfully made the mist available all day. Luxury hotels spend fortunes inventing signature fragrances. Delhi has one that arrives with the airport transfer, free of charge.

Someone should be selling this. I propose…pollution tourism.

In 1968, biologist Paul Ehrlich opened The Population Bomb with a taxi ride through Delhi on “one stinking hot night,” past crowded streets and through a haze of dust and smoke. A satirical Skift column uses the conceit of "pollution tourism" to put a hard number on Delhi's air quality problem and the tourism sector's response to it. In IQAir's 2025 rankings, Loni in Uttar Pradesh was the world's most polluted city and New Delhi the most polluted capital, with India holding three of the world's four most polluted cities. Delhi's official daily AQI averaged 376 on Diwali, November 8 — the worst festival reading in five years — while private tracker AQI.in reported a post-Diwali peak of 2,283 on a different scale. Delhi's worst pollution lands in the middle of India's peak inbound tourism season. Officials have struck an optimistic tone: Chief Minister Rekha Gupta said November brought relief "for the first time in the last 10 years," and Environment Minister Manjinder Singh Sirsa said the official citywide AQI had not crossed 400 in 2026. Meanwhile, a parliamentary committee found the entire ₹3.5 crore (about $362,000) allocated for overseas promotion in 2026–27 was earmarked for mandatory international tourism-organization contributions, leaving nothing for actual promotion. On the ground, Skift reported Intrepid Travel's Delhi guides now carry N95 masks and shorten outdoor activities on bad days, and a Radisson executive said some guests are postponing trips.
