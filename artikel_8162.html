As the growing season winds down, some plants benefit from a final tidy-up. But knowing what to cut – and what to leave alone – is crucial.

While the cold weather and dropped leaves of November make it seem as if the gardening season is over, there’s still one task left: pruning. Certain plants benefit from a pre-winter trim, while others shouldn’t be touched with pruners until next year.

Some perennials should be cut back all the way right now, while other types of plants, like fruiting vines and certain shrubs, can be touched up more lightly. Exact timing for these tasks depends on your location, weather, and the plant.

Here, we’ll outline some late fall pruning basics and list some of the plants you can tidy up or trim back now, before winter fully takes hold. We also share which plants are better trimmed next season.

Peonies are notoriously susceptible to fungal diseases. The deciduous foliage will die back in fall and, if left in place, can allow fungal spores to overwinter and reinfect plants next year. Trim back foliage once the leaves have yellowed or browned, or earlier if they look diseased.

November is the right time for this chore in many places, but if you garden in a warmer climate, the timing might be later. Remove all of the foliage without cutting it too close to the plant’s crown.

Another herbaceous perennial common in many gardens, hostas die back in fall. Clearing away the spent foliage tidies up beds, makes spring cleaning easier, and prevents many pests and diseases from overwintering and spreading. Cut leaves back to the base without getting too close to or cutting into the crowns. As long as leaves are still green, keep them in place to collect energy. Wait until they die back.

Gardeners are often tempted to cut back the leaves of these early bloomers soon after flowering, but it’s important to wait. The green leaves continue collecting energy to store for next year’s flowers. As long as they are healthy, keep the leaves in place until they start dying back in fall. November is a typical time for pruning bearded irises in many gardens. Cut the fans back to four to six inches (10 to 15 cm).

As with irises, you don’t want to trim back leaves just because the plants have finished flowering. Let healthy, green leaves stay in place to gather energy. For many gardeners, daylily leaves will be ready to cut back by November. In warmer climates, you might not trim them back until December. Cut daylily leaves back to a few inches above the ground.

Summer-fruiting raspberries develop fruit on canes that are two years old. Once they’re done fruiting, these older canes use up energy that the plant could be using on new canes and next year’s fruit. Identify the older, woodier canes that fruited this year, and trim them back to the ground.

You’ll also see newer, greener canes. Do not cut these back, or you’ll lose next year’s berries. Tie them together or to a support, like a fence, to protect them from winter winds.

Grapevines need regular pruning to maintain their size and structure and to support abundant fruit production. Timing is important. The vines should be dormant, so they don’t lose too much sap or sprout fresh growth. You also don’t want to prune too late, after hard freezes turn vines too brittle to make clean cuts.

When you find the right time to prune your grapevines, remove dead, damaged, or poorly positioned vines and canes. Identify healthy, young vines and trim around these. Don’t remove all older wood, especially the main trunk and arms.

Lavender hedges that are cold-hardy, like English lavender, benefit from a late fall or early winter trim to maintain their shape and health and protect them from winter weather. Too much material left on the plants can accumulate snow and ice that damage the crowns. Trim these lavenders back by about a third before the real freezing weather begins.

While these are examples of plants you can and should tackle this time of year, there are also plants that should not be trimmed now.

As a good general rule, you can trim back damaged, dead, or diseased stems and branches from any plant at any time of year. However, harder maintenance pruning needs careful timing. These plants are examples of those that can take a hard trim as winter is about to set in. Most importantly, know which plants and varieties you have and what the best practices are for these before you begin any pruning project.
