Rocket Failure Destroys 40 Amazon Leo Satellites And Complicates Delta Air Lines Wi-Fi Plans

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Delta Air Lines made a calculated bet when it chose Amazon Leo over Starlink for its next-generation Wi-Fi. That bet became a little riskier on Sunday when a rocket carrying 40 Amazon satellites failed before reaching orbit.

A United Launch Alliance Vulcan rocket lifted off from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station on November 15, 2026, carrying 40 satellites for Amazon Leo, the low-Earth-orbit network Amazon is building to compete with Starlink.

About 10 minutes after launch, the rocket suffered what ULA called an “anomaly” and failed to deliver the satellites into orbit. The entire payload was lost. ULA, a joint venture between Boeing and Lockheed Martin, issued the following statement:

“Based on data received by our Launch Director, we confirm that the Vulcan vehicle launched from Space Launch Complex 41 suffered an anomaly 10 minutes into the flight and failed to deliver the Amazon mission to orbit.

“Every flight generates data, and every flight teaches us something. Since the anomaly just occurred, we don’t have any specific data yet to understand what occurred today.

“We will work closely with our customer and other appropriate organizations to find the cause, determine the appropriate corrective action, and return to flight as quickly as possible.”

This was the first complete mission failure in ULA’s approximately 20-year history and the first Amazon Leo launch aboard a Vulcan rocket.

The loss of 40 satellites is significant, but it certainly does not destroy the Amazon Leo project. Before Sunday’s failure, Amazon had already placed 398 satellites into orbit across 14 launches. The planned constellation calls for 3,236 satellites to provide global coverage, meaning Amazon had deployed about 12% of the initial network before this launch.

Amazon also has hundreds of satellites reportedly ready for launch and is not exclusively dependent on Vulcan. Its next mission, using an Ariane 6 rocket to carry 36 more satellites, is scheduled for November 23 from French Guiana.

So this is not necessarily a case of the entire Leo launch schedule stopping while ULA investigates. Amazon has contracts with several launch providers and can continue expanding its satellite network using other rockets. Still, Amazon planned to use Vulcan for dozens of missions. Any lengthy investigation or redesign could slow the deployment schedule and place more pressure on its other launch partners.

Delta has committed to installing Amazon Leo on an initial 500 aircraft beginning in 2028. The carrier promises download speeds of up to 1 Gbps and upload speeds of up to 400 Mbps through a purpose-built antenna, with service remaining free for SkyMiles members.

But unlike Starlink, which is already operating on hundreds of commercial aircraft, Amazon Leo remains a system under construction. Delta chose a product based on what it is expected to become rather than what passengers can use today.

That choice was defensible. Delta has a broad relationship with Amazon Web Services and wants to integrate connectivity with Delta Sync, its loyalty program, seatback entertainment, and broader customer data strategy. I have defended the logic behind that decision: Delta was choosing a technology and commercial partner that would allow it to retain more control over the passenger interface and potentially build a much broader digital ecosystem. I’d rather have Delta retain contorl over its system than Elon Musk do the same…

But choosing an unfinished network carries execution risk, and we just received a vivid demonstration of it. One Mile At A Time puts it well: “One of the risks with picking a service that’s still in development is that the timeline is really quite fluid, and this is an example of that.”

United Airlines is already installing Starlink throughout its fleet and expects to complete the project by the end of 2027. Delta does not plan to begin installing Amazon Leo until 2028. In theory, United could finish bringing Starlink to its entire fleet before Delta installs Amazon Leo on its first aircraft.

Delta is not leaving passengers without internet in the interim. Free Delta Sync Wi-Fi is available across most of its fleet, and in my experience it generally works well on most flights. Delta also says it is adding Viasat capacity and deploying Hughes Fusion technology as it works toward full fleet connectivity.

The suggestion that Delta passengers are flying around without functioning Wi-Fi is simply not accurate. But Starlink is better than any inflight internet system I have used. It is fast, low latency, and remarkably consistent. I increasingly choose United flights where I know Starlink will be available because it allows me to work in the air almost as easily as I can on the ground.

Delta CEO Ed Bastian has defended passing on Starlink, citing disagreements over the business model and customer access rather than any personal dispute with Elon Musk.

Fair enough. But every delay to Amazon Leo gives United more time to widen its lead.

One failed rocket does not prove that Delta made the wrong choice. Amazon has the resources, technology, and launch contracts to build a serious Starlink competitor, and Leo may ultimately deliver an excellent onboard product. But Delta selected a network that does not yet exist at the necessary scale. Sunday’s launch failure is precisely the sort of development that can disrupt a carefully constructed timeline but is also hardly unique to getting new technology up and running.

Delta understood that risk when it passed on a proven product in favor of a deeper relationship with Amazon. Now it needs Amazon Leo to recover quickly and keep its 2028 installation promise. Ultimately, passengers will not be amused if faster Wi-Fi remains years away.
