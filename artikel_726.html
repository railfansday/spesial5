The biggest news story out of Germany this evening had to do with the Left candidate Elif Eralp having sparked controversy with internal remarks to Der Spiegel.

A German news magazine's decision to publish private interview notes has sparked a controversy surrounding Berlin's potential next mayor.

On Thursday, Der Spiegel published internal comments on an interview by Elif Eralp, who led the socialist Left Party (Die Linke) to victory in September's state election. Her office had accidentally included the remarks while approving an interview for publication.

In the notes, Eralp expressed fears for her family's safety if she publicly named Ibrahim Ibrahim, a pro-Palestinian activist who is reported to have links to the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine (PFLP), which the EU designates a terrorist organization. Ibrahim had attended the Left Party's election-night celebrations and was photographed with prominent Left Party Bundestag member Ferat Koçak.

Eralp also cited advice she received from Berlin police against publicly naming individuals linked to organized crime, though that advice was reportedly related to a separate case. Der Spiegel questioned whether her fears could affect her independence as mayor. Eralp rejected suggestions that she could be intimidated and pledged firm action against terrorism and organized crime.

The timing is significant. Eralp, the daughter of Turkish immigrants, could become Berlin's first Left Party mayor and the first woman of Turkish descent to hold the office. Her party won 25.7% of the vote in September's election and hopes to form a government with the center-left Social Democrats (SPD) and the Greens. Both prospective partners have approved formal coalition negotiations.

The Left must decide whether to follow suit at a party conference this Saturday, October 31. The far-left party is also facing accusations of antisemitism involving some of its members. Newly elected lawmaker Vanessa Emde faces possible exclusion from the Left's parliamentary group following comments questioning whether states, including Israel, have an inherent right to exist. The Left has been part of Berlin coalition governments before but has never led one. The controversy comes at a pivotal moment in its effort to do so.

Germany's leaders are on a foreign relations push, with Chancellor Friedrich Merz on a trip to South Korea, German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul in Panama and German President Frank-Walter Steinmeier in China.

Merz spoke of the threat North Korea posed to Europe's freedom by supplying troops to fight in Russia's invasion of Ukraine. He wraps up his visit with a trip to the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) on Friday.

German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul, meanwhile, is in Panama where he is visiting ahead of traveling to Atlanta for the G20 meeting.

Wadephul is there to promote free trade and also handed over humanitarian supplies to his Panamanian counterpart Javier Martinez-Acha Vasquez.

And then German President Frank-Walter Steinmeier ditched the formalities in China and picked up a ping-pong paddle for a game with Germany’s most famous table tennis player, Tino Boll, who is accompanying Steinmeier as a special guest on his state visit.

German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul landed in Panama on Wednesday evening for a visit.

After his arrival at Panama City airport, Wadephul handed over humanitarian supplies to his Panamanian counterpart Javier Martinez-Acha Vasquez.

The aid shipment is made up of about 20 tons of supplies. Panama was hit by a powerful earthquake earlier this month.

The supplies include water filters, blankets, tarpaulins, cots and kitchen sets worth over €150,000 ($167,000).

More than 5,000 people are still being housed in emergency shelters, weeks after the magnitude-7.7 quake on October 9, which resulted in injuries and damaged hundreds of buildings.

Wadephul also thanked the Central American country for maintaining the Panama Canal "so responsibly and ensuring it is fit for the future, as well as more generally focusing on preserving balance in the region."

Restrictions on other sea routes due to the conflict in the Middle East has meant that the Panama Canal is more important to global trade than ever.

Wadephul is due to hold talks on Thursday with Martinez-Acha Vasquez and President Jose Raul Mulino.

Wadephul will travel to the US state of Georgia for the G20 summit in Atlanta on Friday and Saturday.

Several names are being floated to replace Frank-Walter Steinmeier as Germany's ceremonial head of state — including some that could be the first woman to hold the post.

After delivering his speech at Peking University, German President Frank-Walter Steinmeier ditched the formalities to play ping-pong with Germany’s most famous table tennis player, Tino Boll.

In the university sports hall, the two faced each other, accompanied by two Chinese participants — a referee and the university president. Right from the start, Steinmeier downplayed his chances, and subsequently lost 1–7.

Boll, who won silver at the Beijing Olympics in 2008, is the most successful German table tennis player of all time.

He won several Olympic medals, as well as titles in World Cups and World Championships during his storied career, which saw him ranked as the number 1 player in the world in 2003, 2011 and 2018.

To this day, Boll remains one of the best-known Germans in China, where ping-pong is unofficially considered the national sport.

Boll is accompanying Steinmeier as a special guest on his state visit.

Germany's President Frank-Walter Steinmeier on Thursday called on China to reduce economic imbalances between the two countries, as well as between China and Europe.

Steinmeier, who was speaking at the renowned Peking University, also urged China to work with Germany and other European nations to strengthen the international, rules-based order and "to advocate for peace and free global trade."

The German president, who holds a largely ceremonial role, spoke of his respect for China's rapid development in its economy, technology and politics, calling China a "defining world power."

But Steinmeier added, "With greatness and power comes great responsibility."

"We are not concerned about China's rise. We are watching how China rises," he said.

"We do not want to decouple from China; on the contrary, we want to structure trade in such a way that it continues to benefit both sides." This, he said, requires fair and uniform rules.

Steinmeier was referring to the high barriers European companies face when trying to operate in the Chinese market. Since China heavily subsidizes its exports to Europe, the European market comes under pressure too quickly.

This poses "the danger of one-sided vulnerability," Steinmeier said. Europeans cannot want this for their economy, just as "China does not want it for its economy."

He also called on China to exert more influence on Russia to bring the war in Ukraine to an end.

According to Steinmeier, China's interests are also at stake in this war, just as they are in the Middle East. As a major trading power, China also needs free sea lanes, intact supply chains, and a secure energy supply.

"The longer these conflicts persist, the weaker the global economy as a whole becomes — and with it, one of its largest economies: China," the president said.

Steinmeier addressed future issues in which China should take a particular interest: climate change and artificial intelligence. He appealed to his hosts, saying that when it comes to AI, "we should jointly establish international guidelines so that the technology remains under human control," he said.

On the final day of his state visit, Steinmeier will meet with China's leadership and hold talks with, among others, President Xi Jinping.

Steinmeier is making his first visit to China in eight years, as he seeks to boost bilateral ties.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz on Thursday said North Korea was threatening "freedom throughout Europe" by sending soldiers to fight for Russia in its war against Ukraine.

After a meeting in Seoul with South Korean President Lee Jae Myung, Merz highlighted that Russia was supplying weapons to North Korea.

The chancellor said this arrangement therefore shows how "Korea's security affects Europe's security. And Europe's security also affects Korea's security."

North Korea has boosted its military ties with Russia since the start of the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

Pyongyang has provided Moscow with artillery ammunition and missiles. It has also, according to South Korean intelligence services, sent about 15,000 soldiers to fight alongside Russian forces.

In exchange, Russia provides financial backing, military technology and security guarantees to North Korea.

Before Merz's arrival on Thursday, North Korea fired about 10 short-range ballistic missiles toward the Sea of Japan, according to the South Korean military.

At the start of his visit, Merz said he hoped that Korea would eventually be unified. The peninsula has been split along the 38th parallel since the end of hostilities in the Korean War in 1953.

Merz also agreed with Lee to deepen their two nations' strategic and security ties, which includes joint military exercises and exchange of intelligence.

The chancellor is expected to conclude his trip to South Korea with a visit to the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) on Friday.

The South Korean leader made the announcement after a meeting with German Chancellor Friedrich Merz in Seoul.

At a joint press conference, Lee told reporters that he had asked Merz for Germany's backing in securing favorable treatment for South Korea as part of the EU's Industrial Accelerator Act.

An eight-year-old girl sustained serious injuries after she was attacked by at least one wolf at a wildlife park in Brandenburg in east Germany.

The child was brought to hospital by a rescue helicopter on Wednesday.

Local officials said a zookeeper had helped prevent other children from being attacked.

Police are investigating how and why the attack occurred, which took place during a special program for children.

Police on Thursday said their investigation was centered on a staff member who was in the enclosure with the children, a spokeswoman said.

The Teltow-Fläming district administration said the incident happened before the wildlife park's regular opening hours as part of an "Animal Keeper Day" for children.

The administration also said the attack had taken place in the polar wolf enclosure.

"While the animals in the wildlife enclosure were being watered, the wolves kept there attacked a girl and seriously injured her," the district administration said.

The zookeeper's quick action prevented "further harm to two other girls" who were also at the scene, the district added.

The park will remain closed for the time being, officials said, as authorities launch a safety review at the wildlife park, which is about 60 kilometers (37 miles) south of Berlin.

Police are still probing the circumstances that led to the children being inside the enclosure.

The Johannismühle Wildlife Park said it was cooperating with authorities but could not provide further details due to the open investigation.

"We deeply regret the incident, and the affected family has our full support," the park said.

The wildlife park was closed until further notice "due to technical reasons," a sign at the entrance said on Thursday.

A wolf bit a woman in a busy shopping area in Hamburg this year, in what is thought to be the first such attack since wolves were reintroduced to the country in 1998.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, who is visiting Seoul, said at the start of his visit that he hopes North and South Korea will eventually be reunited.

The Korean Peninsula has been divided into two states since the Korean War ended in 1953.

Merz pointed to his own country's division between West and East Germany for much of the second half of the 20th century.

"Korea and Germany are united by the painful experience of division," he wrote in the guest book during his meeting with South Korean President Lee Jae Myung.

"I also wish for the people of Korea what we are fortunate to experience in Germany: unity in freedom."

North Korea is a largely isolated dictatorship ruled by Kim Jong Un, while the South is a democracy that has close ties with the West.

The two Koreas technically remain at war since a truce ended hostilities.

Merz is set to visit the demilitarized zone (DMZ), which divides the two countries, on Friday.

The German chancellor's visit to South Korea is the last stop on his tour of Asia this week, which has also seen him visit Japan and Singapore.

Volkswagen (VW) reported third quarter losses of €5.8 billion ($6.5 billion) on Thursday, as it faces rising competition from China.

Germany's biggest car maker had reported a loss of just over €1.07 billion in the same period last year.

Volkswagen, like other car makers, has struggled to keep pace with fierce competition from China, the world's biggest car market. VW has also struggled with the transition to electric vehicles and with declining demand.

Volkswagen last month warned that its 10-brand group, which includes makers such as Audi, Seat and Skoda, was facing a 10-billion-euro hit to its annual profits.

The most significant hit came from Porsche, Volkswagen's luxury sports car and SUV subsidiary. Porsche, which was once a major driver of profits for the VW group, was also one of the main factors behind VW's quarterly losses in 2025.

The luxury brand has been hit by a sales slump in China, US tariff policy and its electric vehicle strategy.

VW announced this year that it plans to cut over 100,000 jobs across the group by the end of the decade as it tries to reduce costs. It announced on Thursday that its restructuring would cost the company 31 billion euros.

The company is considering or has decided to shut down multiple German factories — including plants in Zwickau, Emden, Hanover, and Neckarsulm.

"What matters now is swift and consistent implementation," Arno Antlitz, VW's chief financial officer said in a statement. "We cannot lose any time and need to act quickly and decisively."
