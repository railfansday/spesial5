The Biggest Snubs and Surprises at the 2026 ARIA Awards

The 2026 ARIA Awards are in the books, the empties have been discarded and the hangovers (mostly) settled. With the benefit of hindsight, the Australian recording industry’s 40th annual ceremony at Sydney’s Hordern Pavilion on Wednesday (Nov. 20) was an appropriately large, and noisy, night for such an occasion.

Not for the first time, Kevin Parker was anointed the Australian music community’s boss. And Ecca Vandal deservedly emerged as a hero. For every hero, a villain. Step on up, Artificial Intelligence, the two dirty words on almost every artist’s lips. And for several talents, the night was a real bummer.

Kevin Parker’s psychedelic pop outfit was the big winner, his haul including album of the year for Tame Impala’s fifth studio album, Deadbeat; best solo artist and song of the year for “Dracula.” Tame Impala also won the second annual Global Impact Award presented by Spotify, a category that launched in 2025 and is designed to sit alongside the ARIA Hall of Fame.

This year’s ARIAs didn’t induct any legends into the ARIA Hall of Fame. That was done in June, when six acts entered the special club with a standalone Hall of Fame presentation. One of those inductees, the beloved rock trio Spiderbait, had the honors of closing the 2026 ARIA Awards with a medley of “Calypso,” “Buy Me A Pony” and “Black Betty.”

The broadcast opened with a proper surprise — Darren Hayes performing Savage Garden’s “Affirmation,” the title track from the world-conquering Brisbane pop duo’s second and final studio album. With the Olympic Games coming to the Sunshine State in 2032, surely Hayes will be thrown the mic for the Opening Ceremony, the Closing Ceremony, or both.

From Kevin Parker’s big night, to Genesis Owusu’s quiet one, Billboard rounds up the snubs and surprises from the 2026 ARIA Awards.

Kevin Parker winning ARIA Awards, even bunches of them, shouldn’t come as a surprise. What did was the Western Australian production maestro scooping the award for best solo act, for Tame Impala’s fifth studio album Deadbeat. Tame Impala is Kevin Parker — a one-man creation in the studio that grows into a fully-formed band for live performances. Parker won best group, for Tame Impala, in 2013, 2015, and 2020. Until this year, he’d never been nominated for best solo artist. “Hopefully once and for all,” he quipped during his victory speech, “everyone can accept and realize and not need to joke about it that Tame Impala is in fact just one guy.”

Like Tame Impala, Genesis Owusu had eight nominations for the 2026 ARIA Awards. No artist had more. But unlike Tame Impala, he went home empty handed. It was a strange old night for the Canberra-raised force of nature, who typically cleans-up on awards nights. Just last week, he collected triple j’s J Award for Australian album of the year, for Redstar Wu & The Worldwide Scourge, his third studio album, and doubled-up with the rage Australian music video of the year, winning for the clip to “STAMPEDE,” directed by Isaac Brown. His heaving collection of silverware includes the Australian Music Prize, the Vanda & Young Global Songwriting Competition, AIR Awards, and seven ARIAs. Owusu wasn’t absent from this year’s ARIAs. He got the blood running with a performance of “STAMPEDE,” and on Monday night kicked off ARIA Week with a headline performance of his showcase project Clubstar Wu for Spotify’s ARIA Artist Party.

It’s been a wicked year for Ecca Vandal, in the best possible way. There was her debut performance on U.S. late-night TV; her appearance at Coachella, marking her first-ever spot at a major U.S. festival; a sold-out tour of Australia; and earlier this month, the Australian Music Prize, for her second album Looking for People to Unfollow. Based in Melbourne, Australia, and born in South Africa of Sri Lankan heritage, Ecca Vandal’s fine work wasn’t missed by the ARIAs academy, which awarded her seven nominations. By night’s end, she had four pointy trophies, including the Michael Gudinski breakthrough artist and best independent release. She’ll enjoy that winning feeling for some time yet; Ecca Vandal has been tapped to open for Limp Bizkit on two upcoming international dates next month, in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia (Dec. 9) and Johannesburg, South Africa (Dec. 13). Her performance of “Dance in Debt” and “Cruising to Self Soothe” at the ARIAs kicked ass (watch below).

There are only so many ARIA Awards to go around (29 categories this year, down from 30). Not everyone will win. Some of the big names who failed to launch included Troye Sivan, Kylie Minogue, Paul Kelly, Keith Urban, and Hilltop Hoods. The Hoods might feel hardest done by, having completed their biggest ever arena tour of Australia, the Never Coming Home Tour, and extended their streak last year with Fall From the Light, their sixth consecutive No. 1 on the ARIA Albums Chart (and seventh in total). Then again, the Hoods, who were recently feted with a own lane in their hometown, Adelaide, don’t stress over such things. Their career collection of ARIA Awards is already in double figures, with 10.

Australia’s music community knows its enemy. On the eve of the ARIA Awards, the country’s creative and media industries united to insist the Federal government hold the line on copyright. And that AI platforms must get permission and agree payment, by way of a license, before even considering training on Australian creative work. The “Not AI’s To Take” campaign was everywhere at the ARIAs, on guitar pics distributed on industry tables, from the mouths of artists and presenters, including Elton John with a taped speech. “I have long admired the talent and creativity of Australian artists,” John said. “So I want to send an important message to the Australian government. You have a duty to protect Australian artists and their work. Don’t let AI use any Australian artist’s work without full permission. Please, protect the next generation of Australian artists. Thank you.”

ARIA’s CEO Annabelle Herd doubled down. “For more than a year, everyone in this room has held the line against some of the richest companies on the planet who want to train their AI on your music without asking,” Herd said from the stage. “Last month, we polled more than 1,500 Australians and 78% said the companies should have to ask permission before they use Australian creative work. Next week is parliament’s final week of sitting for the year, and before they leave for summer, every MP and senator needs to hear one message from this room. It’s not AIs to take. Every song that you’ve written has taken blood, sweat, and tears. And that’s what AI wants to train on without your permission, and it’s not theirs to take.”

Tash Sultana, the singer, songwriter and guitarist, used fewer words to get their point across, while presenting the independent category: “roses are red, violets are blue, if you support AI music, f— you.”

With their swagger, Britpop spirit, boisterous sing-along choruses, and frontman Tommy O’Dell’s impressive lungs, DMA’S has no shortage of fans. The band has cracked the U.K. in a big way, where three DMA’S albums have entered the top 10, including their latest, self-titled effort, which peaked at No. 9 in September. The LP, the first through a new deal with Wonderlick and Sony Music, gave the band a long-overdue No.1 on the ARIA Chart, following consecutive podium finishes. The wins piled up at the ARIAs, where DMA’S won a brace, for best group and best rock album, for what was a career night. Previously, the trio owned just one ARIA, for best group in 2023.
