Blend public cash and private innovation to unlock drug development

For decades, most countries have followed the same model of biomedical innovation. Basic research is undertaken mostly by universities and academia, and private industry leads the translation of research into health solutions1.

This approach has generated extraordinary drugs and therapies as well as economic success. In 2022, the pharmaceutical sector contributed US$2.3 trillion — or around 1.7% — to global gross domestic product (GDP) and supported 75 million jobs2.

However, market failures have been common. Companies have underinvested in developing therapeutics for common diseases with poorly understood biological mechanisms, such as depression, anxiety and kidney disease, because they consider them too commercially risky or unprofitable to pursue3.

To address this gap, we propose an adapted innovation model based on mission-driven public–private partnerships (PPPs), in which government acts as a leading strategic investor. By bringing together the best scientists in academia and industry and lowering risks in industry development in areas of public need and for diseases with limited understanding, this model would accelerate translation, advance health and increase economic returns.

Here, we outline how it would work. It will require systems to ensure affordable access to drugs and therapies, as well as revenue sharing between public and private partners to guarantee public benefits and to support investments in innovation.

Complex diseases that affect swathes of the population create persistent health and economic burdens. Yet, the current biomedical innovation model has proved limited in addressing evolving health needs. Because private investment is guided by assessments of risk and return, it tends to favour diseases with well-defined biological mechanisms and high commercial potential. Diseases that are biologically complex, varied or associated with uncertain markets remain underexplored.

For example, in 2023, more than 300 million people globally had depression. The condition will cost the global economy an estimated $12 trillion between 2025 and 20504. In the United States in 2024, one in five people reported that they had received a diagnosis of major depressive disorder during their lifetime, resulting in an economic burden of $334 billion annually across health-care, household-related and workforce costs5. Yet, the leading drug class for depression (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) has remained unchanged since the 1970s6.

Other chronic diseases impose similarly large burdens. For example, chronic kidney disease affects around 800 million people worldwide — nearly one in seven — and is projected to become the fifth leading cause of death globally by 2040 if current trends continue7,8. Yet, existing therapies cannot stem its toll on human lives.

Antimicrobial resistance is another example, which is associated with 4.7 million deaths globally each year and could contribute to 10 million deaths annually by 2050. Increased treatment costs for people with resistant infections amount to $1 trillion every year9. The antibiotic development pipeline has been depleted over time as economic factors render companies’ return on investment lower than that for other drug classes9. These factors include insufficient funding for research and challenging business models, given the short duration of antibiotic treatments and the need to reserve successful new antibiotics as drugs of last resort, to be used after all other options have failed.

Bringing a therapy to market from discovery can take 10–15 years and cost anywhere between $880 million and $2.3 billion10,11. The majority (90%) of drug candidates fail after entering clinical studies, clinical trials and drug-approval processes12. And companies won’t invest if they are unsure whether a drug will be profitable.

Consequently, many diseases and conditions have no or few available therapies or only modestly effective options13. What’s needed is a mission-driven strategy to guide scientific capabilities and industry development towards areas of public need by elucidating biological mechanisms and aligning incentives for diseases in which market forces alone are insufficient14.

We propose addressing this misalignment of incentives by reconceptualizing the government’s role in therapeutic advancement through a model of mission-driven PPP. Complementing existing grant-based systems, the model would position the government as a principal investor in select areas of therapeutic development.

The government would thus influence which areas of health and science receive concentrated attention and how successful outcomes are deployed for the public good. Such a model could address specific national needs and global challenges. Here are seven components.

Select disease targets. We propose the establishment of a government-appointed independent advisory body or commission tasked with identifying priority disease targets using transparent, evidence-based criteria, including disease burden, economic cost, scientific readiness and demonstrable market failure. Further criteria to gauge and consider public input into the selection process could be implemented to ensure public trust.

Set up collaborations. A national government, acting alone or as part of an international consortium, would assume a leading role in supporting basic research into biological mechanisms. It would do so by bringing together basic scientists from academia and translational experts from industry to reduce risks in translational development. Private industry would contribute expertise in drug development, clinical trials, regulatory strategy, manufacturing and commercialization.

Provide initial investments. The proposed PPP would not replace existing grant-based systems or market-driven innovation; rather, it would act as a complement by targeting disease areas in which market risks and incentives consistently fail to generate sufficient private investment. The approach could yield fresh therapies and reduce the cost of care to society while generating revenue from new products.

In the United States, we propose a federal investment of one billion dollars annually over ten years, directed to specific disease areas. That is less than 1.8% of total US government spending on biomedical research, which was $54.5 billion in 2024. This would be supplemented by smaller investments of 10–20% of one billion dollars from a consortium of philanthropic foundations and industry partners joining the initiative in response to a request for application. Given the high costs of bringing a new drug to market, a $10-billion total investment should provide the scale and time needed to pursue about three major disease targets10–12.

Feed revenues back in. Public return is a defining feature of the PPP. Existing biomedical funding structures are mainly grant making, with the implication that public benefit will emerge indirectly through the downstream health improvements and economic growth spurred by discovery. By contrast, this PPP framework would directly incorporate public accountability as well as returns.

The government would need to leverage its status as a majority investor to ensure access, fair pricing and affordability. It might require conditions such as agreements to ensure access. Pricing frameworks could incorporate a value-based approach to pricing, in which health insurers offer reimbursements determined by a drug’s value for patients. Affordability conditions, such as reasonable-pricing clauses, would also serve as powerful leverage.
