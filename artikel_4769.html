Marsha Linehan’s Bold Legacy Extends Far Beyond DBT

Posted November 12, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

This post was written with input from Ursula Whiteside, Ph.D., a former student of Marsha Linehan.

Earlier this week, the field lost an icon. Marsha Linehan, creator of dialectical behavior therapy, passed away in Seattle on November 10. She was 83.

Marsha earned her Ph.D. in experimental psychology from Loyola University and received her clinical training as a postdoctoral fellow at Stony Brook University. She then embarked on a storied clinical and academic career at the University of Washington, starting in 1977.

Most of the world knows Marsha the scientist. Some of us were lucky enough to know Marsha the person. One of my great career fortunes was to join the University of Washington faculty in 2000, where I got to know both.

Marsha was a true trailblazer. She formulated DBT, a treatment that’s helped thousands of people live more satisfying lives, during an era when women held little power in the social sciences. Marsha’s formidable personality, clinical acumen, and advanced training in experimental psychology and behavior modification all contributed to her success in creating DBT. None of these traits would have been sufficient, however, without her lived experience. Those of us who collaborated on Marsha’s research projects and attended her clinical teams saw this every day.

Marsha suffered from symptoms of the very condition she created DBT to treat. She knew, through subjective experience, the life challenges and acute despair that so often accompany mental health struggles. After ascending through the ranks of academia and establishing herself as an exemplary social scientist, Marsha described her mental illness in a 2011 New York Times article.

It’s nearly impossible to convey just how bold Marsha’s decision to reveal her personal experiences with mental illness was. As many of us know, revealing one’s own mental health issues is almost always disqualifying for careers in academia. Marsha herself waited 25 years after her first writings on DBT to do so.

Had Marsha come out before or during graduate school, or before she was an established leader in the field, her career would likely have ended before it began. Marsha, like so many of us, hid her mental illness for decades because it was necessary to be taken seriously in a field that stigmatizes and marginalizes its own. As I've discussed elsewhere, little has changed in this regard in the 15 years since Marsha came out.

In honoring Marsha’s legacy, those of us in the field should take a hard look in the mirror. Without Marsha’s lived experience informing her treatment development, the world would be without DBT, which has helped tens of thousands of adults and adolescents and saved many lives.

Do we really value lived experience, or do we acknowledge it performatively? Are we still writing off and marginalizing the subjective views of those with mental illness—whether inside or outside the field? Do we, through acts big and small, discourage or even prevent those with lived experience from becoming the deeply caring, transformative clinicians Marsha so boldly embodied?

A common refrain in the helping professions is that we’re not defined by our mental illness. The sentiment behind these statements is almost always sincere, and surely we’re not fully defined by our foibles or frailties. Yet if stigma were eradicated—something Marsha championed—there would be no need for such statements. We could embrace our mental illness, not reject it. These traits would be mere parts of us, not our foibles or frailties.

When a field devoted to treating mental illness marginalizes those who experience it, we promote stigma and shame from the very top. The message to individuals is to reject being defined by core, enduring parts ourselves. We reinforce negative views of mental illness within our ranks and among the public, and we promote and internalize shame. Although our intentions are just the opposite, we stigmatize yet again.

Marsha taught us another way. Let’s honor her bold legacy by carrying her life's work forward.
