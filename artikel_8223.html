Why Everyone Is Suddenly Talking About Van Deer Skis

Backed by Red Bull and fresh off Paula Moltzan’s first World Cup win, Van Deer is turning up in shops across North America. Here’s what we know about the skis, the price and the market. (Photo: Getty Images)

Overview: Van Deer is a ski brand created by racing legend Marcel Hirscher and majority-owned by Red Bull. Launched in 2021, it makes World Cup race, groomer, freeride and touring skis, priced from about $1,000 to $1,700. Paula Moltzan won her first World Cup race on Van Deer skis in Sölden. Distribution in North America is still limited, but the brand plans to open more storefronts this year.

If you watched the opening women’s World Cup GS in Sölden, Austria, you probably did a double take at Paula Moltzan’s skis. The U.S. racer won by 0.01 for her first career World Cup victory, and she did it on Van Deer-Red Bull Sports, a nascent brand she switched to this season. Van Deer is the brainchild of ski-racing GOAT Marcel Hirscher, and Moltzan’s win brought instant attention to a brand with a unique origin story.

The brand launched in 2021 and gained notoriety in 2022 with Norwegian Henrik Kristoffersen’s success on its skis, including a slalom crystal globe, an Olympic medal and a World Championship gold. Now Van Deer skis are suddenly turning up everywhere in places like Deer Valley and Vail—and even at big retailers like Evo. They’re not cheap: prices run from about $1,000 to $1,700, but it seems skiers are willing to pay. “Last year we had a phenomenal year with Van Deer skis,” said Billy Schoff, equipment buyer at Gorsuch, a high-end ski shop, which has sold them for three seasons. “We get people out on them once; they love them and then the word spreads like wildfire.”

So what is Van Deer, who are its skis for, and where does it fit in the North American market? Here’s what to know.

The details of Van Deer are deeply woven into what’s meaningful to Hirscher, who originally retired in 2019 (he came out of retirement in 2024, only to tear his ACL and be sidelined again). The brand’s name is a witty wordplay on his heritage—he’s half Dutch, half Austrian, and the German word “hirsch” means deer, hence: Van Deer. The H-Power 68 ski model nods to Hirscher’s Project 68, his initiative to get another win after his 67 career World Cup wins, even if he’s not the one racing anymore.

As a startup ski brand, it’s incredibly difficult to compete in a saturated ski market full of legacy brands, but along with Hirscher’s passion and precision, Van Deer has something other brands struggle with—resources. Hirscher was a Red Bull-sponsored athlete and maintains a strong relationship with the company. The partnership with Red Bull Sports, a multi-billion-dollar global brand, affords Van Deer-Red Bull Sports the ability to build their own factory stocked with state-of-the-art machines, hire top people to work for them, and support top athletes like Moltzan. (Red Bull has a 51 percent majority stake in Van Deer.)

“Marcel is obsessed with building the best-quality and best-selling skis,” said Schoff, who along with Dave Gorsuch was brought in early on Hirscher’s project. Moltzan sees the Red Bull effect from the athlete side: “Van Deer is still a new brand, so having Red Bull behind it makes a big difference. They provide a lot of support, which lets us focus on building the best skis we can.”

From the outside, Moltzan’s move looked like a risk. She had skied for Rossignol for 18 years and was coming off a season with four second-place World Cup finishes and an Olympic medal. “I know the change might look risky from the outside, but I was ready for it,” she said. “I was looking for new motivation and inspiration, and the chance to work with a team that has the ability and resources to build equipment specifically for me was a huge draw.”

She didn’t have a direct conversation with Hirscher before making the switch—they chatted briefly when she arrived at the factory. “I was going to be the only woman on the skis, and I knew there was a lot of work ahead of us, but that was also part of what made it exciting,” she said. “The first time I got on them, they felt completely different from anything I’d skied before, but I could immediately tell they were going to be good.”

Her husband and longtime serviceman, Ryan Mooney, was sold at the factory. “Ryan was really impressed when we toured the factory, the level of precision during the build process is certainly something that sets the brand apart from others,” Moltzan said. “He thought the powerful nature of the ski could be a great match for my style of skiing.”

Van Deer’s consumer skis are all unisex for now. On the race side, Moltzan says Van Deer didn’t start from an existing ski. “We’ve built completely new skis for the women’s side, so my feedback has been part of the process from the beginning,” she said, along with input from Mooney and Berni Arnitz, her second technician, who oversees women’s development. What makes them different? “Women’s skis are different from men’s skis, including the length, shape, and construction,” she said. “But I don’t think it needs to be more complicated than that.”

For now, finding the right ski comes down to length and model. “There are shorter sizes like 147 in H-Power 68 and 159 in H-Power 78,” says Max Wingard, Van Deer-Red Bull Sports race and promotions manager. “The SL model is the ski we’d sell as an easier to ski slalom ski. The GS Pro could be a masters ski, but comes in longer lengths.”

We haven’t had a chance to test the skis ourselves yet, but according to those who’ve tried them, they’re not just for racers. “I think the best customer for Van Deer is someone who really loves skiing and cares about their equipment,” Moltzan said. “You don’t have to be a racer. There are skis for everything from race courses to touring, but they’re built for people who want high performance and appreciate the details that go into a great pair of skis.”

Eric Lipton, National Alpine Team alumnus and director of Destination Ski Camps, skied the HP-78 and described it as “predictable and stable at speed.” “I’d describe the skis as having a race ski heart and soul in a consumer package,” he said.

The North American hardgoods market is a different animal from Europe’s piste-focused market, and Van Deer is making a clear play for more traction in the U.S. According to Schoff, the brand plans to build a North American warehouse, and it has recently hired George Couperthwait, formerly of Stöckli, to help build the brand and its dealer network here.

Schoff sees the target clearly. “I spent time in Austria with Leon (Hirscher’s brother, formerly of Atomic who now works on product for Van Deer) and his father, Ferdinand,” he said. “They were obsessed with building unbelievable race products and now the obsession includes commercial skis. They are doing it the old craftsman way, but with new machines that put on the best finish and even rip the sidewalls back. The quality of construction and finish is top quality and it’s clear they are going after high-end brands like Stöckli.”

Distribution is still limited, says Wingard, but the goal this year is to open more storefronts and grow the relationship with their existing retailers as well.

Wingard, who previously worked for Marker Völkl Dalbello and Stöckli, thinks Van Deer will catch on in both the East and the West. The line includes multiple narrow-waisted skis tailor-made for East Coast conditions. Out West, Van Deer will be pushing the Freeride 98 and 108, with former World Cup racer brand ambassador Travis Ganong giving feedback on the series and stores like Gorsuch in Vail, Aspen and Park City on board.

As a ski coach and former racer, I have a fondness for racey skis in a consumer package. You can feel the high-end construction and finish right out of the box, even in the way the bases glide on snow. A close comparison is Stöckli. For years, Stöckli skis have been a favorite among ski instructors, because skiers who focus on carving, like me, tend to gravitate toward skis with rebound energy, edge grip and stability. Van Deer shares Stöckli’s high cost and a degree of exclusivity, but the top-grade race materials and hand-tuning steps are meant to justify the price.

Van Deer’s advantage is that it has pulled together European craftsmanship, enticing long-time legacy-brand employees to come aboard, with state-of-the-art ski technology like the Wintersteiger Jupiter tuning machine and a brand-new factory, thanks to Red Bull’s resources.

All models except the Freeride 108 (which has some carbon) and the Touring series have a game-on construction: a double layer of Titanal and a woodcore of poplar, ash or beech. The Van Deer models available this season best suit strong intermediate to expert skiers who can drive a ski through a turn transition and want the ski to initiate a turn without too much effort, while still feeling edge grip underfoot and acceleration out of the turn.

You don’t have to ski like Paula Moltzan to appreciate a pair of Van Deers, but perhaps you aspire to turn like she does.

Current models are available online as well as at speciality retailers. Skiers interested in demoing or buying a pair can find a dealer on the company website. As for Moltzan, she’s racing around the globe, with Levi, Finland, next, followed by Killington, Vermont. As good as the skis already are, she says, they’re still a work in progress: “Skis are always evolving, so there will definitely be more changes to come.”
