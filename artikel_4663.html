Gen Z, Careers, and Money: Navigating an Uncertain Future

You've probably heard the advice: Work hard, build a career, and save for the future. But how much does that reflect the working world you're navigating?

The employment landscape keeps changing, with AI introducing new uncertainty about which jobs will exist, which skills will matter, and what building a career will require. Meanwhile, the income you are working toward may not seem sufficient to afford the life that work is supposed to make possible, making your future feel uncertain too.

You may have watched others devote years to an employer and still lose their jobs. Perhaps you have pursued an education, developed skills, and taken your career seriously, only to encounter limited opportunities or disappointing pay.

Dedication and hard work do not ensure an employer’s loyalty, and careful career decisions cannot always protect you from economic shifts.

It makes sense to reconsider how much of your identity and self-worth you want to tie to productivity, professional status, or earnings. Losing a job or struggling to earn enough can have significant practical and emotional consequences, but these experiences do not mean that you lack ability, have not tried hard enough, or are worth less as a person.

If homeownership, a wedding, having kids, or other traditional milestones feel financially out of reach, making sacrifices for them can feel less worthwhile. How much enjoyment are you giving up for something you may never be able to afford? Some milestones may not appeal to you anyway.

A concert with friends or a long-anticipated trip offers something you can experience now, without sacrificing for a future that may not be attainable.

However, this reasoning can shift into financial fatalism: If the future is so uncertain, what is the point of saving or planning at all?

Notice the all-or-nothing thinking. Not being able to guarantee a particular future does not mean you have no influence over what happens next.

Meet John*, whose story reflects experiences we encounter among young adults in our practices.

John followed the conventional roadmap to success: solid grades, a four-year degree in a field that promised job security, extracurriculars, internships, and networking. Yet, he finds himself struggling to enter the workforce.

After submitting hundreds of applications, John is exhausted and increasingly discouraged. When he does land an interview, it may be with an AI interviewer, followed by being “ghosted” afterward. Still living at home with his parents, he feels stuck in a cycle with no clear way forward. He begins to wonder, “Why did I even go to college?”

For John, buying a concert ticket or treating himself to an iced coffee offers more than momentary pleasure. Choosing to spend money, and deciding what to spend it on, provides a sense of autonomy and control amid so much uncertainty. Enjoying his coffee is an escape before the slog of yet another job application. Having fun at a concert with friends lets him live in the present, instead of always feeling weighed down by the pressures of building a life and career.

Let’s be clear: An occasional iced coffee now is not why homeownership might be out of reach later. At the same time, relatively small amounts saved and invested when you are young can grow by compounding over decades in ways that are difficult to fully appreciate today. Similarly, what will be within financial reach (and what you will want to purchase) in the future is hard to predict today. Debt can also build over time, as balances accumulate and interest or fees add to what you owe, creating significant financial strain.

As financial psychologists, we empathize with John's frustration while also recognizing an important tension: The very spending behaviors that help young adults cope with feelings of powerlessness in the short term may begin to create a pattern of spending (and even a sense of financial identity) that will not be helpful in the long term.

Money set aside might help you move, change careers, or leave an unhealthy workplace. Planning can help you prepare for needs and choices that differ from the milestones you originally imagined.

Of course, if your income barely covers necessities, saving may not be possible. That financial constraint should not be treated as a failure of discipline or willingness to plan.

Spending on a nice dinner or vacation with friends can be enjoyable. But how much of its meaning comes from the setting, and how much comes from having time to connect?

If connection is important to you, there may be other less costly ways to experience it: cooking together, taking a regular walk, or simply making time to talk.

Investing time in friendships, family, and community can support both your present and your future. You can enjoy those relationships now while building connections that deepen and provide support through life’s changes.

There is no easy answer to how much you should invest in your career, save for later, or spend today. Your circumstances matter, and the balance may change over time.
