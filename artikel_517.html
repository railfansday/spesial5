After 8 years in space, a groundbreaking X-ray telescope finally opens its eyes

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

After eight years in space and a journey covering 10 billion kilometers, a pioneering X-ray telescope traveling to Mercury has captured its first observations of a distant cosmic object. The instrument, built in the UK, successfully detected X-rays from the Crab Nebula, one of the brightest X-ray sources in the sky, marking an important milestone for the European Space Agency (ESA) and Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency's BepiColombo mission.

Scientists at the University of Leicester are now evaluating the Mercury Imaging X-ray Spectrometer (MIXS), which is traveling aboard the two-spacecraft mission. Their initial results are highly encouraging, arriving just as BepiColombo prepares to enter orbit around Mercury on November 21.

The telescope has spent eight years traveling through space, making it essential to confirm that its delicate components are still working properly. Although engineers monitored MIXS throughout the journey, the instrument could not look directly into space until September. That opportunity came after the Mercury Transfer Module (MTM) separated from the two science orbiters, the Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO) and Mercury Magnetosphere Orbiter (MMO) or Mio.

With its view finally unobstructed, MIXS could begin the critical tests needed before the spacecraft enters its planned orbit around the solar system's smallest planet.

A Telescope's First Look Across 6,500 Light-Years

For its first astronomical target, the team selected the Crab Nebula, located approximately 6,500 light-years from Earth. This well-studied remnant of an exploded star produces a strong, predictable X-ray signal, making it an ideal reference for testing the telescope's performance.

By examining how MIXS observes this distant object, researchers can determine whether the instrument still performs as expected after its lengthy journey. They can also compare the results with laboratory measurements collected in 2015, when the telescope was last tested on Earth.

The main observation campaign took place on September 11 and 12. Over 39 hours, the spacecraft carried out 55 carefully controlled changes in orientation to collect the data.

One important measurement was the telescope's angular resolution, which describes its ability to distinguish small details in an image. Scientists determined this by examining how widely the Crab Nebula's X-ray signal spread across the detector. Remarkably, MIXS delivered slightly sharper resolution than it had during its laboratory tests in 2015.

That improvement is particularly encouraging because the instrument will eventually use its X-ray vision to investigate the composition of Mercury's surface.

Better Than Expected After Eight Years in Space

Professor Emma Bunce, Principal Investigator (PI) for MIXS and Director of the Institute for Space at the University of Leicester, welcomed the results as a major achievement for the international team responsible for developing the instrument.

Professor Bunce said: "We have waited a long time to make measurements with MIXS and it is fantastic to see it operating brilliantly in space. This is testament to our engineering and technical team, and our international partners who we have worked with over many years to build this incredible instrument. 80% of the technology on MIXS is new!

"During our calibration observations we have determined that MIXS is generally performing fantastically well in space! We have no effects in our images that are associated with 'stray light' (optical photons getting in when they should not). We continue to see effects of galactic cosmic rays as we did during cruise, but we understand these and can filter them out. We see some self-fluorescence which appears when the Sun is illuminating the optic, but this won't affect our dayside observations of Mercury's surface. And the detector has suffered no radiation damage after 8 years in space.

"We see some effects in the instrument that we are still studying and expect to undertake some optimization procedures during the Mercury commissioning phase to fine tune MIXS in preparation for the science phase."

The results suggest that the telescope has weathered its long exposure to the harsh space environment exceptionally well. Engineers have found no unwanted visible light interfering with its images, and the effects of high-energy cosmic rays are understood well enough to be removed during data processing.

The team has also identified a small amount of X-ray emission originating within the instrument itself when sunlight strikes its optics. However, this effect is not expected to interfere with observations of Mercury's sunlit surface. Most importantly, the detector shows no signs of radiation damage after eight years in space.

Some remaining effects still require investigation, and scientists expect to make further adjustments after the spacecraft reaches Mercury to ensure that MIXS is ready for its full scientific mission.

How X-Rays Will Reveal Mercury's Hidden Chemistry

BepiColombo is designed to investigate Mercury in unprecedented detail. As the smallest and one of the least explored rocky planets in our solar system, Mercury offers scientists a rare opportunity to understand how planets form and change under the extreme conditions found close to a star.

One of the mission's major goals is to uncover the planet's chemical composition and geological history. These findings could help explain Mercury's unusual characteristics while providing broader insights into the formation and evolution of the solar system.

MIXS, carried aboard the Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO), will play a central role in this investigation. Developed by a European consortium led by the University of Leicester, it draws on the university's long history of X-ray astronomy research.

It is the first imaging X-ray telescope to operate on a planetary science mission. Working alongside other scientific instruments, MIXS will help produce a global map showing the elements that make up Mercury's surface, revealing its atomic composition in greater detail than ever before. It is also the only UK-led scientific instrument aboard BepiColombo and receives support from the UK Space Agency.

The instrument works by detecting fluorescent X-rays released by rocks on Mercury's surface. When powerful X-rays from the Sun strike these rocks, they can cause atoms within the material to emit X-rays of their own. Different elements produce characteristic X-ray signals, allowing scientists to identify what the surface is made of without collecting physical samples.

MIXS will make these measurements with assistance from the Solar Intensity X-ray and particle Spectrometer (SIXS), which monitors the solar radiation and particles reaching Mercury.

The telescope will also examine X-rays emitted from Mercury's nighttime surface when charged particles associated with the planet's magnetic environment strike it. These measurements could reveal important connections between Mercury's surface and the surrounding space environment.

A 10 Billion Kilometer Journey With a Personal Legacy

For Dr. Adrian Martindale, MIXS Instrument Scientist at the University of Leicester's School of Physics and Astronomy, the successful observations represent the culmination of more than two decades of work.

Martindale joined the project in 2003 while still an undergraduate student and has been involved in nearly every aspect of the instrument's development.

Dr. Martindale said: "BepiColombo, and MIXS in particular, are like part of my family. I've been involved since I was an undergraduate and touched almost every part of the instrument before it was launched.

"This first data is mind-blowing to me. The last time we could see through this telescope it was in the basement under the School of Physics and Astronomy in Leicester, but after a rocket launch, eight years in space and 10 billion kilometers traveled it looks just the same. I can't wait to see the science data it produces when we get into orbit."

The milestone also carries special meaning for the team because MIXS represents the scientific legacy of Professor George Fraser. Fraser was the instrument's original Principal Investigator and the driving force behind its innovative optical design before his death in March 2014.

Professor Bunce added: "We are thinking of George as we operate MIXS fully in space for the first time and know that he would be very, very proud of the team and delighted at our first light observations."

BepiColombo Prepares for Its Final Arrival at Mercury

BepiColombo began its arrival phase on September 3, when the Mercury Transfer Module (MTM) separated from the spacecraft assembly.

The remaining connected spacecraft are scheduled to enter orbit around Mercury on November 21. The two science orbiters, MPO and Mio, are then expected to separate on December 9 to 10, allowing them to prepare for their respective scientific operations.

The mission's primary science phase is scheduled to begin in April 2027. Once underway, BepiColombo will investigate Mercury's surface, internal structure, magnetic field, and surrounding environment, helping scientists piece together the history of a planet that remains surprisingly mysterious despite its proximity to the Sun.

For the MIXS team, the successful first observations of the Crab Nebula offer an early indication of what the instrument may accomplish when it finally turns its attention to Mercury.
