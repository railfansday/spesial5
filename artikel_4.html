Live Updates: Trump’s Whirlwind Asia Trip Begins in the Philippines

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

President Trump’s 48-hour trip will start with a meeting with Southeast Asian leaders in Manila. He will later meet Xi Jinping of China and Vladimir Putin of Russia in Shenzhen, a Chinese economic hub.

President Trump landed in Manila on Tuesday to begin a 48-hour Asia trip in which he will meet with Xi Jinping of China and other world leaders, with an unpredictable agenda that could include the future of American tariffs, nuclear arms control, the war in Ukraine and U.S. restrictions on advanced A.I. chips.

Mr. Trump was scheduled to meet with President Ferdinand R. Marcos Jr. of the Philippines and attend a gathering of Southeast Asian leaders. After less than 24 hours in Manila, he will head to the APEC economic summit in Shenzhen, China, where he was expected to meet with Mr. Xi and President Vladimir V. Putin of Russia.

The brief itinerary appears to reflect Mr. Trump’s preferences. He dislikes big summits where he often is not the sole subject of attention. And he enjoys meeting with leaders of powerful nations — especially Mr. Xi and Mr. Putin, two authoritarian leaders he often talks about in admiring terms.

Mr. Trump will be meeting Mr. Xi for the third time this year, and a three-way meeting between China, Russia and the United States would be the first of its kind.

On Tuesday, Mr. Trump caught the end of a dayslong meeting of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, or ASEAN, after issuing tariffs on countries across the region, even allies. Those moves added to a sentiment in Asia that the United States was not a reliable partner but rather one that treasured its commercial interests over its security interests. The only event of substance on the president’s schedule on Tuesday — signing what he had called a “trade deal” with Thailand — did not happen because Mr. Trump said it was still being finalized.

Mr. Trump met with leaders from the so-called Quad — the United States, India, Japan and Australia — soon after his arrival. All three partners are smarting from high energy prices triggered by the Iran war, a conflict Mr. Trump never discussed with them before launching it in late February.

In Shenzhen — the heart of China’s robotics and artificial intelligence industries — Mr. Trump will pursue détente with Mr. Xi. He needs to extend a soon-expiring trade truce that limited tariffs on Chinese goods entering the United States in return for a Chinese commitment not to cut off rare earths and magnets.

Then comes the main event: the meeting with Mr. Xi and Mr. Putin. Referring to the trilateral meeting, Mr. Trump told reporters before leaving Washington: “We are going to be talking about denuclearization, a tough subject because it is very hard for any of us to say we’ll get rid of our nukes.” Chinese officials have made clear they are not interested in nuclear talks until their arsenal is roughly the size of the American one.

Mr. Trump also said, “We will talk about Iran a little,” insisting the situation was in “very good shape.” Analysts say it is unlikely that the meeting will make any progress on Iran, with Russia happy to see energy prices elevated since the war began, and China eager to see them rapidly decline.

North Korea: Mr. Putin could meet with Kim Jong-un if the North Korean leader also travels to China for the APEC summit, Russian state news agencies reported, citing the Kremlin’s spokesman. It was not clear whether Mr. Kim would attend the summit.

Ukraine: Mr. Trump also said he would talk about Ukraine, on which Mr. Putin’s aides are engaging in talks with the U.S. and Ukrainian officials, but also renewing demands that NATO pull back its troops to where they were before the alliance expanded toward Russia.

President Vladimir V. Putin of Russia could meet with Kim Jong-un if the North Korean leader travels to China for the APEC summit this week, the Kremlin said, reflecting the deepening of ties between their countries in recent years.

Asked about the possibility of a meeting, Dmitri S. Peskov, the Kremlin spokesman, said on Monday that it would “of course” happen if Mr. Kim went to Shenzhen, the Chinese economic hub and host city, according to Russian state news agencies. North Korea has not confirmed whether Mr. Kim plans to attend the summit.

Mr. Putin and Mr. Kim have publicly met three times since 2022, and North Korea has been a key supporter of Russia’s military actions in Ukraine.

As the war in Ukraine nears its fifth anniversary, with no end in sight, the Kremlin is expected to use Mr. Putin’s many meetings at the APEC summit to showcase how Russia is regarded as one of the major international powers alongside the United States and China.

A visit by Mr. Kim would be a rare foray outside North Korea for the leader, and it would further raise the prospect that he could meet with President Trump in China. On the first day of his 48-hour trip to Asia, Mr. Trump told reporters that Mr. Kim “would like” to come to the APEC summit. The president has repeatedly said he would like to meet with Mr. Kim again.

On Wednesday, Mr. Putin is scheduled to meet with Xi Jinping, China’s leader. On Thursday, he is set to take part in a trilateral summit with Mr. Xi and Mr. Trump, the first meeting of its kind. The Kremlin said on Monday that it was working to arrange a bilateral meeting between Mr. Putin and Mr. Trump.

Before his departure for China, Mr. Putin met on Monday in the Kremlin with Jo Yong-won, a secretary of North Korea’s ruling Workers’ Party. During the meeting, Mr. Putin said that North Korea’s help in driving Ukrainian forces from the Kursk region of Russia last year “marked a new chapter in the history of military cooperation between our countries.”

Moscow planned to unveil a monument in Kursk by the end of the year to honor the North Korean troops, Mr. Putin said.

During the final meeting of the day, with ASEAN leaders, President Trump told President Ferdinand R. Marcos Jr. of the Philippines:  “I don’t want to date myself, but I knew your parents very well. They were great. I believe in genetics. They did a good job. You have very good genetics, I can tell you that.”

It was not the first time Mr. Trump has praised the family of Mr. Marcos, who is the son of the dictator Ferdinand E. Marcos, who ruled the Philippines for 20 years, imposed martial law and was ousted in 1986.

The only event of substance on President Trump’s schedule today, the signing of a trade deal between the United States and Thailand, did not happen because it was still being finalized. Trump told reporters that “Thailand will eliminate all tariffs on U.S. imports” and that the United States would provide tariff relief, but the two sides signed only a memorandum about the agreement. The president said the deal would be done in the coming weeks.

During an address to Quad leaders, President Trump called Prime Minister Narendra Modi of India, which is a member of the group, “a great leader and a friend of mine, highly respected by everybody all over the world.” The president also praised Modi as he stood with other leaders and took a few questions, calling him a “very tough man.”

India and the United States are navigating a rocky patch in their relationship, largely tied to trade and India’s purchases of Russian oil. Although Modi and Trump signed an interim framework for a trade deal in February, talks have dragged on, frustrating both parties.

The Nvidia turmoil and its implications for the U.S.-China relationship is a backdrop to President Trump’s visit. On Tuesday, shares of technology companies in Asia tumbled after the Trump administration announced rules to restrict overseas access to advanced A.I. chips like those sold by Nvidia. In Hong Kong trading, Alibaba fell 5 percent, while Tencent’s stock dropped nearly 4 percent. The Chinese A.I. firms MiniMax Group fell 7 percent, and Z.AI Co. declined 5 percent.

In the United States, tech stocks stabilized from a sell-off the day before. Nvidia shares were up about 1 percent before the start of regular trading in New York. The company is set to report its latest financial results later on Tuesday.

President Trump is expected to meet with Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul of Thailand on Tuesday in Manila to sign what the White House has described as a “trade deal.”

But Thailand’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs said in a statement on Tuesday that Bangkok was expecting to agree only on a “joint statement marking progress in negotiations.”

The dissonance comes amid growing signs within ASEAN, the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, that the United States is not a reliable partner. The meeting between Mr. Trump and Mr. Anutin is taking place on the sidelines of an ASEAN summit.

For more than a year, Washington and Bangkok have been negotiating a new trade agreement that covers items like tariffs, food imports and labor rights.

Last year, the United States battered Thailand with a 36 percent tariff, one of the steepest rates levied by Mr. Trump after his return to office. The United States is one of Thailand’s largest export markets, including for goods like pet food.

Mr. Trump later reduced the tariffs to 19 percent, before the U.S. Supreme Court struck them down in February. The administration eventually found a way to still impose 12.5 percent tariffs on Thai goods.

The signing of a U.S.-Thai trade deal might still be far off, requiring Thailand’s parliament to pass a law, but the meeting delivers an easy publicity win for Mr. Anutin and Mr. Trump, who is known to revel even in perceived success.

Pavida Pananond, an economist at Thammasat Business School in Bangkok, said, “The level of trust Thailand has in the U.S. is not at the same level as before all this economic weaponization of tariffs.”

After handing over the chairmanship of ASEAN to Singapore, President Ferdinand R. Marcos Jr. of the Philippines held a news conference. “President Trump has just arrived,” he said. “He is waiting for us to go to him and to invite him to this really very informal kind of summit.”

Mr. Marcos ended his comments by apologizing for not taking more questions from journalists. “I cannot keep the president of the United States waiting,” he said. “Sorry.”

At his hotel, President Trump sat down for a meeting with Quad leaders and took a few questions. He said that he was “not worried” about bank outages and that the United States would be “looking into” reports of plans by the Iranians to drop a dirty bomb in Washington. He also said that Kim Jong-un, the North Korean leader, would “like to come” to the APEC summit in Shenzhen.

Several hundred activists massed on Tuesday in central Manila to march to the Philippine International Convention Center, the venue for the Association of Southeast Asian Nations summit.

By the time President Trump landed in the city, the ranks of the protesters had swelled to thousands.

Most of the demonstrators were university students who said that they were fed up with the government, which they said was more focused on preserving its relationship with the United States than helping impoverished Filipinos.

Cinna Sison, 18, who studies European languages at the University of the Philippines, said they were inspired by the recent mass protests in France.

“I love the French,” she said. “They know how to protest.”

Ms. Sison said she had worn colorful makeup to mark her first time attending a demonstration. “I am here to show my solidarity with fellow young activists. We are here to show our disgust to the fascist Trump and to Marcos for kowtowing to his every wish,” she said, referring to President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. of the Philippines.

Mr. Trump is expected to hold a bilateral meeting with Mr. Marcos on Wednesday before he heads to China for an Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit.

The Philippine president, who earlier this year described Washington as a “steadfast partner,” has said little publicly about meeting Mr. Trump. His administration has hinted that the two sides are working on a military aid agreement.

In Manila, Ms. Sison wore a red scarf on her head and a red shirt with the words “Silicone not Silica” — a reference to Pax Silica, a U.S.-led consortium of countries aiming to create artificial intelligence supply chains independent of China.

Ms. Sison added that Pax Silica was a symbolic return of the Americans to the Philippines, the site of former U.S. military bases, as Washington leaned on a regional ally to counter China.

“Neighborliness is important, but it is not important to be subservient to U.S. interests,” she said.

Nearby, Raja Bausing, a 22-year-old visual artist, was busy putting the finishing touches on a papier-mâché model of Trump holding Marcos.

“We want to show how Trump is a warmonger, while Marcos is a lap dog,” she said. “They are very disrespectful to Filipinos. Trump was welcomed in a grand way and yet we Filipinos are maltreated.”

President Trump landed in Manila on Tuesday, beginning a trip to Asia in which he will attend an ASEAN summit, greet members of the Quad, then travel to China for an APEC meeting.

Here’s what those acronyms mean, as well as some highlights from previous summits — including gold crowns and awkward handshakes.

The Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum is a group of 21 member economies, most of which are from that region. Its members also include Canada, Mexico, Australia, Russia and the United States. It promotes trade in Asia, but is not a free trade bloc. Its leaders meet annually, with the location rotating each year.

Meetings between world leaders on the sidelines of the summit can often overshadow the main proceedings. This year, in the Chinese tech hub of Shenzhen, Mr. Trump is expected to meet with President Xi Jinping of China and President Vladimir V. Putin of Russia, as well as with Mr. Xi separately.

Last year, when Mr. Trump visited the South Korean city of Gyeongju during the week of the APEC meeting, President Lee Jae Myung of South Korea gave him a replica of a gold crown that researchers believe was worn by an ancient Korean ruler. Mr. Trump also met with Mr. Xi in the nearby city of Busan, where the two struck a truce in their countries’ trade war.

A notable incident happened in Sydney, Australia, in 2007, when a group of local television comedians brought an Osama bin Laden look-alike through security. The team came within yards of a hotel where President George W. Bush was staying.

The Association of Southeast Asian Nations is a grouping of 11 countries — Brunei, Cambodia, Indonesia, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar, the Philippines, Singapore, Thailand, East Timor and Vietnam — which promotes political cooperation and stability across the region. Its leaders meet twice annually.

The bloc was formed in 1967 and represents nearly 700 million people. Its economy and its role in global markets have strengthened in recent years. The United States also sees ASEAN as a geostrategic bulwark against China.

In 2009, an ASEAN meeting in Thailand fell into acrimony when Secretary of State Hillary Clinton compared North Korea’s leaders to “small children” when discussing their nuclear program. The country’s leadership, in turn, described Ms. Clinton as a “schoolgirl” and a “pensioner.”

During the 2017 ASEAN summit in Manila, Mr. Trump grimaced while jostling with Southeast Asian leaders for an awkward cross-armed handshake that was captured in a group photograph.

The Quadrilateral Security Dialogue is an informal partnership between Australia, India, Japan and the United States, which was formed in part to counter China. It was proposed by Prime Minister Shinzo Abe of Japan in 2007.

Beijing opposed the initial discussion, lodging formal diplomatic protests. Australia withdrew in 2008 and the dialogue was put on hold for some time. But the grouping was revived in 2017 on the sidelines of an ASEAN conference.

President Biden was the first U.S. president to convene a meeting among the leaders of the nations as a foursome. In 2024, he hosted the Quad leaders at his home in Wilmington, Del. Mr. Trump has appeared to be less enthusiastic about the partnership. Last year, he did not attend the Quad summit in India.

Reporting from the village of Manabayucan in the Philippines and the capital, Manila

In May, Roseller Rodriguez, a mayor in the Philippines, was startled to learn from news reports that U.S. officials had arrived in his district to start an artificial intelligence hub.

He saw a video of senior American and Filipino officials standing on a makeshift stage, unveiling a new initiative called Pax Silica.

Mr. Rodriguez had not been invited. Nor had he ever heard about the hub. Was it a data center? Where would the water and electricity come from? What would happen to the farmers living there?

Months later, he says he has few answers.

“I’m not concerned with the geopolitics of Pax Silica,” Mr. Rodriguez said in his office in the municipality of Capas. “My problem is Pax Silica will be built in our community.”

The United States started Pax Silica to unite partners around A.I. supply chains independent of China. The initiative is part of President Trump’s effort to counter Beijing’s economic and technological influence.

Trump administration officials have cast it as a 21st-century successor to Pax Americana, the era of relative peace among major powers after World War II.

The significance of Pax Silica, which 26 signatories have joined, has become heightened after revelations that Nvidia’s former chief executive, Jensen Huang, was targeted by Chinese intelligence, exposing the growing geopolitical stakes of the global A.I. race.

In the Philippines, America’s oldest ally in Asia, the backlash has been fierce.

Dozens of Filipino performers greeted President Trump as he descended the stairs of Air Force One onto the tarmac of Ninoy Aquino Airport in Manila, waving flags and playing “Y.M.C.A.” by the Village People on drums and trumpets.

President Vladimir V. Putin could meet with Kim Jong-un if the North Korean leader also travels to China for the APEC summit this week, Russian state news agencies reported, citing Dmitri S. Peskov, the Kremlin’s spokesman. Before his departure for China, Putin met on Monday with a secretary of North Korea’s ruling Workers’ Party. It was not yet clear whether the North Korean leader would attend the summit.

President Trump has landed in Manila, lingering on the tarmac for a welcome ceremony before walking alone to Marine One. He has several events tonight, but he will not leave his hotel for them. A meeting with quad leaders, a U.S.-Thailand trade deal signing and a dinner will all be held at the resort where the president is staying. In other words, he showed up late to this summit, and the summit will be coming to him.

Thousands of protesters have assembled in downtown Manila, chanting in Tagalog, “Trump leave the Philippines.” They are opposed to his Pax Silica A.I. supply chain initiative.

President Trump’s stopover at the Association of Southeast Asian Nations summit is part of a state visit to the Philippines, where he will be received with a 21-gun salute.

The last time he met President Ferdinand R. Marcos Jr. of the Philippines, was full of praise for the Filipino leader.

“Great family, great family legacy and highly respected in his country,” Mr. Trump said while hosting Mr. Marcos at the White House last year.

But that legacy was long considered an obstacle to Mr. Marcos’s political ambitions. He is the son of the dictator Ferdinand E. Marcos, who ruled the Philippines for 20 years, imposed martial law and was ousted in 1986 by a nonviolent uprising. The elder Marcos died in exile in Hawaii, without facing trial on U.S. criminal charges that he had plundered the Philippine Treasury. Years later, a court found his estate liable for torture, summary executions and disappearances.

The younger Marcos spent years rehabilitating his family’s image and won election in 2022. He is hosting Mr. Trump at his official residence, Malacañang Palace, which was also his childhood home.

Mr. Marcos and his wife, Liza Marcos, will receive Mr. Trump on Wednesday with a 21-gun salute. Mr. Trump is expected to sign the palace guest book, hold closed-door meetings, pose for a handshake, then attend a state luncheon held in his honor.

It is not clear whether Mr. Trump, who has a well-known affinity for autocratic leaders, will meet with Mr. Marcos’s mother, Imelda, 97, with whom he has socialized in New York. Ms. Marcos wielded immense power when her husband was president and later came to be known for her flamboyant lifestyle, shoe collection and unexplained wealth.

Mr. Marcos has said that when he called to congratulate Mr. Trump on his election victory in November 2024, Mr. Trump asked about Imelda Marcos.

“My mother is really his friend, he knows my mother very well,” Mr. Marcos said, relaying the conversation. “He was asking about her, ‘How’s Imelda?’ I told him she sends her congratulations.”

After the pomp and circumstance at Malacañang, which traces its roots to the Spanish colonial era in the Philippines, Mr. Trump is headed to Shenzhen, China. The highlight of that leg of his trip will be a trilateral meeting with two strongmen: President Xi Jinping of China and President Vladimir V. Putin of Russia.

For the second year in a row, President Trump is attending the annual gathering of Southeast Asian leaders, a show of alliance to a region that the United States sees as a strategic and maritime counterweight to China.

But Mr. Trump reached the gathering, held this year in Manila, only on Tuesday, just as the summit was wrapping up, and he is not expected to make any significant policies or diplomacy moves.

Mixed signals like this — to say nothing of Mr. Trump’s punishing tariffs — are adding to a widespread belief within ASEAN, the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, that the United States is not a reliable partner. That leaves the bloc balancing between Washington and Beijing, with which many of its members have deep trade partnerships.

“They’ve learned that the United States is not reliable, so a lot of the hedging has been away from the United States,” said Ted Osius, a former U.S. ambassador to Vietnam.

The Philippines, America’s oldest security ally in Asia, provides a window into this balancing act.

After taking office three years ago, President Ferdinand R. Marcos Jr. reversed his predecessor’s tilt toward China and pivoted back toward the United States. That stance contributed to many tense encounters between Filipino and Chinese vessels in the South China Sea, where Manila and Beijing have competing territorial claims.

As U.S. patrols in the South China Sea have dropped, Mr. Marcos has appeared to be more conciliatory toward Beijing. In August, he said that there was a “distinct possibility” that the Philippines and China could partner on oil and gas exploration in those waters despite their territorial feud.

“Marcos Jr. already has begun a shift toward China, one that a single visit by Trump is unlikely to stop,” Joshua Kurlantzick, a senior fellow for Southeast Asia and South Asia at the Council on Foreign Relations, wrote last week.

On Sunday, China and ASEAN failed to agree on a code of conduct in the South China Sea — something the Philippines, which has some of the biggest stakes in the dispute, had vowed to secure while chairing the bloc this year.

Chester Cabalza, president of International Development and Security Cooperation, a Philippine think tank, said, “Strategically, Manila opens its doors for conciliation with Beijing to stabilize normal ties. But on the ground, the real battle has yet to subside.”

Domestically, Mr. Marcos has to walk a tightrope, too.

“For Filipino fishermen and coastal communities, the South China Sea is not simply about a geopolitical dispute. It concerns livelihoods, access to maritime resources and national dignity,” said Arnaud Leveau, president of the Asia Centre, a Paris-based think tank. Any diplomatic accommodation perceived as sacrificing Filipino interests, he said, would therefore be politically risky.

The wider region has been battered by the consequences of another Trump policy: the war in Iran. Southeast Asia depends heavily on oil and gas imports from the Middle East and was roiled by soaring prices of fuel. In the Philippines, crops rotted in fields because farmers could not afford to transport them to markets. In Thailand, public servants were asked to use stairs instead of elevators to reduce energy expenses.

ASEAN comprises 11 nations — Brunei, Cambodia, Indonesia, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar, the Philippines, Singapore, Thailand, Vietnam, and, since last year, East Timor — with vastly different degrees of freedom and wealth. The bloc represents almost 700 million people, or almost 10 percent of the global population.

Making decisions on a consensus-basis and pledging not to interfere in one other’s internal affairs, the bloc has often been criticized as slow and ineffective, with the situation in Myanmar often cited as an example. A coup in that country in 2021 unleashed a civil war that ranks among the world’s most violent conflicts.

Myanmar’s leaders remain barred from ASEAN summits, under an agreement that was extended on Monday. Still, almost half of the bloc’s member states have had bilateral engagements with Myanmar since the coup leader, Min Aung Hlaing, used stage-managed elections to take the post of president this year. Washington has also recently re-engaged in direct talks with Myanmar.

Mr. Trump’s attendance at the summit, which concludes with an ASEAN-U.S. meeting, is part of a state visit to the Philippines. He is expected to hold a bilateral meeting with Mr. Marcos on Wednesday before heading to China for an Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit. Mr. Marcos has said little publicly about meeting Mr. Trump; but earlier this year, he described Washington as a “steadfast partner.” His administration has hinted that the two sides are working on a military aid agreement.

In recent days, hundreds of demonstrators have gathered in Manila to protest Mr. Trump’s visit. They oppose his “Pax Silica” initiative, a move to build a supply chain for the artificial intelligence industry that bypasses China, saying it would exploit Philippine natural resources.

Renato Reyes, who led one of the protest groups on Sunday afternoon, said, “Marcos is so desperate to welcome Donald Trump that he will roll out the red carpet, even if Trump arrives late for the summit.”

President Trump sets off this week for meetings in Asia that will culminate in a summit with two strongmen he has often praised: President Xi Jinping of China and President Vladimir V. Putin of Russia.

It will be the first time the three men, who command the world’s largest nuclear arsenals, will have sat down together. Mr. Trump plans to discuss the reduction of nuclear weapons in the world and the war against Iran, according to a White House spokeswoman. He is not expected to bring up Russia’s war with Ukraine.

Mr. Trump will travel first to Manila, then to southern China, and will meet with regional leaders confronting an array of uncertainties. Those include the course of the war between the United States and Iran, high energy prices and mounting concern over the risks of artificial intelligence.

For Mr. Trump, the trip will be a pivot to foreign policy and relationships after American voters delivered a swift rebuke to his party in the midterm elections. His trip also comes as his administration faces scrutiny over a scandal involving the former chief executive of Nvidia, the tech giant, which has raised questions about Chinese espionage and American tech exports.

The highlight of Mr. Trump’s trip is expected to be his meeting with Mr. Xi and Mr. Putin on Thursday in China.

The prospect of their summit has drawn comparisons to the 1945 conference in the Crimean city of Yalta, where the leaders of the United States, the Soviet Union and Britain met to decide the shape of postwar Europe.

The White House has said that Mr. Trump wants to discuss the threat of nuclear proliferation. U.S. officials have expressed concerns about China’s accelerating nuclear buildup, expected to reach 1,000 nuclear warheads by 2030.

The last nuclear arms control treaty between the United States and Russia expired in February. Trump administration officials have said that any new agreement on nuclear arms control must include China, but Beijing has shown no interest in the topic. China has an estimated 620 nuclear warheads, compared with the more than 5,000 that Russia and the United States have each, according to the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute.

U.S. administration officials said they did not expect a deal out of the meeting, which they characterized as a starting point for discussions.

The gathering has prompted concern in Ukraine that Mr. Putin might push for a deal, deciding Ukraine’s fate without Kyiv’s involvement. A U.S. administration official said Ukraine was not expected to be part of the discussion between the three leaders.

Mr. Trump will start his trip in the Philippines, the oldest American treaty ally in the Asia-Pacific region, as the United States faces questions about its support for its partners in a region where China’s military and economic might continues to grow.

In Manila, President Ferdinand R. Marcos Jr. plans to discuss with Mr. Trump the South China Sea, where the Philippines and China have competing claims. Philippine officials have said that they hope Mr. Trump’s visit might result in more American military assistance.

Mr. Trump is also expected to meet with leaders of ASEAN, a Southeast Asian grouping of 11 nations, as well as a gathering of the so-called Quad grouping of the United States, India, Australia and Japan.

Prime Minister Anthony Albanese of Australia said on Sunday that he expected “lots of engagement” with Mr. Trump during the meetings.

“Obviously, that’s our most important ally,” he said. “But we want to see something constructive come out of that, including we want to see a de-escalation in the conflicts that are around the world.”

Chinese analysts described the meeting of the Quad group, formed in part to counter China, as an effort by Mr. Trump to project power after his party’s defeat in the midterms. “Its symbolic value outweighs its practical value,” Xu Liping, a senior scholar at Guangdong University of Foreign Studies, told Shanghai Observer, a state-run outlet.

After Manila, Mr. Trump will travel to the Chinese tech hub of Shenzhen, where he will meet Mr. Xi on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation.

Jensen Huang, a key adviser who accompanied Mr. Trump on his last trip to China, will not be by his side. Mr. Huang helped shape the president’s views about technological competition with China and persuaded him to loosen restrictions on selling advanced chips to China.

Mr. Huang stepped down from his role at Nvidia after revelations that he had an affair with a Chinese businesswoman, Alice Huang, who U.S. intelligence officials believe was collecting intelligence on Mr. Huang for Beijing. China has rejected the allegations, calling them “smears.” Ms. Huang has not responded to requests for comment.

So far, the president has not raised the issue with Mr. Xi and is not expected to during their meeting on Thursday. But the saga is likely to cause unease in China about whether Washington will impose tighter controls on the export of technology to the country.

As a result, U.S.-based analysts speculated that Mr. Xi might try to use his personal relationship with Mr. Trump to forestall such restrictions.

Yun Sun, director of the China program at the Stimson Center in Washington, said Mr. Huang’s downfall would likely mean “there is going to be more anxiety on the Chinese side about their access to American chips.” She expects Mr. Xi to emphasize the importance of easing U.S. controls on chip exports to China during their meeting.

Cooperation between China and the United States on artificial intelligence may be more difficult after the Nvidia scandal.

Still, there may be progress on the creation of a notification system that would allow the countries to alert each other about national security issues related to artificial intelligence, which the two sides agreed to in September.

“There’s some shared concern on both sides that U.S. or Chinese models might be misused by bad actors and cause damage to the U.S., China, or other countries,” said Kyle Chan, a fellow at the Brookings Institution focusing on China’s technology development.

When Mr. Xi sits down with Mr. Trump in Shenzhen, he will be dealing with a president who will soon face a new constraint at home: a Democrat-controlled Congress.

Lawmakers may pressure Mr. Trump to tighten export controls against China or accelerate weapons sales to Taiwan, according to Ali Wyne, a senior research and advocacy adviser focusing on U.S.-China relations at the International Crisis Group. That means that “Xi will have to work much harder to keep Trump on side,” Mr. Wyne said.

Mr. Trump will be the first American president to visit China twice in one calendar year.

Analysts say the leaders’ summits, which have included state visits in Beijing and then in Washington, have been the only thing holding together a fragile détente between the two countries as they clash over A.I., export controls, Taiwan and other issues.

The two sides declared a trade truce last year when they met in Busan, South Korea. That deal, which was to expire this month, has been extended until January.

China’s restrictions on the export of crucial rare-earth metals and rare-earth magnets, which are needed to manufacture most modern technology, remain one of the most difficult unresolved issues.

Mr. Trump is likely to raise this with Mr. Xi, according to a senior U.S. administration official who said that China’s continued restrictions is one reason Washington only agreed to a two-month extension of the trade truce.
