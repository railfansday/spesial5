Nessa Carey's career has straddled both academia and industry. “In industry you tend to have much more of a sense of shared purpose, and you operate in teams,” she says.

Academia, on the other hand, is more personality-driven, and feels more individualistic. “Failure tends to be very binary: you either failed or you succeeded, and it's very personal,” she adds.

But as global research collaborations become more multidisciplinary, a culture change is needed in higher education, says Carey, who now runs a training business that supports biotechnology and drug discovery spinoffs.

Earlier this year, Carey spoke at a one-day conference on failure in science, with a focus on careers and innovation. It was held at Imperial College London in partnership with Nature.

In the penultimate episode of a six-part podcast series that revisits some of the conference themes, Carey urges academia to look at how industry handles failure, where struggling projects are abandoned more quickly.

So, in the opening session of the conference at Imperial College, Professor Jem Woods suggested that we have become too risk averse and that that has really negative consequences, for example in academia.

And I think I absolutely agree with that, but I don't think it's just academia that is negatively impacted by this.

And I think the conversation has shifted, and not necessarily in a way that's helpful. And I think it's particularly a lot of pressure on younger people.

So I do quite a lot of outreach work in schools, and I see young students who are under enormous pressure to do well at their GCSEs or their A levels or whatever is the equivalent in another country, because they have to get onto the right course at the right college. They have to get a career trajectory that's working in the right direction.

And so I think there's a much bigger fear of failure because the stakes have been made artificially high.

In reality, as long as you do a course that you're happy on at a university where you're happy living, you'll do fine.

But we've built in this terrible sequential success demand on the young, and I think it's also exacerbated by [the fact we] we now have much more public discourse because we have social media, and everybody posts their triumphs. Everybody posts their success. You look at LinkedIn, and it's just full of people celebrating their promotion, their latest certificate, etc.

The trouble is that's not real. That's not how life generally progresses. You try stuff, you stuff up, you learn from it, and you move on.

And if we always class that as failure rather than a normal part of learning, then I think we do create really, really big problems for the future because everybody will try and stay inside their comfort zone.

Everybody will be frightened of doing something wrong.

It's a big problem in science because science thrives on innovation, but it's a big problem in any creative sector, and we run the risk of situations where people would rather do nothing than take a risk.

And you see that in organizations as well, and that's a really big problem.

I think what academia could do with learning from industry in terms of failure is to depersonalize it somewhat.

I think that's extremely difficult to do in reality.

One of the things that I enjoyed about working in industry, as opposed to working in academia, is in industry you tend to have much more of a sense of shared purpose, and you operate in teams. And yes, you may be the leader of a team, but you're still a team.

Academia, I find generally is much more individualistic.

If you have a much more individualistic environment, it is much harder to acknowledge failure as being simply a consequence of good decisions that are made by a team.

In academia, failure tends to be very binary: you either failed or you succeeded, and it's very personal. And it's very personal, in particular, to the Principal Investigators.

In industry, you can be doing really interesting work on a project, and then you do the killer experiment and realize your project can't go anywhere. And as a team, and as the leader of a team, you make the decision that that project needs to be terminated.

Now, that in academia would feel like failure because you can't then go on and apply for grants for it.

In industry, there's much more an emphasis in good companies on “did we make the right decision?”, and if you made the right decision, then the target failed or the program failed, but you didn't necessarily fail as an individual.

And I think it's that difference between viewing things as projects and teams and viewing things as individual endeavours.

And given that more and more science is becoming multidisciplinary, multi-team, it would make so much more sense if academia could start to change its culture towards that, because on the whole, the big papers that we see coming out now – they're big team efforts, and yet reward still tends to remain very individual.

So it's a structural issue. It's not just about people.

I think academia could adapt well by adopting some of the good managerial and people development skills of industry.

Academia is, in my experience, much more driven by personalities, and that creates enormous emotional heft behind things.

In industry, of course, there are big personalities. There are in every sector, but because you're working as an organization, you don't have quite this same strange mix of your personal emotional life with your professional life, and so you can fail professionally without it having quite the same impact on you as an individual emotionally, if that makes sense.

Something that the industry has got very, very good on is identifying killer experiments, carrying them out, and doing the thing that is considered a cliche now – but is absolutely critical – of fail fast, fail quickly, fail early, fail cheaply: that you don't keep trying to progress something that cannot get to the end point that you need.

And I think industry, on the whole, is very good at that. It used to be very bad. It's got better.

Academia is terrible at that in my experience, in that what will happen is somebody has their favourite target, their favourite process, their favourite enzyme, and they just keep working on it because they have a reputation for that and because they can keep working on it, rather than thinking “do you know what? It'd be really good to set up this experiment, see if I'm on the right track, and if I'm not, I'll kill it.” Instead, what they tend to do is just find other things they can keep working on in the same field.

Now, sometimes that's entirely appropriate, and sometimes it's that real curiosity-led stuff that takes us in a weird direction and opens up all sorts of things.

But there is always a risk that you will just keep chugging away at something because you know you'll at least get a couple of papers out of it, or because your PhD student needs the data for their thesis, etc.

It's a very different environment, and so it's not that I'm saying we should import everything from industry into academia, but I do think sometimes academia could benefit from the critical thinking of industry that goes: take away the emotion and actually look at this from scratch and say, “would we carry on if we were starting now?”

So I mentioned these grand challenges; We often talk about them in science circles, The grand challenges. What's the role of science in helping society address these grand challenges?

And the emergence of innovation in technologies, I think has now switched to hyper disruptive technologies, and that's specifically around how we manage, or [how] information is managed. (When I say we, maybe it's not managed).

And then societal and community and individual access to those innovations in technology, and I think it's often labelled as ‘The BBC's Post-Truth Society’, because there were many of the BBC journalists (and some of them may be in the audience I don't know) who talked a lot about this emergence of that technological system in the post-truth society. And we have real questions in science to answer around that understanding of the way that information is generated and the way it's perceived and used societally.

So, in the opening session, Jem Woods was talking about the fact that there's been a drop of trust in science, and I wouldn't say it's just science.

There's a drop in trust in multiple areas, including in our trust in politicians, and our trust in writers, and our trust in the media.

And I think it's phenomenally important that scientists continue to do their work to the highest level of probity, and the vast majority of scientists do that, and that is true whatever sector you're in.

Scientists are remarkably similar, whether they're in academia or they're in industry; they're there because they want to do good science.

I think it's vital that everybody continues to do really good science. I think it's also vital, though, that we do challenge one another appropriately and professionally.

But what is also really important is that scientists continue to communicate, to a wider public, what is important about science and the scientific method, and also to be very upfront about the limitations of our research and the limitations of our findings.

This again, I think, ties into the issue of success and failure, because one of the things that's happened is public engagement and communication of science have been added to the academic workload.

The academic workload never decreases; new stuff always gets added to it, and it becomes another box to tick.

And there is a risk that people not only need to do good science, but they need to do science that can be communicated well.

And I think the most dangerous thing for academia is that it's constantly under pressure to justify why its science is being done. What is the benefit, the direct, very tangible benefit for humanity?

And that creates pressure to overstate one's outcomes, to be too black and white about it because black and white stories sell – it's much easier to sell a black and white story – but we have to be really careful not to do that because every time we do that and get challenged, we undermine the faith not so much in our science, because each member of the public has probably forgotten us, but the faith in science itself.

We shouldn't be scared of saying it's complicated, and so I think there is a real risk there that by being unsubtle about how we communicate our science, we actually undermine faith in a field which has essentially added enormous benefit to humanity and to the planet, and we need to be very careful not to lose that.

So I've worked in the field of innovation for about 25 years, and it's just as difficult now as it was 25 years ago.

But I think there are particular issues now that we're facing as a world that we didn't face back then. AI is the obvious one of having created a technology that actually may outrun us, and no one's quite clear how that's going to play out now.

But I think there are bigger issues at stake here as well.

I think it's very difficult when so much science is being published and being done to work out which of it genuinely has the potential to shift the needle on a particular issue.

But I think it's actually at risk of us having such a focus on innovation, in the sense of ‘where's the next great widget? Where's the next great drug?’, that we forget that technological innovation may not actually be what we require to solve particular problems.

So one of the things that really really frustrates me is seeing more and more emphasis, and more and more funding, in climate change going into carbon capture.

Carbon capture at scale is an entirely unproven technology, and it's not clear at all that it can solve the climate change problem.

We do, however, know that moving to electric cars makes a huge difference, and yet we are not in the UK legislating to do that appropriately.

We also have a situation where drug companies are spending billions of dollars to develop drugs for chronic health conditions that we actually know are caused essentially by social inequality and poverty.

We would be so much better as societies if we would put our money into more equitable healthcare systems, more equitable food availability systems.

We would have an enormous return on investment, but we don't see that return on investment for a very long time, and it's not a capitalist return on investment, it's a government return on investment.

So my concern is that our biggest failures will come because we constantly try to ‘tech’ our way out of problems that don't actually require technological solutions.

They require different, longer-term, less glossy solutions that don't make money for companies, and our systems on the whole run in a way that have placed an awful lot of, say, healthcare and environmental responsibilities and opportunities on big capitalist companies, when actually they are not the right structure to deliver those outcomes.

So that is where I think our biggest failures will come: We will ‘tech’ when we shouldn't be ‘tech-ing’. We will try and ‘tech’ very expensively when actually we could have made smaller investments with much more impact.

And it is deeply frustrating to me that also we as scientists will go “don't worry, we'll come up with a technological solution.” instead of saying “we may never come up with a technological solution, but if you talk to our friends in the social sciences, they already know what needs to be done. Why on earth aren't you doing this?” And that, I think, is a failure on the part of science to pretend that we are the ones who will solve everything.
