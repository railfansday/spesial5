The Pentagon Wants Public Lands ‘Offered Up’ for a New Army Base. Wyoming Is Interested

U.S. Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth wants to build a new military base, and some public land states like Wyoming say they have just the spots.

The base would house between 15,000 and 25,000 active-duty service members, according to a red, white and blue competition flier for what Hegseth is calling the Next Great American Base contest. And ideally it would include 100,000 to 250,000 contiguous acres.

Hegseth said the base should be a “dynamic, self-sustaining power projection platform” with its own power generation, modern air defenses, and advanced technology, reported Newsweek in early October, when the call for pitches first arrived. He went on to say the best proposals would cut through “the red tape, offering up the land and rolling out the red carpet for our military families.”

But much of the “red tape” associated with building a base on hundreds of thousands of acres of unoccupied land is likely federal government red tape. And no clear path currently exists for how to requisition and build a new military installation on federal public land, said Mark Squillace, a natural resources law professor at the University of Colorado Law School.

The Department of Defense would need to work around current Bureau of Land Management or U.S. Forest Service management plans that include stipulations for wildlife like migration corridors and winter range closures.

It’s also unclear if the base needs to support the U.S. Navy, which would limit public-land options since the bulk of the country’s public lands are in the Interior West like Nevada, Idaho and Utah, though the fact sheet says all locations, even those without water access, are on the table.

The DOD already administers 8.4 million acres in the U.S., but is looking to acquire lands managed by other agencies.

Wyoming, for its part, is eyeing both state and federal land for the base. The state’s adjutant general, Maj. Gen. Greg Porter, told a legislative committee in early November that Wyoming has identified at least four “chunks” of state and federal land, as reported by the Cowboy State Daily last week. Which chunks have not yet been disclosed.

Squillace lined out how building a military base on public land could — or could not — become a reality.

The DOD would first likely need to discuss any public land acquisition with the BLM or Forest Service. If the land use agency agrees that a military base would fit the current land management plan, it would need to prepare a draft Environmental Impact Statement that brings up potential complications and confirms compliance with laws like the Endangered Species Act or National Historic Preservation Act.

“A positive review would likely lead to temporary withdrawal of the land from the operation of certain public land laws (like mining and leasing) that could interfere with the operation of the base,” Squillace says, while also noting that existing rights would be protected. Any particularly valuable mining claims would have to be bought out in what he describes as a “time consuming and expensive” process.

Ultimately, however, Squillace says any large-scale, long-term use of land would likely need to be approved by Congress, “since such transfers of public land are not authorized under law.”  Whether or not the dramatically different Congress to be sworn in next year would green light a public-land transfer requested by Hegseth also remains uncertain.

Christine Peterson is a freelance writer based in southeast Wyoming covering hunting, fishing, outdoor recreation, wildlife and the environment for Outdoor Life, High Country News, National Geographic and others.
