Each new GTA moves the series closer and closer to the original Mafia, the open-world game that bettered Rockstar

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

This week sees the release of Grand Theft Auto 6, the open-world crime 'em up that's been in development for at least eight years and has been waited on by fans for even longer. Naturally, over on PC we're still waiting for a port to be announced, let alone a release date to circle in our calendars. But, as happens whenever a new GTA is on the horizon, I think about Mafia: City of Lost Heaven.

Released less than a year after Grand Theft Auto 3, Mafia is perhaps a more prescient example of Rockstar's future than their own.

Set in Lost Heaven, a shoe-in for 1930s Chicago, Illinois, you play through Tommy Angelo's rise from a taxi driver, who has a chance run in with the Salieri crime family, to one of the gang's main enforcers in their fight against their rivals, the Morellos.

The influences on developers Illusion Softworks are obviously cinematic, with whole sequences ripped from The Godfather films and Goodfellas (and I'd argue there's a sprinkling of Once Upon A Time In America for good measure). A habit they share with Rockstar, who also pull liberally from crime films that share the settings of their games. But Illusion Softworks are also clearly interested in capturing the history of the Prohibition era, too. The story begins in the aftermath of the 1929 Wall Street Crash and the depression that followed, running through until the late 30s, and you see that time pass in the city around you. Buildings that are under construction at the start of the game are completed by its end, a used car lot in Chinatown sits empty for most of the game until business picks up with the economy, but most enjoyable of all is that the cars driving the streets change. At first the roads are filled with Ford Model-T-inspired vehicles that basically look like horse carriages with engines, but by the end of the game they've all become much sleeker creatures that have left their early carriage influences behind.

The city Illusion Softworks built also has a dedication to verisimilitude that we see Rockstar adopting in their later games. As you drive Lost Heaven's streets, you must pay attention to speed limits and red lights, as speeding and running traffic lights will both earn you a fine if a policeman spots you. For fans of public transport, there are streetcars and overhead trams you can ride to navigate the city, too. Two other touches I love: when you reload your gun, you dump the whole clip instead of returning the unfired bullets to a magic ammo reservoir, and vehicles can run out of fuel if you drive them too far.

Much had been made of all the simulations running under the surface of Grand Theft Auto 6, such as fully realised cloud formation, a smell reaction system, and… fuel tanks that can run empty. No doubt they're significantly more impressive than what's on show in Mafia, and this isn't about putting the two games against each other (even if one of them was likely made for less than the other studio spent on coffee runs across its eight-year development). Nor is it about saying history recognised the wrong game: Grand Theft Auto 3 was a huge step forward in open-world gaming and Mafia was recognised and celebrated in its time. It's about something else.

Back in 2002, Illusion Softworks offered a different kind of open-world crime game. One that was more mature despite having a lower age rating. Grand Theft Auto was undoubtedly more influential, sparking imitators that mostly opted to follow its tone and sensibilities – Saints Row began in a similar place to GTA 3 and as the series continued leaned further into the silliness and parody, for instance. But while for many years Mafia stood as a sign to an open world path that could have been taken, the longer Grand Theft Auto series has gone on, the more Rockstar have found their way toward the example set by Illusion Softworks. The games are still aiming for bombastic comedy at times, but they're also interested in the likes of newly-arrived-in-America Niko Belic, or Michael de Santa, a retired criminal haunted by his past. They're much richer protagonists than Claude, the silent character you play in GTA 3.

The Mafia series has continued, marked by ideas that remain as interesting as what was on show in the original. In Mafia 3, you play Lincoln Clay, a black soldier returned from the war in Vietnam and brought into the family. Set in a take on New Orleans, Louisiana, during the civil rights movement, you face overt racism. Linger too long in white, wealthy neighbourhoods and the residents will call on the police to move you along. And the Definitive Edition remake of the first game, released in 2020, is a rare example of a game that both captures the spirit of the original and justifies its existence by expanding and bettering the story. Though I don't think any of its sequels have been as innovative, nor as forward thinking, as the first game.

I don't want to come across as Rockstar-bashing. The studio have continued to push the open-world genre forward with every release and I want to see what other developers lift from GTA 6 for their own games. But I'm even more pleased that as the studio's aged, their work has grown closer to Illusion Softworks'.
