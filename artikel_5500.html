Is Microsoft 365 down, or is the problem on your end? These 5 checks show real-time outage status, from Microsoft's own pages to crowd-sourced trackers

When Outlook refuses to load or a Teams call drops mid-sentence, the useful question is not what broke. It is where it broke. A fault on Microsoft $MSFT's side calls for patience and a reliable source of updates. A fault on your own laptop, network or account calls for action, and waiting will not fix it.

Telling those two situations apart takes more than a glance. Microsoft 365 is not a single product. It bundles separate services, including Outlook, Teams, SharePoint and OneDrive. One service can stumble while the others keep working. A colleague may have no trouble at all while your inbox sits frozen.

That is why the search "is Microsoft 365 down" rarely has a one-line answer. Different sources answer different parts of the question. Microsoft's own channels tell you what the company has confirmed. Crowd-sourced trackers tell you what other users are experiencing, whether or not Microsoft has acknowledged anything yet. Neither replaces the other. The clearest picture comes from reading them together.

This guide walks through five checks in a practical order. It starts with Microsoft's public service status page, which anyone can open without signing in. It then moves to the Service health dashboard in the Microsoft 365 admin center. That is where administrators see incidents and advisories tied to their own organization. From there it covers Microsoft's official status account on X $TWTR, formerly Twitter, as another public channel for incident updates. It also covers independent outage trackers such as Downdetector, which chart spikes in user-submitted problem reports.

The final step turns the question around. If none of those sources shows a Microsoft 365 outage, the cause may sit closer to home. A few quick tests on another device, another network or the browser version of the apps can narrow it down. So can a question to your IT team.

Each source also has limits. A public status page can be less detailed than what an admin sees. A spike in user reports confirms that people are having trouble, not why. Knowing what each source can and cannot tell you turns a stalled workday into a short diagnosis.

Microsoft $MSFT's public status page at status.cloud.microsoft shows whether Microsoft 365 services are running normally or have a reported incident. It is Microsoft's own public view of the suite's health.

The page's older address is status.office.com, which some readers may still have bookmarked.

The page lists services one by one. Outlook, Teams, SharePoint and OneDrive are among them. Each entry shows either a normal status or a reported incident.

That layout lets a reader match the check to the symptom. Someone whose inbox will not load can go straight to the Outlook entry. Someone whose shared files will not open can look at OneDrive or SharePoint. A dropped Teams call points to the Teams entry.

The entries for other services carry less weight. An incident listed for SharePoint does not explain an Outlook inbox that will not load. A normal Teams entry says little about a OneDrive folder that will not sync.

The page needs no account, so it suits anyone, not only IT staff. A remote worker, a small-business owner or a student can check it as easily as an administrator. It can also be checked when a person cannot sign in to Microsoft 365 at all. In that case, the status check does not depend on the very account that is failing.

The result sends the next step in one of two directions. A reported incident on the affected service suggests the fault sits on Microsoft's side. A normal status suggests looking more closely at the device, the network or the account.

The public page also has limits. It gives a general picture of the suite rather than a view of one organization. It is typically less detailed than what administrators see in their own organization's Microsoft 365 admin center. A normal reading here does not rule out a problem confined to a single workplace.

Administrators can check whether a Microsoft $MSFT 365 outage is hitting their own organization by signing in to the Microsoft 365 admin center at admin.microsoft.com. Selecting Health, then Service health, opens a dashboard of active incidents and advisories for that tenant.

The tenant focus is what separates this view from Microsoft's public status page. A problem can affect one organization or one region without appearing on that public page at all.

Service health is where those issues show up. It lists what affects the organization's own tenant, not a general summary for every customer. For an IT team fielding calls about Outlook or Teams, it is the place to confirm whether a reported problem is already known.

Every incident on the dashboard carries an ID, and the letters at the start point to the service involved. An ID beginning with MO relates to the Microsoft 365 suite. One beginning with EX relates to Exchange Online.

The ID gives everyone a shared reference. An admin can quote it to colleagues or a help desk. Every report then points to the same issue rather than a loose description of symptoms.

Each incident also includes status updates posted by Microsoft. Reading them in one place shows how the issue has been described since it first appeared, without piecing the story together from several sites.

Admins do not need to keep the dashboard open to stay informed. Service health supports email notifications for incidents. Admins can set these up to send alerts to an inbox.

Access depends on the account. Only administrators with the right role can open Service health. Regular users who sign in for email, chat or files will not find it in their view.

For those users, the direct route is to contact the organization's IT team or Microsoft 365 administrator. Asking whether an incident appears in Service health, and for its ID, helps show whether the fault sits with Microsoft.

Microsoft $MSFT runs an account on X $TWTR called @MSFT365Status that posts about Microsoft 365 service incidents. It can serve as a quick public signal of trouble when the official status page is slow to update.

X used to be called Twitter. Older help articles and forum threads may still refer to the account as a Twitter feed.

The account belongs to Microsoft. That makes it an official channel, not a fan page or a community-run tracker. A post there reflects an incident that Microsoft itself has acknowledged.

The feed covers the Microsoft 365 service rather than a single app. A post can concern a problem with the whole suite. It can also concern one service, such as Outlook, Teams, SharePoint or OneDrive.

Other Microsoft accounts on X focus on product news. For incident information, @MSFT365Status is the status-focused account.

Type the handle exactly when searching for it. A misspelled search can surface unofficial accounts with similar names. Those accounts have no link to Microsoft. Their outage claims carry no official weight.

Replies under each post come from other X users. They can show that people elsewhere see the same fault. They are not statements from Microsoft. They do not confirm the cause or scope of a problem.

Following the account and turning on post notifications in X brings new updates to a phone or browser. That removes the need to keep refreshing a page during a suspected Microsoft 365 outage.

A post on the account is a public notice, not an organization-specific report. It does not show whether a particular company's tenant is affected. Administrators can check that in the Service health dashboard in the Microsoft 365 admin center.

A single post is short by design and may not spell out every detail of an incident. The public status page at status.cloud.microsoft shows which services are running normally and which have a reported incident. Reading the account alongside those channels gives a fuller picture of a Microsoft 365 outage.

Downdetector and similar sites collect problem reports from users and show a spike when many people report trouble with a service such as Outlook or Teams. A spike signals widespread complaints. It does not prove what caused them.

These trackers run independently of Microsoft $MSFT. Their data comes from the public, not from Microsoft's own systems. A tracker reflects what users say they are experiencing. That can differ from what Microsoft has formally listed as an incident.

Reports are grouped by service. A reader who cannot open Outlook can look at Outlook's page on a tracker. A reader whose Teams calls keep dropping can check Teams instead. Microsoft 365 bundles several services. One of them may show a cluster of reports while another stays quiet.

A spike cannot explain itself. A rise in reports shows that many people are having trouble at the same time. It does not show whether the fault lies with Microsoft, with an internet provider in one area or with something else. A user-submitted report records a symptom, not a diagnosis.

Pairing a tracker with the official status page at status.cloud.microsoft helps separate a Microsoft-side outage from a local fault. Read together, the two sources point in a few distinct directions. A spike on the tracker plus a listed incident on the status page points to a Microsoft-side outage. A spike with no listed incident means many users see trouble, but the cause remains unconfirmed. Checking the official page again later is the next step.

No spike and no listed incident point the other way. If other users are not reporting the same symptom and Microsoft lists nothing, the problem is more likely local. The reader's own device, network or account then becomes the main suspect.

An incident on the status page without a matching spike is also possible. Not every affected user files a report. A quiet tracker does not rule out an official incident. In that case, the official listing is the firmer evidence.

If no Microsoft $MSFT 365 outage is reported, the fault may lie with your own device, network or account. Testing the service on a second network or device, and in a web browser, can narrow down the cause.

Start with the connection. Switch from office Wi-Fi to a phone hotspot, or from a home network to mobile data, and try again. If Outlook or Teams works on the second connection, the first network is the likely source of the trouble. That points to the network rather than to Microsoft.

Next, try a different device. A laptop that cannot load mail while a phone on the same account can points to something specific to that laptop. If every device fails on every network, the problem is more likely tied to the account or to the service itself.

The browser offers a clean comparison. Outlook on the web at outlook.office.com runs without the desktop client. The other Microsoft 365 apps can also be opened in a browser. If mail loads in the browser but not in the installed Outlook app, that points to a client-side fault. The same test applies to Word, Excel or Teams when only the desktop version fails.

Account problems can produce symptoms that look like an outage. Signing out of Microsoft 365 and signing back in is a simple first account check. Confirm that the password is still valid and has not expired or been changed. Check that the account still holds a valid Microsoft 365 license. A lapsed license or an expired password can block access while the service runs normally for everyone else.

People using Microsoft 365 through an employer or school have one more step. The public status page does not show every problem. An issue can affect a single organization or region without appearing there. An administrator can check the Service health dashboard for an incident tied to the organization's own tenant. A quick question to the IT team can confirm a tenant-level incident before anyone spends time on device fixes.
