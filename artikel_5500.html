From carbon monoxide to norovirus: 9 winter health risks that aren't the flu

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These winter health risks, from carbon monoxide poisoning to snow-shoveling heart strain, have nothing to do with the flu and are worth understanding before temperatures drop

Flu shots dominate most conversations about staying healthy in winter. Yet the season's cold, darkness, dry air and ice create a separate set of hazards. Many of them have no connection to any virus. They come from the way people heat their homes, move through icy streets, spend their days indoors and adjust to shorter hours of daylight.

Some of these hazards are environmental. Furnaces, space heaters, fireplaces and portable generators all burn fuel. A poorly vented one can fill a closed-up house with carbon monoxide. Outdoors, prolonged exposure to cold can lower the body's core temperature or freeze exposed skin. The risk rises when clothing gets wet or the wind picks up.

Others are physical. Icy sidewalks, driveways and parking lots turn an ordinary walk into a fall risk. Those falls can end in broken wrists, hips and ankles or in head injuries. Clearing heavy snow places heavy demands on the heart. Cold air narrows blood vessels, and heavy lifting raises heart rate and blood pressure.

A third group is tied to the season's biology. Shorter days are linked to seasonal affective disorder, a form of depression that returns on a seasonal pattern. Winter sunlight at higher latitudes is weaker, and people spend more time indoors. Both conditions mean the skin makes less vitamin D. Low humidity outside and forced-air heating inside pull moisture from the skin and can trigger eczema flare-ups.

Then there is norovirus, a stomach virus often called the winter vomiting bug. It is frequently labeled "stomach flu," but that name is a misnomer. Norovirus has no relation to influenza, and a flu shot offers no protection against it.

The nine winter health risks below are a selection, not a ranking. Each slide explains what the risk is, why the season raises it, which warning signs to look for and what steps reduce it. Many of the precautions are simple. They include a working carbon monoxide detector, dry layered clothing, footwear with good traction, a slower pace with a snow shovel and thorough handwashing with soap and water. Anyone with a heart condition, persistent low mood or questions about vitamin levels should talk with a doctor. A clinician can assess individual risk and recommend testing or treatment.

Carbon monoxide is a colorless, odorless gas released when fuel burns, and it can build up indoors without any warning a person can see or smell. A working carbon monoxide detector is the standard safeguard against poisoning.

The gas comes from any appliance or engine that burns fuel. Furnaces, gas space heaters, wood stoves, fireplaces, water heaters and gas ranges can all produce it. Portable generators and cars left running in garages are other common sources. Winter raises the risk. Homes are sealed tight against the cold, and heating equipment runs for long stretches.

Carbon monoxide harms the body by taking the place of oxygen in the blood. Red blood cells pick it up more readily than they pick up oxygen. Organs such as the brain and heart are then starved of the oxygen they need. Exposure can cause lasting damage or death.

Early symptoms include headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness and confusion. Those signs are easy to mistake for a cold or another viral illness. A few clues point toward carbon monoxide instead. Several people or pets in the same home may feel sick at once. Symptoms may also ease after time spent outside and return once everyone is back indoors.

People who are asleep or intoxicated may never notice symptoms at all. That is why detectors matter most near bedrooms. Place one on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas. Test them regularly, and replace batteries or units according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Generators need particular care during winter storms and power outages. Never run one inside a home, basement or attached garage, even with doors or windows open. Set it up outdoors and well away from windows, doors and vents. Charcoal grills and camp stoves are built for outdoor use and follow the same rule.

Routine maintenance lowers the risk at the source. Have furnaces, chimneys and other fuel-burning appliances inspected by a qualified technician before the heating season begins. Keep vents and flues clear of snow and debris. Never warm up a car inside a garage, even with the garage door open.

If a detector sounds or poisoning is suspected, leave the building immediately and get to fresh air. Then call 911 or local emergency services from outside.

Hypothermia is a medical emergency that sets in when the body loses heat faster than it can produce it, pushing core temperature below 95°F (35°C). Shivering, confusion and slurred speech are among its warning signs, and anyone showing them needs warmth and medical attention.

The body normally holds its core temperature within a narrow range. Cold air pulls heat away from the skin. The body responds by shivering and by narrowing blood vessels near the surface to protect vital organs. Hypothermia begins when those defenses can no longer keep up.

Moisture and moving air speed the process. Wet clothing loses much of its insulating value. Water also draws heat from the body far faster than air does. Wind strips away the thin layer of warm air that sits next to the skin. A person soaked by rain, sweat or a fall into water can become dangerously chilled even when the air temperature is above freezing.

The early signs are easy to dismiss. Uncontrollable shivering usually comes first, followed by fumbling hands, clumsiness and exhaustion. As core temperature keeps falling, a person may become confused, drowsy or slow to respond. Their speech may slur. In severe cases, shivering can stop altogether. That change signals worsening, not recovery.

Confusion makes hypothermia hard to recognize from the inside. People affected by it often do not realize what is happening and may make poor decisions about seeking shelter. Companions, neighbors and family members play a key role in spotting it.

Some groups face higher risk. Older adults can lose body heat in a poorly heated home without noticing. Infants lose heat quickly and may show bright red, cold skin and very low energy. People who spend long periods outdoors, including hikers, outdoor workers and people experiencing homelessness, face prolonged exposure. Alcohol can mask the sensation of cold while increasing heat loss through the skin.

Response starts with getting the person out of the cold and into a warm, dry place. Remove wet clothing and replace it with dry layers or blankets. Warm the center of the body — chest, neck, head and groin — before the arms and legs. Skin-to-skin contact under loose, dry coverings can help. Warm, nonalcoholic drinks are appropriate only if the person is fully alert.

Rewarming should be gradual, and the person should be handled gently. Call emergency services for anyone showing confusion, drowsiness or loss of consciousness. Severe hypothermia requires professional medical care, even after the person seems to have warmed up.

Frostbite is the freezing of skin and the tissue beneath it. It most often affects the fingers, toes, nose and ears. Mild early cases usually heal with careful rewarming, but deep frostbite can permanently destroy tissue.

Those body parts are vulnerable for a basic physiological reason. In cold conditions, the body narrows blood vessels near the skin to protect its vital organs. Hands, feet and the face sit farthest from that warm core, so they lose blood flow first. Cheeks and the chin are also common sites.

The mildest stage is frostnip. Skin turns cold, numb and pale or red, and it may tingle or sting. Frostnip does not cause lasting damage. It is a signal to get out of the cold.

Superficial frostbite goes further. The skin can change color and may start to feel oddly warm. Fluid-filled blisters can form within a day or two after rewarming.

In deep frostbite, the freezing reaches the lower layers of tissue. The skin becomes hard and waxy, and the area may lose all sensation. Nearby joints and muscles can stop working normally. Severe cases can lead to tissue death and sometimes amputation.

Color changes vary with skin tone. Frostbitten skin can look white, bluish-white, grayish-yellow, purplish or ashen. Numbness is often the more reliable clue. Because the area goes numb, a person may not notice the injury until someone else points it out.

Damp gloves and exposed faces freeze sooner because moisture and wind pull heat from the skin faster. Tight boots or gloves that restrict circulation add to the risk. Smoking, alcohol, diabetes and other conditions that affect blood flow also make frostbite more likely.

Rewarming should use warm water, not hot water. Numb skin cannot sense heat, so heating pads, fires and stoves can burn it unnoticed. Rubbing the area, or rubbing it with snow, can damage frozen tissue further. Rewarming is best delayed if the area could freeze again before reaching shelter. Thawing followed by refreezing causes more harm than staying frozen a little longer. Anything beyond frostnip calls for medical care.

Prevention centers on staying dry and covered. Mittens keep hands warmer than gloves because the fingers share heat. A hat that covers the ears, a scarf or face mask, and moisture-wicking socks inside waterproof boots protect the other common sites. Changing out of wet socks or gloves promptly removes a major source of heat loss.

Seasonal affective disorder, often shortened to SAD, is a form of depression that follows a seasonal pattern. For most people who have it, symptoms begin in fall or winter and lift in spring. Reduced daylight is the factor most commonly linked to it.

The DSM-5, the American Psychiatric Association's diagnostic manual, classifies the condition as major depressive disorder with seasonal pattern. That label means depressive episodes recur at roughly the same time each year and then ease.

Winter-pattern SAD has a recognizable set of symptoms. People often sleep more than usual yet still feel tired. Many report carbohydrate cravings, weight gain and a pull toward social withdrawal that some describe as hibernating. Persistent low mood, loss of interest in usual activities and trouble concentrating are also common.

The link to daylight helps explain the timing. Shorter days and later sunrises can shift the body's internal clock, which regulates sleep, alertness and hormone release. That disruption is one leading explanation for why symptoms cluster in the darkest months. Living farther from the equator, where winter days are shortest, is associated with higher risk.

The winter blues and SAD are not the same thing. Many people feel sluggish during a dark January without meeting the criteria for depression. The difference lies in severity, duration and whether symptoms interfere with work, relationships or daily routines.

Light therapy is one recognized treatment. It involves sitting near a bright light box for a set period each day, usually in the morning. Boxes intended for SAD are designed to filter out ultraviolet light. Side effects can include eyestrain and headache. People with bipolar disorder should use light therapy only under medical supervision. Bright light can trigger mania in some cases.

Light therapy does not work for everyone. Talk therapy, particularly cognitive behavioral therapy, and antidepressant medication are other options a clinician may recommend. Anyone whose low mood lasts for weeks, disrupts daily life or includes thoughts of self-harm should contact a doctor or mental health professional.

Vitamin D levels can fall in winter because the skin makes most of its supply from sunlight. At higher latitudes, the winter sun delivers too little of the right kind of light. The body needs vitamin D to absorb calcium, which keeps bones strong.

The process depends on ultraviolet B radiation, known as UVB. When UVB reaches bare skin, it triggers a reaction that produces vitamin D. The liver and kidneys then convert it into the form the body uses.

In winter, the sun sits lower in the sky. Its light travels a longer path through the atmosphere, and much of the UVB is absorbed before it reaches the ground. The effect grows stronger the farther a place is from the equator. In much of northern Europe, Canada and the northern U.S., winter sunlight produces little or no vitamin D in the skin.

Daily habits widen the gap. Cold weather keeps people inside for longer stretches. The skin they expose outdoors is usually limited to the face and hands. Window glass blocks UVB, so sitting in a sunny room does not help the body make vitamin D.

Some groups face a higher risk. Melanin, the pigment in darker skin, reduces how much vitamin D the skin produces from a given amount of sunlight. Older adults make less vitamin D in their skin than younger people do. People who are housebound, or who cover most of their skin outdoors, also get less exposure.

The main consequence involves the skeleton. Severe, prolonged deficiency can cause rickets in children, a condition that softens and deforms growing bones. In adults, it can cause osteomalacia, which brings bone pain and muscle weakness. Mild deficiency often produces no obvious symptoms.

Food can supply some vitamin D, though few foods contain much of it naturally. Fatty fish such as salmon, mackerel and sardines are among the richest natural sources. Egg yolks contain smaller amounts. Many countries fortify milk, breakfast cereals or plant-based drinks with it.

A blood test is the reliable way to know whether levels are low. A doctor can order the test and advise whether a supplement makes sense, and in what amount. Very high doses of vitamin D taken over time can raise calcium in the blood to harmful levels.

Cold outdoor air and indoor heating both draw moisture out of the skin, leaving it dry, tight and prone to cracking in winter. For people with eczema, also called atopic dermatitis, the same conditions often trigger flare-ups of itchy, inflamed patches.

The problem starts with the air. Cold air holds less water vapor than warm air. A furnace or radiator then warms that air indoors without adding any water. As the air heats up, its relative humidity drops further. The result is dry indoor air that pulls water out of the outer layer of the skin.

That outer layer, the stratum corneum, works as a barrier. It is made of flattened skin cells held together by fats such as ceramides. When the barrier loses moisture, gaps open between those cells. Water escapes more easily, and irritants get in more easily. Skin can look flaky, feel rough and develop small cracks, especially on the hands, shins and lips.

Eczema makes the problem worse. People with atopic dermatitis tend to have a weaker skin barrier to begin with. Dry winter air can push that barrier past its limit. The resulting itch leads to scratching, and scratching damages the skin further. Broken skin gives bacteria an easier way in, which can lead to infection.

Daily habits can deepen the dryness. Long, hot showers strip away the natural oils that help skin hold water. Harsh soaps and heavily fragranced products can irritate skin that is already compromised. Wool and other rough fabrics can also aggravate eczema-prone skin when worn directly against it.

Small changes reduce the damage. Shorter showers in lukewarm water remove less oil. Applying moisturizer within a few minutes after bathing, while the skin is still damp, helps seal in water. Thick creams and ointments, such as those made with petrolatum, generally hold moisture better than thin lotions. Fragrance-free products lower the chance of irritation.

A humidifier can put moisture back into heated indoor air. It needs regular cleaning, because standing water can grow mold and bacteria. Soft, breathable layers such as cotton next to the skin cut down on friction. Gloves outdoors shield the hands from cold, drying wind.

Some symptoms call for a doctor or dermatologist. Skin that cracks and bleeds, oozes, crusts or shows signs of infection needs medical attention. So does itching severe enough to disrupt sleep. Prescription treatments can calm an eczema flare when moisturizers alone are not enough.

Shoveling snow is a recognized trigger for heart attacks and other cardiac events in people at risk. The danger comes from hard physical work done in cold air, a combination that drives heart rate and blood pressure up at the same time.

Cold exposure causes blood vessels to constrict. That narrowing raises blood pressure and forces the heart to work harder to move blood through the body. It can also reduce blood flow to the heart muscle itself.

Shoveling then adds a demanding workload. Lifting and throwing snow relies heavily on the arms and upper body. Arm work tends to raise heart rate and blood pressure more sharply than walking or other leg-based activity at a similar effort.

Many people also hold their breath while heaving a full shovel. That straining, known as the Valsalva maneuver, causes sudden swings in blood pressure. Wet, heavy snow makes each load harder to lift and adds to the strain.

Timing matters too. People often clear driveways first thing in the morning, when blood pressure naturally runs higher. For someone who is mostly sedentary the rest of the year, a sudden burst of hard labor in the cold places a heavy load on the heart.

People with a history of heart attack, coronary artery disease, high blood pressure or high cholesterol face greater risk. So do smokers and older adults. Anyone in those groups should talk with a doctor before taking on heavy snow removal. Another person or a snow blower can take on the job instead.

Technique can reduce the load. Pushing snow to the side instead of lifting it cuts the effort. Smaller scoops, frequent rest breaks and clearing snow in stages during a storm keep any single session lighter. A few minutes of gentle movement beforehand helps the body warm up. Skipping a heavy meal right before shoveling also keeps extra demand off the heart.

The warning signs of a heart attack call for an immediate stop. They include chest pain or pressure, shortness of breath, and discomfort spreading to the arm, jaw, neck or back. Lightheadedness, nausea and a cold sweat can also appear. Anyone with these symptoms should stop shoveling and call emergency services right away.

Falls on ice are a common cause of winter injuries, including broken wrists, hips and ankles and blows to the head. Shoes with good traction, a slower walking style and treated surfaces all lower the risk.

Someone who throws out a hand to break a fall can fracture the wrist. The break usually occurs at the end of the radius, the forearm bone on the thumb side.

A sideways fall onto the hip is more serious for older adults. Their bones may already be weakened by osteoporosis, and a hip fracture can require surgery and a long recovery. Ankles can twist and break when a foot slides out at an awkward angle.

A backward fall can drive the head into pavement, causing a concussion or bleeding inside the skull. A worsening headache, confusion, vomiting or unusual drowsiness after a fall calls for emergency care. People who take blood thinners should get checked after any blow to the head, even if they feel fine.

Black ice is a thin, clear glaze that takes on the color of the surface beneath it. A sidewalk coated in it can look merely wet. It tends to form first on bridges, overpasses and shaded patches, where surfaces cool faster.

Rubber soles with deep treads grip better than smooth leather or worn-down soles. Slip-on ice cleats stretch over regular shoes and add metal studs or coils for grip on packed snow and ice. They should come off indoors, where the metal can slide on tile and other hard floors.

Short, flat-footed steps with body weight centered over the feet help keep balance. This style is often called the penguin walk. Keeping hands out of pockets lets a person steady themselves or catch a fall.

Rock salt melts ice but works poorly in severe cold. Calcium chloride keeps working at lower temperatures. Sand and cat litter do not melt anything, but they add grit underfoot. Clearing steps and walkways before foot traffic packs the snow down helps keep ice from forming.

Norovirus is a highly contagious stomach virus that causes sudden vomiting and diarrhea, and it has no connection to influenza. Washing hands with soap and water is the most reliable everyday defense against it.

The illness is often called "stomach flu," but that name is a misnomer. Influenza is a respiratory virus that infects the nose, throat and lungs. Norovirus infects the gut instead. It inflames the stomach and intestines, a condition known as gastroenteritis.

In the U.K., norovirus is widely known as the winter vomiting bug. Cases tend to rise during the colder months in the Northern Hemisphere. Outbreaks are common where many people share space, such as schools, nursing homes, hospitals and cruise ships.

Symptoms tend to come on suddenly. Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and stomach cramps are the most common. Some people also develop a low fever, headache or body aches. Most healthy adults recover within a few days.

The virus spreads through contaminated food and water. It also spreads when people touch a contaminated surface and then their mouth, or through close contact with someone who is sick. A very small amount of virus is enough to cause illness. People can remain contagious for days after they feel better.

Alcohol-based hand sanitizer works less well against norovirus than against many other germs. Scrubbing with soap under running water removes the virus more reliably. Hand washing matters most after using the toilet, after changing diapers and before eating or preparing food.

Norovirus can survive on countertops, doorknobs and other hard surfaces. Cleaning with a chlorine bleach solution, or a disinfectant labeled as effective against norovirus, helps limit spread at home. Soiled clothing and bedding should be handled carefully and washed promptly.

No specific medicine cures norovirus, and no vaccine is currently available. Treatment focuses on replacing lost fluids. Dehydration is the main danger, especially for young children, older adults and people with other health conditions.

Signs of dehydration include reduced urination, a dry mouth and dizziness when standing up. Anyone showing those signs, or unable to keep fluids down, should contact a doctor. People who are sick should stay home and avoid preparing food for others until well after their symptoms end.
