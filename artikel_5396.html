From frequency to cold-load pickup: 10 things to know about rolling blackouts

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Rolling blackouts are planned, rotating power cuts that grid operators order during shortages, and these 10 explainers cover how they work and why utilities rely on them

Electric utilities sometimes switch off power to their own customers on purpose. They do it to keep a much larger share of those customers connected. That trade sits at the center of rolling blackouts, one of the last tools grid operators use during a supply emergency.

The logic starts with a constraint built into the power system. Electricity cannot be stored cheaply at grid scale. Power plants have to produce what homes, offices and factories draw at every moment. When demand climbs past what generators can deliver, the gap has to close somehow. Operators can close it in a controlled way, or the grid can close it on its own terms.

The second path carries far more risk. A shortage shows up first as a drop in grid frequency, the steady rhythm of alternating current. Generators can trip offline to protect themselves when frequency keeps falling. Each one that drops off makes the shortage deeper. That chain reaction can end in a widespread, uncontrolled blackout.

A planned, rotating outage interrupts that chain. Operators cut a defined amount of demand, then rotate the cuts among groups of customers. No single neighborhood absorbs the whole shortfall. The rest of the system stays protected.

The practice is not limited to one country or one kind of crisis. California's grid operator ordered rotating outages during an August 2020 heat wave. The Electric Reliability Council of Texas ordered load cuts during a February 2021 winter storm. South Africa's state-owned utility, Eskom, has used scheduled outages, which it calls load shedding, since 2007–2008.

The 10 slides that follow explain the mechanics behind those events. They cover why supply and demand must stay balanced and what grid frequency reveals. They also explain how automatic relays and human operators each shed load. Later slides look at how utilities pick which circuits go dark and how a brownout differs from a full cutoff. The final slides cover the heat, cold and equipment failures that trigger shortages, along with the real cases above. They close with the steps operators try first and the staged process of turning the power back on.

An electric grid has to generate exactly as much power as its customers use, moment by moment. There is no large, low-cost reserve to draw from when demand runs ahead of supply.

That sets power apart from most other commodities. Oil can sit in tanks, and natural gas can be held in underground storage for months. Electricity on the grid is produced and consumed almost at the same instant. Pumped hydro plants and large battery systems do store energy. That storage adds cost on top of generating the power in the first place.

Demand never stays still. It typically climbs as homes and businesses start their day, holds through working hours and falls overnight. Weather and holidays can shift that pattern. Every switch flipped, motor started and charger plugged in adds to the load the system must serve.

Grid operators plan for this by forecasting demand ahead of time and scheduling enough power plants to meet it. Some plants run steadily for long stretches. Others, such as natural gas peaking plants, are built to start quickly and cover short spikes. Operators adjust output throughout the day as actual demand moves away from the forecast.

Operators also hold operating reserves. These are generators kept ready to ramp up or come online if another plant fails without warning. Reserves give the system a cushion, but that cushion is finite.

The balance matters in both directions. When supply exceeds demand, operators cut back generation, sometimes by curtailing wind or solar output. When demand exceeds supply, the gap affects the entire connected system almost immediately, not one isolated area.

A supply gap can close in only two ways. Operators can add generation or import power from elsewhere. The alternative is to reduce how much customers draw. If extra supply cannot arrive fast enough, cutting demand becomes the only option left.

Grid operators accept a planned, limited cut in demand because an unresolved imbalance threatens the stability of the whole system. That controlled reduction is the core of a rolling blackout.

A rolling blackout is a deliberate, temporary power cut that moves from one group of customers to the next. Each group loses electricity for a limited stretch, then gets it back while another group is switched off.

The word "rolling" describes that movement. A shortage is not handed to a single town or neighborhood for its full length. The outage instead passes through a sequence of customer groups, and the loss is shared among them.

That sharing is the purpose. A grid in shortfall has to remove a certain amount of demand to stay in balance. Rotation keeps that total cut in place while changing who carries it. One group's power comes back only as another group's power goes out. The overall reduction holds steady through each cycle.

The planned nature sets these outages apart from more familiar ones. A storm that brings down a line or a transformer that fails causes an outage nobody chose. A rolling blackout is a decision. A grid operator has judged that cutting some customers on purpose is safer than letting the shortage run unchecked.

The decision and the work of carrying it out often sit with different organizations. A regional grid operator can set how much demand must come off the system. Local utilities then choose which of their own customers take each turn without power.

The practice goes by other names. Rotating outages means the same thing. Load shedding is a broader term that covers any deliberate removal of demand from the grid. That includes cuts made automatically by protective equipment.

Each rotation is meant to be temporary in two ways. Every group's turn without power is limited. The rotation itself also ends once supply can again cover demand. Short, shared outages are the price a grid pays to avoid an uncontrolled blackout that could leave far more customers without power.

Grid frequency is the clearest real-time sign that a power system is running short. When customers draw more electricity than power plants produce, frequency sinks below its target of 60 hertz in North America or 50 hertz in Europe.

Frequency measures how many times per second alternating current changes direction. Conventional generators on a large grid spin in step with that rhythm. A turbine on the North American grid turns at a speed tied directly to 60 hertz.

That physical link turns frequency into an early warning. If demand suddenly exceeds supply, the missing energy comes from the rotating mass of turbines and generators across the system. Those machines slow slightly, and frequency falls with them. When supply exceeds demand, the machines speed up and frequency rises.

Generators on an interconnected grid run in synchrony. Frequency is therefore essentially the same everywhere on that grid. An operator watching a single reading can spot an imbalance affecting the whole system. There is no need to tally the output of every plant and the use of every customer.

Small deviations happen all day as demand shifts. Generators carry controls called governors that raise or lower output automatically as frequency drifts. That response steadies the system and buys time for operators to bring more supply online.

A larger or longer drop carries more risk. Power plants run protective systems that disconnect them if frequency strays too far from normal. Running well away from design speed can damage turbines and other costly equipment.

Every protective trip pulls more supply from a grid that was already short. Frequency then falls further, which can push other plants past their own limits. That sequence is how a manageable shortage turns into a system-wide collapse.

The amount of spinning mass on a system shapes how fast this unfolds. Heavy rotating machines resist sudden changes in speed, which slows the slide in frequency. Solar panels and batteries connect through power electronics called inverters rather than large rotating machines. On grids with less rotating mass, frequency can change more quickly after a sudden loss of supply.

For grid operators, a falling frequency reading shows the gap between supply and demand as it opens. It arrives before plant trips can compound the shortage, which leaves a narrow window to act.

Under-frequency load shedding is an automatic safety system that cuts power to blocks of customers when grid frequency falls to preset levels. It acts faster than any human operator could, and it serves as the grid's last line of defense against collapse.

The core equipment is the protective relay. A relay watches electrical conditions on a line and trips a circuit breaker when those conditions cross a set limit. An under-frequency relay watches one measurement: how fast the grid's alternating current is cycling.

In North America, the normal value for that measurement is 60 hertz. Each relay is programmed with a threshold somewhere below that mark. If frequency sinks to the threshold, the relay opens a breaker and disconnects the load connected behind it. No dispatcher has to approve the action.

The settings come in steps. A scheme is built with several preset levels, and each one removes another block of load. If the first step stops the decline, the later steps never fire. If frequency keeps falling, the next relays trip in turn. This staged design sheds only as much demand as the emergency requires.

Speed is the reason the system exists. The sudden loss of a large power plant can pull frequency down within moments. That leaves too little time for a control room to assess the problem and order cuts by hand. The relays are set to act first, while the decline can still be stopped.

Automatic shedding differs from the rotating outages that grid operators order during a supply crunch. Those manual cuts are timed and moved from one customer group to the next. Automatic shedding is decided in advance. Engineers choose which feeders to wire into the scheme and the frequency setting for each one. In the moment, the relays respond only to frequency.

In North America, the North American Electric Reliability Corp. sets reliability standards that govern how these schemes are designed and coordinated across a region. Coordination matters. Relays in neighboring areas need settings that work together rather than against one another.

An automatic trip means the measures that come earlier did not close the gap. Operators aim to balance supply and demand well before frequency nears the relay settings. When the scheme does operate, it buys time. Operators use that time to bring back generation and steady the system before customers are reconnected.

Manual load shedding is a power cut that a grid operator orders on purpose when supply can no longer cover demand. In North America, that order follows a tiered warning system. The North American Electric Reliability Corp., or NERC, defines it as Energy Emergency Alert levels one through three.

NERC sets and enforces reliability standards for the bulk power system across the U.S., Canada and part of Mexico. Its alert levels give grid operators, utilities and neighboring systems a shared vocabulary. When one region declares an alert, others can tell how tight its conditions have become.

The first level signals that all available generation is in use or committed to run. The operator has little room left if demand climbs further or a plant fails. At this stage, the alert is a warning. Customers see no change in service.

The second level means the operator has put its load management procedures into effect. It is drawing on emergency resources to keep generation matched to demand. Power still flows to every customer, but the system has almost no margin left.

The third level means firm load interruption is imminent or already under way. Firm load is the ordinary demand of homes and businesses that utilities have committed to serve. At this point, operators expect to cut some of it or have already started.

Regional grid operators use their own names for these tiers. The California Independent System Operator, which runs most of the state's high-voltage grid, calls its top tier a Stage 3 emergency. The meaning lines up with NERC's level three. Rotating outages are imminent or already in progress.

Manual shedding works on a slower timescale than the automatic relays built into the grid. The operator sends instructions to the utilities in its area, telling each one to drop a set amount of demand. The utilities then switch off chosen parts of their distribution systems. The process takes minutes rather than fractions of a second.

That human step has a purpose. A deliberate order lets the operator size the cut to the actual shortfall. It can then divide the reduction among the utilities it oversees. Each utility decides how to spread its share across its own customers. When supply catches up with demand, the operator lowers the alert level and lifts the order to shed load.

Utilities usually pick which neighborhoods lose power by distribution circuit, not by individual home or street. Each circuit is a distribution line that serves a set group of customers. The utility switches that whole line off and on as a unit.

A household is not singled out or spared on its own in a circuit-based rotation. Whether a home goes dark depends on which circuit it sits on. It also depends on whether that circuit is in the rotation.

The grid operator and the local utility play different roles. The operator decides that load must come off the system. The utility then carries out the cut on its own lines. It chooses circuits that together add up to the reduction the operator ordered.

Some circuits are typically kept out of the rotation where possible. These are lines that serve hospitals, emergency services and other critical loads. The aim is to keep facilities tied to health and public safety running through the shortage.

Because the circuit is the unit, the exemption reaches beyond the critical site itself. Homes and businesses on the same line as a hospital keep power along with it. Neighbors a few streets away may sit on a different circuit and take their turn in the rotation.

Exempting circuits also affects everyone else. Each line removed from the pool leaves fewer circuits to share the shortage. With a smaller pool, the remaining circuits can face longer or more frequent turns without power.

These exemptions apply where possible, not in every case. Sparing a critical facility means sparing its whole circuit. That protection is a goal in each utility's plan rather than a guarantee for every site.

No single rulebook sets which circuits go into rotation. Each utility works from its own load-shedding plan. The details differ from one company to the next. Customers who want to know where they stand generally need to check their own utility's plan. A neighboring provider's rules may not apply.

A brownout lowers voltage without cutting service, a rolling blackout switches power off completely for a set period, and a cascading failure is an uncontrolled collapse. Operators control the first two. The third is the result both are meant to prevent.

The term brownout comes from the way incandescent bulbs dim and turn yellowish when voltage sags. Customers stay connected throughout. Lights glow weaker and some appliances behave oddly, but service never fully stops.

Lower voltage still affects some equipment. Many electric motors, such as those in refrigerators and air conditioners, try to do the same work at reduced voltage. They draw more current to compensate. That extra current makes them run hotter. Sustained low voltage can shorten the life of the equipment.

Grid operators in some regions can order a small, deliberate voltage reduction as an emergency step. It trims demand slightly without taking anyone offline. The effect is modest. A voltage reduction cannot close a large gap between supply and demand on its own.

A rolling blackout takes the opposite approach. Power to a group of customers drops to zero, then returns after a fixed window while another group takes its turn. The cut is total, but it is scheduled, limited and shared. Operators accept short, full outages for some customers so the rest of the system keeps running normally.

A cascading failure has no schedule and no rotation. It starts when one failure pushes extra strain onto the remaining equipment. Overloaded transmission lines can disconnect under that strain. Each loss shifts more burden onto what is left. The outages then spread across a connected region faster than operators can respond.

Recovery marks the sharpest difference. After a rolling blackout, operators switch a circuit back on. The rest of the grid is still energized, so the returning circuit has live power to reconnect to. After a full collapse, there may be no live grid at all. Operators must rebuild it from black-start units, which are power plants able to start without drawing electricity from the grid. They then extend power outward piece by piece. That process can take far longer than any planned rotation.

Brownout is sometimes used loosely to describe planned power cuts. In grid engineering, the three terms describe distinct events with different causes, controls and consequences.

Rolling blackouts usually begin with one of two problems. Demand climbs faster than generation can follow, or supply drops without warning. Extreme heat, extreme cold, plant failures, fuel shortfalls, transmission limits and drought can each open that gap.

Heat waves push demand up mainly through air conditioning. When high temperatures last for several days, buildings hold more heat and cooling systems run longer. Homes, offices and stores across a wide area all draw on the grid for cooling at the same time. Hot weather also lowers how much current transmission lines can safely carry.

Cold snaps strain both sides of the balance. Electric heaters, heat pumps and other heating equipment raise demand as temperatures fall. Freezing weather can also force power plants offline. Water lines, sensors and valves at a plant can freeze if the site was not built or weatherized for severe cold. A single cold snap can raise demand and cut supply at once.

Unplanned generator outages remove supply with no notice. A mechanical or electrical fault can trip a large plant offline in seconds. Grid operators hold reserve capacity to cover the loss of a big unit. Trouble starts when several units fail together. It also starts when one unit fails during peak demand and reserves are already thin.

Fuel supply problems can idle a plant whose equipment works normally. Gas-fired plants depend on pipeline deliveries. A drop in pipeline pressure or flow can leave them short of fuel. Coal plants depend on stockpiles and rail shipments. When fuel cannot reach a plant, the plant cannot generate, whatever the condition of its machinery.

Transmission limits create shortages that are local rather than systemwide. Each power line can carry only so much electricity. A region can run short even when a neighboring area has spare generation. That happens when the lines between them are already at capacity. The same constraint caps how much help an operator can bring in from outside during an emergency.

Drought cuts into hydroelectric output. Lower river flows and reservoir levels leave less water to run through dam turbines. Grids that lean heavily on hydropower lose part of their supply in dry years. That kind of shortfall can persist through a whole dry season rather than a single afternoon.

These triggers often overlap. A broad heat wave can raise demand in several regions at once. That leaves neighboring grids with less spare power to share. A severe freeze can push up heating load while also disrupting gas deliveries to the plants that need it.

California in August 2020, Texas in February 2021 and South Africa under Eskom show three different ways planned power cuts can play out. California ran a brief rotation. In Texas, the rotation broke down. South Africa turned scheduled cuts into a long-running practice.

The California Independent System Operator ordered rotating outages on Aug. 14 and 15, 2020, during a heat wave. These were the first such orders in the state since the 2000–2001 energy crisis.

The California episode follows the standard pattern for a rolling blackout. The grid operator directed the cuts. They rotated among groups of customers instead of falling on a single area. Each group lost power for a limited stretch. Service then came back while another group took its turn.

Texas shows what happens when that pattern fails. In February 2021, during a severe winter storm, the Electric Reliability Council of Texas, known as ERCOT, ordered utilities to cut load. The outages that followed lasted far longer than a planned rotation would. For many Texans, the cuts did not arrive as brief, shared turns without power.

That breakdown shows the limit of the method. Rotation depends on having enough supply to restore one group before cutting the next. When the shortage runs too deep, there is not enough power to bring groups back on schedule. The planned rotation then gives way to long outages for the customers who were cut.

South Africa follows yet another model. Eskom, the country's state-owned utility, has used scheduled outages since 2007–2008. It calls them load shedding. That is the same term grid operators in North America use for deliberate cuts to demand. Eskom labels each round of cuts by stage.

The South African program differs from the U.S. cases in its duration. California's orders came on two days in August 2020. Eskom's scheduled outages have recurred in the years since they began. That makes them a repeated practice rather than a single emergency.

Taken together, the three cases cover the range of what the term means in practice. A rolling blackout can be a short emergency step, a rotation that collapses under strain or a long-running schedule.

Grid operators try several measures before ordering rolling blackouts: importing power from neighboring grids, paying large customers to cut their use and asking the public to conserve. When outages do happen, service returns in stages to limit a demand surge known as cold-load pickup.

Imports are one of those options. A grid connected to neighboring systems can draw extra electricity over transmission lines when its own plants fall short. That option has limits. Transmission lines can carry only so much power. Neighboring regions may also be facing the same heat wave or cold snap. A shortage that covers a wide area leaves less spare power for anyone to sell.

Demand response works on the other side of the balance. It removes demand instead of adding supply. Utilities and grid operators enroll large customers, such as factories and commercial buildings, in programs that pay them to reduce electricity use when the grid is strained. A manufacturer might pause an energy-intensive process. An office complex might scale back its cooling. Each of these customers uses a large amount of power. A small number of them cutting back can free up capacity that would otherwise require outages for many homes.

Public appeals reach everyone else. California's Flex Alert is a named example of this kind of request. It asks residents and businesses to use less electricity during periods of grid stress, for instance by putting off running major appliances. Participation is voluntary. That makes the effect of an appeal harder to predict than a paid demand-response contract.

Restoration after an outage is not a single flip of a switch. Operators bring circuits back in stages. Each step lets them confirm that supply can keep up with the demand coming back online.

The main hazard during this phase is cold-load pickup. In normal operation, refrigerators, air conditioners, water heaters and furnaces cycle on and off at different times. That staggering keeps total demand on a circuit relatively steady. After an outage, many of these devices have been waiting for power. They all start at once when it returns. Electric motors and compressors also draw extra current as they start up.

The combined surge can push a circuit well above its usual load. That can strain transformers and trip protective equipment. A tripped circuit would put customers back in the dark and could add stress to a grid that is still recovering. Reconnecting load in measured blocks spreads that surge out over time.
