Thirteen dead as mine disaster highlights illegal hunt for gold

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

The flooded mine is in Bogor in West Java

It's been three days since Rani last saw her father as he left for work on Monday. Early on Tuesday, she received news that he was trapped underground.

Rais, 50, had been working as an illegal gold miner in Bogor, Indonesia's West Java province. On Monday night, he and at least 25 other miners were digging in an open-pit mine about 100m (328ft) deep, when torrential rain caused a nearby river to overflow and flood the two shafts.

Authorities on Thursday confirmed that 13 people had died and their bodies were recovered from the site. At least 13 more are thought to still be missing.

Search-and-rescue operations are ongoing, with personnel using ropes to access the depths of the dark, narrow and slippery mine shafts, which are continually being flooded from below.

Above ground, ambulances and medical personnel are ready and waiting to transport victims.

"We are coordinating with all search and rescue personnel involved in the operation to monitor water conditions and assess access to the mine shaft," Desiana Kartika Bahari, head of the Jakarta Search and Rescue Office, said in a statement on Wednesday.

"The depth of the shaft, narrow access and limited lighting are key considerations in determining the search strategy so that personnel can operate safely."

Rescuers have been working day and night to find survivors in the flooded mine shaft

Small-scale gold mining is banned in Indonesia, but remains widespread in rural parts of the mineral-rich archipelago.

Multiple studies show that over the past ten years the illegal industry has rapidly flourished, often in correlation with rising gold prices. In early 2026, those prices reached an all-time high â spurring a gold rush that prompted mine operators across the country to expand their operations.

This included state-owned mining company Aneka Tambang (Antam), which owns the business permit for the area in which this week's flooded mine is located. Antam have said the mine was not theirs and was operating without their authorisation.

On Wednesday, while rescue operations there were underway, West Java Regional Police separately announced that they had uncovered 24 cases of illegal mining across the province between October and November alone.

But Bisman Bakhtiar, Executive Director of the Center for Energy and Mining Law Studies, told the BBC earlier this year that the actual number of illegal mines in Indonesia is higher than the government's figures.

"[The government figures are] what's been recorded. That means there must be more than that," he said, noting that law enforcement operations have had little impact on the sector.

"It's still very massive and very big because this has been going on for a very long time."

Others allege that the illegal industry is underpinned by corruption.

Laode M Syarif, former deputy chairman of the Corruption Eradication Committee, said that unofficial mining is difficult to eradicate because every illegal mine has "backing", usually from "police and military or high-ranking officials in local and provincial governments".

"As long as these backers are not dealt with firmly, illegal mining will continue," he said.

In many cases, though, the allure of these mines is simple: they offer an economic lifeline to people with few other viable prospects.

Despite the value of gold having dipped in recent months, countless illegal miners throughout Indonesia are still eagerly digging for paydirt.

But the lack of regulation and poor construction in the pits also means accidents are relatively common â whether from landslides, floods, or exposure to hazardous gases, all of which have claimed lives in recent years.

Relatives of the missing Bogor miners have been waiting near the site for news of their loved ones

Earlier this year, in the same area as this week's flooded mine, 11 illegal gold miners reportedly died after a carbon monoxide leak inside a shaft.

Elsewhere, in West Sumatra, nine people were killed at an illegal gold mine in May after heavy rain triggered the collapse of a 30-metre cliff, burying 12 miners in total.

There are numerous reports of other such incidents across the Indonesian archipelago. But the illegal nature of the small-scale gold mining sector obfuscates the true number of casualties.

Even this week, when asked how many miners were in the pits in Bogor when they flooded, M Adam Hamdani, head of emergency and logistics at the Bogor Regency Regional Disaster Management Agency, said the total casualty figure "is still unclear".

"Some say 20 people, others say 29 people," Hamdani told Indonesian news outlet Detik. "The problem is that no one reported it and no one wants to report it."

The Indonesian Ombudsman on Thursday urged law enforcement officials to conduct a thorough investigation of the incident and to address the larger systemic issue behind it.

"This should be a momentum for all parties to resolve the [illegal gold mining] problem comprehensively, not just taking action after the incident or closing the holes discovered," said Abdul Ghoffar, a member of the Indonesian Ombudsman.

"We need to uncover the entire [illegal gold mining] chain," he continued. "We must not allow only the miners in the field to be seen and prosecuted, while those who control the activities, provide capital and facilities, store, and market the mined products remain untouched.

"Law enforcement must target the actors and networks that keep this illegal activity going."

As rescue operations continue and more bodies are recovered, the picture is becoming less murky. But even if there are survivors among those who remain missing, time may be running out.

More than three days since the shafts flooded, the relatives of the anonymous men in the mines are desperately waiting to hear whether their loved ones survived.

As she waits for news of her father, Rani says: "I hope he's found soon."
