8 Setup Tips to Fine-Tune Your Trap-Bar Deadlift

Al Gerared invented the trap bar in the late 1980s, and it quickly became the deadlift tool of choice for lifters whose backs say no to the barbell.

Standing inside it improves alignment with your center of gravity, often with a more upright torso than in the conventional deadlift, which is great for a cranky lower back.

In the conventional setup, the barbell is over your midfoot, but where should your feet be inside the trap bar? Should you use the high or low handles? Where should your hips be?

Nail these details before the first pull, and your form will improve; that’s the goal of the Trap Bar setup guide.

I’ll cover everything from choosing the handle height and positioning yourself inside the trap bar to finding your ideal hip height, engaging your upper back, and building full-body tension before pulling.

Next, I’ll walk you through the trap bar deadlift setup step by step to improve your form and gains.

Before stepping into the trap bar, decide whether to use the high or low D handles. This decision affects range of motion and starting position.

High handles shorten the range of motion and let you start with a more upright torso with less hip and knee flexion. They’re great for beginners, taller lifters, or those who struggle to keep a neutral spine while hinging down.

Low handles increase the ROM, requiring more hip and knee flexion to reach the bar. This creates a deeper starting position and increases the distance you lift.

Coach’s Tip: Your choice depends on your mobility, body proportions, training goals, and your ability to create tension. If reaching the low handles requires you to round your lower back or shift your weight forward, switch to the high handles.

Where you stand inside the trap bar determines everything that follows, including your balance, hip height, and torso position. Lining up your ankles with the loading stubs puts you in a better position to lift with good form.

Internal Cue: Feel your feet on the floor.

External Cue: Line your ankles up with the stubs.

Coach’s Tip: Your ankle position sets up everything else. If the bar feels unbalanced or tilts when you begin, check your stance and adjust.

Your feet are the foundation; creating tension there gives you a stronger base to pull from, puts your lower-body joints in an optimal lifting position, and helps transfer force more effectively to the bar.

Internal Cue: Feel the tension in your hips.

External Cue: Rip the floor apart with your feet.

Coach’s Tip: If your arches collapse or your knees cave in during the first rep, you’ve lost your foundation. Reset your feet and rebuild tension.

If you start too low, your hips may shoot up before the weight moves. Start too high, and you’ll turn the exercise into more of a stiff-legged deadlift. Your sweet spot depends on your body proportions, mobility, and handle height.

Internal Cue: Feel the hamstring tension.

External Cue: Hinge back, chest up and shoulders down.

Coach’s Tip: Feeling tension in your hamstrings is key here. If you feel it excessively anywhere else, adjust your hinge.

Your grip connects you to the trap bar and creates the tension needed for proper form. The arms should act like hooks, allowing your lower body to do most of the work.

Internal Cue: Crush the handles, arms straight.

External Cue: Leave fingerprints in the handles.

Coach’s Tip: Place the handle in your palm near the base of your fingers to help prevent calluses.

Engaging your lats and upper back creates tension that stabilizes your shoulders, maintains your torso position, and transfers force more effectively from the ground up.

External Cue: Squeeze oranges in your armpits.

Coach’s Tip: If you lose your neutral spine before the first pull, you’re missing tension. Re-engage your lats before pulling.

The core acts as a bridge between the lower and upper body, and if the bridge is out, your lower back will have nasty words to say to you. A good core brace solidifies your setup before the first pull.

External Cue: Push your midsection against an imaginary lifting belt.

Coach’s Tip: If you feel your lower back rounding, reset your breath and brace.

Taking the slack out means pulling the handles until your body and the bar are loaded with tension. This prevents you from jerking the weight off the floor and losing your starting position.

Internal Cue: Full-body tension, no slack.

External Cue: Chest up, shoulders down and arms long.

Coach’s Tip: Like the conventional deadlift, you should hear the click that signals you have pulled the slack out of the bar.

Before the pull, mentally run through the list below.

The trap bar might be more forgiving than the barbell, but a poor setup can still rob you of gains. Here’s what to look for during your setup so you don’t do it again.

When you’re not centered in the trap bar, your first pull requires more work from your lower back.

The Fix: Start with your ankles roughly aligned with the loading stubs. Adjust slightly until your weight is balanced over your midfoot.

Using the low handles when you lack the mobility to maintain a strong starting position can cause your lower back to round.

The Fix: Use the high handles until you can reach the low handles without sacrificing your spinal position.

Loose lats and shrugged shoulders will cause rounding in both the upper and lower back and unnecessarily increase the range of motion.

The Fix: Squeeze the handles, engage your lats, and keep your arms straight without pinching your shoulder blades.

Gripping and ripping before pulling the slack out of the bar can put you out of position before the lift even begins.

The Fix: Root your feet, brace your core, engage your lats, and remove the slack from the bar before initiating the pull.

The trap bar deadlift is easier to learn and more forgiving than its straight-bar cousin, but that doesn’t mean you should neglect the setup. Reviewing this setup guide again shows how important setup is for good form.

At first, this checklist will take time and thought, but soon it will become automatic, and you’ll be ready to pull without overthinking every detail.

Put yourself in the best position to express your strength, and the gains will follow.
