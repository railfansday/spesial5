Dirty air actually breaks down the smell of flowers and stops bees and moths from finding their food

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Ozone and nitrogen oxides cut pollinators' ability to find flowers by about half, and ozone will stay high until around 2050 as fuels change.

A bee finds a flower the way you find a bakery, by following the smell. Exhaust from cars and machines breaks those scent molecules apart in the air.

A review of 22 experiments now puts a number on the damage. Polluted air cut how well pollinators found and used flowers by roughly half, and two pollutants together cut it by more than two thirds.

The surprise is in the timing. Even as the world burns less fossil fuel, one of those two pollutants, ozone, isn’t expected to drop below today’s levels until after about 2050.

James Ryalls at the University of Reading led the review with colleagues Jacob Bishop and Carol Wagstaff. In an interview with Earth.com, Ryalls said the result that surprised him most was what the two gases did together.

“It was the first time this interaction had been quantified for pollinators,” he said.

Flowers advertise with chemicals that drift off their petals, and pollinators follow those chemicals to the food. Ozone and nitrogen oxides react with the scent molecules in the air. They break some down and change others, so the trail an insect is following gets weaker and stranger.

Researchers have also linked the gases to duller antennae, poorer learning and memory, and weaker colonies in bees. However, the review ranks that direct damage behind the scent damage.

Ozone is a gas that forms in sunlit air from other pollutants. Nitrogen oxides come mostly from engines, and diesel engines above all.

Ryalls’s team pulled together every experiment it could find that measured flower pollinators in clean air against polluted air. There were 22 studies in all, with 87 comparisons between them.

Most used honeybees or bumblebees. A few used a hawkmoth, a fig wasp or a mix of wild insects, and the setups ranged from lab chambers to greenhouses to open fields with pollutants piped in.

Across those studies, increased ozone lowered pollinator performance by an average of 42 percent, and increased nitrogen oxides lowered it by 46 percent.

Performance here covers three things: how well insect pollinators found flowers, how well they fed on them, and how many turned up.

Only three research teams tested both gases at once, and in those experiments the average loss was 68 percent. That is worse than either gas alone, but well short of the 88 percent you would get by simply adding the two. The gases don’t stack.

Ryalls had an explanation for the gap. “This reflects real atmospheric chemistry, where ozone and nitrogen oxides can partially react with and offset each other,” he told Earth.com.

In the three studies that measured each gas and the pair against the same clean-air control, the extra harm from adding the second gas was smaller still.

The damage was not spread evenly across what insects do. Searching efficiency, the measure of how well a pollinator locates flowers, fell by 67 percent on average. Feeding, once an insect reached a flower, and the number of insects present changed little.

Ryalls said he wanted people to notice that pattern. The insects were not sickened so much as lost.

Specialists have it worst. A bee that visits many kinds of flower can switch cues when one scent breaks down. An insect tied to one plant, like the tobacco hawkmoth or the yucca moth, has no backup.

Timing matters too. Ozone peaks in daylight, when bees and butterflies are out. That is bad timing for crops such as almonds and oilseed rape, whose flowers open in daylight when ozone peaks.

In contrast, nitrogen oxides are often highest at night, when moths do their pollinating. So the two groups face different pollutants, and cutting one gas doesn’t protect both.

Burning less fossil fuel cuts nitrogen oxides. Ozone, however, is made in the air from several ingredients, including methane and carbon monoxide, and those stay high for longer.

Under the middle-of-the-road emissions path used by the IPCC, the authors expect ground-level ozone to stay elevated through mid-century and only drop below today’s levels after about 2050.

On a high-emissions path, ozone is predicted to keep rising into the 2060s. It will only fall below today’s levels near the end of the century.

Diesel vehicles complicate the picture further, because a diesel sold today can stay on the road for more than 20 years. Britain’s decision to push its ban on new gasoline and diesel cars from 2030 to 2035 is the kind of delay the authors said will stretch the hard years out.

One oddity cuts the other way: in cities, nitrogen oxides break down some of the ozone, a buffer that fades in the countryside where there is less exhaust.

“Most of the food we grow depends on healthy pollinators, so protecting them from pollution cannot wait until 2050,” Ryalls said.

Asked by Earth.com what gardeners and farmers can do, Ryalls said cutting local nitrogen oxide emissions would help most.

He suggested replacing older diesel machinery and generators, keeping high-emission equipment idle during peak flowering, expanding green spaces, and letting lawns run wild and flower.

The evidence is lopsided. Of the 22 studies, 19 were done in Europe and 16 used bees or their relatives. That matters because about 70 percent of crop species get some benefit from insect pollination. The countries with the dirtiest air, such as India and China, are the ones likely to be hit hardest.

Flies, beetles and most moths are barely tested, and the tropics, where pollution is often heavier and more insects are generalists, are almost absent from research studies.

The doses were high as well: the average ozone level in the experiments was 161 parts per billion, more than twice the U.S. limit of 70. Harm clearly happened at those levels, but the studies can’t say where it begins.

Whether it starts at a sharp threshold or builds gradually is still an open question, and so is where a safe level might be.

Ryalls wants the next experiments run where the air is already dirty. “I’d like to test these effects in higher-pollution regions and tropical systems, where baseline pollutant levels are often higher and pollinator communities differ,” he said.

His group is also testing treatments that could boost the scents flowers give off, to help insects find them in polluted air.

The full study was published in the journal Frontiers in Ecology and the Environment.
