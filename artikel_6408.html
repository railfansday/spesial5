More parents say no to vitamin K shots, and bleeding cases in babies are rising

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Vitamin K deficiency rose among U.S. infants as fewer newborns received the shot that helps prevent dangerous bleeding.

Most babies in the United States get a vitamin K shot soon after birth to protect them from serious bleeding. But a growing share are missing it.

A new study found that recorded diagnoses of vitamin K deficiency more than tripled among babies between 2017 and 2025.

Diagnoses of bleeding linked to the deficiency more than quadrupled.

Elizabeth Wolf, a children’s doctor at Children’s Hospital of Richmond at VCU, led the study. She is also an associate professor in VCU’s Department of Pediatrics.

“We knew from prior studies that vitamin K refusal had been increasing in the U.S., and we wanted to learn if there were any real-life consequences of this trend,” Wolf said.

Vitamin K helps blood form clots that stop bleeding. Having too little is called vitamin K deficiency.

Babies start life with very little of this vitamin. Only small amounts pass from a mother to her baby before birth.

Newborns also lack the established gut bacteria that help produce vitamin K. Breast milk contains only small amounts, too.

The American Academy of Pediatrics has advised giving every newborn a vitamin K shot since 1961. The shot cuts the rate of bleeding that starts a week or more after birth by 98%.

Without enough vitamin K, a baby may struggle to stop bleeding. Doctors call this vitamin K deficiency bleeding.

One form starts between two days and one week after birth. Babies may bruise or bleed from the belly button.

Another form starts between one week and six months. It can affect breastfed babies who miss the shot and babies whose bodies have trouble taking in vitamin K.

Among babies with this later form, 30% to 60% have bleeding inside the skull. This can cause seizures, lasting brain damage, or death.

The team used Cosmos, a large collection of health records from places that use Epic software.

It brings together records from more than 2,300 hospitals and 50,000 clinics and other care sites, with names and other identifying details removed.

The study included about 11 million babies up to six months old who received care between 2017 and 2025.

All had been born at 35 weeks of pregnancy or later.

The team checked when doctors first found low vitamin K or bleeding linked to it, and whether babies also had bleeding inside the skull or brain.

The number of births in the records roughly doubled during the study. To make a fair comparison, the team worked out how many cases occurred for every 100,000 babies.

In 2017, 97.7% of newborns had a recorded vitamin K shot. By 2025, that fell to 95.0%, so the share without a recorded shot had more than doubled.

Low vitamin K rose from 11.8 to 37.3 cases per 100,000 babies. Bleeding linked to low vitamin K rose from 24.0 to 107.4 cases per 100,000.

Both rates were highest in 2025. That year, a little more than one in every 1,000 babies born in the records had bleeding linked to low vitamin K.

Doctors found most cases during the first week of life. That was true for 62% of babies with low vitamin K and 88% of those with bleeding.

About 97% of all babies in the study had a record of getting the shot. But among babies with low vitamin K, only 39.7% did.

Among babies with bleeding linked to low vitamin K, only 19.2% had a recorded shot. Babies without a recorded shot were a small part of the full group, yet they made up most of the bleeding cases.

Some babies also had bleeding inside the skull or brain. This affected 6.9% of babies with low vitamin K and 2.4% of those with bleeding linked to it.

An earlier study found that the share of newborns who missed the shot rose from 2.9% in 2017 to 5.2% in 2024. A study in Sweden also linked missing the shot to bleeding in babies.

Wolf’s team pointed to growing distrust of public health after the COVID-19 pandemic. They also noted more doubts about vaccines and more worries about medical care given to newborns.

The authors wrote that the vitamin K shot “may be a victim of its own success.” Because it has made this bleeding problem rare, many families may not know how dangerous it can be.

“Similar to the recent measles outbreaks, we are seeing the real-world implications of growing mistrust of doctors and public health professionals,” Wolf said.

“We need clear messaging from all levels of public health about the benefits of vitamin K administration for newborns.”

Some countries give vitamin K by mouth, either as the usual choice or as another option. These include France, Germany, the Netherlands, Switzerland, and Canada.

Vitamin K taken by mouth can work less well than the shot at preventing bleeding that starts later.

Still, the authors said U.S. doctors may need to consider it as a second choice if more parents refuse the shot.

The study cannot prove that fewer shots caused more bleeding. It also left out babies who received care outside health systems using Epic, so it may not show the full picture across the country.

Some records may have listed the wrong diagnosis or missed bleeding inside the skull. The team also looked for other reasons that might explain the rise.

Breastfeeding rates changed little over the past decade. Diseases that can cause low vitamin K, such as cystic fibrosis, did not become more common.

Wolf noted that these problems could keep rising if more parents refuse the shot.

“We need to better understand why parents are refusing vitamin K and what can be done to improve the uptake of this important medication,” she said.

The full study was published in the journal JAMA Pediatrics.
