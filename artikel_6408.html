Common blood pressure drug may activate the body’s calorie-burning fat

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Scientists discover that losartan, a common blood pressure drug, may activate calorie-burning brown fat and offer unexpected health benefits.

Doctors prescribe losartan to lower blood pressure, but new research suggests the medication may have another unexpected benefit.

In the medical records of more than 100,000 cancer patients, people on the drug were more likely to have active brown fat, the kind that burns calories to make heat.

In mice and in lab-grown fat cells, losartan switched that fat on through a pathway separate from the one that lowers blood pressure.

That makes losartan a candidate for a job no approved drug does today, turning up brown fat in people.

Active brown fat has been linked to lower rates of diabetes and heart disease. The new study also found associations with lower rates of anxiety and depression.

Paul Cohen, who heads the Laboratory of Molecular Metabolism at Rockefeller University, led the work.

First author Nicolás Gómez-Banoy is an endocrinologist in his lab who also holds posts at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center and Weill Cornell Medicine.

The patient data can only show that losartan users had more brown fat, not that the drug caused it, and the direct tests were in mice and in cells.

Brown fat burns calories to make heat, so it takes up the sugar tracer used in PET scans. That makes it visible on the scans cancer patients get to track their disease.

The team pulled every such scan from Memorial Sloan Kettering between 2018 and 2024 and added them to an earlier set, for 101,243 patients in all.

Brown fat appeared on at least one scan in 8,897 of them, just under 9%. It was more common in women, in younger patients, and on colder days.

Each patient with brown fat was matched to roughly two without it, on age, sex, body mass index, and the outdoor temperature on the day of the scan.

The team then compared 511 diagnosis codes between the two groups. The cancer itself and its treatment were controlled for.

Of 98 diagnoses that differed between the groups, 92 were less common in people with brown fat.

The strongest were type 2 diabetes, at less than half the odds, and chronic ischemic heart disease, at about half.

Anxiety disorders and depression were on the list too, with odds about a fifth and a third lower.

“We know that metabolically healthy people tend to have lower incidence of psychiatric diseases, so it could be that having brown fat makes you more metabolically healthy, and this correlates with fewer psychiatric issues,” Gómez-Banoy said.

The six diagnoses more common with brown fat were mostly skin conditions, and people with brown fat also had slightly lower bone density in the spine.

The team listed both as possible downsides to study.

A machine learning tool measured how much brown fat each person had and where.

Brown fat in the armpits was tied to the lowest odds of high blood pressure. It was also associated with the least fat around the organs, the belly fat that carries the most risk.

The team then checked 125 medications the patients were taking a month before their scan. Thirty-nine lined up with brown fat one way or the other.

Two kinds of drugs already known to dampen brown fat, beta blockers and the antidepressants called SSRIs, came out on the negative side.

Losartan and its cousin valsartan came out on the positive side: 2.4% of patients with brown fat were on losartan, against 1.9% of those without.

“The scope of this new analysis is far more expansive,” Cohen said. “We doubled our number of patients and considered more than 500 codes, which allowed us to unearth many new insights.”

Losartan blocks a receptor for the hormone angiotensin, which is how it lowers blood pressure. In brown fat cells, it still worked after that receptor was knocked down, so the fat effect runs through something else.

The experiments pointed to a protein called PPAR-gamma, a master switch involved in regulating fat cells.

Losartan turned it on, and a chemical that blocks PPAR-gamma blunted losartan’s effect.

The version of losartan doing the work was a breakdown product that doesn’t touch the blood pressure receptor at all.

“The fact that its different effects are mediated by different molecular pathways is advantageous, since it means we could potentially isolate them in future therapies,” Gómez-Banoy said.

“That way you would be able to stimulate brown fat in people with normal blood pressure.”

In mice, 10 days of losartan in the drinking water raised energy use and oxygen consumption. That held at room temperature and in the cold, without changing body weight or activity.

After two weeks on the drug, the brown fat between a mouse’s shoulders made more heat when it was stimulated.

Everyone in the study had cancer. Brown fat tracked with some cancers and some treatments, so the team controlled for both but can’t rule them out.

The scans were taken without any cold exposure, which likely undercounted brown fat.

A PET scan measures sugar uptake, not heat, and the two don’t always agree. The mouse experiments were small, with five to 10 animals per group, and the direct tests of the drug stop at mice.

“There were plenty of other medications associated with brown fat activity in our large cohort,” Cohen said. “This is just a start.”

Whether losartan’s brown fat effect adds up to anything for a patient’s weight, blood sugar, or mood is the open question.

The researchers say prospective studies in people are needed to determine whether these effects translate into meaningful health benefits.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.
