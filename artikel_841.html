The Women’s World Cup is coming to the UK – but what will it look like?

The United Kingdom has been confirmed as the hosts of the 2035 Women’s World Cup, in what is set to be the largest single-sport event ever to be staged in the country.

The joint “All Together” bid from the football associations of the four home nations – England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland – was approved by acclamation and officially announced during an online meeting of the Fifa Congress on Monday, although it was the only bidder in the process.

The tournament will nevertheless be the first Women’s World Cup to be staged in the UK, as well as the first football World Cup to be held in the UK since England hosted the men’s World Cup in 1966.

Although the UK will be hosting the men’s Euros in 2028, the 2035 Women’s World Cup will be twice the size in terms of competition format: with 48 teams playing 104 matches across a period of five weeks, and around 4.5 million tickets set to be available for fans.

It also promises to build on the legacy of the Lionesses, who won the Euros at Wembley in 2022 and have played in three consecutive major tournament finals – also reaching the Women’s World Cup final in 2023 and retaining the Euros in 2025.

“On behalf of the Football Associations of England, Northern Ireland, Scotland, and Wales, thank you for your trust and confidence in awarding us the Fifa Women’s World Cup 2035,” FA chair Debbie Jevans said following the confirmation.

“We’re deeply honoured by your support, and grateful for the opportunity to host the pinnacle of women’s football on behalf of the global football family. Together, we will celebrate the power of women’s football, inspire the next generation and unite fans from every corner of the world.

“We look forward to welcoming you to the United Kingdom in 2035 and to deliver an exceptional Fifa Women’s World Cup that the entire football community can be proud of.”

The Women’s World Cup only expanded to 32 teams ahead of the tournament in Australia and New Zealand in 2023; by 2031, it will expand to 48 teams – matching the format of the men’s World Cup – for the tournament in the United States, Mexico, Costa Rica and Jamaica. The competition will have doubled in size in the space of 16 years.

With such a strong portfolio of existing stadiums across the country, the UK’s bid “significantly exceeded” the minimum requirement of 14 venues. The original proposal includes 16 stadiums in England, three in Wales, two in Scotland and one in Northern Ireland, and some that have yet to be built.

Manchester United have signalled their ambition to host the 2035 Women’s World Cup final in their planned new 100,000-capacity stadium. But United’s timeline around the construction of the new £2bn stadium, or who is going to pay for it, remains ambiguous, so the current Old Trafford was included in the UK’s bid as well.

If the new stadium is built in time, it could mean the Women’s World Cup final is held outside London, with Wembley previously hosting the men’s World Cup final in 1966 as well as men’s and women’s Euros finals in 1996, 2021, 2022 and 2028. Prime minister Andy Burnham said he wanted Manchester to host the final when he was previously mayor of the city.

Birmingham City’s proposed Sports Quarter Stadium was also named in the UK’s bid, with plans for a 62,000-capacity stadium unveiled last year. Chelsea are exploring whether to expand Stamford Bridge or move to a new home in Earl’s Court, with their development also included in the UK’s bid.

As for existing stadiums in England, Aston Villa’s Villa Park, Brighton’s Amex Stadium, Bristol City’s Ashton Gate, Leeds United’s Elland Road, Everton’s Hill Dickinson Stadium, Manchester City’s Etihad Stadium, Newcastle’s St James’ Park, Sunderland’s Stadium of Light and Nottingham Forest’s City Ground add to an ambitious list of venues.

There are four further London venues on the list: Wembley, which could also host the final, Arsenal’s Emirates Stadium, Crystal Palace’s Selhurst Park and the Tottenham Hotspur Stadium. The Principality Stadium in Cardiff, Cardiff City’s Cardiff City Stadium and Wrexham’s Racecourse Ground are named, while Hampden Park in Glasgow, Easter Road in Edinburgh and Windsor Park in Belfast complete the proposed venues.

The final list of host cities and venues would be confirmed by Fifa closer to the start of the tournament, but the UK’s bid team said that 63 million people live within two hours of a proposed venue, making the World Cup the “most accessible tournament ever”.

By the time of the 2035 World Cup, many of the England players who played such an important role in the rise of women’s football by winning the Euros at Wembley in 2022 and becoming role models for the next generation will have likely hung up their boots.

Given that Lucy Bronze appears determined to appear at another major tournament for England next summer at the age of 35, it is not inconceivable that a 33-year-old Lauren James, or a 34-year-old Hannah Hampton, or a 35-year-old Lauren Hemp, or a 36-year-old Alessia Russo could still be representing the Lionesses by then.

But it is far more likely that the majority of the Lionesses squad for the World Cup, a tournament they have yet to win, will be built from players who are only just coming through the England pathway now and are currently representing teams from the Under-17s to the Under-23s.

Sarina Wiegman revealed she was excited about the potential of the next generation when naming her latest England squad last week. Midfielder Erica Meg Parkinson, 18, has kept her place after making her debut last month, while Chelsea’s Lexi Potter, 20, has also enjoyed a breakout year.

“Hopefully these talents go from high potential to world-class players,” Wiegman said. “While I’m talking about that, I’m getting excited. It does excite me. I hope I have a contribution to that, but it starts with the players themselves.”

After the initial wave brought through by the Lionesses, the next generation also now have a major target to aim for with a home World Cup in 2035. It could be game-changing for player development, while current stars such as Maya Le Tissier and Michelle Agyemang will look to further solidify their place in the England set-up in the years to come.

“The thought of another generation growing up knowing that the biggest stage in the game is coming to their home country is amazing,” Agyemang said. “To have the chance to play in a home World Cup would be a dream, but even more exciting is thinking about all the girls watching who might see 2035 and believe they can be part of it one day.”

One of the most pivotal figures in English football over the last decade has been Wiegman, who led the Lionesses to three consecutive major tournament finals with her run as a manager stretching to five when you include previous achievements with the Netherlands. The FA recently announced that Wiegman had extended her contract with England through to the end of Euro 2029, which will mean she had been in the job for eight years. “I tend to think that’s the end point, but you never know,” Wiegman said when asked if she could stay beyond 2029. “I don’t plan that far ahead. So let’s see where it gets to.”

Wiegman will leave enormous shoes to fill when she eventually leaves England and the task of replacing one of the best coaches in the game is a sizeable one for the FA, but there is currently no obvious succession plan. The FA should be able to produce up-and-coming English coaches as well as players, but all the top jobs in the Women’s Super League are filled by foreign managers.

However, two attractive candidates could emerge in the shape of former England international Casey Stoney, who is now in charge of Canada, while Emma Hayes, after a decade at Chelsea, is preparing to lead the United States into next summer’s World Cup. Whether they could be available in future years depends on how they perform in the next two cycles. But knowing England will be hosts of the 2035 World Cup heightens the need for the FA to plan for life after Wiegman, too.

The hope for Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland has to be that the World Cup can act as a catalyst for the growth of women’s football at home, in the same way as the Lionesses winning the Euros at Wembley in 2022 did for England.

In terms of grassroots participation and the development of domestic leagues, England’s victory four years ago was a boost for the other home nations, too, but nothing compares to the feeling of staging games in front of a nation on home soil.

The tournament could be transformative, especially when you consider that the other home nations cannot take playing in major tournaments for granted. Scotland have only appeared at a Women’s World Cup once, in 2019, while Wales made history by qualifying for their first major tournament when they reached Euro 2025 and Northern Ireland did the same when they made it through to Euro 2022.

“Wales has been a football nation for 150 years and we have never hosted anything on this scale,” said Noel Mooney, the chief executive of the Football Association of Wales. “World Cup football played here, in front of Welsh children, would change what this game looks like in Wales: in the clubs, in the schools, and in the number of girls who grow up seeing a pathway into it.”

“A Women’s World Cup in Northern Ireland would be felt in every club and every age group, in every part of the country,” the Irish Football Association’s chief executive Patrick Nelson said. “Our young players would grow up having watched the best in the world play at home, and there is no substitute for that.”

Scottish Football Association chief executive Ian Maxwell said hosting the World Cup would be “the most significant thing to happen to the women’s and girls’ game in Scotland”. He added: “The impact would go far beyond just the matches themselves. The level of investment into coaching, facilities and player pathways in the years beforehand will shape the future of the game for the generations to come.”

The announcement of the Women’s World Cup hosts was made by Fifa president Gianni Infantino, who lost the support of the English FA, Scottish FA, Welsh FA and Irish FA following his scrapped World Cup sell-off plan earlier this year. The FA chair, Debbie Hewitt, recently confirmed that they considered launching a legal challenge to Infantino’s eligibility to stand for a fourth term as Fifa president, highlighting the delicate politics that were at play while the bid for the 2035 Women’s World Cup was considered.

Infantino is standing for re-election in March and, under current rules, would be ineligible to run for a fifth term from 2031. Infantino should therefore be out of the picture by the time the UK hosts the World Cup in 2035, but they will have at least four years of working together on the World Cup if the Fifa president win his re-election bid. Infantino retains the support of the majority of Fifa’s member associations, but it’s a different story in the UK and prime minister Andy Burnham has previously called for his resignation.

There is already one area that could lead to some friction between the UK government and Fifa. The home nations’ bid received a score of 4.3 out of five in Fifa’s evaluation report. All but one of the categories – which include stadiums, transport and sustainability – were deemed “low risk” but the exception was “contractual framework”, with Fifa’s report stating “certain government guarantees contain material qualifications, particularly regarding tax and safety and security”.

The Guardian has reported that Fifa has demanded tax-free status from ticket sales and broadcast and commercial partnerships, something that the UK government has not allowed when previously hosting major events such as the 2012 Olympics and will not offer to Uefa when they stage Euro 2028. The Independent reported extensively on the huge cost many US cities faced by hosting men’s World Cup matches last summer, and the UK will be keen to ensure that it will not face similar shortfalls in nine years’ time.

The 2035 World Cup will now act as the cornerstone for transforming football for women and girls in the UK, with the government working with the football associations from the four countries on three areas: making the game more accessible to girls and under-represented communities, developing coaching, officiating and leadership, and attracting more investment into women’s football.

A joint statement from the chief executives of the four associations said: “What follows now is eight and a half years of work; on facilities, on coaching, on officiating, on the pathways that take a girl from her first game to a professional contract. The players who will light up 2035 are in our parks and playgrounds right now.”
