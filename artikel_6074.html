We Need a Better GOP

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

President Donald Trump attends the Republican National Midterm Convention in Dallas, Texas, September 9, 2026.

The old Republican establishment could be out of touch, unimaginative, and too comfortable with revolving-door practices that enriched former officeholders and staffers, among other failings.

Beginning in 2016, Donald Trump burned this establishment to the ground and created a new one that has its own myriad deficiencies, some of which echo the failings of yore; the extremely online elements of today’s establishment, for instance, have found a whole new way to be out of touch.

We say this not to pine for yesterday’s establishment — parties never go back, only forward — but to make the point that every party elite reaches its point of exhaustion, and that’s where the Trump establishment is now.

There is a robust intramural argument on the right over how to interpret the historic loss in the midterms. We have our own views, but the most elemental need is a better GOP — one that is not beholden to the will of one man, doesn’t tolerate corruption, insists on strict adherence to the rule of law, encourages Congress to take up its allotted role in our constitutional system, rewards policy entrepreneurship on the part of officeholders, and focuses on results rather than performative provocations.

It’s possible to imagine any number of people leading such a national party, and it could have different ideological colorations depending on that leader and underlying circumstances. All that is TBD and should be a matter of fierce debate and competition. But unless the party moves beyond today’s establishment and renews itself, it risks subsequent devastating votes of no confidence from the American public.

Consider the issue of corruption, which was one of the biggest concerns of voters in the midterms. It is one thing for a party to suffer politically in the course of making progress on an important priority — say, stepped-up immigration enforcement. It is another to suffer from the perception — and, sadly, the reality — that politically connected people are engaged in untoward schemes for their own enrichment. The former is an inevitable political trade-off; the latter is a party’s elite feasting on its power and connections with no heed to the consequences for the larger cause.

President Trump, who has an overawing dominance over the party, is the source of many of the GOP’s current discontents. His influence is not going to drastically diminish anytime soon, and we know that many good Republicans have considered him better than the alternative, or have thrilled to his no-holds-barred assaults on the left. However one evaluates the Trump phenomenon, though, attempts to rely on the tropes that fueled his rise beginning in 2016, or ape his political style, will almost certainly fail — for the simple reasons that times change and Trump’s persona is not replicable.

The GOP could do much worse than look to the states, where Republican governors and legislators have cut taxes, restrained spending, pared back regulations, protected unborn children, and pursued reforms of schooling and housing. One reason that Florida constituted high ground beyond the reach of the blue wave is that it has been so well governed for so long, and it has had a series of reformist Republican governors focused on results.

At the national level, the GOP obviously needs a cost-of-living agenda that addresses high costs in health care, medicine, and education, and there’s much work that’s been done on these issues at the state level and among conservative policy thinkers. But arriving at a coherent agenda will require a seriousness of purpose and substantive focus that have been lacking for years now. That doesn’t mean the party should be dull and conventional — much can be learned from Trump’s zest for combat and vivid political branding — but it needs to recover a sense of institutional self-respect.

The new establishment, via President Trump, has worked its way back around to what were widely considered weaknesses of the old one. Republicans were once determined to make another push for amnesty after every election loss, and now, the president has floated that idea in response to the Democratic midterm victory; Republicans were once too soft on China, and Trump has been notably conciliatory in his second term; the GOP once talked a good game on deficit spending but didn’t do nearly enough to curtail it, while we are now adding to the debt hand over fist during a time of economic expansion and relative peace; the Tom DeLay–led Republican House was much too cozy with lobbyists, but prior GOP ethical lapses have been eclipsed over the past two years.

The old establishment had reached its expiration date and, regardless, isn’t coming back, while the current establishment is harmful to the party’s image and interests and an obstacle to change.

As Lincoln said, it is a time to think anew and act anew. The GOP desperately needs something fresh and different, with ideas and policies designed to meet the challenges of the day.
