Pornography Perception: Shame Intensifies Distress Over Use

How much pornography is too much? People often assume that the more someone watches, the more likely they are to develop an escalating, progressive addiction.

A new study by psychologist Joshua Grubbs and colleagues complicates that story. Their findings suggest that understanding perceived problematic pornography use requires looking beyond frequency to people’s beliefs about what they are doing. The researchers also found that periods of unusually high perceived problems tended to be followed by a return toward the person’s usual level, challenging assumptions of inevitable worsening.

Published in the flagship Journal of Psychopathology and Clinical Science, the study followed American adults across five assessments over two years. Its analyses included 2,903 people who reported pornography use during at least one assessment.

Researchers measured how frequently participants watched pornography, how strongly they morally disapproved of it, and their self-reported problematic pornography use, or PPU. That last measure assessed experiences such as feeling unable to stop using pornography. It measured perceived problems rather than establishing a clinical diagnosis.

The central concept was moral incongruence: the shame and conflict people experience when their behavior violates their values. Someone who believes pornography is wrong but continues watching it may experience distress and interpret their distress over doing something they regret as evidence of addiction.

The researchers examined both differences between people and changes within the same person. That distinction matters. Asking whether frequent viewers report more problems than occasional viewers is different from asking whether someone feels more troubled during periods when they watch more than usual.

People who morally disapproved of pornography generally watched it less often. Among those who watched porn, however, greater moral disapproval was associated with more perceived problems.

Moral disapproval also amplified the relationship between viewing frequency and perceived problems. When participants watched more than their own usual amount, the association with perceived problems was roughly three times stronger among people with high moral disapproval than among those with low disapproval.

As Grubbs explained in an emailed statement about the findings:

“It's not just that people who disapprove of pornography more are more likely to interpret their own use as problematic; it's that people interpret fluctuations in their own use in light of those deeply held moral beliefs.”

Imagine two people who each increase their viewing by a similar amount. Someone who regards pornography as acceptable might experience little additional concern. Someone who believes it is morally wrong might interpret that increase as evidence of losing control.

An important qualification: the study did not find that frequency mattered only when moral incongruence was present. The association remained positive at lower levels of moral disapproval. The finding was that underlying moral disapproval substantially strengthened that relationship.

The broader message remains consequential: frequency alone cannot tell us whether someone has a pornography problem. The meaning people attach to their behavior helps shape how problematic it feels.

Because this was an observational study, it cannot prove that moral beliefs caused the reported problems. It does show why simply counting viewing occasions provides an incomplete picture.

Another finding challenges a familiar narrative: that pornography problems necessarily become progressively worse.

When participants reported PPU above their own usual level, they tended to report relatively lower PPU six months later. The researchers described this as regression toward the mean.

In everyday language, an unusually bad period tended to be followed by a less extreme one, probably because men often increase use of porn when they are struggling emotionally due to life events, and such life events often subside or lessen over time.

Likewise, watching more pornography than usual at one assessment did not reliably predict greater perceived problems six months later. The relationship between increased viewing and increased concern appeared primarily at the same assessment, rather than demonstrating a clear chain of future deterioration.

These findings are inconsistent with a simple story in which every increase in viewing signals a progressive downward spiral.

They also require careful interpretation. Returning toward one’s usual level does not necessarily mean the person has no struggles with pornography use. Someone’s usual level may still involve considerable distress. Overall differences between people were fairly stable, and the study did not establish that everyone improved or that persistent problems resolve without help.

This was also a community sample, rather than a study specifically of patients seeking treatment. Its findings cannot determine the course of every severe clinical case.

Still, a temporary spike in perceived problems should not automatically be treated as evidence of an escalating disorder.

People distressed about pornography deserve to have that distress taken seriously. Understanding its source is essential.

A useful assessment explores whether someone is experiencing persistent difficulty controlling their behavior, concrete consequences, conflict with personal values, or some combination. Moral conflict and difficulties with control can coexist.

Addressing distress rooted in moral incongruence should respect the person’s values while helping them examine shame and frightening interpretations of their behavior. Assuming that every unwanted viewing episode proves addiction risks reinforcing the very distress bringing someone into treatment.

This research encourages a more careful question than “How often do you watch?”

Instead, let's ask: What happens in your life when you watch—and what does watching porn mean to you?

Reference: Grubbs, J. B., Floyd, C. G., Carrasquillo, K. D., & Kraus, S. W. (2026). The dual-level dynamics of moral incongruence: A multilevel analysis of problematic pornography use. Journal of Psychopathology and Clinical Science. Advance online publication.
