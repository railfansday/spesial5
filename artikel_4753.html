In the words often attributed to the Buddha, “Nothing ever exists entirely alone; everything is in relation to everything else.”

Consider a tree. It can only grow by drawing nutrients from the soil below while relying on rain and sunlight from above. Its roots interact with fungi and microorganisms underground, while its branches provide shelter and nourishment for other living things.

We humans are no different. Our thoughts, feelings, and behaviors don't exist in isolation. They are continually shaped by our relationships and environments. In turn, our own actions, words, tone of voice, and body language influence the people around us, often in ways we don't immediately recognize.

Understanding this interconnectedness is a core competency of spiritual intelligence. [i] It also offers a practical way to understand why we sometimes find ourselves repeating the same frustrating patterns in our relationships, families, and everyday lives.

My appreciation for systems thinking began during my junior year at MIT in a class taught by organizational theorist Peter Senge.[ii]

Systems thinking invites us to look beyond individual events and recognize the larger networks of relationships in which they occur.[iii] Every action produces reactions elsewhere, and those reactions feed back into the system again. Of course, we only discussed this framework in the context of business, operations, and leadership, but I was immediately struck by how much this perspective explained about human behavior on the whole.

I see it all the time in the couples I counsel. One partner feels unheard and raises their voice in frustration. The other gets defensive and withdraws, protecting themselves. That withdrawal makes the first partner feel more ignored, so they try even harder and more forcefully to break through the "wall," only perpetuating the cycle. From either person's perspective, the problem may appear to be the other's behavior. But when we look at the relationship as a system, we begin to see something different: each person's response is influencing the other's, creating a loop that neither necessarily intends.

It made sense to me to learn that such system dynamics and feedback loops are the heart of several attachment-based couples therapy modalities such as Psychobiological Approach to Couple Therapy (PACT)[iv] and Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT)[v].

At the heart of systems thinking are two fundamental dynamics: positive and negative feedback loops. Despite their names, positive and negative don't necessarily mean good or bad.

Negative feedback loops stabilize a system, like a thermostat adjusting the temperature in a room. Positive feedback loops, meanwhile, reinforce existing patterns, whether healthy or unhealthy. Appreciation can breed more appreciation, just as frustration can breed more frustration.

Consider what happens when someone close to you expresses genuine appreciation for something you've done. You feel seen and valued, which may inspire you to be more generous or attentive in return. Your response makes the other person feel appreciated, too.

Unfortunately, the reverse is equally possible. Imagine walking into the kitchen and seeing that your partner has left dirty dishes all over the counter. You gripe, "Why do you always leave your dishes for me to clean?" Formulated as a question, through a frustrated tone of voice, this statement is likely to be interpreted by your partner as a criticism. They get defensive and a fight ensues. Alternatively, a more authentic and vulnerable response might sound like, "I am feeling exhausted and disconnected, and I need your help in keeping our space clean. Would you be willing to put your dishes in the dishwasher when done?”

These patterns can become so familiar that we stop noticing them. We begin to see our partner as defensive, or intrusive, our child as difficult, or our friend as inconsiderate, without recognizing how our own reactions may be contributing to the very behaviors that frustrate us.

One of the most powerful lessons of systems thinking is that changing even one part of a system can alter the entire dynamic.

Suppose your child is struggling with schoolwork. Your frequent reminders to study could feel like pressure, making them more resistant, and their procrastination will only increase your frustration. Instead of pushing harder, you might ask what's making the work difficult. Responding with curiosity rather than frustration can help break the cycle.

This is where spiritual intelligence becomes especially valuable. Understanding our interconnectedness and interdependence, along with qualities such as self-awareness, compassion, humility, and presence, can help us recognize our automatic reactions and choose how we want to respond.

We may not be able to control how others behave, but we can become more conscious of what we contribute to the systems we share. And sometimes, that small change is enough to set something much larger in motion.
