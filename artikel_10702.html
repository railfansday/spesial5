‘Millennials need to retire,’ say frustrated Gen Z job seekers on TikTok

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Millennials need to retire. That’s the sentiment in certain corners of TikTok, where some Gen Zers struggling to find jobs are blaming the generation directly above them for their inability to get a foot on the corporate ladder. 

The problem is that millennials are only in their thirties and forties and not even close to retiring. And they don’t have anything to do with Gen Z’s struggles anyway.

A few videos have been doing the rounds in which Zoomers complain about their lack of job prospects. In one from earlier this year, a creator argued that millennials “monopolize the job market” because they know Gen Zers would surpass them if they were given the same opportunities.

Another lamented, “millennials are taking all the jobs that we went to school for,” claiming that Gen Zers, meanwhile, have to take lower-paying jobs in retail and fast-food restaurants. Others angrily suggest millennials “retire” so Gen Z can take the jobs for themselves.

Gen Z’s frustrations are valid. They have plenty to be worried about, from an entry-level job crisis to the AI boom. But none of this is millennials’ fault. 

Born between 1981 and 1996, many millennials were graduating college just after the 2008 financial crisis. Some entered a job market with few opportunities, and later experienced layoffs or had to pivot careers as industries were disrupted.

Fast-forward to now, and many are dealing with yet another period of economic instability while trying to manage the financial pressures of housing, bills, children, and aging parents.

Plenty of workers would gladly retire if they could afford to. Singer-songwriter Adiv, who also makes TikTok content, responded to some of Gen Z’s viral videos by arguing that life is “completely unaffordable” for most millennials.

“I’d love to retire but unfortunately for me, gas is $6 a gallon, the average house is $1 million for a shack, I am in student debt, I have credit card debt,” he said. “We’ve been in not one, not two, but three recessions. We’ve been in a war since we were born. We had 9/11, we had COVID, we have everything. Everything has happened to us, but you want me to retire before I’m even 50.”

So there’s a lot more going on than one generation hoarding another’s jobs. Earlier this year, college graduates faced the worst entry-level job market since the pandemic, with the underemployment rate among recent college graduates reaching 42.5%—its highest level since 2020, according to New York Fed data.

AI and automation are also putting pressure on the bottom rungs of the corporate ladder. The tasks that have traditionally been assigned to junior employees are being automated or absorbed by more senior workers, potentially eliminating the roles young people used to get to gain experience.

Even when jobs are available, young people can find themselves at the back of a long queue. New research from Kickresume highlights how employers are demanding years of experience for junior roles. In an analysis of 1,400 job postings, 34% of entry-level jobs in the U.S. require at least 18 months of experience, while a quarter expect candidates to have leadership skills.

It’s easy to see why Gen Zers are frustrated. But the reality is that we’re all trying to navigate a difficult labor market while also dealing with rising living costs and changing expectations around work.

Millennials haven’t broken the corporate ladder, pulled it up behind them, or simply refused to jump off. The real problem is that there are fewer opportunities to go around. Using one generation as a scapegoat isn’t going to change that.
