Can Your Callers Reach You? A Regulator Will Want The Evidence That Says So

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Liam Dunne is the Co-founder and CEO of Klearcom, a global leader in customer call path testing & real-time contact center optimization.

When a regulator looks at your voice channels, like interactive voice response (IVR) and toll-free numbers, it ultimately comes down to one thing: When someone needs to reach you, can they, and can you prove it?

Most contact center leaders I am in contact with can explain who owns the agents, the platform and the workforce plan. Ask, though, who owns the customer’s phone line, from the carrier to the last word of the call, and the room goes quiet. Someone points to IT, and someone else points to the carrier. That answer holds up okay until a regulator dials the number to actually check it.

Credit where credit is due. Compliance teams have grafted through cyber resilience, data protection and third-party risk programs. The voice channel tends to get left out of that work because it’s treated as plumbing. Yet in pharmaceuticals, banking and healthcare, it carries some of the most regulated conversations a business has, and more of them are now answered first by AI agents. A call doesn’t have to fail for a call to fail, and very few organizations can show which of their calls did.

Medicare’s 2026 Star Ratings still score health plans on whether a caller who needs an interpreter or TTY can get one on the phone. In April, CMS finalized a rule that drops that measure from 2028. The caller’s need stays where it was, though, and plans lose one of the few outside checks on their own phone lines. It’s like taking the smoke alarm down because there hasn’t been a fire in a while.

My company runs test calls into enterprise phone lines across more than 100 countries, and what we find rarely matches what an organization believes it has. When a U.S. health insurance customer of ours recently tested close to 200 of its numbers, 17 calls failed to connect, and four more played a leftover test recording where the menu should have been. That’s roughly one in 10 lines where a member could have rung and gotten nowhere.

In pharmaceuticals, the stakes get higher. U.S. rules give drug makers 15 calendar days to report a serious, unexpected adverse event to the FDA, as do the MHRA and the European Medicines Agency. In March, the FDA warned a major drug maker over patient deaths it hadn’t reported in time. That clock only starts once the company hears about the case. A call that never connects starts no clock at all, and if an AI agent mishears the caller or never passes the case to a person, the company is still on the hook.

On phone lines a life sciences customer of ours runs in China, nearly 15% of test calls failed to connect or played the wrong message. The problems clustered around the emergency option a trial doctor would use after a serious reaction. The team got stuck in and fixed it, but every caller who hit a failure in the meantime dropped out of the record.

The worst failures look all okay from the inside. For another customer, a toll-free number in Belgium stopped connecting while the desk behind it answered normally when dialed directly. Every internal check passed. Think of your car’s brakes: The dashboard tells you they should work, and the person standing behind the car tells you whether they do.

Banking regulators have been pretty clear on this: Hand a service to a supplier and the accountability stays with you. In March, U.K. regulators finalized a single framework for reporting incidents and material third-party arrangements, and the FCA’s first annual review of operational resilience flagged firms claiming they could recover from any scenario without evidence of testing anything severe enough. That’s marking your own homework.

A single call can pass through a carrier, an aggregator, a contact center platform, an IVR menu and an AI agent, each reporting on its own leg of the journey while nobody is watching the whole journey. Last month, a major U.K. bank apologized after an outage left customers unable to use its app, online banking, phone or chat lines.

A couple of years back, we saw a financial services customer of ours U.S. service lines starting to tell callers the call center was closed. The outsourced provider had stopped taking the calls and never said a word. They told us they wouldn’t have known without our human-verified alert.

Four habits can separate calm organizations from the ones left scrambling the jets.

Firstly, name one owner: a single person accountable for the voice channel from first ring to last word, across every supplier. Second, rank your lines, and hold those with a regulatory obligation, such as adverse event, fraud reporting and card blocking lines, to a higher standard.

Third, test like a caller. That means testing from outside your own systems and in-country, and where an AI agent answers, checking that it understands the caller, captures what it should and hands the call over to a person at the right moment. Finally, keep the record and retest after every change to routing, menus, platforms or AI models because that’s when things quietly break.

AI agents add another layer because a call can connect perfectly and still go wrong in the conversation. A recent Gartner survey reported by CMSWire found customers still expect a route to a person whenever a company puts AI on the line. Nobody has fully cracked that across every language, accent and edge case, us included, and it’s where most of our work is focused.

So, can someone reach you when they need to, and are you able to prove it? Leaders who can open a record in response will find that conversation a lot shorter.​
