What US Airlines Owe You After An Overnight Diversion: See The Full List

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

When a flight diverts late in the day, passengers can find themselves spending the night at an airport, sometimes hundreds or thousands of miles from their destination. What the airline provides in these situations, from hotel rooms and meals to ground transportation, varies between US carriers and depends largely on what caused the diversion.

Some of the rules are also set to change from January 2027, when a new US Department of Transportation (DOT) rule will reclassify several common causes of diversions. Here's what federal rules guarantee, what each major US airline promises passengers, and what the changes mean for travelers in the new year.

A recent high-profile example of diverted passengers being stranded overnight occurred earlier this month. On November 7, Alaska Airlines flight AS322 from Dallas/Fort Worth to Seattle diverted to Yakima, around 100 miles from Seattle, due to storms. However, the flight was then held overnight because of a fuel-related issue, leaving 137 passengers in a difficult situation.

Around 50 of those passengers would end up sleeping in cots provided by the Red Cross, as Alaska declined to provide hotel rooms or meal vouchers because the diversion was weather-related. The airline told Simple Flying that "for weather delays or cancellations, airlines typically do not provide accommodations," and no federal rule required it to do otherwise.

This is because federal protections for passengers stranded after a diversion remain limited. The DOT's tarmac delay rule requires airlines to let passengers off a diverted aircraft within three hours for domestic flights and four hours for international flights, while also ensuring access to food, water, working lavatories, and medical treatment.

Meanwhile, its automatic refund rule also entitles passengers to a refund if they reject an alternative following a significant change to their journey, which includes a change of arrival airport. However, neither rule requires airlines to provide hotel rooms or meals. Instead, passengers must largely rely on voluntary commitments from airlines through the DOT's Airline Cancellation and Delay Dashboard.

While some airlines explicitly include diversions in their accommodation policies, others only cover disruptions within their control, leaving passengers in a grey area when the cause is less clear. American Airlines is the only US carrier that explicitly names a diversion as a reason to provide a hotel.

It states that if "the disruption is our fault or you're diverted to another city," and passengers are not boarded before 11:59 PM local time on their scheduled arrival day, the airline will arrange or cover an approved hotel. It also does not clearly explain which provision applies when a diversion is caused by weather. JetBlue also includes diversions in its definition of a controllable disruption and provides a hotel when such a disruption delays arrival by at least three hours overnight.

Meanwhile, Delta Air Lines, United Airlines and Alaska Airlines take a different approach, only offering hotel accommodation for disruptions they are responsible for. Low-cost carriers generally offer less protection: for example, Frontier Airlines considers diversions within its control to be controllable disruptions, but says it does not provide hotel accommodation for delays or cancellations. However, it does offer meal vouchers after three hours.

The way airlines classify diversions could also have an impact on what passengers receive when they are stranded overnight. Currently, airlines report delays and cancellations using different cause codes, including an 'Air Carrier' code for disruptions attributed to the airline. However, under a new DOT rule coming into effect in January, ten events will be removed from this category.

Among these events are certain unscheduled maintenance, unruly passenger removals, medical emergencies, and aircraft damage caused by weather or foreign object debris. Consequently, this could make it easier for airlines to exclude certain incidents from warranting hotels or other assistance.
