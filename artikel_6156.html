Is Lufthansa Really Hiring 1,000 Pilots By 2033 While Cutting 1,000 Hub Jobs?

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Lufthansa has made headlines in recent weeks for multiple potential moves that would impact its workforce. On the one hand, proposed cuts could eat into its workforce at its two key hubs, Frankfurt Main Airport (FRA) and Munich Airport (MUC). On the other, an offer has been tabled that would guarantee new pilot hires ahead if accepted.

Each respective proposal involves reducing or adding the same number of staff members as the other. It would appear, therefore, that Lufthansa — Europe’s largest airline by fleet count — is heading toward two fairly drastic efforts that would see one area slimmed down for the other to be bolstered by equal measure. The reality is far more complex, though. While there is a sense of irony behind the overall thinking, each decision stems from an entirely isolated issue the carrier is navigating today. So, what is really happening?

On paper, yes, Lufthansa has suggested both cutting 1,000 jobs and hiring 1,000 pilots. Proposals put forward by Lufthansa to employees internally include the cuts, as reported initially around November 19. In contrast, proposals made to pilot union Vereinigung Cockpit and detailed by Lufthansa just a few days earlier on November 17 would see the airline commit to recruiting the pilots over the coming years.

The thinking behind each decision is not so straightforward, however. Cuts appear to have been tabled on the back of Lufthansa’s own deteriorating financial performance. In contrast, Lufthansa offering to hire new pilots looks to be an attempt to break a stalemate with the union that has followed months of talks. As such, the two are not necessarily completely interlinked.

Nonetheless, were both to materialize, it would indeed leave the airline in a position where it is reducing its workforce in one area, while bolstering it in another. It is unclear whether either will in fact materialize, with Lufthansa telling Simple Flying it had “nothing to add to these media reports” when approached for comment. What is clear is that this year has been tough on carriers, and heated union negotiations have added another headache in Lufthansa’s case.

The reasoning given behind the cuts is a worsening financial performance currently, not least due to rising fuel prices. In the letter to employees, obtained by the German Press Agency and reported on by various outlets, the airline’s board clearly laid out its intentions.

“At the Frankfurt and Munich hubs, we are planning to cut a total of 1,000 full-time positions within the Lufthansa Airlines subgroup,” the letter — signed by chief executive Jens Ritter among others — said. “Lufthansa Airlines is currently not competitive [and] we have reached a point where we need to make even more far-reaching changes,” it added.

The group’s latest financial results, released on November 3, backed up the bleak diagnosis. Stronger revenue and income during the first nine months of the year were clouded by higher operating expenses. Notably, fuel costs rocketed 21% to €6.7 billion ($7.4 billion), despite hedging and reduced consumption, including as flight and passenger numbers fell by 4% and 1%, respectively. As for subsidiary Lufthansa Airlines — where cuts are planned — an adjusted pre-tax loss of €180 million ($198 million) was recorded, against a profit of €147 million ($162 million) a year prior.

The cuts, which apply until October, will impact short-haul flights.

So, simply put, Lufthansa has found itself grappling with a deteriorating underlying performance at a group level, with its flagship business slipping into the red. It is in this context that staff reductions within the latter were floated to employees. But unfortunately for the carrier, that makes up just one of the areas where pressure has been building.

The years-long dispute with Vereinigung Cockpit — which initially related to pension scheme changes dating back to 2017, but has since evolved to cover the likes of job progression and security — has only added to its woes. Owing to the deadlock between the two, the union called pilot strikes at the carrier in February, March, and April. Subsequent second-quarter results from Lufthansa detailed that the industrial action had cost it at least €150 million ($165 million), and that six days' worth of strikes in April knocked capacity down 3%. Despite work towards a resolution since, talks between Vereinigung Cockpit and Lufthansa have collapsed recently.

A period of arbitration was followed by one of mediation that ended in mid-November, at which point a deal emerged that the union backed but the airline did not. Lufthansa instead tabled a counteroffer, which it said was based on arbitrators’ recommendations and included the proposal of 1,000 new cockpit hires. Vereinigung Cockpit responded in turn by setting a deadline of November 23 for the airline to withdraw it and return to the negotiating table, which then passed to no avail. That left the union to slam the airline the day after, when it wrote: “Seven months of exploratory talks, three rounds of conciliation, and a total of 18 rounds of discussions with the mediator have apparently been in vain.”

Whether the prospect of job cuts was raised because negotiations were reaching a tipping point has been questioned by Vereinigung Cockpit. “Coincidence? Hardly,” it noted in response to reports of the cuts, “what is being built up here is primarily a lot of threatening talk”. That said, it is no secret that the wider aviation industry has faced struggles this year.

KLM Royal Dutch Airlines, another European carrier, said in early November it would look to reduce its workforce by 15% in response to an “increasingly complex” operational environment. Shortly after, Australia’s Qantas signaled it would be cutting 850 corporate roles and outsourcing the work overseas.

Such measures are being taken in each case against the backdrop of a sustained increase in fuel prices since the outbreak of conflict in the Middle East earlier this year. To put that into perspective, the price of jet fuel averaged 71.9% more over the week to November 20 than it did in the same period a year before, per the International Air Transport Association. Needless to say, ballooning costs have been an issue for airlines globally of late.

Most of the cuts will impact German workers in administrative roles.

Unsurprisingly, Lufthansa did refer to such cost pressures upon telling staff of the cuts, writing: “The price of fuel exposes and exacerbates [...] structural weaknesses.” As with its offer to Vereinigung Cockpit, though, there are several questions over what actually comes next.

November’s third-quarter figures detailed that Lufthansa Airlines had 39,622 employees as of the end of September, meaning a reduction of 1,000 positions would mark a 2.5% cut to its workforce. Though stark, the results also showed there had been a 0.9% drop — equivalent to 341 employees — in headcount at the subsidiary since a year earlier. Aside from impacting “full-time positions,” Lufthansa has yet to detail how the roles will be axed, which will be affected, and over what timeline. Companies can avoid directly slashing jobs more gradually through attrition, for instance, a tool KLM said it was to use. Another thing to flag is Lufthansa’s announcement last year that 4,000 administrative positions were to go, and so whether the latest round of cuts would be linked to these remains to be seen.

As for its offer to create new cockpit positions, the headline figure of 1,000 new pilot hires arguably implies a more drastic expansion on Lufthansa's part than would actually materialize. The promised recruitment drive runs through 2033, giving the airline several years to fill the positions. However, previous figures from pilots' union Vereinigung Cockpit, cited by Aviation Direct, point to hundreds of age-related departures over the same period. Around 300 retirements are anticipated in 2026 and 2027 alone, with a further 740 expected between 2029 and 2034. Note that these cover both Lufthansa Classic — where the additions would be made — and Lufthansa Cargo. Either way, they suggest much of the recruitment could simply be needed anyway to replace departing personnel.

That could be by design, of course, with Lufthansa’s cost-saving efforts potentially set to go as far as reducing its mainline narrowbody fleet in favor of buoying long-haul operations. Ultimately, though, time will tell what comes next.

Indeed, Lufthansa has told staff 1,000 positions are to go. It has also floated the idea of committing to hiring 1,000 pilots in the coming years. Details of the former remain few and far between, however. The latter, meanwhile, has by no means been met with enthusiasm from the union it was designed to win over.
