Data Center Opposition, Power Demand, and the Economics of AI Infrastructure

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

On November 3, American voters faced 41 local ballot measures about data centers across ten states. They favored limiting them in about 40 of the 41. Five were outright bans. In Perry, Ohio a financial-guarantee requirement passed with more than 87% of the vote. In Granville it was 84%. Ashville voted to block a campus that was already under construction.

The obvious reading is that AI just lost a political fight, and that the fight will now be settled in Washington by whichever party is in charge.

I think that reading is wrong in three separate ways, and the third one is the only part that matters if you build things.

Gallup asked for the first time in March and found 71% oppose a data center near them, with majorities opposed in every single party group. Strongly opposed ran 56% among Democrats, 48% among independents, 39% among Republicans. For scale, 53% oppose a local nuclear plant. Data centers now poll worse than nuclear power.

The structure underneath is stranger than the toplines. Heatmap’s project-level data finds roughly 55% of opposed projects sit in red, sparsely populated areas, with opposed projects in Republican places up more than 300% in six months. Republican primary voters in 2026 removed pro-data-center Republicans in Calvert County, Maryland, Box Elder County, Utah, and Spartanburg County, South Carolina.

Meanwhile the officials who actually stopped statewide moratoriums were Democrats. Maine’s governor vetoed one. Michigan’s opposes the bills. New York’s declined to sign the one her legislature passed.

Whatever this is, no partisan variable predicts it. If you are modeling your siting risk off which party controls a chamber, you are modeling the wrong thing.

This is where most of the commentary goes wrong, and where I nearly went wrong myself.

The affordability anger in this election was real. It was mostly not about electricity. The September CPI shows gasoline up 34.2% and fuel oil up 74.2% year over year, against electricity at 4.4%, in a year dominated by an oil shock. The national exit poll contained no question about electricity or utility bills at all.

The geography makes the point harder. Two-year residential price changes through August 2026 run about +8.1% nationally, but the increases cluster in the PJM footprint: DC +38.8%, Maine +30.1%, Illinois +19.3%, Pennsylvania +18.6%, Virginia +18.0%, Ohio +16.7%. Now look at the biggest data center states outside PJM. Texas, with roughly 474 GW of large load in its interconnection queue, about 90% of it data centers, came in at +6.5%, below the national average. Georgia +7.1%. Arizona +2.0%. California fell.

Geography tracks market structure, not data center density. And the best national statistical work, from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, finds that between 2019 and 2025 states with higher load growth generally saw real retail prices fall, not rise.

Do not overcorrect, though. In PJM specifically the case is strong and comes from PJM’s own independent market monitor, which re-cleared the actual auctions with the actual offers and attributed $29.4 billion of $63.6 billion in capacity charges across four auctions, about 46%, to data center load, concluding that the 2025/26 auction was “not competitive.” A separate Dallas Fed working paper models the national wholesale effect at 6.9% by 2025, and does not estimate how much of that reaches a household bill. That monitor also proposes the remedy, which is worth knowing when you read its number. The fair summary is that this is a regional capacity-market problem that became a national story.

While everyone argued about moratoriums, almost none were enacted. Sixteen states considered outright bans. Zero became law.

What did become law is duller and far more consequential. Nine states passed ratepayer-protection rules in 2025 and 2026, signed by governors of both parties: Oregon, Texas, Minnesota, South Dakota, Florida, Tennessee, Oklahoma, New Jersey and California. Consultancy E3 counts 38 new large-load tariffs since 2018, 30 of them in 2025 and 2026 alone.

They mostly do the same thing. If you are a very large electricity customer, you get your own rate class, and you must commit to paying for most of the capacity you reserve whether or not you use it, for ten to fourteen years.

Here is the result, and it is the most interesting number in this entire story.

By February 2026, AEP had 5,642 MW of data center load signed under the tariff. And its load forecast came in 6,521 MW lower than its previous submission, a drop the utility attributes to the tariff itself.

That number is not an anomaly. It is a measurement of something the industry has known informally for two years and rarely says out loud.

Grid Strategies found that utility data center forecasts filed with FERC ran roughly 40% above credible industry projections through 2030. Aterio counted 3,969 announced US data centers against 802 under construction. Bernstein estimates only about 35% of announced North American capacity will ever be built. JPMorgan found roughly 60% of capacity planned for 2027 completion had not broken ground.

The mechanism is not mysterious. When an interconnection request is free, you file several, in several states, and keep your options open. Developers were not lying. They were doing what anyone does with a free option. The tariff did not suppress demand. It priced the option, and most of the option holders walked.

This is why “data centers are being blocked” is a bad description of 2026. A project that evaporates when asked for a twelve-year commitment was not a project. It was a placeholder.

Here is the part that gets no coverage at all, and it is the reason I am not writing a doom piece.

CBRE counted 7,481 MW under construction across eight major North American markets in the first half of 2026, about 25% more than a year earlier. Construction set a record in the same months opposition peaked. Approvals kept happening in the very jurisdictions passing restrictions. Coweta County, Georgia approved a $17 billion campus in April and passed a moratorium in June.

Both things are true. The speculative queue is collapsing and the real buildout is accelerating. Those are not in tension. They are the same event seen from two sides, which is what a filter looks like while it runs.

The useful translation for anyone building on this infrastructure is that the governing question changed, quietly, from a political one to a financial one.

It used to be: will they let us build here. It is now: can this load pay for its own power under a decade-long take-or-pay commitment. That is a capital structure question. It has an answer, it can be modeled, and it does not depend on an election.

Three things follow. Site by market structure rather than by headline incentives, because the states advertising the loudest subsidies are not the states with the cheapest marginal power. Assume you will be asked to commit, because thirty of the thirty-eight large-load tariffs arrived in the last two years and the direction is one-way. And read the collapsing queue as competitive information rather than as bad news, because when your rivals’ announced gigawatts evaporate on contact with a contract, the capacity picture you are planning against was never the real one.

I run AI workloads every day across my own systems and nothing I do approaches this scale. But the shape of the lesson is the same one I keep running into and wrote about in October: when something is free to produce, people produce enormous quantities of it, and the quantity tells you nothing until someone attaches a price.

Announced megawatts were free to produce. Now they are not.

Two real uncertainties. Results from November 3 were unofficial and uncertified when I wrote this, with roughly nineteen House seats and two Senate races still uncalled. And poll numbers on data centers swing hugely on wording: the 63% to 75% opposition range is for a facility “near where I live,” while POLITICO asked about one within three miles and got 37% support against 28% opposition.

But the direction is not ambiguous. Nine states, both parties, thirty tariffs in two years, a flipped utility commission in Georgia, and a federal preemption order whose own Section 8 declines to touch state data-center regulation. Siting and retail rates are state powers, and the states are using them.

Nobody voted to stop AI. They voted to stop paying for it.
