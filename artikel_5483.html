Opinion: A Chinese research entity FOIA’d my scientific work. I had to give it to them

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Duncan, Ph.D., is the Thomas J. Watkins memorial professor of reproductive science in the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology in the Feinberg School of Medicine at Northwestern University.

Team science — or collaborative science across disciplines and institutions — is essential for scientific progress as modern problems are too complex for a single person, laboratory, or even field to solve alone. Some of the best work happens when scientists from different countries collaborate.

However, U.S. policies in place and in development increasingly curtail this vital foreign collaboration. There are foreign funding restrictions, visa and entry restrictions for researchers, mandatory institutional compliance and audits with respect to foreign collaborations, and tight export controls. These policies were implemented as biosecurity measures to protect from “countries of concern,” including China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea.

Meanwhile, proprietary scientific research is easily accessible to anyone, including foreign actors, through a policy meant to protect Americans’ right to government information. In other words, we are locking the front door while the back window is still wide open.

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) is a U.S. federal law enacted in 1966 in response to concerns that the government was too broad and unaccountable. FOIA gives anyone the right to request access to records held by federal government agencies.

This means that through FOIA, anyone — U.S. citizens, noncitizens, and foreign nationals — can access U.S. government-funded science by completing a simple online form and paying a minimal fee. While FOIA is an incredibly important tool for citizens in a free society, it was never intended to allow anyone around the globe to access proprietary U.S. science.

People are readily climbing in through the open back window. As a scientist in the U.S., I personally experienced the impact of such a FOIA request. I was notified by a FOIA coordinator at an NIH institute that my scientific ideas, supported by a funded grant from the U.S. government, were being requested by a Chinese entity through a simple online request form. They only had to pay a $25 fee.

By law, I had to comply and release the grant to them. The requester was listed as a “Consultant” and identified by a generic email address and physical address in China that could not be verified.

I am not the only one to have this experience. The FOIA coordinator told me offhand that this specific individual had a track record of requesting numerous grants from different NIH institutes every year. More broadly, over a decade, the NIH received more than 13,300 requests for grant proposals through FOIA. (Meanwhile, journalists’ FOIA requests often face major delays and other hurdles.)

This loophole has significant negative ramifications on our society. Scientific grants contain unpublished scientific data and sensitive intellectual property. The release of this material could:

1) harm personal and institutional financial interests by divulging patentable ideas, product concepts, and precompetitive information;

2) mislead the public through exposure to unvetted and untested hypotheses and preliminary data;

3) ruin careers and livelihoods of U.S. scientists; and perhaps most importantly,

4) damage U.S. interests by allowing foreign nationals or foreign corporations to be the primary beneficiaries of work funded by the U.S. taxpayer.

Our policies are fundamentally not aligned to protect science. We say we are blocking competing foreign interests, yet through FOIA, anyone can access this science.

If the U.S. truly wants to protect science, Congress needs to close the open window that FOIA affords. Proprietary U.S. science should be exempt or excluded from FOIA. Failing that, those who can access our material must be scrutinized.

Almost exactly four years after receiving the FOIA request, a research team from China published findings that closely matched my team’s original hypothesis — one our own later experiments did not support. While I cannot say whether the FOIA request, my grant, and this recent publication are related, the timing has made me think more about how research, including my own, is ultimately used in these situations.

Francesca E. Duncan, Ph.D. is the Thomas J. Watkins memorial professor of reproductive science in the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology in the Feinberg School of Medicine at Northwestern University. She is also an associate professor in residence at the Buck Institute for Research on Aging.  The views presented here are her own.
