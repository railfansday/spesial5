What a strong El Niño could mean for your heating bill, region by region

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Free daily briefing on global business news.

El Niño heating bills could ease across the northern U.S. this winter while the South may see little change, and these 10 regional and historical angles explain why

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) says El Niño is set to become "very strong" this fall and winter. Some people have already nicknamed it the "Godzilla" El Niño. For households, the practical question is not the nickname. It is what the event could do to the cost of staying warm.

El Niño is a recurring climate pattern that returns every two to seven years. Exceptionally warm temperatures in the Pacific Ocean drive it. Its reach extends to winter weather across the U.S.

In the U.S., the pattern has historically raised the odds of a warmer, drier winter in the northern part of the country. Southern regions tend to face better odds of a cooler, wetter season. Those are odds, not promises. The experts quoted by Newsweek stressed that the impact is never guaranteed and is hard to predict.

That uncertainty shapes how El Niño heating bills play out. A milder northern winter can mean less fuel burned for heat. The size of any saving depends on more than the thermometer, though. It depends on the fuel a household relies on, whether natural gas, propane, heating oil or electricity. It also depends on how quickly the price of each fuel responds when demand changes.

Regional differences matter as much as the national picture. The Pacific Northwest's power supply, the South's exposure to heavy rain and Texas's experience with severe cold each raise separate questions. The 2015-16 El Niño winter also offers a benchmark for how far demand for heating fuels can fall when a season runs warm.

This piece draws on Newsweek reporting by Giulia Carbonaro to examine 10 angles on the question, region by region and fuel by fuel. They are a selection, not a ranking. Each one looks at a different part of the bill. Some cover the weather that drives demand. Others cover the energy sources that meet that demand, or the rate structures that decide when changes reach customers. Read together, they show why one forecast can mean lower bills for some households and little change for others.

A strong El Niño tilts the odds toward a warmer, drier winter across the northern U.S. Milder weather means heating systems run less often. That can lower heating demand.

Newsweek reports that this northern pattern is the historical tendency of El Niño winters. That makes it a guide drawn from past seasons rather than a set outcome for any one state.

The northern tier covers a wide stretch of the country. It takes in the Upper Midwest, the Northern Plains, the Great Lakes and the Northeast. Newsweek's reporting gives no separate heating figures for any of these regions. The warmer, drier tendency is the only outlook it sets out for them.

The National Weather Service describes how the pattern tends to play out in southern Wisconsin. There, El Niño winters usually run warmer than average or close to it. They also tend to bring less snow. That wording leaves room for seasons that land near normal rather than clearly warm.

The weather service frames that pattern as a seasonal average. Cold days can still arrive within a mild season. Heavy precipitation can also still occur. A warm outlook for the season as a whole says little about any single week. Homes in the region would still need heat during those colder stretches.

The fuel a home burns also shapes how a milder winter registers. Heating oil is a much more common home heating fuel in the Northeast than in other U.S. regions. Any drop in heating demand there would show up partly in heating oil use, a fuel households elsewhere lean on far less.

A strong El Niño could cost Pacific Northwest households later in the year what it saves them on winter heating. The reason is water. Smith, quoted by Newsweek, says the region produces roughly half of U.S. hydropower.

That share ties the region's electricity supply closely to its winter weather. A winter that eases heating demand can still leave less water for power generation.

The chain starts with snow. Smith told Newsweek that El Niño winters can shrink the region's snowpack. A smaller snowpack can, in turn, leave reservoir storage lower.

Snow that piles up in the mountains over winter melts in spring and runs into reservoirs. Hydroelectric dams generate power from that stored water. Less snow means less water to run through the turbines.

Smith said hydropower supply could then fall in the spring and summer after an El Niño winter. That timing separates the two effects by several months.

Relief from a milder winter, if it arrives, shows up first, during the heating season. A drop in hydropower supply would come later, once the heating season is over.

In Smith's view, that later drop would cancel out any relief consumers feel this winter. On his reading, a mild winter in the Pacific Northwest would not by itself mean lower energy costs across the full year.

For households in the region, that makes a single winter bill a partial measure. The fuller picture covers the heating months and the warmer months that follow.

Smith's wording also stops short of a prediction. He said El Niño winters can shrink snowpack, not that every one will.

A cooler, wetter El Niño winter could push electric heating load up slightly across the Southeast and Gulf Coast. Newsweek reports that the increase would be too small to change utility costs in any meaningful way.

Electric heating load is the power that homes and businesses draw to keep indoor spaces warm. That draw climbs as outdoor temperatures drop. A cooler season would add some of that demand to the regional grid.

The northern states may see heating demand ease. This region faces a nudge the other way. Households in the Southeast and along the Gulf Coast are not in line for the winter relief the pattern can bring farther north. Their exposure is to slightly more heating, not less.

Scale matters as much as direction. Newsweek describes the rise in heating load as small. On a typical utility bill, the added cost would not stand out.

The effect is measured in electricity. The reporting frames the Southern change in terms of electric heating load. It speaks to power bills rather than to bills for other heating fuels.

The reporting gives no percentage or dollar estimate for this region. It describes a small upward nudge in electricity demand for heating. It stops short of pointing to noticeably higher bills. On this evidence, the forecast points to little change in Southern winter electricity bills.

Extreme rain during a strong El Niño could disrupt power supply and cause outages in the South, Miller, quoted by Newsweek, said. He added that this outcome is less certain.

The concern follows from the pattern's usual footprint. El Niño has historically raised the odds of a cooler, wetter winter across the southern U.S. Rain, not cold, is the part of that outlook tied to this outage concern.

Temperature and rain affect a power grid in different ways. Colder or milder weather changes how much heating a home needs. That link to demand is direct. Miller said the effect of precipitation on electricity demand in the South is less clear.

That gap shapes how a Southern household can read a winter forecast. A wet season does not translate neatly into higher power use. The more direct risk Miller raised sits on the supply side. Severe downpours could interrupt service rather than push up consumption.

Even that supply risk comes with a caveat. Miller treated outages from extreme rain as a possibility he considered less certain, not an expectation. Newsweek's reporting does not attach a probability, a dollar cost or a specific state to that risk.

El Niño shifts the odds of certain conditions rather than locking them in. For the South, the outlook leans toward more rain this winter. What that rain would mean for power supply and household costs remains an open question in Newsweek's reporting.

One severe cold outbreak can erase a month of lower heating demand. A winter that looks mild in a seasonal forecast is no guarantee of a smaller heating bill.

Smith, quoted by Newsweek, made that point. He cited Winter Storm Uri, which struck Texas in February 2021, as his example.

His comparison is about scale. In Smith's account, the extra heating demand from one cold outbreak can cancel the savings built up over a month of lower demand.

That gap shapes how a forecast should be read. An El Niño outlook describes the odds for a whole season. It does not say whether a single stretch of extreme cold will arrive within that season, or when.

The distinction carries extra weight in Texas. The state sits in the southern U.S. There, El Niño's historical tilt points toward a cooler, wetter winter. The pattern gives Texas households no lean toward milder weather to offset a cold outbreak.

Smith chose Uri to show what that risk looks like in practice. The storm struck a southern state during the heating season. A favorable seasonal outlook in any region does not rule out a severe cold event.

U.S. demand for propane fell 16 percent and demand for heating oil fell 18 percent during the 2015-16 El Niño winter, according to Energy Information Administration data cited by Newsweek. EIA data show that winter ran 15 percent warmer than the one before it.

The EIA's measurement period runs from October 2015 through March 2016. It covers the coldest stretch of the season and the autumn and early-spring months on either side of it.

The two fuels did not fall by the same amount. Heating oil demand dropped two percentage points more than propane demand over the same mild winter. Newsweek's reporting does not give a reason for that gap. The figures record the difference without explaining it.

The two fuels are not spread evenly across the country. Heating oil heats homes far more often in the Northeast than in any other U.S. region. The heating oil decline is most relevant to households in that part of the country.

The 2015-16 figures describe one past winter. They show how far demand for propane and heating oil fell in a season that ran warm. They are not a forecast, and they do not set an expected size for any decline this winter.

Household electricity demand in the U.S. dropped 6 percent during the 2015-16 El Niño winter, Energy Information Administration data show. By April 2016, natural gas in storage stood 69 percent higher than a year earlier.

Both figures come from EIA data for the season running from October 2015 to March 2016. That winter was warmer than the one before it. The two measures capture different corners of the energy system.

The 6 percent decline measures residential demand. It tracks how much electricity homes used over those six months. It does not show what households paid for that power.

The comparison runs year over year. The EIA figure sets home electricity demand in the 2015-16 winter against demand the previous winter. It covers the residential sector as a whole, not any single region or utility. A national figure can blend areas where demand fell sharply with areas where it barely moved.

Electricity in a home does more than heat it. Lighting, refrigeration and other appliances draw power in every season. Only part of residential demand rises and falls with outdoor temperature. Warm weather trims that portion rather than the whole total.

The storage figure measures something else. It compares the volume of natural gas held in U.S. storage in April 2016 with the volume held a year earlier. Gas is pulled from storage through the cold months to meet heating needs. A larger stock in spring can point to a lighter drawdown over the winter.

April falls right after the October-to-March window the EIA data cover. The storage number is a snapshot of what remained once the heating season ended. It sits outside the six-month demand window but reflects what happened inside it.

Neither figure is a forecast. Both record what happened in one past winter. A similar outlook this year would not lock in the same results.

Electricity prices move more slowly than heating fuel prices. A 2016 study by the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) reached that conclusion, and Newsweek cites it in its reporting on El Niño and household energy costs.

The EIA traced the gap to how power prices are set. In many areas, electricity rates come out of regulatory mechanisms. Those mechanisms carry long time lags.

Heating fuels sit on the other side of the gap. Their prices respond sooner when conditions change, while electricity rates trail behind.

The lag works in both directions. A slow-moving rate is slow to fall when conditions favor lower prices. It is also slow to rise when conditions favor higher ones. A household paying for heating fuel, by contrast, sees price shifts reach it sooner, whether they help or hurt.

A monthly bill reflects two things: the rate charged and the amount used. The rate lag touches only the first. A household that heats with electricity and runs its system less during a mild winter still uses less power. What the lag affects is whether the price per unit changes during that same season.

Where a household lives can affect which side of the gap it sits on. Heating oil is much more common as a home heating fuel in the Northeast than in other U.S. regions, according to the EIA. That places more Northeastern homes on the faster-moving side of the price gap the 2016 study describes.

The EIA study describes a general pattern in how prices respond. It does not set out a specific delay for any one state, utility or winter.

A strong El Niño could trim about $3.16 a month from the average electricity bill in affected Northern states. That figure is an illustration, not a forecast. It rests on a 2 percent saving that Newsweek uses as an example, attributed to Miller.

The starting point is a national average. SaveOnEnergy.com puts the typical U.S. residential electricity bill at about $158 a month as of October. Newsweek applies Miller's 2 percent figure to that average. The math is $158 multiplied by 0.02, which comes to about $3.16.

Subtracting that amount from the $158 average would leave a monthly bill of about $154.84 for the average consumer.

The 2 percent is an example assumption. It is not a measured result from this winter. It is not a prediction for any one household either. A home's actual saving could be larger, smaller or nothing at all.

Newsweek frames the example for Northern states only. Those are the areas where a strong El Niño tends to favor milder heating weather. The $3.16 figure is not meant to describe bills in Southern states.

The base figure is an electricity bill. It leaves out what households pay separately for other heating fuels. Many homes heat with a fuel other than electricity. For them, any El Niño effect on heating costs would show up on a different bill.

The $158 figure is also a national average. It blends homes in every state, climate and rate structure into one number. Under the same 2 percent assumption, a household paying more than $158 would save more in dollars. A household paying less would save less.

The estimate rests on two inputs. One is an average monthly bill reported for October. The other is an example percentage used to illustrate the possible size of a saving. Neither input guarantees that any household will pay less this winter.

A more typical El Niño may lower national heating demand by a smaller amount than a very strong one, Smith told Newsweek. He adds that the effect in any region hinges on its heating fuel mix.

Smith's comparison separates two kinds of winter. A very strong event is the kind NOAA expects this season. A more typical event follows the same broad pattern of a mild North and a cool South. In his assessment, the national drop in demand from a typical event is smaller.

The comparison is about scale, not direction. Both kinds of event tilt the North toward milder weather and the South toward cooler weather. What separates them is how far the national total of heating demand falls.

Fuel mix decides where any saving lands. A warm winter cuts demand for whichever fuel a household burns. The bill that shrinks is the one tied to that fuel.

The Northeast shows how uneven that mix can be. Heating oil is far more common as a home heating fuel there than in other parts of the U.S. For households that heat with oil, a mild winter would register mainly as less fuel burned from the tank.

The same logic applies elsewhere. Where many homes run on electric heat, a change in demand appears on power bills. Where natural gas dominates, it appears on the gas bill instead.

Two regions that share the same mild winter can still see different effects if their homes heat with different fuels.

Smith's point also limits how far a national figure can stretch. An estimate of lower demand across the U.S. does not tell a household in a given state what its own bill will do. That depends on the local weather and the fuel the household uses. For a typical event, that national starting point is already smaller.
