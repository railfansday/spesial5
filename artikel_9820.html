Is AI Going to Replace Programming in 2027?

I'm building a SaaS that use Minimal Viable Kubernetes and am writing a book in public about how we can survive AI using this stack. This work, some of my history and how we are facing off, what some would have us believe, existential threats that AI poses are the subject of an interview with cntrl alt dev and the article that follows...

As is oft the case, it depends. And it depends primarily on how we perceive the role of a programmer in 2027.

It is fair, to say that what we understand as a programmer prior to 2023 has changed irrecoverably. When a software engineer is AI literate, using AI in daily working patterns, and one or more of a:

our jobs are not likely going anywhere right now.

However, if, the programmer is not AI literate and is:

it could be time to up skill, as AI is already competent at repetitive tasks, even though those tasks are complicated. AI Agents can, when engineered to do so, replace even these roles.

My gut, says no. Even with the latest frontier models, a human is often found at the helm of at least a prompt or two where real outcomes hit the headlines. And this of course depends on how we describe that role.

So my answer could, to me, be a certain yes, if the 'job' of a programmer is defined as one that is not AI literate and as described above. AI is more than capable right now to simulate the workflows of simple spec / feature scenarios.

Where AI still fails, I believe is in judgement of one PR over another. It fails when it comes to the application of the KISS principle and where something needs to be minimal and viable. It is very bad at being pragmatic, unless strongly prompted to the contrary.

Human in the loop seems to me to be the only way to be sure that lines of code do not escalate out of control and overengineered solutions become the default.

Perhaps, before AIs were trained, many organisation already overengineered solutions. These ideas became documented on line. Blogs and mailing list responses became the training data that built the LLMs of today. Could we have only ourselves to blame for the convoluted views that LLMs often have toward what could be simpler, more pragmatic architecture?

AI is being hyped as a potential cause of an event that leads to human extinction. I grew up in the 60s and 70s under the axe of mutually assured destruction (MAD) and the world possibly ending at any minute. There have always been extinction events that are entirely of human origin since the turn of the last century.

It is not necessary, I believe, to reach 'super intelligence' for this to happen. If you have not seen the 1980 film War Games perhaps now could be a good a time to watch it. The scary computer that nearly starts a thermonuclear war in this story is primitive if it were to be compared to ChatGPT of 2023. We don't need full on AGI to bring the end of all life as we know it. AI of today when given the ability to access to the launch codes could be quite sufficiently bad already.

To replace humans for any role, AI needs to be intelligent in the way we understand humans to be now. We can debate the nature of intelligence and find that there is no agreeable definition. But common sense dictates that a reasoning person can make value based decisions that lead to a well designed outcome. This process is based on a mixture of intuition and experience that is influenced by learning and knowledge. Aspects of each can be simulated by AI but they cannot, at present, be replaced entirely.

AGI seems always to be between 6 months and 50 years from now.

If the writings of Gil Duran are anything to go by, where he speaks of predictions of a human ascendance to immortal synthetic super beings, we have a problem. This dystopian vision thankfully still remains a distant unrealised dream. Even now, we have little idea how the human mind and intelligence actually works and in a way that could be implemented in software.

The mistake we could be making is to ignore the present and fail to see that many of the risks that are being predicted regarding threats to humanity are already present and have been mostly for decades.

Waiting for some kind of quantum event will be too late for the event to have taken place already.

There is capability enough in AI, right now, to do damage to our way of life. Many people are being affected detrimentally already by AI in ways that are devastating.

Unless we are members of the Senate in the US, it is unlikely that anything we as individuals can do much about existential threats to humanity. It could be debated that even those senators and the ones above them could do much either, but that is another debate, article or podcast of which there are already plenty.

As individuals we need to adapt and prepare for change. If you've been working in technology for 20 or more years, you cannot have survived if you were not capable of adapting to change.

Back in the 1950s Kathleen Booth and a team of researchers demonstrated a working prototype of what became know as an assembly language (ASM)

Up till this point programmers entered hex codes into computers to program them. Assemblers were seen by some as the end of programming as they knew it.

Failure to embrace new tooling is certain to lead to our current roles becoming redundant, if we stick rigidly to just those we are currently proficient in.

Often we need to specialise in something if we are to succeed. But the overspecialisation brought about by a philosophy of Ford like division of labour leads to individuals becoming expendable in their roles. This can seen as a byproduct of division of labour or, as the desired outcome of an acquisitive venture capitalist.

The role of the programmer can no longer be pigeonholed into a cubical dwelling jobber that receives tickets and kicks out features at the end of each working day.

I have lived experience of the end of non programmer operations roles in any real sense and the rise of DevOps taking over that of ops in many workplaces.

Similar has happened in software engineering. At one time it was possible to limit one's scope to just writing software, having nothing to do with delivery. Now it is necessary to work at least in software and continuous deployment.

We need to be able to work across more than one discipline and often well out of our comfort zones if we are to remain relevant in programming post AI.

Once, the term 'full stack' suggested the ability to write some JavaScript and React for the frontend and a bit of server side rendering on the server using perhaps react components or just Express.js. Post AI, this is proving to be not enough as vibe coders are able to create full stack apps without any programming knowledge.

We all need to become truly 'full stack' if we are to survive AI and remain relevant.

We still need to know how to code. We still need to understand best practice. But we also need to work outside of the cubicle where only features are built. We need to appreciate how systems are built as a whole, how they are managed into production and how they are manged as ongoing services.

Service management has often in my experience been forgotten by sales driven product development. Focusing on only short term return on investment (ROI) is a common human failing.

If we truly implemented the agile manifesto, we would have already adopted many if not all of these ideals. Business units over the years, however have taken ownership of what would have been run by self deterministic 'agile' units. In order to control and measure productivity in ways that the business roles can understand, they have implemented extra layers of administrative overlays upon teams of agile developers. Some of these extra layers have become known as ceremony and passed off as a part of the original agile manifesto.

The outcome of these restructures has often made developers, testers, even operations, SREs, DevOps and platform engineers highly specialised and siloed off into separate units. This may help businesses, in their perceptions, to control costs and above all, salaries, by defining job roles and scope. But it works counter to the original intent of agile.

AI further compounds this situation as it excels at replacing clearly defined roles and repeatable patterns of complicated workflows. It threatens roles limited in their scope from being in existence - but it also offers opportunities.

If we use AIs as a means to become multi skilled, we can continue to have our own specialist knowledge and augment our activities with an expanding working knowledge of other disciplines.

Our unique selling points as human beings with real, human intelligence, is to be able to make rational, human design choices that have better outcomes.

We become hybridised, essentially 'code managers', not code syntax writers but without losing the knowledge of what code is and how it works.

We become multi-skilled and cross team workers in the spirit of agile, DevOps, and platform engineering.

We become software professionals, able to contribute to all aspects of software engineering, be it security, development, operations, infrastructure, architecture, testing, or quality assurance.
