A new genetic discovery points to how sloths manage to survive while using so little energy

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Scientists mapped the two-toed sloth genome and found a record number of extra gene copies. These unique energy-linked genes may explain how sloths survive on so little daily energy.

Hanging motionless in trees, sloths are doing something no other mammal on record does. They can run their entire bodies on less than half the energy expected for animals their size, and biologists still can’t explain how.

A new genome of the two-toed sloth points to one possible part of the answer. The sloth carries dozens of extra gene copies that it shares only with other sloths, and many of them are tied to how cells make and manage energy.

The researchers think those copies may be part of what lets a sloth live on so little, though that idea hasn’t been tested in living cells yet.

“Evolution has already run billions of experiments,” said Marcela Uliano-Silva, a bioinformatician at the Wellcome Sanger Institute in Cambridge, England, who co-led the work. “By studying unusual animals like sloths, we sometimes uncover biological solutions that humans never evolved.”

The sequenced sloth was Lama Su, a two-toed sloth from Tierpark Berlin. When zoo veterinarians euthanized the animal for reasons unrelated to the study, the team collected its spleen, blood, brain, lung and liver and froze them at once.

DNA from the spleen became the finished genome, about 3.2 billion letters long, with almost every letter placed on a chromosome.

A southern anteater named Anton, from the same zoo, supplied a second new genome. Sloths, anteaters and armadillos make up Xenarthra, the only major branch of placental mammals that arose in South America.

The team compared the two new genomes with three published ones, from a maned sloth, a second species of two-toed sloth and the nine-banded armadillo. A catalog of mammal genomes, 41 species in all, from mice to people, supplied the wider comparison.

A big share of any mammal’s genome is DNA that can copy itself and insert the copy somewhere else. Biologists call them jumping genes, and in people most of them are old and no longer active.

The machinery a jumping gene uses to copy itself doesn’t only copy the jumping gene. Sometimes it copies the message a cell has just read off an ordinary gene and writes that message back into the DNA as a new copy of the gene.

The copy lands without the switches that turn a gene on, so some geneticists have classed these copies as genes that don’t work.

The two-toed sloth’s genome has 15,898 of them, nearly twice as many as a human genome, which has around 8,000. Other mammals typically carry between 2,000 and 8,000 such copies. No mammal, and no other animal, has been reported with more.

The other four xenarthrans all had more than 10,000. And in the two-toed sloth, some single genes had been copied more than 100 times each.

About a third of the two-toed sloth’s copies, 32%, also exist in at least one of the other two sloths and in no other animal the team checked.

That puts their origin on the branch leading to the ancestor of all living sloths, roughly 30 million years ago, after sloths had split from anteaters and armadillos.

The copying didn’t stop there. Of the copies found in the two-toed sloth and nowhere else, nearly 16% are so close to their parent gene that they were made recently or are still being made.

The jumping genes that do the copying are themselves switched on in the sloth’s tissues, especially in blood.

The anteater and the armadillo show neither sign. Their jumping genes are old, and their gene copies show no sign of recent copying.

To find copies that might be doing a job, the team started with the 5,123 copies the two-toed sloth shares only with other sloths and kept narrowing.

A copy had to be switched on in at least one of the five tissues sampled, and 2,347 were. It had to keep at least 70% of the instructions for its parent protein, and 525 did.

The third test looked at the DNA itself. Mutations that change the protein had to be less than half as common as mutations that don’t, which is the mark of a gene that natural selection is protecting. That left 123.

The team then checked each one by hand and kept only copies with sequencing reads that could have come from the copy alone, not from its parent gene. Thirty-eight remained.

Many of the 38 are copies of genes that work in mitochondria, the parts of a cell that produce its energy. Others come from genes that handle fats or help a cell respond to stress. The energy-related copies are most active in blood, brain or liver, and the stress-response copies in blood.

Why a sloth would keep spare copies of energy genes is the question the team raises next. Some of the same researchers have early, unpublished results on the DNA inside sloth mitochondria.

Those results suggest that mitochondrial DNA in sloths is under less pressure than usual to stay intact, possibly because so little energy is demanded of it.

If that’s right, the extra copies might be compensating for mitochondria that have picked up more mutations than they otherwise would.

“Our findings suggest that sloths might have evolved genetic ‘backup systems’ that help compensate for their ‘relaxed mitochondria’ and support their unique lifestyle,” said Camila Mazzoni, who co-led the study at the Leibniz Institute for Zoo and Wildlife Research in Berlin.

The researchers describe the link between the 38 copies and a sloth’s metabolism as a hypothesis, not a result. They haven’t yet tested what any of these copies does inside a living cell, and the mitochondrial finding behind the idea isn’t published yet.

The team also had only one anteater and one armadillo to compare against, out of 11 anteater species and 25 armadillo species alive today.

More genomes will be needed to tell whether what the team saw in those two animals holds across their whole groups.

Uliano-Silva’s team plans to grow sloth cells in the lab next and test what the copies do there.

For now, the 38 copies are candidates. Whether any of them helps a sloth live on so little energy is the question those cell experiments are meant to answer.

The full study was published in the journal BMC Biology.
