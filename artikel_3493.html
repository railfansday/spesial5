What’s Real and What’s Not in Netflix’s ‘The Altruists’

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Even if you followed the news closely in the early 2020s, the sheer audacity of the fraud committed by Sam Bankman-Fried and Caroline Ellison—as dramatized in Netflix’s new limited series The Altruists, starring Anthony Boyle and Julia Garner—will shock you anew. The show, which follows the stunning multibillion-dollar collapse of the crypto exchange FTX, starts off a bit slow, but accelerates episode by episode until its high-octane finale.

I found the series to be especially striking in the context of last week’s historic Anthropic IPO, which ironically fell on the same day as the premiere. Some of that company’s founders and employees share similar intellectual roots to The Altruist’s protagonists; watching its fictionalized Caroline Ellison, in particular, justify astoundingly reckless behavior with her system of mathematical-optimization-of-everything felt uncannily like watching frontier labs proceed with a seemingly inexplicable appetite for risk.

The Altruists also depicts a surprisingly tender romance between two socially inept, possibly sociopathic adrenaline junkies. (According to people involved with the project, the working title for the project was Untitled Love Story.) But as with any fictionalized account, the series takes some liberties with the characters and plot. If you’re anything like me, you want to know what exactly is fact, and what is fiction.

I spoke with Kevin T. Dugan and Jen Wieczner, two of the reporters who exposed the FTX fiasco in real time, and who consulted on the series. “When Jen and I started working with Graham Moore, the showrunner, he made it really clear that he wanted this to be very character driven. Nobody wants an eight-hour lecture on how the blockchain works,” Dugan tells me.

“The bones of the show are rooted in reality,” says Wieczner. Understanding these flawed and complicated characters helps explain “how one decision leads to another decision and a small oopsies turns into a multibillion-dollar fraud and this huge criminal conspiracy.”

Even if there’s a little fiction in the nitty-gritty, Dugan says that there is one overarching truth he hopes the series will reveal: “That even the prettiest ideas still have to turn into reality through imperfect people, and those imperfect people will end up making mistakes that they cannot foresee.” Here’s what the show ripped from real life—and where it took some creative license.

CAROLINE ELLISON WROTE SEXY HARRY POTTER FAN FICTION

Not quite. In the first episode, Caroline is humiliated when her raunchy writing titled “A Ride on Draco’s Nimbus” surfaces online. And while it’s true that Ellison was a huge Harry Potter fan, there is no evidence she wrote erotic fan fiction herself. Instead, this plot point is likely gesturing toward Ellison’s 2010s-era Tumblr, which was called WorldOptimisation—a reference to a 660,000-word-long work of Harry Potter fan fiction by Silicon Valley philosopher Eliezer Yudkowsky. Yudkowsky’s work popularized the kind of mathematically strategic thinking Ellison applies to love and business alike.

In episode six, Sam Trabucco (played by Alex Lawther) makes the mistake of introducing Sam and Caroline to a hack that proves irresistible: FTX can mint its own coin, FTT, and then allow its crypto trading firm, Alameda, to take out large loans using off-market FTT as collateral. Alameda can then use those loans to place highly leveraged, incredibly risky bets on FTX—which in turn increases the value of FTT. (TL;DR: it’s a perfect loop.) Appalling as it sounds, in real life, this practice is more or less exactly what brought SBF’s empire tumbling down.

“One of the things that led to the FTX bank run,” explains Wieczner, “was the leaked balance sheet that showed that much of FTX and Alameda’s holdings were in FTT, which was FTX’s own coin. So that if that went to zero, it was like a house of cards and things just would’ve fallen apart. And that’s why you see at the end why is everything is falling…you have that kind of death spiral effect.”

As for Trabucco, he and his yacht (yes, it was really named Soak My Deck) didn’t get off unscathed. Trabucco didn’t face criminal charges, but was forced to liquidate many of his assets in order to help return about $80 million to defrauded investors.

Real! In the final episode of the series, SBF presents a last-ditch plan: He wants to repurpose his early stake in Anthropic to cover the losses of billions of dollars in customer funds. Indeed, FTX did use $500 million in customer funds to purchase a 13.5% stake in Anthropic’s Series B in late 2021. Of course, Sam’s plan did not pass muster. Yet if it had, he’d have been in the clear. After the company’s IPO last week, Alameda’s stake would be worth about $35 billion, according to The New York Times—enough to solve the disgraced firm’s liquidity issues several times over. Oh well.

CAROLINE ELLISON CONFESSED IN FRONT OF HER STAFF

True. In the penultimate episode, a visibly cracking Caroline delivers a halting confession amidst fits of nervous giggles to a shocked staff, telling them that she knew about FTX’s shortfall of user funds that would add up to more than $8 billion. I was sure this scene had been invented, but lo and behold: Ellison did admit that she had been aware of the inappropriate transfers between the two firms at a December 2022 all-staff meeting. You can even listen to the leaked recording online.

CAROLINE ELLISON BEARS MORE RESPONSIBILITY THAN SBF

I felt the show tilted towards placing the blame on Ellison, who is portrayed as incorrigibly risk-addicted and the muse behind SBF’s worst decisions. Constance Wang, who is played in the series by Madison Hu, is just one of the former FTX employees who found the show misrepresentative. In a lengthy X post, she rebutted its narrative: Ellison, said Hu, “wasn’t nearly that reckless, and she was never the one pushing to go all in. The idea that she was the mastermind behind everything made us laugh.”
