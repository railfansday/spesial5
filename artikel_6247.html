A South African dig shows early humans were already showing signs of advanced, complex thinking 60,000 years ago

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

60,000-year-old red ochre from a South African cave revealed hidden antelope proteins, proving early humans intentionally mixed animal parts into their ancient recipes.

It is a 60,000-year-old cold case that scientists finally solved using modern technology. By hunting for ancient proteins hidden in a patch of red dirt, researchers discovered that early humans deliberately mixed crushed clay with the remains of a massive African antelope.

This proves that modern science can now unlock the exact recipes used by humans in the Stone Age.

Between 2011 and 2013, archaeologists at Klipdrift Shelter in South Africa dug through a thin red ochre surface that may have been over 3 feet (1 meter) long. The shelter sits in the coastal cliffs of De Hoop Nature Reserve.

Bits of it were bagged and archived at the University of the Witwatersrand‘s satellite lab in Cape Town.

Years later Zahra Haghighi of the University of Bergen in Norway pulled five of those bags for protein analysis. Two held collagen from a common eland, a large antelope native to southern Africa.

The layer is about 60,000 years old. Haghighi’s team reads the result as eland material mixed with ochre powder on purpose, or as an ochre-treated eland hide left at the site. Two positive samples are not enough to pick between them.

Klipdrift Shelter is about 93 miles (150 kilometers) east of Cape Town. It is one of three sites of the Klipdrift Complex, with Klipdrift Cave and Klipdrift Cave Lower.

Bergen’s SFF Centre for Early Sapiens Behaviour (SapienCE) lists the complex among the sites its team has exclusive access to. Crews excavated there in 2011, 2012, 2013 and 2018.

Single-grain luminescence dates the shelter’s 23 Middle Stone Age units to between about 52,000 years ago at the top and 72,000 at the bottom. Out came stone tools, marine shells, animal bones, hearths, engraved ostrich eggshell and 597 pieces of ochre.

Layer PBE, one of the dig’s numbered sediment layers, where the red surface lay, is part of the Howiesons Poort, a stone tool phase of southern Africa.

Patches of ochre powder of various sizes turned up all through those layers. This one was exceptional, possibly 16 inches (40 centimeters) wide, with surviving pieces 5 to 7 millimeters thick.

Its precise extent was not documented during the dig, and the surface was taken out entirely in 2011 to 2013. The size estimate therefore comes from site notes and from photographs, some of them by Magnus Haaland and Ole Unhammer.

Haghighi, first author of the paper, chose five archived samples from layers PBD, PBE, PCA and PBA-PBB, weighing 30 to 150 milligrams each.

None had been collected with protein work in mind. The export permits came from Heritage Western Cape and the South African Heritage Resources Agency.

Her co-authors include Takumi Tsutaya of Kyushu University in Japan and Enrico Cappellini of the University of Copenhagen. Sediment does not give up proteins the way bone does, so the group devised a method it calls AB (acid and base) digestion.

Two samples of about 130 milligrams sat in a guanidine buffer at 99°C (210°F) for 10 minutes. Then came hydrochloric acid overnight at 4°C (39°F), and then sodium hydroxide. Enzymes then chopped up whatever protein had come loose.

That screen turned up mammal collagen. A second extraction with strong acid and no enzyme followed on all five samples.

The peptides were read on an Exploris 480 Orbitrap mass spectrometer. The search used 711 mammal collagen sequences plus those of 44 African bovid species.

Collagen was confidently identified in two of the five, one sample from PBE and one from PBD, which sits right above it.

The COL1A1 chain, one of the two protein chains that make up type I collagen, matched several African bovids, the cattle-and-antelope family. Fragments of the COL1A2 chain matched one species alone, the common eland, Taurotragus oryx.

Sixty-nine peptides were identified, adding up to 171 amino acids or 16.5 percent of COL1A2. A proline at position 693, a serine at 720 and a threonine at 878 occur in eland only. The 720 and 878 sites were picked up in both layers, in at least three spectra apiece.

The giant eland, the other member of the genus, now lives in central Africa and has no published collagen sequence. The authors assumed the two species kept their present ranges 60,000 years ago.

Nearly every asparagine and glutamine, two of the amino acids in the peptides, had deamidated, a chemical change that builds up over long spans of time.

The paper suggests iron in the ochre may have helped the collagen last. The oldest ancient DNA recovered in Africa is about 18,000 years old. Older material calls for protein work, as with the ancient proteins pulled from Homo naledi fossils in South Africa.

Stray eland bone in the sediment would also leave collagen. So 15 samples went under optical microscopes, and some under a Zeiss Supra 55VP scanning electron microscope at the University of Bergen’s ELMILAB.

Only four of the 15 had anything resembling bone, one or two fragments apiece, none over about 0.1 inch (3 millimeters). Three of those four were from PBD, and PBE had none at all.

A thin section of the PBE surface mixed rounded ochre particles of around 1 micrometer with fragments of 2 to 5 millimeters.

That range, plus the quantity of ochre needed, supports the authors’ view that people formed the feature. They described the eland collagen as “most probably due to human action.”

Earlier work on the shelter’s animal bones had already identified eland among the remains.

The paper offers two scenarios. Either eland protein went into an ochre mixture that became part of the surface, or an ochre-coated eland hide was left in the shelter. In the second case the hide decayed and its ochre stayed in place.

Elsewhere in South Africa, two abalone shells at Blombos Cave, along the same coast, held an ochre-rich compound around 100,000 years old. At Sibudu, milk proteins on a stone flake pointed to a paint of ochre and milk about 49,000 years old.

Ochre has been linked to hafting glue, hide work, medicine and sunscreen. Sunscreen comes up in research on how Homo sapiens coped with intense UV light.

In an October 21 release, Kyushu University said evidence of organic components mixed with ochre had been “extremely rare, especially in Africa.”

All of this hangs on two of five small samples from a single feature at a single site. Which eland tissue the collagen came from is not known. The authors wrote that working it out would need a database built to tell tissues apart.

Eland are revered in San mythology, the paper notes, yet the authors called this one case “not sufficient to support the ritual significance of eland” in the Middle Stone Age.

They want the work repeated at other sites from that period. In Senegal, Middle Stone Age tools stayed in use 20,000 years longer than once thought.

The mass spectrometry data are in the PRIDE archive under PXD069507. The red surface in layer PBE no longer exists at Klipdrift. What is left of it is a handful of small bags of sediment in Cape Town, and two of them held the eland.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the Royal Society B.
