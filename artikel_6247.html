Among my friends and family, I see couples manage their finances and credit cards in many different ways. But across all the couples I know, I've found that two people can work together to earn more rewards and enjoy more benefits than they could individually.

So, here are some credit card strategies you should consider if you're part of a couple.

Couples who manage their finances separately will generally want separate credit card accounts. However, even if you manage your finances separately, you may want one or two cards you both use for shared expenses.

Likewise, even if you generally manage your finances jointly, having a few separate cards can be nice for purchasing surprise gifts.

You'll be approved or denied for a card based on your credit score and history, independent of your partner's credit score and history. But depending on your age and circumstances, you may also be able to include income from your partner that you can reasonably access when applying for a new card.

Some banks offer joint credit card accounts, which you apply for together and are jointly responsible for the bills. But joint accounts are relatively rare, so this article instead considers the more common situation in which one person opens an account and then adds their partner as an authorized user.

Primary account holders can make their partner (or anyone else) an authorized user on their account. Authorized users can make purchases on the account, but the primary cardholder is responsible for all the charges (including those made by authorized users).

One of the biggest advantages couples have over solo travelers is access to more welcome offers. Specifically, each member of a couple could earn the welcome bonus on the same card if they each apply separately.

This can be particularly useful if you're earning welcome offers for a specific trip. You could use one person's welcome offer for hotel bookings and the other person's for flights. Or, you could each use your welcome offer to book an award flight for the same trip.

But while both partners can potentially earn welcome offers on the same card, it's still important to coordinate application timing and avoid taking on more minimum spending than you can comfortably manage. Plus, each individual must keep in mind issuer application restrictions, such as Chase's 5/24 rule, when deciding which cards to apply for and when to apply for them.

If you and your partner each want to apply for a new card, you must decide whether to get the same cards or different cards.

The biggest reasons to get the same cards are an exceptional welcome offer or perks you'll each want.

For example, each member of a couple might want their own Capital One Venture X Rewards Credit Card if they frequently travel separately and value airport lounge access, especially since lounge access for authorized users now costs extra.

And I love that my husband and I each have our own , as this lets us use our anniversary night certificates for consecutive nights to make a two-night stay.

However, it often makes sense for couples to have complementary cards. By applying for different cards that complement what's in your partner's wallet, you can unlock better bonus-category coverage, different travel perks and access to different transferable rewards currencies.

This can be particularly useful if one person in a couple travels more frequently, as they may want to carry more cards with travel benefits, such as lounge access and airline or hotel elite status.

Be sure to weigh the combined annual fees against the benefits you'll actually use. And keep in mind that some cards let you share benefits with other travelers. For example, many airline credit cards offer a checked baggage benefit that extends to other travelers on your same reservation.

In some cases, you may want to add your partner as an authorized user on a card. This can be an easy choice when there's no fee. But it can also make sense to pay an authorized-user fee in some situations.

For example, many couples have one primary and pay $195 per additional Platinum card for each other family member (see rates and fees). By doing so, these family members can enjoy many of the Platinum Card's perks, including lounge access and select hotel elite statuses (enrollment is required for select benefits).

However, keep in mind that some benefits don't extend to authorized users. For example, purchases on additional Platinum Cards count toward many of the Platinum Card's statement credit benefits, such as up to $200 in airline incidental fee statement credits per calendar year (enrollment required). But additional Platinum Cards don't get their own statement credits.

So, while it might make sense for one partner to hold a premium travel card and add the other as an authorized user, it can make sense for both partners to independently hold cards that offer valuable benefits you'll use.

Adding your partner as an authorized user may also help them build credit if the issuer reports the account to credit bureaus and the account is managed responsibly. However, missed payments or high credit utilization could hurt their credit instead.

Whether you and your partner earn the same currencies on your credit cards or different currencies, and whether you combine your finances or not, it makes sense to coordinate how you earn and redeem rewards.

Consider what types of rewards you should both be earning based on the trips you want to take. For example, if you have your sights set on visiting a far-flung international destination, spending on a Southwest credit card likely isn't the best choice. Instead, earning hotel points in a program with properties in your desired destination, airline miles with a program that serves that destination or transferable rewards is a better bet.

The benefit of both partners earning the same transferable currency is that you can each transfer to the airline or hotel program of your choice once you're ready to redeem. But there's also value in diversifying your earnings in case flight availability appears in a program that only some transferable currencies can access.

Before deciding which rewards to earn, consider how you'll redeem them together. Some credit card issuers let you move rewards between eligible household accounts, while some airline and hotel loyalty programs allow members to pool points or transfer them to each other. Even when you can't combine rewards, you may be able to use your points or miles to book travel for your partner.

Couples have plenty of opportunities to earn more rewards and enjoy more credit card benefits by coordinating their credit card strategies. Whether you and your partner decide to get the same cards, hold complementary cards or add each other as authorized users, the best approach depends on your spending habits and travel goals.

My husband and I have found that having our own credit card accounts while coordinating which rewards we earn and redeem works well for us. But you don't need a wallet full of cards to benefit from a two-player strategy. Even a few carefully chosen cards can help you earn more rewards and enjoy more perks together.
