Every drop of water has a history, and satellites are tracking the clues

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Eight satellites have tracked heavy water isotopes in the air since 2002, and one open database now lines up every reading on a single grid.

A glass of water gives no hint of the ocean it evaporated from or the clouds it rode in on. The molecules do.

A small share of them carry a heavier form of hydrogen, and that share shifts a little with each round of evaporation and rainfall along the way.

This proportion helps researchers trace where water has been.

Eight satellite instruments have measured this proportion in atmospheric water vapor over different periods since 2002, each recording its results in its own format.

A team led from Rice University has now combined their readings into one open database, called WISO-SAT, that anyone can download.

Mampi Sarkar, a research scientist at Rice, led the work with Sylvia Dee, an associate professor in the university’s Earth science department.

Eleven co-authors in the United States, the Netherlands, Germany and Norway brought experience with the individual satellites, including scientists at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

“Most scientists and satellite missions want to share data,” Sarkar said.

“But it’s not always an easy task. Each satellite was designed to answer slightly different questions, so they have all different types of data even though they fundamentally measure the same thing.”

The heavy hydrogen is deuterium, and water that carries it is one of the water isotopes the satellites track. The measure they report is the ratio of those molecules to ordinary ones.

Four of the instruments read heat glowing up from the atmosphere and return a rough vertical profile of that ratio.

The other four read sunlight bouncing off the surface. They return a single value for the whole column of air, weighted toward the lowest layers.

The missions also started and ended in different years, flew at different times of day and stored their results in their own file layouts.

Combining two of them could mean a year of cleaning before any analysis began. Groups around the world could be repeating that labor without knowing it.

“Satellites access regions that field researchers can’t, and they’re incredibly important for consistent, long-term data collection,” Dee said.

“But for this to become a solid resource capturing the enormity of the water cycle, we needed to be able to treat it like one dataset.”

WISO-SAT organizes each instrument’s readings into monthly grids at two resolutions: 1 degree and 5 degrees of latitude and longitude.

A 1-degree cell is about 69 miles (111 kilometers) on a side at the equator. The files run from 2002 to 2024 and carry uncertainty estimates and observation counts where the original data had them.

The files are posted on the Zenodo archive with the code that built them.

“That means you can more easily compare all the datasets, while accounting for differences between instruments, and know exactly where each measurement was taken on the planet and what it’s telling you, no matter which satellite it came from,” Sarkar said.

The instruments keep their own quirks. A profile from one and a column value from another aren’t the same quantity, and the team left them separate rather than blending them.

“It’s so important to highlight that it’s not just the collection of data that matters; it’s also the effort it takes to make the data truly usable for the broader research community, students and even the public,” Dee said.

The database comes with a warning the team tested itself. Satellites see the lower atmosphere poorly. In dry or polar air, their readings drift toward the starting guess built into the software.

The team checked two instruments against water vapor sampled on nine Atlantic research cruises.

One instrument’s readings averaged about 23 parts per thousand higher than the ship measurements, while the other’s averaged about 18 parts per thousand lower.

Compared with measurements from 25 long-term land stations, the second instrument showed an average difference of 67 parts per thousand, with variation around that average roughly twice as large.

A statistical correction based on ground measurements removed those average offsets and improved agreement with the ground data.

The corrections were based on small samples, the authors noted, and one proved unreliable when they repeated the analysis with individual stations removed.

The surface checks covered only the two instruments with vertical profiles and enough overlap with ground data.

How well the sunlight-based column products match the surface remains untested in the database.

For that comparison, the team points users to ground-based column measurements and aircraft profiles.

Dee listed the questions she expects the archive to take on next, from cloud formation and moisture transport to El Niño, deforestation and soil moisture.

The archive is a first version. Its processing code is public so that other groups can grid their own satellite data the same way, and what gets added next depends on who submits it.

The full study was published in the journal Scientific Data.
