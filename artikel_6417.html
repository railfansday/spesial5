Hubble discovers something strange is missing from nearby galaxies

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Hubble reveals a mystery in nearby dwarf galaxies, where powerful stellar explosions appear to have swept away much of their oxygen.

The universe is filled with mysteries, and sometimes the most surprising discoveries involve something that should be there but isn’t.

NASA’s Hubble Space Telescope has uncovered one such puzzle in nearby dwarf galaxies.

These small collections of stars appear to be missing enormous amounts of oxygen that should have accumulated over billions of years.

A new study of 37 dwarf galaxies suggests that powerful explosions and stellar winds have been sweeping oxygen out of these galaxies far more efficiently than scientists previously thought.

The findings offer a new perspective on how galaxies evolve and how the chemical ingredients of the universe spread through space.

The spectacular Hubble image above shows NGC 4449, an irregular dwarf galaxy located approximately 12 million light-years from Earth in the constellation Canes Venatici.

Unlike the graceful spiral structure of the Milky Way, NGC 4449 has a chaotic appearance.

Bright blue stars are scattered across its irregular shape, while glowing pink clouds mark regions of active star formation.

These colorful features reveal a galaxy filled with energetic young stars and enormous clouds of gas and dust.

But beneath this dazzling appearance lies a surprising chemical mystery.

NGC 4449 was among the 37 galaxies examined in the Galaxies Losing Oxygen via Winds (GLOW) project.

Researchers discovered that dwarf galaxies retain only a small fraction of the oxygen their stars are expected to produce.

Oxygen is one of the most abundant elements in the universe. Much of it forms inside massive stars through nuclear reactions.

When these stars reach the end of their lives, many explode as supernovae, releasing oxygen and other elements into their surroundings.

Over time, these materials help enrich the gas from which future generations of stars and planets form.

Scientists expected to find more of this oxygen remaining inside dwarf galaxies. Instead, the new observations suggest that much of it has escaped.

“Oxygen is produced by stars, which are inside of galaxies,” explained Grace Telford, an assistant professor of physics and astronomy at the University of Utah.

Because dwarf galaxies have relatively weak gravitational fields, they struggle to hold onto gas propelled outward by powerful stellar winds and supernova explosions.

The study suggests that these winds are even more effective at removing oxygen than existing computer models predict.

The research team used archival observations from Hubble to examine individual stars and reconstruct the galaxies’ histories of star formation.

They combined these observations with data from NASA’s retired Spitzer Space Telescope and ground-based radio telescopes.

By examining oxygen associated with stars, gas, and dust, the researchers could estimate how much oxygen the galaxies had produced and how much remained.

The missing material may be drifting through the diffuse gas surrounding the galaxies or may have escaped farther into intergalactic space.

Exactly where most of it ended up remains uncertain.

The findings are important because oxygen serves as a useful marker for other heavy elements created by stars.

Understanding where these elements travel helps astronomers explain how galaxies develop and enrich their surroundings.

The discovery raises new questions about how small galaxies influence the wider universe.

If dwarf galaxies regularly expel large amounts of oxygen and other elements, they may help distribute materials throughout the universe.

These materials could eventually form new stars and planetary systems.

Kristen Wingfield McQuinn is the GLOW project’s principal investigator and an associate professor at Rutgers University.

“It also helps us learn where and how much galaxies are enriching the space between galaxies, called the intergalactic medium,” said McQuinn.

Researchers hope to investigate even smaller galaxies using NASA’s Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, followed by detailed observations from Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope.

For now, NGC 4449 offers a striking reminder that galaxies are not isolated collections of stars.

They constantly exchange matter with the space around them, shaping the chemical history of the universe.

The study was published in The Astrophysical Journal.
