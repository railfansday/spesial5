Automated Assembly in Space is the Future and NOM4D is Showing the Way

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

If we want big things in space, we'll have to build and assemble them there. Rocket fairings are not large enough to accommodate the large things we want to have in space. Not only that, but the stubborn rocket equation refuses to bend itself to our desires.

Telescopes like the JWST feature ingenious folding designs that unfold in space like insects metamorphosizing. That was a very impressive way to bypass the restrictions imposed by rocket fairings. But that's not practical in every case.

What's far more feasible is sending smaller components into space inside fairings and then assembling them into a larger structure once there. That's what NOM4D, the Novel Orbital and Moon Manufacturing, Materials and Mass-efficient Design program is demonstrating. NOM4D reached an important milestone recently when it successfully demonstrated automated in-space assembly for the first time.

The demonstration involved a gantry robot, also known as a linear robot, building a typical truss structure. Gantry robots move in straight lines along x, y, and z axes. The payload came from Caltech and consisted of individual components with custom joints put together to form the structure. The individual components were not only assembled, but a thin reflective mesh that mimics what a space-based antenna might have was also simultaneously applied.

Decades ago, thinkers proposed different types of rotating space stations that could be homes for thousands of people. The O'Neill Cylinder is probably the most well-known one of these, and space-heads and sci-fi dreamers of a certain age may have carried around a dog-eared copy of Gerard K. O'Neill's "The High Frontier: Human Colonies in Space."

"More important than material issues, I think there is reason to hope that the opening of a new, high frontier will challenge the best that is in us, that the new lands waiting to be built in space will give us new freedom to search for better governments, social systems, and ways of life, and that our children may thereby find a world richer in opportunity by our efforts during the decades ahead," O'Neill wrote.

The O'Neill Cylinder lies in the realm of science fiction or the distant future for now, as do O'Neill's hopes for humanity. But that was 50 years ago, and technology has advanced considerably, even if how humans conduct themselves hasn't advanced at the same pace.

But there are other proposed space structures that are closer to reality, and they may be stages towards O'Neill's hopeful vision that can be built by NOM4D or its successor.

It may begin with Low-Earth Orbit transfer stations and lunar orbit stations that would expand our presence in space. The proposed Lunar Gateway was one of these, and was envisioned as a modular design built in space from pre-made components. It would be a docking station and communications relay facility for trips to the lunar surface and operations there. These types of orbital facilities could be hybrids of pre-made components and structural frameworks made by NOM4D or something like it.

This concept image shows how a NOM4D-inspired device might autonomously construct a circular truss structure in space. Image Credit: DARPA | Colie Wertz

Another future use for scalable construction concerns lunar lava tubes. The Moon has what appear to be networks of subsurface lava tubes that provide natural protection from hazards. Robotic assembly could feasibly be used to reinforce these natural tubes and turn them into habitats.

Another idea is liquid mirror telescopes. Building a huge telescope mirror and sending it into space would be near impossible, but a liquid mirror telescope could be built in a lunar crater out of components shipped individually into space. And NASA's Lunar Crater Radio Telescope (LCRT) is a concept for a crater-based telescope on the lunar far side, isolated from Earth's radio pollution. These ideas are waiting for the engineering to catch up.

There's still another test upcoming for NOM4D now that it's demonstrated successful on-orbit assembly. Next, the NOM4D team from University of Illinois Urbana-Champagne will launch a test project to the ISS. This will be more than just assembly; it will demonstrate composite material processing. A monomer resin will be used to build a high-strength structure in space. It will use an energy-efficient method to heat the resin and create a strong polymer. This is similar to how resins are used in Carbon Fiber Reinforced Polymer (CFRP) manufacturing to build things like aerospace components, where strength and light weight are critical.

If that test is successful, then it really will be time to start thinking big, according to Detor.
