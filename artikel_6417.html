Children are not actually more creative than adults but they are way more confident

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Blind judges scored adults as more creative than preschoolers on every task, yet most adults still believe children are more creative.

Most adults, when asked whether preschoolers or grown-ups are more creative, pick the children. When both were put to the test, the adults produced the more creative work on every task.

The children won on something else though: more enjoyment, more self-trust, less worry. That confidence may be what adults mistake for talent.

Janika Saretzki and Mathias Benedek of the Department of Psychology at the University of Graz in Austria ran the comparison. Their co-authors were Ines Fratzl of Graz and Baptiste Barbot of UCLouvain in Belgium and the Yale Child Study Center.

They tested 92 preschoolers, from nine kindergartens, in their last year before school. Most of them were five or six years old.

The 70 adults were mostly in their 20s. Each person was tested alone for about half an hour between August 2021 and April 2022.

Because the two groups were compared once, at one age each, the study can’t say how any one person’s creativity changes as they grow up.

In an interview with Earth.com, Benedek said the ratings ran against his own expectation. “I had assumed children might produce highly unusual ideas that were less appropriate or useful,” he said.

“Instead, adults’ ideas were both more unusual and more effective.”

The first task gave everyone a real tin can to hold and three minutes to name as many creative uses for it as they could, out loud.

The second handed out a sheet of paper and colored pens and asked for a free drawing, framed as play rather than a test, with no time limit mentioned. The third was a test: draw a fantasy animal that doesn’t exist, in up to eight minutes.

After each task, everyone answered five questions on a scale marked with smiley faces. The questions covered how much fun the task was, how much they liked it, and how sure they felt beforehand.

Two more asked how well they thought they had done and how worried they were about doing badly.

Parents reported how often their children did something creative, such as pretend play, in their free time. The adults reported on themselves.

Five experienced raters then scored all 670 tin-can ideas and 324 drawings without knowing whose they were. Each one got a mark for novelty, for effectiveness, and for overall creativity.

On the tin can and the fantasy animal, the adults’ work was rated more novel, more effective and more creative than the children’s.

In the free drawing, the adults still scored higher on effectiveness and creativity. The two groups tied on novelty there, because adults tended to pick the same everyday subjects children did.

The adult lead was widest on effectiveness in the fantasy-animal task, the one that asked outright for something new.

The children also finished faster, spending about two minutes on the three-minute tin-can task against roughly two minutes and 40 seconds for the adults.

On the fantasy animal, the children were done in under three minutes while the adults took six and a half. On the free drawing, with no clock, both groups drew for four to five minutes. Adjusting for the time spent didn’t change the pattern.

Asked by Earth.com what else in the study deserved attention, Benedek pointed to the shape of the tasks. “The adult advantage was clearest in structured tasks with a defined goal,” he said.

Everything the children reported about their own experience ran the other way. They were more motivated on the tin can and the free drawing, more sure of themselves going into both drawing tasks, and less anxious about the tin can.

On all three tasks, they rated their own performance higher than the adults rated theirs, showing a confidence that the adults didn’t match.

The children had more fun on the tin can and the free drawing too. The fantasy animal was the exception: children and adults enjoyed that one about equally.

“Children enjoyed the open-ended tasks more, but in the structured drawing task their enjoyment was no higher than adults’,” Benedek said.

When asked to rate their own creativity, most children picked the top of the scale.

“So children’s creative strength seems to lie in the confidence and motivation they bring to the task, whereas adults feel more comfortable with clear task goals,” said Benedek.

The children also practiced more. Most did something creative nearly every day, by their parents’ account, while the adults most often said about once a week.

Of the 70 adults, 78.6 percent said children are the more creative group. Only 12.9 percent picked adults, and the rest couldn’t choose.

The children themselves were split almost evenly, with 46.7 percent naming children and 43.5 percent naming adults.

The adult belief is not an Austrian quirk. The authors wrote that international surveys find 68 to 77 percent of people agree children are more creative. Among teachers surveyed in the United States and Japan, 63 to 68 percent agree.

Benedek has a reading of why the belief survives. “Most likely because children visibly engage in creative activities, like pretend play, of their own accord in their leisure time,” he said.

“That enthusiasm is easy to see and easy to mistake for greater creative ability,” he added.

The authors put it in one comparison: a “sporty” person, they wrote, is usually someone who plays sports regularly rather than someone who wins competitions.

A caution goes with the children’s answers about fun and confidence.

Young children tend to pick the extreme ends of a rating scale, the authors wrote, and their grasp of a word like “creativity” is still forming. Those self-reports are the softest numbers in the study.

Every participant did the three tasks in the same order, so tiredness could have shaped the later ones. Adults judged all the work, and children might have judged children’s drawings differently.

The raters didn’t know who made each drawing, but a five-year-old’s drawing can look like a five-year-old’s, and the authors wrote that the raters may sometimes have guessed.

Only one narrow age band in one country was tested. The study also didn’t record what the adults did for a living or whether any had trained in an art. How much that training matters is an open question.

In Benedek’s view, the practical lesson runs toward the adults. “Preschoolers enjoy creating without worrying about what others will think of their ideas,” he told Earth.com.

“Adults could often benefit from that kind of playful, judgment-free attitude, at least in the early stages of coming up with ideas.”

Adults in this study rated their own creativity lower than the children rated theirs, and the study can’t say which group had it right.

The next step, the authors wrote, is to find out which it is: a real decline with age, or simply a more accurate view of themselves.

Benedek and his colleagues also want to show these results to adults who picked the children and ask them to explain the choice.

Until then, the authors’ best guess is that children look more creative because they so plainly enjoy creating.

The full study was published in the journal PLOS One.
