A failed decision at COP31 doesn’t stop tourism being able to take action on the ground. Without a guiding goal, destinations risk losing a long-term investment signal and political backstop for climate resilience and adaptation.

COP31’s failure to reach agreement on the Antalya Pact has been called a setback, but others argue no deal is better than a bad deal.

The final text for the Antalya Pact, which had a goal to boost electrification and clean energy, climate finance and acknowledged the overshoot of the Paris Agreement’s 1.5 degrees Celsius goal, did not mention fossil fuels — a distinct reversal of what was achieved at COP28 in particular.

It also focused on carbon capture and storage, a technology encouraged by petrostates and considered a false solution by critics. The worry was that the mooted deal would have undermined the Paris Agreement.

“We will not endorse a cover decision that does not even mention fossil fuels, the driver of climate change. And we will not accept negotiating backward in time. COP31 in Antalya ended without a cover decision after 90 countries, including the UK, Norway, Panama, the Marshall Islands, and the EU bloc, rejected a final text that never mentioned fossil fuels and leaned on carbon capture and storage. Ireland's climate minister Darragh O'Brien and European Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra said in a joint statement they would not endorse a decision that greenwashes fossil fuels. For travel, the absence of a consensus signal leaves the host's flagship pledge — lifting electricity to 35% of global final energy consumption by 2035, signed by around 80 countries — voluntary and silent on whether that electricity is clean. A UN Tourism report presented at a thematic day found 60% of the latest national climate commitments explicitly mention tourism, 49% tie it to adaptation, and 43% recognize tourism's own vulnerability to climate change. The industry's own work moved anyway: Iberostar, WTTC, and the Ocean & Climate Platform announced the Ocean Catalyst Group, the Glasgow Declaration simplified reporting rules on its fifth anniversary and added an adaptation pathway, and a Recipe of Change food waste pilot launched. The open questions are who pays for adaptation finance, which the rejected text would have tripled without naming a baseline year, and whether solidarity levies — backed for study by the UK, Spain, Kenya, and small island states — gain traction before COP32 in Ethiopia.
