Why Airport Food Costs So Much—High Rents, Costly Operations And Price Rules Shops Get To Work Around

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

A $7 bottle of airport water is bad enough. Being told the airport protects you from excessive prices, while it approves the price and collects money from the shop, is actually worse. There’s a new, good investigation into airport food prices with the reporter comparing terminal prices with stores outside the airport, then confronting the people supposedly enforcing the rules that prices are supposed to match. Airports usually defended the prices. I detest the hypocrisy here, but the video largely dismisses the economics that explain those rules, the exceptions, and the weak enforcement. Those are the reasons we get both the pricing promises and the prices that don’t match. Airports make concessions expensive to operate, then collect money from businesses selling to passengers who have limited alternatives. The starting point is understanding that passengers aren’t the customer, they’re the product. The Airport Sells Access To You An airport sells merchants access to people who have already cleared security, can’t easily leave, and may have only 20 minutes before boarding. You can compare restaurants, and if you did the different brands are often run by the same company with products sourced from the same vendor. And airport concession agreements frequently include not just rent but the greater of a minimum guaranteed payment or a percentage of sales. The airport wants successful merchants. Their sales support its budget. And airlines can benefit, too. Dallas–Fort Worth’s 2025 budget showed $120.1 million of non-airline net revenue going toward terminal costs and another $23.9 million toward airfield costs, reducing what airlines otherwise had to pay. That money came from parking but also concessions like restaurants. I’ve written about airports treating passengers as the product for years. The clearest example is removing moving walkways so people stop bypassing the shops. Dallas–Fort Worth approved a roughly $1 million project to remove walkways in terminal D and add retail and restaurant seating. The airport’s concessions executive said the project was expected to generate about $2 million in revenue within two years. Similarly, United removed the eight moving walkways inside Chicago O’Hare’s C concourse. Getting you to your gate efficiently and getting you to wander into shops and spend money are in tension, and there’s a strong preference for the latter. Street Pricing Depends On Which Street You Choose Many airports promise that vendors will charge the same prices as comparable businesses outside the airport, or those prices plus an allowed premium. The airport industry’s 2025 concessions survey found street pricing plus a percentage at 80% of responding large hubs. Chicago’s sample concession agreement calls for value pricing tied to approved off-airport stores, without a general percentage premium. The Port Authority Of New York New Jersey’s manual uses three to six approved comparison locations. Branded restaurants and shops generally must be compared with locations of the same brand. In the video, the Port Authority defends a $6.89 bottle of water by saying Hudson is a specialty market rather than a bodega. That’s especially rich given that its own manual describes travel essentials shops as also being known as news and gift shops or convenience stores, and specifically requires comparisons for bottled drinks and snacks. At Denver, most of the high prices out of sync with off-airport were expressly approved by the airport. Airport Starbucks are often compared to other airports rather than non-airport locations. You Can’t Wish Away The Cost Of Operating Inside An Airport I’ve explained why airport restaurants are expensive to operate. Rent is only part of it. Hiring is harder. Employees have to get to the airport, often park remotely and take a shuttle, clear security, and have to pass background checks. That limits the pool of available workers, makes the job less attractive, and drives up wages needed to attract employees. Deliveries require more handling. Supplies have to be screened and brought through controlled routes and delivery windows. Some airports require centralized distribution. DFW just opened a 90,000 square foot receiving and distribution center. Screening, facilities and handling and third parties that manage those add costs. Space and construction are expensive. There’s limited storage, kitchens face airport constraints, and building or renovating behind security is harder than fitting out a storefront in a strip mall. The airport controls the operating schedule. Merchants may have to open early and serve late flights even when those hours aren’t profitable. High prices at peak cross-subsidize slow times where merchants must remain open. The video answers the cost argument largely by pointing to high passenger volume. But merchants bid for that valuable traffic, and some of the benefit gets captured by the airport through rent and concession payments. Nor is expensive rent entirely an outside force the airport suffers from. The airport is the landlord. It’s choosing how much revenue to extract, what operating conditions to impose, and which merchants get access to the passengers. Many airports impose even higher wage costs than the market would require on its own. That costs money, too. But since U.S. airports are predominanelty owned by government, there’s political pressure to claim that passengers are being protected from the high prices that result from the government airport’s own policies. Airports Even Approve Fees To Get Around Their Own Caps Sometimes there’s no need to fudge price comparisons to support higher pricing needed to cover an airport’s higher cost environment. The airport passes a rule to allow vendors to charge more on top of the allowed price. I’ve covered airports allowing surcharges alongside their street pricing rules. The Port Authority’s current regulation permits a separate employee wage and benefit fee of up to 3%, on top of its street price plus 15% rule. (And that ‘wage and benefit fee’ doesn’t actually go to workers as such.) Earlier this month I covered a JFK self-checkout purchase of a $21.99 book carrying a 3% employee retention fee. I’ve also covered Minneapolis’s earlier 4.5% hospitality surcharge, with a sign explaining that it isn’t a tip: Then there’s the tip screen. In Austin, I couldn’t pay by card at self-checkout for a bottle of water without entering at least a penny as a tip. Zero wasn’t an option, and entering a tip manually rejected $0. A separate Austin self-checkout I flagged in January defaulted to 18% while adding a card processing surcharge. Portland Shows The Rules Can Work When The Whole Deal Works None of this means street pricing is useless, or that charging outside prices inside an airport is impossible. The video finds much closer compliance in Portland. And Portland hasn’t eliminated airport operating costs. Its 2024 convenience retail lease package combined street pricing with rent tiers of 18% to 22% of sales for locations past security, a separate 1% delivery fee, metered utilities and storage charges. And caps can restrain prices even when they’re imperfect. In February I covered a comparison of Starbucks prices across 24 airports: a grande coffee was $3 in Salt Lake City and $5.05 in Phoenix, Las Vegas and Los Angeles. Phoenix had dropped its street pricing requirement. That snapshot doesn’t isolate the effect of the rules (Los Angeles had a cap and was still expensive), but it certainly doesn’t suggest that removing limits makes airport coffee cheap. I’d much rather airports were honest about the bargain: we charge merchants for access to you, operating here costs more, and the revenue helps pay for the airport and can reduce airline costs. Most major U.S. airports are publicly owned. The politicians responsible don’t want to put their names on that message. So we get a promise of street pricing, an expansive definition of the street, and an employee benefits fee at the self-checkout. And then the politicians act shocked. Shocked!
