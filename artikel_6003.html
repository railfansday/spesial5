American war hero details dire living conditions US troops face as Hegseth taps him to 'fix' it

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Medal of Honor recipient Dakota Meyer details troops living with mold, no hot water and broken AC as he takes on Hegseth’s push to fix military housing.

Some troops have gone a year without hot water. Others are sleeping in their cars to escape barracks without air conditioning. And some are asking for little more than drywall, spackle and paint so they can make repairs themselves.

Those are among the conditions Medal of Honor recipient Dakota Meyer says he has encountered while touring military installations on a mission from War Secretary Pete Hegseth: Fix a barracks system plagued by decades of neglect.

"I would tell you that there's only a couple that I've seen that are good," Meyer told Fox News Digital in an interview Thursday.

Meyer, who serves as senior adviser to the Pentagon's Barracks Task Force, has visited more than eight installations across the Army, Navy, Air Force and Marine Corps since taking on the assignment and plans to visit 30 before the end of the year.

"What I thought was gonna be a professional job very quickly turned into personal," Meyer said.

At one installation, Meyer recalled asking a 19-year-old service member what she would change about her living conditions if she could "wave a magic wand."

"If she could just have a hot shower," Meyer recalled. "It's absolutely heartbreaking."

The Pentagon's barracks problems long predate Hegseth and have survived repeated attempts at reform.

A sweeping 2023 Government Accountability Office investigation documented serious health and safety hazards and found that the department lacked reliable information about the condition of its own barracks, could not determine how much of its facilities spending went toward them and provided insufficient oversight.

Congress and the military services have since committed billions toward improvements, including initiatives ranging from the Marine Corps' "Barracks 2030" to a Navy overhaul of unaccompanied housing. Yet serious problems persist. As of this year, 12 of GAO's 31 recommendations remained outstanding.

Hegseth has sought to centralize the effort under a department-wide task force and accelerate improvements, with Meyer saying the secretary has made clear that identifying problems is no longer enough.

"The secretary has made it clear to me he doesn't expect me to observe this, study it, or to make it better. He expects me to fix it," Meyer said. "And that's exactly what we're going to do."

A damaged ceiling hangs over a bed in military barracks visited as part of the Pentagon's effort to assess living conditions for service members. (Courtesy of Dakota Meyer)

Meyer, the only Medal of Honor hero to reenlist after receiving the award, said one of his first requests was for Hegseth to give the task force an "authoritative role," rather than simply create another layer of oversight.

The goal, he said, was to give the team the ability "to go out and make decisions, to be able to have impact, to be able to make change, and to be able to direct where that funding goes and how fast it gets to our service members."

Meyer argued the problem is not simply a lack of money. Funding shortfalls, continuing resolutions and years of deferred maintenance have all contributed, he said, but so have layers of contracting rules and bureaucracy that can slow even relatively basic repairs.

A separate watchdog review found the Pentagon facing a $285 billion maintenance backlog across its broader military facilities' portfolio, underscoring the scale of the department's infrastructure challenges.

"When you look at the contracting aspect, it's easier for people to say no," Meyer said.

Funding is also part of the problem, according to Meyer, who pointed to Congress' failure to enact full-year defense appropriations for fiscal 2027. The Pentagon is currently operating under a continuing resolution through Dec. 11, which generally funds ongoing programs at prior-year rates while lawmakers negotiate a broader spending package.

"When you can't get a budget, when you're waiting on Congress to pass a budget, to be able to get the funding there, I mean, that is a huge issue," Meyer said.

A barracks room shows a sink without a basin and makeshift lighting installed around a bathroom ceiling during an inspection of service members' living conditions. (Courtesy of Dakota Meyer)

But he also argued that the Pentagon has too often measured progress by how much money has been obligated or put under contract, rather than whether troops actually see improvements in their living conditions.

"Our service members don't, they don't live in our budget lines," Meyer said. "They live in the rooms that those budget lines are there to impact."

For Meyer, that has meant getting out of Washington and hearing directly from the troops living with the consequences.

"You can't fix these problems or understand these problems by standing on the carpet of the Pentagon," he said. "You've got to go out there and talk to these service members."

What he has heard, Meyer said, is striking partly because of how little troops are asking for.

"They're asking for a hot shower. They're asking for a place to wash their clothes. They're asking for a door that locks. They're asking to be able to go to sleep in a place that doesn't have mold. I mean, they're asking for light bulbs."

"They're willing to be part of the solution," he added.

Meyer stopped short of placing the blame solely on installation commanders, saying many want to improve conditions but have been constrained by a system that has normalized poor barracks for years.

"I think this is a multidimensional problem, and it's complex," he said. "I think that you have commanders out there that want to take care of their service members. I've seen that across the board. But ... they still have to have the ability to do that."

Meyer was far less forgiving of previous Pentagon leadership, arguing that years of investments and reform initiatives had failed to translate into adequate living conditions for troops.

"Don't kid yourself," Meyer said. "In the past ... it wasn't a priority to the leadership."

A window air-conditioning unit and apparent mold and water damage are seen during inspections of military facilities as the Pentagon works to improve service members' living conditions. (Courtesy of Dakota Meyer)

Previous administrations and military leaders did launch barracks improvement efforts and devoted billions of dollars to construction, renovation and maintenance, even as government watchdogs continued to document serious deficiencies.

Meyer said Hegseth has made the issue a personal priority.

"We have the Secretary of War who is putting people first, and he's not just saying that, he's doing it through his actions," Meyer said.

Meyer has put his own name on whether the latest effort succeeds, saying previously that if the barracks aren't fixed, there is "only one person to blame" — himself.

Asked what "fixed" would actually look like, Meyer offered a simpler standard than any Pentagon metric.

"People are living in places that I would be okay for my daughters living in," he said.
