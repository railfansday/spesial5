At the Races: Regional realignment

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

By Daniela Altimari, Mary Ellen McIntire and Niels Lesniewski

New England is the ancestral home of moderate Republicans, from the Saltonstalls to the Bushes, whose fiscal conservatism and social moderation defined the national GOP for decades.

Last week, Yankee Republicans suffered a near-total wipeout. Maine’s Susan Collins, New England’s last GOP senator, lost her seat to Democrat Troy Jackson, leaving former Maine Gov. Paul LePage, who just won a House seat, as the lone Republican headed to Congress from the region. (The picture was a bit brighter at the state level, where GOP Govs. Phil Scott of Vermont and Kelly Ayotte of New Hampshire were both reelected.)

One Republican operative sees a path out of the wilderness in a post-Trump era. In an opinion piece in The Boston Globe, consultant Kristin Tate urged New England Republicans to focus on fiscal discipline and recognize that “independence from national party leadership can be a political asset, not an act of betrayal.”

After all, as the midterm election results remind us, no political realignment lasts forever.

Three decades ago, there were plenty of moderate Democrats from Southern states serving in Congress, governor’s mansions and even the White House. But in the years since Bill Clinton’s exit from the political stage, Democrats have largely written off the South.

Which is partly why last week’s Democratic wins in the Carolinas, Georgia and, perhaps most notably, Mississippi were so stunning. All the elements that powered the party to victory nationally – disillusionment with Donald Trump, a rise in economic populism and hunger for change – played a role in the South as well.

But other factors were unique to a region that has seen renewed attempts to dilute Black political power with the redrawing of House districts to favor the GOP.

The Republican redistricting gambit in Southern states did not fully yield the desired results and seems to have helped galvanize Black voters and their allies, powering Democrats to unlikely victories.

It’s too soon to say whether the GOP’s reduced hold on the South represents a temporary blip or an enduring shift.

But Joe Trippi, a longtime Democratic strategist who advised candidates in Alabama and Florida this year, has a message for his party: “We need to invest in the South.”

#MSSEN: Democrat Scott Colom defeated GOP Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith to become Mississippi’s first Black senator since Reconstruction and the first in his party to win a Senate seat in the Magnolia State since 1982.

Tar Heel tidal wave: Democrats in North Carolina have picked up at least five House seats, a striking development given the steep structural disadvantages the party faced. Prior to last week, no House Republican incumbent had lost a general election in the state since Republicans won control of the redistricting process more than a decade ago. And the Democratic gains could grow: In the 5th District, former journalist Chuck Hubbard narrowly leads 11-term Republican Rep. Virginia Foxx, and in the 3rd District, former state Rep. Raymond Smith holds a razor-thin edge over Republican Rep. Greg Murphy. The Associated Press had not called either race at publication time, though Murphy has conceded.

Republicans pick campaign chairs: Ohio Sen. Bernie Moreno and Georgia Rep. Brian Jack will be the chairs of Republicans’ respective campaign committees for the 2028 cycle. GOP lawmakers held their leadership elections Tuesday, as our colleagues Savannah Behrmann and John T. Bennett reported. Senate Republicans are sticking with South Dakota’s John Thune to lead them in the next Congress, while House Republicans picked former Freedom Caucus Chair Jim Jordan as their leader.

Redistricting doubts: Maryland could be getting a new congressional map after voters approved a constitutional amendment that would clear the way for new lines in 2028. Our colleague Victor Feldman has more on the result and other election-related ballot measures across the country. But it’s not clear how many other states may look to redistrict next cycle after Republican efforts over the past 18 months largely didn’t pay off in last week’s elections.

Run the table: With Rep. Darren Soto winning a sixth term in Florida, no Democratic incumbent lost reelection to a Republican this year. Soto’s win in a redrawn district also meant that Florida Democrats defended all four seats the GOP had targeted under a new congressional map.

California contest: Rep. Ken Calvert defeated fellow Republican Rep. Young Kim in a rare member-versus-member contest for a redrawn Southern California seat that turned acrimonious. Republicans are poised to hold just four House seats in the Golden State after Democrats made good on five pickup opportunities under a new congressional map.

Cheesecake Factory caucus: The latest episode of the Political Theater podcast, hosted by Roll Call editor-in-chief Jason Dick, breaks down how candidates in both parties fared in counties where The Cheesecake Factory operates, after the popular chain restaurant became something of a side character in the closing weeks of the midterm campaign.

Back to school: Newly elected members of Congress are on Capitol Hill this week for freshman orientation, an experience Massachusetts Rep.-elect Dan Koh told our colleague Nina Heller is “like high school – but it’s fun high school.” The incoming lawmakers took their class photo Thursday morning and will get to pick their Capitol Hill offices after next week’s famed lottery, Nina writes.

Tracking the count: As of 4:30 p.m. Eastern time Thursday, House Democrats had flipped 44 seats, while Republicans had picked up three. Twelve races remain uncalled, including a pair in Louisiana that will be decided by December runoffs. On the Senate side, Democrats have flipped eight seats, with only Alaska’s race still undecided.

Guv update: Democrats flipped at least five governorships last week and will hold a majority of governor’s mansions next year. Two gubernatorial races remain uncalled by the AP: Kansas, where Democratic state Sen. Cindy Holscher is narrowly ahead of Republican state Senate President Ty Masterson, and Alaska, where Democratic former state Rep. Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins is just below the 50 percent threshold to win the election outright. In territorial elections, Republicans flipped the governor’s office in Guam while voters in the Northern Mariana Islands will pick their next governor in a Nov. 20 runoff.

#TNSEN: Republican Sen. Marsha Blackburn, who was elected Tennessee governor last week, is poised to pick her Senate successor. According to the Nashville Banner, the short list includes Knox County Mayor Glenn Jacobs, a former professional wrestler, Reps. Tim Burchett and Chuck Fleischmann, and Tennessee Deputy Gov. Stuart McWhorter.

2028 on the brain: A handful of House Democrats are already considering 2028 Senate bids, according to Axios, including Kansas’ Sharice Davids, North Carolina’s Don Davis and Pennsylvania’s Chris DeLuzio.

Senator Morrison?  Minnesota Rep. Kelly Morrison is earning buzz as a potential appointee to fill the Senate seat that Democrat Amy Klobuchar is vacating to become governor, Semafor reports. House Democrats aren’t likely to be as concerned about open seats as their Republican counterparts were in recent years, given the size of the incoming Democratic majority.

AOC’s future: Progressive activists have launched a new organization to encourage New York Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez to run for president in 2028. Meanwhile, Axios reports that Ocasio-Cortez has parted ways with the digital fundraising firm she’s worked with for several years and has brought on the same firm that Vermont Sen. Bernie Sanders used during his two presidential campaigns.

Women lead: Shirley Emehelu is New Jersey’s new lieutenant governor after Democratic Gov. Mikie Sherrill picked her to succeed Dale Caldwell, who resigned in September amid sexual harassment allegations and other ethical lapses. New Jersey is now one of five states to have women in both the governor’s and lieutenant governor’s offices, along with Massachusetts, Arkansas, Iowa and Virginia. Iowa will come off the list next year after an all-male Democratic ticket swept this year’s gubernatorial race, though five other states elected women to both offices last week.

Congress will once again be filled with losers – i.e, new members who have lost previous bids for elected office. Roll Call elections analyst Nathan L. Gonzales of Inside Elections returns to his biennial tradition as he runs down the latest list of “losers.”

Ducking the wave: Democrats notched big wins in unexpected places, but one state grew redder after last week’s elections. The New York Times checks in with voters in South Dakota, where the GOP won all nine statewide elections.

Here come the MBAs: Democrats with corporate connections were among the winners in last week’s elections, countering the idea that candidates with serious business credentials have struggled within the party, The Wall Street Journal reports. The field of 19 winners with M.B.A. degrees includes Costco board member Helena Buonanno Foulkes, who won the Rhode Island governor’s race, and Paige Cognetti, a Goldman Sachs alum who flipped a House seat in Pennsylvania.

How Ricketts did it: Nebraska Sen. Pete Ricketts’ election night success was one of the few bright spots for the GOP. Ricketts fended off a challenge from independent Dan Osborn by painting him as a Democrat, questioning his authenticity and highlighting leaked audio of his comments denigrating Trump voters, writes the Nebraska Examiner.

Was it necessary? California redrew its congressional map to favor Democrats in response to Texas taking a similar step to boost the GOP. But some are questioning whether the state needed to cast aside its independent redistricting commission to rejigger the lines, given the scale of Democrats’ wins. “What they were literally saying is, ‘Let’s undo democracy to protect democracy,’” Lincoln Project co-founder Mike Madrid told CalMatters. “There’s something very Orwellian about it.”

Song of the cycle: The New York Times reviews “Talafreako,” an unlikely viral anthem celebrating the newly elected Democratic senator from Texas.

History does rhyme: An opinion piece in The (Charleston) Post & Courier argues that the win by Democratic Sen.-elect Annie Andrews is the latest in a long line of cases in which South Carolina voters bucked the idea of party bosses picking their leaders.

The man behind the grill: The New York Times profiles Russ Hudson, the Mississippi man in Colom’s now-legendary “Pick another protein” ad.

As of Thursday afternoon, they appear on track to match that number. They’ve already won nine seats – a mark they last cleared in the 1982 midterms – and they’re narrowly ahead in two more races.

If those leads hold, Democrats’ 11-member delegation would look much different than the one sworn in some 66 years ago in 1961. Tar Heel Democrats taking office in January would include four women, six Black members and one Hispanic member.

Their 11 representatives (out of 12) in the 87th Congress were all white men who consistently voted to uphold racial segregation and oppose major federal civil rights legislation.

Vote counting in Alaska is expected to conclude by Wednesday, Nov. 18, the targeted date for ranked choice tabulations in races in which no candidate took more than 50 percent of the vote. As of Thursday, Democratic former Rep. Mary Peltola narrowly leads Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan in the Senate race, though she’s below the 50 percent threshold.

Meanwhile, Democrats are gathering in Denver beginning Thursday to celebrate their historic wins and plot their next steps.
