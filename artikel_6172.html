You might have seen Serbia in the news lately. The country’s recent national elections enacted a political sea change spearheaded by student groups seeking greater freedoms and opportunities.

The jubilant energy unleashed, combined with the Serbian capital’s soon-to-shine spotlight as it prepares to host the 2027 World Expo make now the perfect time to plan your visit to Belgrade. That’s why the city made our list of top destinations for next year.

Sleepier than some other European capitals, yet vibrant and buzzing with a construction boom, Belgrade is coming into its own. It’s also easier than ever to get there, thanks to plentiful airline connections not only to other European hubs but also to the U.S. via the country’s national carrier, Air Serbia.

So start your points planning and make Belgrade a part of your next transatlantic trip.

The fastest way to get to Belgrade’s Nikola Tesla Airport (BEG) from the U.S. is aboard Air Serbia from Chicago’s O’Hare International Airport (ORD) or New York’s John F. Kennedy International Airport (JFK). The airline also recently announced that it will launch twice-weekly service to Miami International Airport (MIA) in May 2027.

That said, award availability is hard to come by since these flights only operate a few times a week. Instead, you might want to consider redeeming Flying Blue miles for flights on Air France via Paris-Charles de Gaulle Airport (CDG) or via Amsterdam Airport Schiphol (AMS) on KLM.

For Star Alliance flyers, award availability between the U.S. and Belgrade is plentiful on carriers like LOT via Warsaw Chopin Airport (WAW) and Lufthansa via Frankfurt Airport (FRA) or Munich International Airport (MUC) and Swiss via Zurich Airport (ZRH), starting at 42,500 Aeroplan points each way in economy and 75,000 Aeroplan points each way in business class. Taxes and fees range from about $100 to $150 in each direction.

Belgrade is expecting to see its first Intercontinental open in early 2027, just in time for the World Expo. Until then, there are plenty of other hotels where your points will come in handy.

Located in the new riverside Belgrade Waterfront District, which is home to a sprawling luxury mall, a promenade with tons of bars, cafes and restaurants, and more than a few high-rise apartment buildings, the St. Regis Belgrade opened in November 2024 in a striking glass-and-steel tower. The hotel has just 119 spacious rooms and suites with floor-to-ceiling windows, a beautiful spa with an indoor pool, a cafe-patisserie, a continental restaurant and an outpost of the brand’s signature St. Regis Bar. Rates start at $290 or 43,000 Marriott Bonvoy points per night.

The Hyatt Regency Belgrade is a comfortable, modern hotel in Novi Beograd, the city’s main business district, across the Sava River from the historic core, so it’s not the best for leisure travelers. That said, you can’t beat nightly rates that start at $148 or 6,000 World of Hyatt points per night.

The Hilton Belgrade is centrally located near Slavija Square, within walking distance of attractions like the Nikola Tesla Museum and the Church of Saint Sava. It has 242 rooms and suites and a restaurant that serves contemporary Serbian cuisine. Rates start at $170 or 50,000 Hilton Honors points per night.

You won’t find Uber in Serbia, but taxis are plentiful and can be hailed via apps like Yandex, Pink Taxi Nova and Car:Go. The apps make it easy to pay with a credit card. Just be sure to use a credit card that earns bonus points on ride-hailing services or on travel charges in general.

Sometimes drivers will not accept rides unless you are paying cash, though, which you can also book through the app and then take a metered ride. So be sure to pull some cash out at the ATM at the airport before heading into the city.

Once in Belgrade, it’s easy to get around by public trams and buses, which are free to use within the city center.

For a city of its size, Belgrade’s restaurant scene is surprisingly diverse. Of course, you should make it a priority to try typical Serbian dishes like grilled cevapi sausages with fluffy bread and ajvar red pepper sauce, and sarma, which is pickled cabbage stuffed with spiced meat and rice, not to mention the plethora of cured meats and mountain cheeses. Bela Reka is a great place to try a smattering of Serbian specialties, many of them sourced from the restaurant’s own farm in the Homolje Mountains.

Perched on the riverbank, Suvenir serves traditional dishes like roasted lamb and bacon-wrapped prunes in a beautiful setting.

For a contemporary take on Balkan cuisine, Iva is a sophisticated hideaway tucked into a courtyard near Saint Sava church where age-old dishes are reinterpreted through a modern lens. The piping-hot burek pastries stuffed with cheese, baked peach, hot pepper and honey are not to miss ($12), as is the confit river pike-perch with gnocchi and charred Swiss chard in a white wine sauce ($20). A glass of crisp Deuric Severna Morava white wine from the Fruska Gora region is the perfect accompaniment ($7).

Be sure to book ahead for a table at S5 by Angie, a fine-dining bolthole hidden in an otherwise unremarkable apartment building in the city’s quiet Zemun district. Spend a while enjoying a cocktail in the living-room-like parlor, complete with a vinyl record collection, then settle in for a multicourse feast prepared by chef "Angie" Risimic that might include dishes like creamy labneh with tart tomatoes, fluffy ravioli with mild ricotta-style cheese doused in a green herb and blackberry sauce with trout roe, and tender pork in a rosehip demi-glace with end-of-summer morels. Prix-fixe menu prices vary but range from $75-$150, though a la carte ordering is also possible.

For something more casual, spend an evening strolling cobblestones of Skadarska Street in the bohemian neighborhood of Skadarlija, just a short walk from Republic Square, where you will find over a dozen traditional kafane, or taverns, serving similar menus of hearty dishes along with plenty of plum-flavored rakija spirits.

Tri Sesira, or the Three Hats, was a favorite of the Serbian Romantic poet and painter Dura Jaksic, a statue of whom sits outside his humble former house nearby.

Belgrade has a handful of worthwhile museums, but not so many as to commandeer your entire timeline.

The National Museum has exhibits ranging from prehistoric artifacts to medieval Balkan icons, Italian Renaissance and Flemish masters, French Impressionists and modern artists, including pieces by well-known Yugoslav painters like Sava Sumanovic. Entrance is $3 for adults.

It’s free to stroll the grounds of Belgrade Fortress and Kalemegdan Park, which overlook the confluence of the Danube and Sava Rivers and are popular places for sunset meetups.

Some of the museums at the fortress have their own entrance fees and operating hours. The citadel dates to the third century B.C.E., though it has seen hundreds of battles and been destroyed, rebuilt and expanded dozens of times in the ensuing millennia.

Even if churches aren’t your thing, no visit to Belgrade would be complete without a stop at the brilliant white, copper-domed Church of Saint Sava, which is one of the largest Eastern Orthodox churches in the world.

The interior mosaics gleam with gold and are utterly breathtaking. Entrance is free.

Nearby the Nikola Tesla Museum is a fun, interactive affair. It offers guided tours every 45 minutes, but they fill up fast, so get there at least half an hour before the next one to line up. Entrance costs $7.50 and includes a short film, then demonstrations using Tesla coils before a small exhibit spanning the inventor’s life and some of his personal effects.

Sitting in a small park of its own, the Museum of Yugoslavia is a memorial to the former country’s late strongman, Josip Broz Tito, who rose to prominence in the resistance to the Nazi occupation of World War II and eventually oversaw every aspect of life in Yugoslavia until his death in 1980.

The complex includes his mausoleum — the so-called “House of Flowers” — an older building with some of his personal belongings and displays about daily life in Yugoslavia and a larger wing with temporary exhibits including a recent one on themes of propaganda in wartime. Admission is $7.50 for adults.

The pedestrian shopping street of Knez Mihailova is also well worth a wander, if only for unique souvenirs produced by Serbian craftspeople at the Belgrade Window boutique.

It might not have been on your radar before, but Belgrade is well worth a visit, especially with all the events on the calendar for 2027, including hosting the World Expo. Along with some luxurious new hotels and a growing crop of exciting new restaurants, plus a small but impressive cadre of museums and galleries, it might just become one of your favorite European capitals.
