Scientists designed a new way to hunt for signs of life in space using electricity and artificial intelligence

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

An electrical sensor told mirror-image amino acids apart, one molecule at a time, taking a step toward small instruments that can look for signs of life.

Your left and right hands are made of the same parts, yet a left hand won’t fit properly into a right glove. Many of the molecules in living things come in the same two mirror-image versions, and life on Earth builds its proteins overwhelmingly from just one of them.

A team led by researchers in Japan has now told those two versions apart, one molecule at a time, using only an electrical current.

The sensor could one day help a small spacecraft instrument look for that lopsided mix on another world. The team also tested it on extracts from a meteorite and from desert soil, where it caught the broad pattern but missed on several details.

Takahito Ohshiro and Masateru Taniguchi of the University of Osaka led the work with colleagues at Georgia Tech, NASA Goddard, MIT and the University of Chicago. Everything so far has happened in a lab, and no flight instrument exists yet.

Amino acids, the small molecules that link up into proteins, mostly come in mirror images, a left-handed form and a right-handed form. Scientists label them L and D. Chemistry without life tends to make the two in equal amounts, while living things lean heavily toward one, the L form.

A strong tilt toward one hand is therefore a possible sign of biology. Some researchers have proposed an excess of more than 20 percent in several amino acids as a threshold that would point to life.

Meteorites once seemed to carry such a tilt, but those results are now generally blamed on contamination from Earth. Samples brought back from the asteroids Ryugu and Bennu showed roughly equal mixes.

In an interview with Earth.com, Taniguchi was careful about what the measurement can show. “Importantly, chirality should not be viewed as proof of life by itself,” Taniguchi said.

“It would become much more powerful when combined with molecular identity, abundance, chemical context, and other biosignatures.”

The sensor starts as a thin gold wire that is pulled until it snaps. The two broken tips sit a little more than half a billionth of a meter apart. That’s more than 100,000 times narrower than a human hair.

A small voltage pushes a trickle of current across that gap. When an amino acid drifts in, the current jumps for a few thousandths of a second, then drops back.

Each jump has a shape, and the L and D forms of the same amino acid leave slightly different shapes. A machine-learning program trained on thousands of these spikes learned to sort them.

“We are not measuring only an average signal from a large population of molecules,” Taniguchi said.

Standard lab methods for telling the two mirror-image molecules apart rely on light or chemical reagents. The electrical approach needs neither. Taniguchi said spacecraft instruments have to be small, sturdy, sensitive and energy-efficient, and dropping the optics could help with that.

The team tested 19 amino acids that come in both forms. Working from a single spike, the program picked the right form about 88 percent of the time on average. The hardest pair, glutamine, came in at 78 percent, and the easiest, isoleucine, at 97 percent.

One spike is a weak basis for a verdict, and a five-minute run collects hundreds. By the team’s calculation, a program that is right 90 percent of the time on each spike would be right 99.9 percent of the time after 16 spikes.

A harder test asked the program to name both the amino acid and its hand from all 39 possible forms at once. It was right about half the time, far better than the 1-in-39 rate that a guess would give.

Mixtures were harder still. In test solutions holding four or five amino acids, some counted shares landed close to the true values. One was off by 56 percent.

Next came real samples. The team ran extracts from a fragment of the Murchison meteorite, supplied by the Smithsonian. It also tested soil from two sites in Chile’s Atacama Desert, a Mars-like environment. A standard lab instrument analyzed the same extracts for comparison.

The program only counted spikes it was confident about, and it rejected about 93 percent of the spikes from a blank sample that contained no extract.

Both methods found glycine to be the most common amino acid in the meteorite, at 48 percent by the sensor and 63 percent by the standard instrument.

Other results drifted. The sensor put serine at nearly 12 percent of the meteorite’s amino acids, compared with well under 1 percent by the standard method. It also found no glutamic acid at all in either desert soil.

For handedness, the sensor matched the standard method on aspartic acid in the meteorite, but not on alanine or valine.

“In that sense, the real samples were not only a demonstration of the technology, but also an important test of its current limits,” Taniguchi told Earth.com.

The authors wrote that the method should be treated as a targeted tool for a shortlist of known molecules, not a full chemical inventory. It can’t yet recognize a molecule it wasn’t trained on, and a messy natural sample adds background signals that blur the counts.

Taniguchi listed what a flight version would need. It would have to measure amounts more accurately, flag unfamiliar molecules, prepare its own samples and keep out contamination from Earth.

It would also need shrunken electronics that survive vibration, radiation and temperature swings.

“Ultimately, we envision a compact instrument in which sample preparation, single-molecule electrical detection, and AI-based molecular identification operate as one system,” Taniguchi said.

Only a working prototype can show whether the sensor works away from a clean lab bench, on a sample from Mars, an icy moon or an asteroid.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Communications.
