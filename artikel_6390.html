The 7 Best Smokeless Fire Pits We Tested (and Yes, They Produced Hardly Any Smoke)

From set-it-and-forget-it backyard centerpieces to grab-and-go options for weekend camping trips, we tried well-regarded options from Solo Stove, Breeo, Blue Sky, and more.

Engineered to contain flames and burn fuel more completely, smokeless fire pits deliver ambient warmth with a lot less smoke in your eyes than a traditional campfire. They achieve this through two points of combustion: The first happens at the bottom, where airflow feeds burning wood; the second occurs at the top, where the fire’s smoke and gas output are burned off. And while no smokeless fire pit is 100 percent smokeless, if you use best practices (like burning dry wood) they come pretty darn close.

We tested smokeless fire pits in our New York and Pennsylvania backyards, building fires rather than judging them on specs alone. Along with smoke output, we considered size, which affects wood capacity and where the heat actually lands, as well as how easy each pit was to empty of ash and store once done, among other factors. We also used them repeatedly over time to see how their finishes held up, because a brand-new pit can look very different after several fires.

Solo Stove is the brand most people associate with smokeless fire pits (though Breeo was the first game in town). The latest joiner in Solo Stove’s lineup is the Summit, an upgraded take on the ever-popular Bonfire. Its 23-pound weight makes it just as easy to lift and store off-season, and there’s even less setup required. All-in-all, it’s the best smokeless fire pit for most people who want to enjoy a backyard fire pit that can be stored off-season.

Because the Summit is four to five inches shorter than the Bonfire, it does a better job of keeping your feet warm, one of the major gripes our Test Editor Brad Ford had of the earlier model. He says that while it holds less firewood, it undoubtedly produces a higher flame that extends well above the rim.

Another improvement he points out is the Summit’s integrated stand, which he says is sturdier and allows for better airflow than that of the Bonfire’s detached stand. There’s also a deeper ash pan, which is particularly helpful because the Summit burns through wood fast and needs frequent reloading. In terms of price, the 19.5-inch Summit is $100 more than a Bonfire of the same size, an extra cost that’s well worth the investment.

Before lighting a single fire, we evaluated the things that can make or break a smokeless fire pit before you ever use them. We assessed the packaging and how well each pit was protected in transit, weighed every model, and evaluated assembly. We read the instructions, noting whether they were clear, complete, and actually useful.

We considered whether it could be disassembled for storage, and what came in the box versus what required an additional purchase. While We had plenty of outdoor space to work with, safety always came first: we set every pit on heavy-duty pavers and kept a fire extinguisher close at hand.

Then we lit them up. We used comparable loads of seasoned firewood to assess how easily each pit started, how much smoke it produced, how evenly it burned, and how much heat it put out. We paid attention to flame height and consistency, too, and whether the fire actually warmed people sitting around it—not just looked good from a distance. We measured logs to see exactly what each pit could accommodate and tested wood pellets where models supported them.

Finally, we evaluated the less glamorous but equally important details: how easy each pit was to tend, how much ash accumulated, how easily that ash could be removed, and what it took to clean and put each model away. And because a fire pit can be beautiful at 7 p.m. and a pain in the ass at 10 p.m., we tested for both.

Whether you want a smokeless fire pit to heat your outdoor space or be more of an ambient seasonal moment is an important consideration. For long nights by the fire with family and friends, you’ll likely want to invest in a larger model that measures 24 inches in diameter. In testing, models that size and up exuded warmth for several feet, and could easily warm four to six people.

They could also handle 14- to 16-inch-long logs. The trade-off: Models this size are a lot heavier and harder to move. If you’re looking for more of a warming accent, narrower fire pits work wonders. You can also maximize some models’ output by adding a heat deflector that sits above the flames and pushes heat out laterally, instead of letting it escape upward.

Ideally, the smokeless fire pit should work right out of the box, with limited-to-no setup, besides setting the pit on a base. It should also break down easily. If you’ll be storing it between fires or seasons, a model that’s easy to disassemble, carry, and put away is key.

The shallower and wider the smokeless fire pit, the easier it is to make a fire from scratch. You simply have more base room to work with to set up your paper, kindling, and wood, and less depth to maneuver to light your fire once ready. No matter your choice, you’ll definitely want a lighter with a long metal wand and a safety catch. None of the models we tested included one.

Burning pellets instead of wood was the closest we got to a truly smokeless fire during our testing. Made of compressed wood waste like sawdust, chips, and shavings, pellets are easy to light, always bone dry, and produce a hotter, more consistent flame than logs. There are trade-offs, though: They lack the classic crackle and pop of a wood-burning fire and that wonderful smell.

Bags of the stuff also typically cost more than a handful of logs at the hardware store, yet burn about the same length of time. But, if you’re allergic to smoke, don’t want your sweaters to smell of it, have wet wood drying out, or want a reliable and even fire every single time, pellets are an excellent way to go. Some fire pits work with them right out of the box; others need an adapter.

Every smokeless pit burns wood. Many smokeless fire pits can also use wood pellets right out of the box. Some, like Solo Stove, need an adapter, so the tiny pellets don’t drop right through the pit’s air holes into the ashcan below. Some portable options may only be wide or deep enough to handle twigs (pellets may be a better option for those).

Pro tip: Wet or green wood will always be slow to burn and emit a lot of smoke; use a moisture meter to suss out whether your logs are dry enough to light up before putting a match to them.

If you plan to use your fire pit regularly, easy ash removal should be a deciding factor. The last thing you want to have to do is pick up and tip a heavy steel drum over a trashcan in order to empty it of ash. Instead, look for a removable ash pan or tray that allows you to lift the ash out cleanly once the pit has fully cooled (usually 12 to 24 hours after a fire has ended). Otherwise, you’ll need a shovel to scoop out the ash.

If you don’t have space to leave your fire pit outside all year, think about who will move it and how often. Even a small pit designed for camping trips can weigh as much as 20 pounds. Fortunately, these options usually come with a carrying bag, so you can sling them over your shoulder. Meanwhile, larger models can top 45 pounds (the 24-inch Breeo weighs just over 60 pounds), which matters when you have to move it under cover or into the garage at the end of the season. Units that come apart make this easier; having a dolly or hand truck on hand helps, too.

Material affects how long your new pit will look the way it does on arrival. While stainless steel looks shiny and modern from the start, heat from the flames will discolor it, and potentially quite quickly, as some testing showed. (You can restore its original shine with a stainless-steel cleaner such as Bar Keepers Friend, but that’s extra work.) Corten-steel usually costs more but forms a protective rust-colored patina that looks attractively weathered over time. Painted or powder-coated steel usually costs less, but can scratch and chip with repeat use.

Whatever finish you choose, keeping your smokeless fire pit empty of ash and covered when it’s not in use will help extend its life. Rainwater sitting in a fire pit full of ash can create a corrosive, alkaline slurry that accelerates rust and can damage the metal. Some models come with a cover as part of their bundle; others charge extra for it. Our reviews note whether a cover is included in every pick.

Smokeless fire pits can be a lot more than a place to warm your hands on chilly nights. In some cases, you can turn them into an outdoor kitchen. Brands like Breeo, Solo Stove, and Cuisinart have grill grates, griddles, pizza ovens, or roasting sticks that let you cook over an open fire. Other add-ons also make a difference, like a cover, if you plan to leave it outside when not in use; a spark screen, particularly if you have toddlers and pets around (though, warning: they can also get hot to the touch); and fire pokers or ashtray lifters, so you don’t get your hands dirty. Definitely check the accessory lineup before you buy your pit; they’re often model-specific. A pair of fire-resistant gloves is always a good investment.

While product photos often depict smokeless fire pits sitting on wood decks or backyards, it’s worth noting that their bases can get hot enough to scorch or discolor lawns, wood decks, composite, and asphalt. Ideally, yours should sit on brick, concrete, stone, or sand. But in the absence of that, a dedicated base (sometimes included, other times sold separately) helps raise the unit off the ground. For an even safer setup, consider adding a fireproof mat underneath. You should also keep a bucket of water or a fire extinguisher nearby and check for local burn bans before lighting.
