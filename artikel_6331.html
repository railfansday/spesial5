Deadly eel virus can spread in a surprising way

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Scientists found that infected European eels can spread a dangerous virus through bites, raising new concerns for conservation.

A herpesvirus that kills European eels can jump from one fish to the next when they bite each other.

A Belgian study provides the first evidence that biting can transmit this virus.

Fish live in water, so the assumption has been that their viruses travel through it too. For plenty of aquatic diseases, that holds up. But anguillid herpesvirus 1 does not last well outside its host.

At 77°F (25°C) about 90% of its infectivity had gone within 12 hours in lab tests. The mucus coating an eel’s skin works against it as well.

The virus spreads between eels all the same. A team led by Natacha Delrez at the University of Liège says biting may help explain how.

When infected and healthy eels were made to bite one another, every healthy animal involved became infected. That held whether the contact was at the mouth or the tail, and whichever animal did the biting.

The fish were anesthetized and kept in laboratory tanks. So the work shows a bite can pass on the infection.

Whether that happens regularly in the wild is left open.

Infected tissue in fish is normally located by examining organs after the animal has been euthanized.

The method gives detailed information. But it does not allow one infection to be followed in the same living animal.

Delrez and her colleagues inserted two genes into the virus.

One of them was a firefly gene for light production. In eels infected with the altered virus, tissues where it was multiplying emitted a faint glow.

The light is invisible to a person looking at the animal, but specialized imaging equipment picks it up.

The images recorded which parts of the body were infected first and where infection appeared later.

The modified virus was compared with the original. Growth rates in cell cultures were the same.

Disease symptoms and mortality in infected eels were comparable. On that basis the team used the altered virus for the rest of the work.

Four strains were used at first, collected in the Netherlands, Denmark, and the United Kingdom. Genome analysis was done with the MRC-University of Glasgow Centre for Virus Research.

One strain was used in the later experiments, which reduced the number of animals needed.

Age turned out to matter. European eels arrive on the continent’s coasts as glass eels, tiny and close to transparent. They darken once they push into rivers and other inland habitats.

Then they settle into a yellow eel stage, which may go on for years of feeding and growing before the final migration back to sea.

Glass eels held in contaminated water showed no detectable infection.

Among slightly older juveniles, infection was detected in one of six animals on both day three and day six, and in two of six on day nine.

Yellow eels did worst, with all six animals infected within six days.

Imaging put the earliest signals in the gills and in tissues inside the mouth, areas around the teeth and lips among them.

That detail became important later. In three separate waterborne exposure experiments, all yellow eels showed symptoms between days eight and 12. About 20% died.

The virus appeared to enter at the mouth and gills. But in eels kept together, imaging showed glowing patches elsewhere.

The patches were farther down the body, particularly around the tail. They did not match the pattern seen earlier.

Yellow eels housed in groups of 20 were then compared with infected yellow eels housed individually.

At nine days, group-housed eels had several glowing patches around their heads and tails. Eels housed alone had visible signals around the mouth and gills and none along the rest of the body.

Solitary eels were still seriously infected. Tests found virus in internal organs, including the brain and heart. They lacked only the separate patches on the outer body.

The team turned to behavior. Yellow eels generally live alone, can defend territory aggressively, and sometimes bite one another when sharing limited space.

Eel farmers documented the behavior decades earlier. They described bites around the mouth and nips at the tail, particularly in crowded conditions.

The patches were in those same locations. Some, the researchers proposed, had developed where another infected eel had bitten.

They were not part of the ordinary progression of the disease. The team tested that directly.

For the biting experiments the eels were anesthetized. Three kinds of contact were then arranged between infected and healthy animals.

In one, an infected eel bit a healthy eel around the mouth. The other two involved the tail. The healthy eel delivered the bite in one case, the infected eel in the other.

The healthy animals were afterward placed in separate tanks, which ruled out further biting between them.

Every one became infected, and some showed glowing tissue three days after the bite. The team took that as strong evidence that the physical contact itself could transfer the virus.

Handling might still have moved contaminated mucus or water from one fish to another by accident.

To separate that from the bite, they washed the heads or tails of infected eels in small quantities of water. Healthy animals were then exposed to the wash water.

Much less infection followed. None of the healthy eels tested positive three days after exposure to the head-washing water, and two showed weak signals at six days.

In the biting experiments, every exposed animal was infected. A bite, the team concluded, does more for the virus. It is not simply moving a little contaminated water from one animal to the next.

None of this rules water out. The team drew water from a tank of 15 infected eels and transferred it to tanks of healthy fish.

All six exposed animals became infected. So did all six when the water had been standing for 24 hours.

The picture changed once the scientists started diluting.

Water diluted a hundredfold infected five of six eels while fresh. Twelve hours later the same dilution reached only three animals, and a day later it reached none at all.

Water diluted ten-thousandfold and left to stand for 12 hours caused no detectable infection in the tested eels.

Infectivity also dropped fast in ordinary water, and eel skin mucus cut it further.

Viral concentration helps determine whether water spreads the disease.

A crowded eel farm keeps it high. A river does the opposite: the water moves, the virus thins out, and fish may be far apart. A bite skips all of that.

Numbers matter too. Young eels reaching European coastlines are now at roughly 1% of early-1980s levels, a fall linked to dams, overfishing, pollution, and climate change.

In a tank, under anesthesia, with the bites arranged by hand, anguillid herpesvirus 1 passed from eel to eel every time.

What eels get up to in a river is harder to know. Their interactions are difficult to observe. Little is known about how often they bite one another at today’s reduced population densities.

What the researchers will say is that nothing like it has been reported before.

They call this the first case of a virus spreading between members of the same fish species through biting in water.

No one has yet documented a naturally occurring bite that transmitted this virus between two wild eels.

The findings raise concerns for eel farms, where animals are housed together and aggressive encounters may be more likely.

Restocking programs worry the authors for a related reason. Young eels are sometimes raised or held together, then released into rivers that may already contain infected animals.

European eels are critically endangered, and dams and other barriers divide their remaining river habitats.

Protecting them means understanding diseases, not just releasing more fish.

Researchers suggest exploring vaccines for farmed eels and conservation programs that move young glass eels into rivers.

The findings show that the virus may spread through more than water. A bite between eels can also pass it on.

The full study was published in the Journal of Virology.
