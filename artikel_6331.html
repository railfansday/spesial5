Rose-colored rock found in the Sahara holds a rare piece of ancient Mars

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A rose-colored meteorite found in the Sahara is a piece of ancient Martian crust that formed at least 4.1 billion years ago.

Although most of the surface of Mars is ancient rock, most Martian meteorites that land on Earth are young.

Found in the Sahara Desert in 2022, a rose-colored stone is an exception. It’s a piece of the planet’s early crust that formed at least 4.1 billion years ago.

Scientists rarely get to study a sample of the crust Mars had when it was young. The stone is called Teghaza 001, after the salt plain in Mali where it was discovered.

Yang Liu of NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California led the new study.

“It was only after we started looking closely that we realized this meteorite is really unique,” she said. “One of the paper’s reviewers called it a Rosetta Stone for Mars.”

The rock weighs nearly 2 pounds (800 grams). It’s one of roughly 435 known meteorites from Mars, and the new study is a first description of it rather than a full accounting.

Like every Martian meteorite, Teghaza 001 was blasted off the planet’s surface by an impact and later landed on Earth.

Collectors often find meteorites in deserts and snowy plains, where they stand out against the bare landscape.

Researchers at the University of New Mexico first identified the rock as Martian by checking its oxygen.

Oxygen atoms come in slightly different forms, called isotopes, and the mix of those forms differs from one planet to the next. Teghaza 001’s mix matched the mix in other Martian meteorites.

Scientists also confirm a Martian origin by comparing a meteorite’s minerals and chemistry with data from NASA missions, including the Viking landers and the Curiosity and Perseverance rovers.

At first, Teghaza 001 looked like a shergottite, the most common kind of meteorite from Mars.

Liu studies planetary samples and has seen plenty of those, so she almost passed on the chance to examine it. She looked anyway and found odd textures and unusual minerals she hadn’t seen before.

Liu and her colleagues found zircon crystals in the meteorite. Zircon records clues about how a rock formed, and geologists use it to date a sample precisely.

François Tissot, a coauthor at Caltech, helped the team use a dating method his group developed. Some dating methods require dissolving the sample being analyzed.

Tissot’s method instead measures single mineral grains on the surface of a piece smaller than a fingernail, so most of a rare rock stays intact for future studies.

“Right now, we are one of very few labs in the world able to perform this kind of age-dating, and the first to date such an ancient Martian meteorite,” Tissot said.

“Our hope is to pave the way for dating even more Martian meteorites, or samples returned from other planets, their moons, and asteroids.”

Liu was shocked by the results. Water on Mars had changed the rock sometime around 4.1 billion years ago, which suggests the rock itself formed even earlier.

Only two other Martian meteorites found on Earth are counted among the most ancient. One of them, ALH84001, is about the same age but is made almost entirely of one mineral.

The third, nicknamed Black Beauty, is made of small fragments of volcanic rock.

Teghaza 001’s mix of minerals formed as molten rock cooled slowly into crust. Later, water moving underground changed some of them.

That mix matches what NASA’s Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter has observed in the heavily cratered southern highlands of Mars.

The rock’s exact birthplace on Mars remains unknown, but the team identified several locations that match its minerals and chemistry.

Lee Saper, the study’s second author, did the research as a postdoctoral scholar at JPL and is now a scientist at NASA’s Ames Research Center in Silicon Valley.

“This is really our first piece of Martian crust from a part of Mars we’ve never had a sample of before. It records interactions of water and rock and maybe even an imprint of the Martian atmosphere as it was losing water,” Saper said.

The water that reacted with Teghaza 001 was enriched in oxygen-18, an isotope with two more neutrons than the most common form.

Clues in the oxygen isotopes, the texture, and the chemistry of the rock’s minerals point to an active water cycle on early Mars.

A second study, published in June in Science Advances and led by Saper, measured water in the meteorite’s minerals.

It found that water on Mars about 4.1 billion years ago held at least roughly twice the share of heavy hydrogen that Earth’s seawater does.

The authors wrote that this result supports models in which young Mars lost hydrogen to space quickly.

So far, Liu, Saper and their colleagues have described the rock in broad terms. Later studies of it could help show how water and rock interacted on ancient Mars.

“We’ve only scratched the surface,” Liu said.

Liu and her coauthors now want to see what other research teams can learn from Teghaza 001.

The full study was published in the journal Science.
