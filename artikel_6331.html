Narwhals helped solve a mystery deep in Greenland’s fjords

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Sensors carried by six narwhals showed warm Atlantic water reaching deep into remote East Greenland fjords and areas near glaciers.

Greenland’s glaciers do not melt only from warm air above them.

Ocean water can also reach their underwater edges and melt ice from below, where the process is much harder to measure.

This matters because melting glaciers release freshwater into the North Atlantic. That water can change ocean layers, currents, and climate patterns far beyond Greenland.

Yet many East Greenland fjords are difficult to reach. Sea ice, harsh weather, and long distances often keep research ships away, leaving major gaps in ocean records.

Six narwhals have now helped fill some of those gaps in data from fjords. Sensors carried during their normal dives showed warmer Atlantic water reaching deep fjords and glacier fronts far inland.

Scoresby Sound lies on Greenland’s east coast and forms the world’s largest fjord system.

Fjords are long, narrow arms of the sea that reach deep into the land. Narwhals can move through fjord areas that ships rarely reach.

Professor Mads Peter Heide-Jørgensen of the Greenland Institute of Natural Resources helped lead the work.

“The narwhals have been in parts of the fjords where very few measurements have been taken before. And there, 300 kilometers from the coast, we find traces of Atlantic water.

“It is completely new that we can show this,” said Professor Heide-Jørgensen.

Local hunters helped capture and tag six narwhals near Scoresby Sound. The tags measured water temperature, salt level, depth, and location for between 85 and 332 days.

Between 2017 and 2020, the animals collected 2,060 ocean profiles.

Each profile recorded water conditions at several depths during a dive, giving the team repeated readings from remote waters that ships rarely sample directly.

Some readings came from more than 4,900 feet (1,500 meters) below the surface.

Professor Susanne Ditlevsen of the University of Copenhagen helped analyze the large collection.

“Now we suddenly have measurements from places we can use in our models, giving us a much more complete picture of the ocean. It is absolutely fantastic to use the animals in this way,” said Professor Ditlevsen.

East Greenland has cold Polar Water near the surface and warmer, saltier Atlantic water below it. Salinity simply means the amount of salt in water.

The team combined the narwhal readings with more than 2,200 older profiles from the World Ocean Database.

It then built a model, which is a math tool that finds patterns in large amounts of data.

“We used the two large datasets to build a robust model showing how temperature, salinity and depth are related,” explained Professor Ditlevsen.

“The models show that the layer consisting of Atlantic water has become thicker, while the upper layer of Polar Water has become thinner,” she added.

The results point to a stronger Atlantic influence in the region.

The Blosseville Basin warmed over the study period, with summer records showing warming in both the colder Polar Water and the warmer Atlantic water.

The shape of the seafloor controls where deep water can travel. Shallow ridges may block it, while deep channels can carry it toward glaciers.

Atlantic water became the main water type below about 820 feet (250 meters) in the Blosseville Basin.

It also crossed an underwater ridge and moved into the remote inner parts of Scoresby Sound.

The warmer water reached glacier basins about 186 miles (300 kilometers) from the coast.

Glaciers in deep basins faced warmer and saltier water than glaciers along the shallower Blosseville Coast.

This shows why fjord depth matters. A glacier can sit far inland and still receive Atlantic water when a deep route connects it to the open sea.

The team compared ocean conditions with the amount of ice that broke away from glaciers. This process is called iceberg discharge.

Where water at 1,310 feet (400 meters) was warmer and saltier, icebergs accounted for a larger share of the glaciers’ total summer ice loss.

“This is the water that helps melt the glaciers. We show how the warm water can contribute to accelerating the rate at which glaciers lose ice,” said Professor Heide-Jørgensen.

Ocean heat can reach the ice from below, but the results do not show that warm water caused every change in glacier loss.

Air temperature, surface melting, seafloor shape, and local glacier conditions also matter, so the pattern does not point to one simple cause.

Only six narwhals carried sensors, and the animals chose their own routes.

Because narwhals prefer cold water, they may have spent less time in the warmer Atlantic layer.

The older ocean records were also uneven. Few measurements came from some fjords before 2010, so long-term changes inside Scoresby Sound remain difficult to judge.

The tags were not recovered after use. The team could not check them again for small changes in accuracy, especially in the salt readings.

Winter findings need extra care because narwhals provided most of the recent winter measurements. Their routes may not represent the whole region.

Climate models use real measurements to estimate how Earth may change.

Better ocean records can improve estimates of future glacier melt in Greenland, a point that Professor Ditlevsen connected to the larger climate question.

“In the large climate models used to predict future climate change, glacier melt plays a major role. That is why it is crucial that we improve our understanding of it, particularly because Greenland contains so much ice.”

The new data can strengthen future climate estimates. Narwhals cannot replace ships, satellites, or long-term ocean stations, but they can reach waters that people struggle to measure.

Their dives revealed a clear route for warm Atlantic water into remote fjords.

The study is published in the journal Science Advances.
