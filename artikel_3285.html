‘Fjord’ DP Tudor Vladimir Panduru On Crafting Long Takes With Cristian Mungiu & Challenges Of Shooting In Remote Norway — ToruńCamerimage

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Tudor Vladimir Panduru touched down at the ToruńCamerimage Film Festival in Poland last week where the cinematographer and frequent Cristian Mungiu collaborator reflected on how he crafted the visuals for the Romanian filmmaker’s Palme d’Or winning title Fjord.

Panduru and Mungiu have been working together for more than a decade, working on titles such as Graduation and R.M.N. in addition to Fjord, and the DP notes that the long relationship means they have built a shorthand and “very specific visual language” together.

Fjord, which is released by Neon in the U.S. and UK, follows a Norwegian woman (Renate Reinsve) and her devout Romanian husband (Sebastian Stan) who relocate with their five children to a remote, close-knit coastal town. Their teenage children form a fast bond with a neighboring family as unease begins to simmer around their rigid beliefs. When allegations of abuse emerge, they are forced to confront a system of judgement that blurs the line between concern and prejudice.

“Cristian is a good observer, and he always wants to be truthful,” says Panduru. “And I follow that in that process of trying not to be judgemental in my approach as well.

He continues: “From the point of the script, the story had some specificity. The fact that these two families were close to each other meant that we had to find a place or a geography that would tell that from the start, and that was the hardest thing throughout the process of the scout.”

The team scouted from Bergen to Ålesund before finding neighbouring houses overlooking the water for the main characters. “It wasn’t so common to find two houses in such close vicinity because when we took the first photos and watched them, we were totally immersed in that universe. So, that’s a layer that wasn’t present in the script.”

He admits that scheduling and the remote location made it a “hard production” at times. “It was tricky organizing everything and getting to the locations. You had to either take two ferries or a long road and a ferry, so we were quite stuck there. It was really remote.”

A further challenge, he notes, was the fact that the story takes place across three seasons but was only shot in March and April. “When we scouted the third time in January, there was a lot of snow and we were hoping to have images with small characters in the snow, but it wasn’t like that. We had to use a bit of VFX on the way. The only evening shots that had a lot of snow were shot on the weekend because that’s when it happened.

“Our first AD was the one who proposed we shoot when it was snowing at the weekend and we even convinced Sebastian to come with us for one of the shots. I think that was a really interesting part of doing Fjord, was the fact that the crew, who were from Romania, Norway, Finland, Sweden and all over, was really homogenous. We really were like a family, so we felt support from all sides.”

Thanks to weather conditions, Panduru points out that one of the film’s more emotional scenes when Reinsve’s character’s children are taken into custody, was actually shot on the first day of the shoot “because we were supposed to shoot in the snow.”

“Shooting such an intense scene for the first day was really interesting but, in a way, because we did that at the beginning, everybody understood what the film was about,” he says.

Panduru says that one of the most challenging sequences was the “jump in the water”, a long take which sees the camera follow Reinsve’s character searching on the ground to jumping into the sea.

“Because we shoot in these long takes, the most important thing was to have the emotional trajectory of the situation,” he says. “We thought a lot about how to achieve that moment in a technical way, and we thought about cranes and drones, but it wasn’t the right thing. I proposed to Cristian to test to see if I could physically jump in the water with them and we did it in a weekend and it worked.”
