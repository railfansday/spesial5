How the Newly Elected Congress Can Save the Smithsonian

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

This is the November 6, 2026 edition of True Colors, a look inside the global deals and colorful critiques that make the art world go round, with a weekly guide to the must-see openings, auctions, and fairs.

It’s True Colors. The Democrats have flipped the House and the Senate, which could usher in a new era of funding at the Smithsonian—or, at the very least, a vibe shift in DC. There are a ton of gallery shows opening around New York, and on the West Coast, LACMA Art+Film Gala goes down tomorrow night. We got the rare chance to sit down with one of the night’s honorees, Vija Celmins.

Mentioned in this issue: Donald Trump, Lonnie Bunch, Alma Allen, Tilman Fertitta, Yoshitomo Nara, Loïc Gouzer, Vija Celmins, Matthew Marks, Leonardo DiCaprio, Eva Chow, Denis Villeneuve, Gary Hume, Jacqueline Tran, Michael Govan, Katherine Ross, James Turrell, Riko Mizuno, Matthew Barney, Roni Horn, Meshell Ndegeocello, Steve McQueen, Matthieu Blazy, Rosemarie Trockel, Carrie Mae Weems, Marina Abramović, Wade Guyton, Christopher Wool, Rashid Johnson, Felix Rödder, Calvin Marcus, James Ensor.

Trump has spent the last two years attacking Washington’s cultural hub with executive orders and legally dubious firings issued via fiat. But the Smithsonian gets its funding through Congress—what if Congress actually acted on that authority?

Sure, Congress has more pressing issues to address once the deeply blue House and Senate get sworn in early next year. But here at True Colors I think it’s fair to ask the question: Will lawmakers wind back the years of impact the Trump administration has had on the Smithsonian Institution?

Although Congress has authority over the cultural behemoth, the White House has in just the last two years issued executive orders, been extremely hands-on in its public signage, and mounted pressure campaigns to fire directors and censor artworks. Congress could have stopped all of this, but didn't. Republicans naturally didn’t want to cross Trump and support the beleaguered arts mecca. But Democrats have every reason to—after the new Congress is sworn in they’ll have the power to appropriate massive amounts of funding to each Smithsonian museum—or even create new museums. Plus, that can impact how the whole institution is run on a day-to-day basis, as Congress has the power to shake up the Smithsonian’s board of regents. That governing body will choose the next Smithsonian secretary, after Lonnie Bunch resigned earlier this year.

I’m particularly interested in who takes over as the director of the Hirshhorn Museum and Sculpture Garden. Melissa Chiu, who held the position for 12 years, stepped down earlier this year to be the director of the Guggenheim Museum in New York, and rumors are swirling about who will succeed her in the post. The prospect of a culture-living Congress—and, who knows...a Democratic president come 2029?—could make the Smithsonian seem like a swell place to be long term.

Add in the prospect of a new crop of congressional staffers and the attendant cottage industries of consultants and lobbyists all flocking to Washington, and I could see the Hirshhorn getting caught up in the vibe shift, pronto. There’s expected to be a sea change of DC residents in town, and there’s every reason to believe they’ll make the (free! always free!) contemporary art museum a constant stop on the rotation. Plus, the long-gestating renovation of the Sculpture Garden literally just opened this past weekend. I don’t have any inside intel on who’s in the running for the job —but it’s looking like a pretty great gig these days.

Meanwhile, the artworks that filled the American Pavilion at the Venice Biennale, made by State Department–tapped artist Alma Allen, might be stuck in Italy for the foreseeable future. Funding has run out, and there’s nothing in the budget for moving the works. I wonder how that can be resolved. Seems like a job for an art-loving, restaurant-owning Italian ambassador, Tilman Fertitta!

The massive two-location Yoshitomo Nara show opened at David Zwirner last night, truly one of the great gallery moments here in New York this season. Don’t miss the pop-up one-painting exhibition of Nara’s In the Milky Lake / Thinking One, which will be sold by Loïc Gouzer’s one-lot auction business, Fair Warning. It’s on view at 534 W. 22nd Street. The lot opens for live bidding November 19 and is expected to sell for the eye-popping sum of $10 million to $15 million.

You can also see Nara’s work in Washington, DC, where the aforementioned Hirshhorn just opened a grand survey of the artist timed to the inauguration of its sculpture garden. And if you want to check out some of Nara’s artworks in a very unexpected place, head to the Avenue A dive bar Niagara, where Nara drunkenly created a masterwork on the walls late one night after an opening at Pace. The bar wisely conserved the artwork behind glass, and it remains there nearly two decades later.

It’s not usually worth reporting on the toasts given at gala dinners here in the prized real estate that is this newspaper’s Table Hopping section, but the Dia Fall Night had some good honorary toast content! On Monday, Matthew Barney proved himself to be a very deep Roni Horn fan with his toast. Then Meshell Ndegeocello went super outside the box, honoring Steve McQueen by stepping in front of the podium and leading a band around the gala. The whole evening was like that. Chanel wasn’t just a sponsor—the brand’s creative director Matthieu Blazy, a true contemporary art head, chaired the whole thing alongside artists Rosemarie Trockel and Carrie Mae Weems. Marina Abramović was at my table; she told me she reads Vanity Fair every time she’s on a plane and appreciates how thick the issues have been lately. Marina, it’s an honor. Thank you for reading.

Matthew Marks hosted a party on Wednesday night for Wade Guyton’s excellent new show at Bridges, the best restaurant on Chatham Square. Fun fact: Christopher Wool’s old studio was right next to the restaurant some decades back.

On Election Day, as the results started coming in, Rashid Johnson opened a show of new work at Hauser & Wirth’s Wooster Street gallery, which has recently become my favorite of the mega-gallery’s dozen or so global outposts. It’s a throwback of sorts, a small space in the neighborhood that was once the city’s gallery nexus, with an excellent track record of great shows. The dinner was at the gallery’s restaurant, Manuela, right around the corner on Prince Street. There was caviar, and while it was served to celebrate Johnson, it happened to come out right as the news networks indicated the Democrats were going to flip a Senate seat in South Carolina. Spirits were high.

If you’re making your way to The Met to see “Krasner and Pollock: Past Continuous,” do stop by Rodder, the gallery founded by Felix Rödder, to see one of the most inspired artist pairings in the city right now. Calvin Marcus, the fanatically talented and free-wheeling Los Angeles painter, has new and recent paintings, drawings, and ceramics installed alongside work by the great Belgian weirdo James Ensor. The chemistry between these two artists from different centuries and continents is uncanny. The dinner for the show was held somewhere unusual: House of the Redeemer, the Episcopalian Church in a former Italian Renaissance Revival townhouse once owned by a Vanderbilt heiress.
