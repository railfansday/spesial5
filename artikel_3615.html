World of Warcraft: Forever has, much as the original World of Warcraft did back in 2004, completely consumed my life. I write about games for a living, mind, so the attention vortex it has wrought upon me is primarily professional obligation, rather than just irresponsibility.

What is it?: A reimagining of 2004's World of Warcraft, with new balancing, lighting, dungeons, and items.Expect to pay: A monthly subscription of $14.99/£10.99. But at the time of writing, it has been 443 hours since Forever's launch. I have spent 147 of those hours asleep (less, but let's just pretend I've been getting a solid eight), leaving around 296 hours of my waking mind. 230 of them have been spent playing World of Warcraft.

That's a deeply concerning amount of time if I wasn't getting a salary to review it—it has been part of my 9-5. I've also often been keeping the game open while I, you know, feed and bathe myself and do chores. So we're probably just in the realm of 'moderately worrying' rather than 'cause for a wellness check'.

Don't worry, I intend to take a break soon: But during those 230 hours, I have hit level 60, entered 31 dungeons, completed 941 quests, levelled engineering, mining, and first aid to 300, and apparently /waved at other players 50 times. Which are rookie numbers that I need to get up.

But I've also found myself taking in the scenery, laughing at my poor luck at bottles, whining about the drop rates of basilisk brains, splitting mining nodes with party members, mercilessly grinding ogres into paste for my first mount, and talking to more strangers while questing than I have in years of playing more modern, streamlined MMOs.

I'm not pointing this out to simply cast aside the role of nostalgia in my enjoyment, but I do think nostalgia can only carry an attention span so far. It certainly doesn't keep 230 hours of medically inadvisable gameplay afloat on its own. There's something else going on here; something special that's been unearthed.

All of the things that made an old-school MMO like the original WoW so deeply enthralling back in 2004 are not simply dated, to be cast aside as an inferior form of videogame.

The crappy drop rates, the fighting over tags, the running everywhere (there is so much running everywhere) while not always justified, is part of an experience that's stronger for its inconveniences. The friction burn you get from jutting up against a game that's occasionally just hostile to you. All of that connects you to the world, and the people in it, in a very real way.

First, I want to talk about what Forever itself has actually brought to the table. After all, much of what I've talked about already has been reverence paid to a series of design decisions that were made over 20 years ago.

In the here and now, though, Forever has around 1,000 new quests (the original WoW is estimated to've had somewhere in the mid-2,000s, so that's approximately 30-50% more Warcraft), nine new dungeons, and two new major levelling zones in Mount Hyjal and The Riverglades.

To encourage and support levelling alts, Blizzard's added a new mechanic in the Legacy tree—a genuinely cool feature that encourages you to level alts (or do endgame achievements and raids) to unlock perks, like making levelling slightly easier by giving you a small heal when you kill an enemy in the open world, or by giving you more resources when you gather things.

Then there's the retooled class and item balance, the latter of which is my one, very mild point of disappointment: There's an increasing amount of trinkets and weapons I've kept around for specific use cases, sure, but at least while levelling, the fantasy of equipping an anti-worgen sword to do some worgen-killing quests (as so often quoted in Blizzard's promotional material) hasn't quite panned out. That's not to say it's gone by the wayside completely, though.

I've seen a two-handed axe that lets you get mining nodes, a knife that lets you skin humanoids, gloves that increase your mining skill, boots that let you walk under water, and so on. It's just not a core part of the levelling experience.

It does also improve somewhat when you get into the later levels. For instance, I now have a two-handed questing sword green that I'll keep in my bags basically forever because it lets me spin around like a propeller to slow my fall speed: And I'm a Warrior, so that's a hugely useful traversal tool for someone without any way to slow fall.

Professions have also had a total facelift, and for the first time, I'm actually levelling crafting and gathering skills on my characters because of it.

Not only do crafting ones like blacksmithing actually give you really good gear as you level up (rather than just a couple of big capstone items) but all professions can now turn in writs and crates, buying new recipes via the latter and unlocking them with a rep grind via the former.

The new zones are both excellent—the Riverglades is pretty, but not out of place, whereas Hyjal is a satisfying (and very efficient) climb up a mythological mountain ruined by war, with some legitimately cool questlines to it. In both, questing is enough of a breeze that it does feel almost too easy, with Hyjal smoothing over the typically arduous 58-60 grind completely. But they're not any more efficient than, say, The Burning Crusade's better zones, which is a level of kayfabe-breaking I can get behind.

But until you hit that point, Forever's newness is downright subtle. A chain here, a few fetch quests there. Just enough to make questing in every zone worth it, just enough to even out the occasional, clunky speedbumps.

With Riverglades being level 38, and Mount Hyjal not mattering until the final stretch, Forever partially buries the lede, and you'd be forgiven for thinking it a touch unambitious. But with Blizzard's commitment to add new Riverglade-style zones and questlines to the game in the years to come, it's going to keep morphing into something new with the passing years, and what's there—when you get to it—is plainly impressive.

Such as Forever's new dungeons. I've done seven out of the new nine crawls, and while they're a little more mechanically dense than the vanilla predecessors (bosses sometimes have like, two moves) I've been fascinated by just how well that old-school design has been emulated.

With the exception of Alcaz Island, these things are sprawling, time-consuming romps. And, while controversial, the changes to dungeon XP (putting the majority of it in their quests while lowering the amount you get from mobs) has made completing them an adventure.

I recently knocked out Sunken Temple's questline, but the world-trotting, multiple quest-chain journey to get all my pre-requisites had me doing another unrelated dungeon a second time, fighting my way to the top of a troll fortress in the Hinterlands, helping out a drunk dwarf, and speaking to the spirit of a fallen Horde warrior to get his hipflask.

So when it was time to finally darken the door of the Sunken Temple and get it all done, I felt accomplished. Like I'd not just rocked up to a portal to get some fat loots, but that I'd actually—like an adventurer might—spent considerable time researching, preparing, and bracing myself for the place.

Didn't stop us forgetting that the Shade of Eranikus summons all the currently-alive dragon adds to him and getting absolutely mauled, but hey-ho.

More to the point, my XP reward for that journey felt considerably far more potent than if I'd simply hopped in there to rinse it 5 times. And I might have skipped it if the XP reward wasn't mostly dispensed via questing. I won't say dungeon XP isn't an issue for some players, but for me, the exercise works.

And while you're running around the world, forced to jog ignobly between major locations, Forever's new lighting system and draw distance shines through. The reason these lighting changes aren't in retail yet is because, primarily, they're hand-crafted.

Durotar has these darling oversaturated blue shadows, Felwood bathes everything in a sickly green, Duskwood's mist hangs thick in the air, and the lightning that crackles over the Blasted Lands actually casts electric blue light across the stones of Nethergarde Keep.

But there are subtle changes, too. You can see Nordrassil, the world tree at the top of Mount Hyjal, from Durotar—as well as other zones on the continent. Dun Morogh and Winterspring have uniquely hostile snowstorm blizzards that cut out visibility entirely, similar to Tanaris' sandstorms.

There hasn't just been an RTX-on style filter slapped over Azeroth, this is all bespoke, and it's made all of my journeys that much more atmospheric. I've had to stop just to take in the godrays pushing through the canopy of Ashenvale, or the burning sunset descending on the seas of Stormwind Harbor.

More importantly, these effects really pull out the strengths of that old-school mode of design. Both the 2004 version of World of Warcraft and Forever understand that in order for a world to feel massive, it needs to be difficult to traverse sometimes. In order for it to be social, there needs to be a level of unfairness that being social will overcome.

I thoroughly enjoyed World of Warcraft: Midnight, the latest expansion in modern World of Warcraft, when I reviewed it for launch. I thought the questlines, which I can remember the glossy cinematics and major storybeats of, were engaging enough. And I still think its new revamped Silvermoon is gorgeous, and a complete flex from the studio's art team.

I could not, however, tell you a single thing about my questing experience that wasn't dictated to me by the game. Yes, I liked certain story beats or character conversations, but the actual experience of hitting stuff? It's a blur. I flew somewhere, I pushed my buttons, I interacted with something. I flew somewhere else, and you know what I did there? I pushed more buttons.

In contrast, I've been playing Forever for almost three weeks, and I've already made memories. On a jog to an annoying Scarlet Monastery dungeon questline that sent me to Desolace, I met up with two dwarven brothers (or husbands? They had the same second name) who were making the journey. We joined up with more people to kill a bunch of high-level skeletons, and barely got through an overpull by the skin of our teeth.

In Stranglethorn Vale, a kindly shadow priest six levels higher than me partied up to kill some pirates, then pointed out that there was a good questline that could be done by scavenging beach bottles. They got theirs on their first try. I, for some reason, had to pick up 26 of the damn things.

On another journey, I decided to try and grab my fire-hardened mail armor for a warrior questline, which sent my alliance character all the way into The Barrens, to Razorfen Kraul. This is not a great dungeon to do if you're an alliance character, so the pickings were slim. Which meant that I not only tanked the whole thing as Fury, but that the folks I was with were chill enough to do a quick road-trip to Feralas with me to get another dungeon quest.

The point being, I only had these memorable experiences and spoke to these people because I was being, and I know this is a bad word for a lot of MMO players, inefficient. Absolute bastard questlines made me do something far out of the way. And every single time I've played along, I wound up having a lovely time meeting similarly stubborn folks and making new friends.

A lot of these inconveniences, carried over from vanilla and left alone in Forever, are there to push you and people you meet organically, out in the world, into close quarters and camaraderie. To do "kill X" quests, you need to intentionally party up with that random night elf warrior, rather than just pass them by. To get certain questlines accomplished at the level they're given, you need to ask for help in general chat.

Some chains will send you to some random city, forcing you to learn the most efficient boat route to get there—or to simply leave that quest in your log until you next happen to pop your head into Darnassus. The travel time makes the world feel big. The encouraged cooperation makes it feel alive and social. And all that friction produces friendships—these quests suck to do on your own but are so often a breeze to do together. Or, at the very least, they're more entertaining with company.

This is why it doesn't just feel like nostalgia to me. It's the rub of the whole thing. I am not enjoying these quests because, at some time in the past, I had a nice innocent memory surrounding them that I'm reliving. It's actually all about what's happening in the present: Grouping up with people, striking up conversations, sharing in misery as it takes a full 45 minutes to fill our bags with pirate hats.

I'm honestly just marvelling about the fact that, in 2026, that this sort of design still works. It works tremendously well. Blizzard's new campfire mechanic, which I like a lot, is just a cherry-on-top-nudge towards striking up the conversations I was already having anyway.

So many times, my purposefully chill vibes have been met with genuine kindness: People stay on to help you finish quests they've already completed, they show you new questlines. Sometimes it's a tit-for-tat. A shaman helped me out with my level 50 warrior quest in exchange for me helping him kill an elite somewhere, which hey, fair dos.

This all culminated in an incident where I joined in for a random kill quest in my mid-40s. A priest, around their mid-40s as well, commented that man, a lotta folks had their mounts already! Myself included. I'd spent a few hours mining over 200 Iron Ores and killing cave ogres, like a maniac.

I asked how far off they were. They said they had 80 gold out of 90. I threw 15 gold at them before I realised what I was doing.

This isn't to humblebrag, mind. My grinding had produced a surplus of cash that I wasn't really spending: But it was like I'd been suddenly possessed by the spirit of Christmases past, future, and present. I gave a complete stranger a significant amount of money with the complete understanding I'd never talk to them again. Because I felt like being nice, because so many people had been nice to me.

I do understand the yearning for flight paths, for lower respec costs, for shared mob tagging, and for quests that don't require your character to walk the circumference of the earth. And if none of that's to your taste, then hey, fine—but more than anything, Forever's proven to me that those flying mount doomers back in The Burning Crusade might've been onto something.

I still enjoy 'theme park' MMOs, but Forever is such a deeply exciting prospect to me. It's a second chance for this older, slower, more social form of videogame to have a renaissance. Before booting up Forever on November 4, I couldn't tell you the last time I had an adventure (an organic one, not one delivered to me through a storyline) in an MMORPG. Two weeks later, and I've had several, and I keep logging on and embarking on these long, arduous quest chains because I'm excited to have more.

Forever is such rare air. A major MMO studio has been given the blessing to revamp a 20+ year old version of a game and add to it. And while it's already added a considerable amount, Forever feels like proof that, given enough exposure in the spotlight, people could actually like a new MMO with its design sensibilities.

And as a fan of lots of different kinds of MMOs—be they slow-paced adventures like Forever or on-rails dungeon-rinsing theme parks like Final Fantasy 14—I want this genre to have the kind of actual diversity it deserves. More MMOs than World of Warcraft should get to take this sort of risk. More MMOs should be able to take it slow.

Until that new game comes, however, I'll happily play Forever, and I cannot wait to roll up an alt to start doing it all over again.

A hopeful rebirth for the old-school MMO formula.
