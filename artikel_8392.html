Your filing status sets your tax brackets, your standard deduction, and which credits you can claim. Your marital status on December 31 and who lives with you decide which statuses you can use, so check every status your situation allows and file under the one with the lowest tax.

A wedding, divorce, separation, death, or child moving in can make last year’s status wrong, or leave a cheaper one unclaimed.

Your marital status on December 31 counts for the whole year. Married that day, even after a December wedding, you file jointly or separately. A parent whose spouse lived elsewhere for the last six months of the year may qualify as head of household instead. If you’re divorced or legally separated under a court decree by then, you’re unmarried for the year. Widowed during the year? You can still file jointly for that year unless you remarried before it ended.

Each of the five statuses has its own test.

A widower with a dependent child files jointly the year his wife dies, then as a qualifying surviving spouse for the next two years, so he keeps the joint return’s brackets and deduction.

For 2026 returns, filed in 2027, the standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers and married people filing separately, $24,150 for heads of household, and $32,200 for joint returns and qualifying surviving spouses. A joint return’s brackets are also twice as wide as a single filer’s for every rate up to 32%, and head of household usually gets lower rates than single.

Run the IRS’s filing status tool before you file. It asks about your marriage and who pays for your home, then picks the status with the lowest tax.
