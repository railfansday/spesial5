TL;DR: The SEC Division of Corporation Finance added questions 1.4, 1.5 and 1.6 to its crypto-assets FAQ on November 2, 2026. SEC staff positions may be informative without having the legal force of a statute, judicial judgment or final Commission rule.

This distinction is worth making because crypto regulation often arrives in several formats: speeches, staff statements, enforcement actions, proposals and final rules. They have different implications even when the subject is the same. A dated FAQ update merits attention without being promoted into a sweeping legal turning point.

The SEC Division of Corporation Finance added questions 1.4, 1.5 and 1.6 to its crypto-assets FAQ on November 2, 2026. The new staff answers address governance through foundations or organisations, what counts as control over participation and the difference between a functional network and a decentralised one. The page expressly says its answers represent staff views, have no legal force and have not been approved or disapproved by the Commission. The dated original disclosure is the starting point for the figures and product scope discussed here.

A functioning crypto system can have a native asset that users need for programmatic activity without necessarily being decentralised. Under the staff explanation, decentralisation is a distinct question about whether any party or group holds operational, economic or voting control. A foundation or association involved in broad-based governance is not automatically a controlling central party; the actual powers and decision rules matter. This is a narrower, more useful clarification than saying every governance token has received a new legal classification.

The additions give lawyers and builders more concrete language for evaluating governance arrangements. A protocol may distribute votes widely while retaining privileged access, unilateral upgrade powers or concentrated economic influence. The staff view asks analysts to study those powers rather than rely on the mere existence of a formal foundation. That could influence how firms document their networks, although it does not eliminate case-by-case analysis or statutory legal requirements.

Crypto policy updates arrive as proposals, staff explanations, enforcement cases and final rules. Those documents carry different legal weight even when they use similar language. A careful article tells readers who issued the text, when it was published and whether it changes obligations now. That distinction is especially important for financial firms making compliance decisions.

This development also connects to two subjects our newsroom has been following: DTCC’s tokenization service and settlement controls and financial infrastructure and technology policy coverage. Those background reports help separate established facts from a new, still-developing announcement.

SEC staff positions may be informative without having the legal force of a statute, judicial judgment or final Commission rule. Investors should also resist the leap from regulatory discussion to token-price predictions. A frequently asked questions page is not an approval notice for any specific exchange, security or stablecoin.

Regulation ultimately affects operational choices: where customer assets are held, who can move them, how decisions are recorded and which risks are disclosed. The market can debate policy direction, but firms still need to map any published guidance to existing duties and the specific activity they carry out. General labels rarely answer those questions.

Watch for direct Commission statements, formal rulemaking notices, court decisions and clarifications that identify the affected transactions. Teams reviewing compliance can use the FAQ alongside primary legal sources, while documenting areas where further advice is necessary. Precision about who issued a document is essential to accurate policy reporting.
