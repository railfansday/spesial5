US Seeks to Seize More Than $1.2 Billion Linked to Binance Hack

US authorities say they have obtained court approval to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in digital assets linked to the major October attack on Binance. The action is part of a wider effort to trace and freeze funds attributed to North Korean hackers, and it comes alongside a separate disruption of infrastructure allegedly used to deceive victims through fake video calls.

The distinction between freezing assets, receiving permission to seize them and actually returning money to victims is crucial. A large enforcement announcement should not be presented as evidence that all the stolen cryptocurrency has already been recovered.

The Block reported on the Department of Justice announcement that court approval had been secured to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion tied to the October 29 Binance breach. US authorities have attributed the attack to North Korean operators associated with the TraderTraitor group, with the initial theft described as exceeding $6 billion.

The department said more than $2.7 billion had been frozen through the combined efforts of authorities and private-sector companies. A frozen balance is not the same as funds delivered back to an exchange or its customers. Investigators must still establish ownership, navigate legal processes and prevent assets from being moved through other services or jurisdictions.

Public blockchain records can show a trail of transfers from one address to another. That visibility can be valuable when investigators identify wallets and work with trading platforms or other service providers to block proceeds. But hackers can use bridges, mixers and chains with different transaction models to complicate tracing. Technical identification also has to be connected to a legal basis for seizure.

Another challenge is timing. An asset may be visible on-chain but move before a service can act, or it may already have been exchanged into another token. That is why seizure orders, freezes and completed recoveries need to be reported separately. The value of assets at the time of the theft can also differ from what can be recovered months later.

The DOJ also described action against a scheme referred to as FaceStealer, which allegedly used manipulated or impersonated video calls to compromise victims. Reporting said the operation had been associated with more than $400 million in theft over an 18-month period. Although both announcements involve North Korean cyber activity, they are distinct investigations and should not be merged into a single total of stolen funds.

The underlying lesson is operational. Attackers are increasingly interested in persuading a person to compromise their own device or sign an unsafe transaction, not merely in exploiting smart-contract code. Wallet permissions, staff identity verification and secure approval procedures can therefore matter as much as blockchain analytics after the fact.

Binance has said it arranged coverage for affected customer assets, according to the reporting, but that statement should be assessed separately from the government's legal recovery process. Customers should rely on official exchange communications for account-specific details, and be cautious of unsolicited messages offering compensation or recovery services. Such approaches can become a second opportunity for fraud.

The broader industry is paying attention to withdrawal approval controls and custody practices. NewsBTC's coverage of business Bitcoin accounts with withdrawal approvals and institutional interest in DeFi infrastructure highlight parts of that security conversation. The DOJ action is meaningful, but a final recovery figure will only be clear as investigations and legal proceedings progress.
