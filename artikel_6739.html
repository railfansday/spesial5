Legal Shrooms Are Coming

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Could psilocybin offer a breakthrough for treatment-resistant depression?

Shrooms are passed around at parties as a hangover-free alternative to alcohol. Zoë Kravitz, playing a fictionalized version of herself, accidentally overdosed on them in an episode of Apple TV+’s The Studio. But soon, they may be officially recognized as more than a way to let loose and could be used as a powerful weapon to combat serious depression, with the potential of being legalized in the U.S. by early 2027. Psilocybin is currently legal in some parts of Oregon and Colorado for treating mental health conditions in certain settings; at the federal level, it’s classified as a Schedule I controlled substance, considered to have no medical use and a high potential for abuse. But with biotechnology company Compass Pathways having achieved two consecutively positive Phase 3 trials for its COMP360 psilocybin therapy in treatment-resistant depression, that may change soon. Shrooms could make history as the first classic psychedelic drug (working on a particular serotonin receptor in the brain) approved by the FDA.

In an unaffiliated 2026 study in the journal Nature Medicine, scientists found that people with treatment-resistant depression who were given COMP360’s formulation of psilocybin had lower depression scores than those treated with a placebo. A Johns Hopkins Medicine study published in the Journal of Psychopharmacology in 2022 showed that the antidepressant effects of psilocybin therapy, alongside supportive psychotherapy, may last for a full year. It’s already legal for therapeutic treatment of some mental health conditions in countries like Australia, New Zealand, and Switzerland. Jay Godfrey, co-founder and CEO of Nushama, which is billed as the world’s largest psychedelic wellness center in New York City, uses a familiar psychological construct to speculate about how it might work. “There is a theory that psilocybin causes a quieting of an area of our brain called the default mode network, where your ego is housed,” he says. “Your own righteousness, search for validation, opinions, judgments—all those things are housed in ego. Psilocybin creates what we call ego dissolution: You get to observe yourself in the absence of your judgment, opinions, and all the walls that are around you that keep you safe. It allows you to see your traumas and your life story through a much more loving and compassionate [lens].”

Traditional depression medication, like selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), is typically taken every day to balance serotonin levels in the brain. Psilocybin would likely be different. “You’re taking one to four doses per year,” says Myriam Barthes, the co-founder and CEO of Journey Clinical, the largest psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy platform in the United States. “The experience itself will last about five to seven hours.” Godfrey says that a standard dose of magic mushrooms could likely be 25 milligrams, and both Godfrey and Barthes stress that, unlike legalized marijuana, psilocybin may be regulated as a pharmaceutical drug, with each experience taking place in a clinic under the watchful eye of a medical professional trained in psilocybin therapy. It would be very unlikely that you could have the option to get a prescription for shrooms and take them at home, according to Lief Fenno, MD, PhD, chair of the American Psychiatric Association Council on Addiction Psychiatry. “Patient safety is paramount, so these are highly controlled situations with trained professionals,” Fenno says. “That’s very different than you simply taking a bunch of mushrooms or dropping acid.”

According to the Journal of Clinical Psychiatry, around 2.8 million people have treatment-resistant depression, medically defined by the FDA as having tried two different antidepressants and seen limited improvement (though there’s no prevailing consensus on this definition). Christopher Pittenger, MD, PhD, the Elizabeth Mears and House Jameson professor of psychiatry at Yale School of Medicine, says that there are a few compelling theories for how psilocybin could help them. “One is that it increases brain plasticity,” he says. A healthy brain is more clay than glass, able to be shaped by experience. When someone is severely depressed, their brain is often less able to make new connections and change; psilocybin might be able to make it more pliable. A second theory is that shrooms can alter brain circuits, which control how your mind processes information. One of those circuits is the default mode network that Godfrey mentioned—and Pittenger says that when this network is impacted, you may be able to view yourself differently, which can positively impact your mental health.

Because psilocybin would likely only be available in a clinic under the supervision of medically trained professionals if it gets approved, Pittenger says, there’s little risk of it being abused. “People can use and overuse it, but I don’t think that the medical approval is going to increase the ability to use it recreationally,” he says. The main worry, he adds, is that psilocybin could cause psychosis in people who are already at risk for it, like those with a medical or family history of schizophrenia or bipolar disorder. Headaches, nausea, anxiety, dizziness, and elevated blood pressure have been associated with its administration in clinical trials. The drug may also slightly increase blood pressure, so people with high blood pressure should likely avoid it. A full medical and psychiatric evaluation that covers medical history and past psychedelic use should be done prior to this treatment.

One persistent problem with the trials for psilocybin therapy is that it’s uniquely hard to blind them, which means that patients usually know if they’re being given a psychedelic drug. “Patients in the trials know whether or not they’re getting a compound that creates an altered state of consciousness, or if they’re getting a neutral placebo,” Fenno says. “There are trials ongoing that are trying to understand how much of the very real therapeutic effect we see is due specifically to the psilocybin.”

A recent RAND survey found that public support for legalized psilocybin (currently 23 percent) nearly matches the support for cannabis in the mid-’90s, and Colorado and Oregon have already decriminalized it. But their experience raises caution. “We showed that with decriminalization, the number of adults using psilocybin about doubled in each state,” says Joshua C. Black, PhD, a statistical scientist who recently conducted an analysis of the two states for the American Medical Association. “The size and timing of that increase suggested that many people are using outside of the legal frameworks in both states, at least in the initial years.” The study notes that people in decriminalized states may have felt more comfortable self-reporting psilocybin use, since there are fewer legal repercussions. While this increase might sound alarming, Black says that prescription access will be much more tightly controlled than access under broad decriminalization. “FDA-approved drugs will have substantial controls over manufacturing and distribution, and I don’t think that would generate the same pattern.”

Whether or not psilocybin gets FDA approval, the fact that we’re talking about a different kind of depression treatment makes some experts optimistic. “One of the most exciting parts about all of this research is that it’s got the entire country talking about mental illness, and new treatments for it,” Fenno says. So while shroom chocolates surreptitiously handed out at a dinner party will likely remain illegal and you probably won’t be able to buy them at a dispensary, people with treatment-resistant depression may soon have a new option available under medical supervision.

Editor’s note: If you or someone you know is experiencing a mental health crisis or depression, dial 988 or (800) 662-HELP for emergency help or consult the resources available on the American Psychiatric Association’s website (psychiatry.org).
