German boat dealer suspected of supplying Channel smuggling gangs, BBC finds

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Watch: BBC confronts German boat dealer Marvin Reuter

A German boat dealer is suspected by European authorities of being a key supplier to migrant smugglers operating in the English Channel, the BBC can reveal.

Nearly a third of small-boat crossings in the first four months of this year allegedly involved boats or engines from MaReBoote, a small firm in western Germany, according to files we have seen.

The managing director, Marvin Reuter, is contesting extradition to France, where he could face smuggling, criminal conspiracy and involuntary manslaughter charges.

His equipment is suspected of being linked to two fatal accidents, in which four men died.

We confronted the 35-year-old at his business near Dortmund, but he would not answer our questions. He said he was reporting us to the police.

Reuter cannot be formally charged until extradited to France but could face jail time, if tried and convicted. He is presumed innocent until proven guilty.

Investigators, we have learned, believe they have uncovered clear, persistent links between Reuter and smuggling networks.

Evidence has been gathered through the confessions of alleged accomplices and covert jail-cell recordings of an alleged gang chief - as well as the two deadly crossing attempts.

A press photo from 2021 - migrants in a boat from Marvin Reuter's firm (logo circled)

At least 82 boats from Reuter's firm reached the English coast in just over two years to May, according to French investigations.

There are also press photos dating back five years showing the MaReBoote logo on dinghies crowded with migrants.

In 2021, Reuter told the Sun and Guardian he felt "frustrated" and "anxious" that his vessels had ended up in the hands of smugglers.

There was no suggestion, at that time, that authorities suspected him of wrongdoing as a German detective reportedly told the Sun: "This would be like if somebody buys a Mercedes and does something in another country which is wrong. The Mercedes seller can't help that."

It is not exactly clear when police suspicions were aroused.

French prosecutor Samuel Finielz would not comment on the details of the investigation but confirmed the head of a German company is currently the subject of a European Arrest Warrant.

The warrant was issued in May this year and relates to alleged offences dating back to December 2024.

While selling boats is "perfectly legal" it becomes illegal, said Finielz, "when one is fully aware that they will be used by networks involved in illegal immigration or human trafficking".

Case files we have seen say that German police had learned from UK authorities that 29% of detected crossings this year, up to the end of April, involved boats and/or engines linked to MaReBoote.

In France, five other men have already been charged as part of the investigation - an Iranian, three Iraqis and a Syrian.

In one of the two fatal incidents where Reuter's equipment is suspected to be involved, one of those who died was also Syrian.

Suliman al Husein would have just turned 20 when he died, according to a birth date listed in the files.

News reports at the time say he suffered a cardiac arrest after likely being crushed on a packed dinghy during a failed launch, west of Calais, in January 2025.

He was the first migrant to die in the English Channel that year. His body was found on the floor of the boat which, investigators allege, bore the MaReBoote logo.

One night in May, French police stopped a car and seized boating equipment and fuel cans

The case against Reuter also stems from a chance arrest when French police pulled over a vehicle with German plates, near the northern port city of Boulogne-sur-Mer.

The BMW, which was towing a trailer, was spotted overtaking another vehicle at speed in the early hours of a morning in May this year - police records say.

Inside the car were two German nationals who - according to a subsequent search - were transporting jerry cans of fuel, a boat engine, pumps and a rolled up inflatable dinghy.

Both men, who have since been convicted for smuggling offences, allegedly implicated Marvin Reuter as the man who had hired and paid them for this work.

Police files say both the BMW and the boat it was carrying had MaReBoote logos on them.

Marvin Reuter was arrested in Germany in May and later released, pending extradition.

On the day of his arrest, German police also seized more than 30 dinghies and 9,000 life jackets. Equipment which, we can now reveal, was allegedly linked to Reuter.

The BBC was previously granted access to see this haul, held in a police warehouse at a secret location in western Germany.

Part of the haul of dinghies and life jackets shown to us by German police

Meanwhile, further evidence has been gleaned from the bugging of a jail cell of a suspected gang chief, who is being held in pre-trial detention in France.

Recordings captured the inmate talking about buying nautical equipment from a German firm run by a man with Reuter's first name: Marvin - according to files we have seen.

The MaReBoote company address was also discovered on the alleged smuggler's phone, the case files say.

When we confronted Reuter last month at his business in the town of Werne, in the state of North Rhine-Westphalia, he told us to leave and drove off.

He then returned a short time later and said he was reporting us to the police. He did not answer our questions.

We approached him in writing before and after this encounter including a detailed request for comment. In response to that request, he did not address the specific allegations but accused us of harassment.

It is understood Reuter's lawyers have challenged his extradition, citing overcrowding in French prisons as one reason. Their client should face any allegations in Germany, they argue.

Reuter's firm is based in Werne, near Dortmund

In Germany, it became explicitly illegal to aid smuggling to the UK at the end of last year after a prior BBC investigation exposed the country's role as a storage hub for gangs. Before then, some anti-smuggling laws in Germany had no longer applied to the UK because of Brexit.

The raid that saw the seizure of equipment that was allegedly linked to Reuter marked the first time the new law had been used.

Small boat crossings have dropped significantly in 2026, with British authorities citing law enforcement as a "key factor". However, illegal migration into Europe has also been falling.

The UK National Crime Agency's (NCA) deputy director for organized immigration crime, Dan Cannatella-Barcroft, praised Germany's "commitment" to tackling people-smuggling but acknowledged the country would remain "an important piece of the puzzle".

While a gap in the law has been closed, Germany's proximity to France, wider transport links and the fact that some of the Kurdish-dominated gangs are based there, means it is expected to stay as a transit point in the illegal small-boats trade.

The "vast majority" of small boats equipment in the past few years has come through Germany, Cannatella-Barcroft told the BBC - much of it made in China and having been routed through Turkey.
