Paramount’s 4-Part Sci-Fi Beats ‘Star Trek’ at Its Own Game

Science fiction may not be for everybody, but it certainly provides us with the widest range of stories, from epic sagas of universal proportions to more intimate and philosophical tales. On television, it's certainly the most ambitious, with many series exploring. Sometimes it's easy to understand why it struggles with the uninitiated, though, as many such series often become so preoccupied with their own fictional universes that everything just feels overwhelming... Unless you've got some truly human drama at the core. That's what Battlestar Galactica is all about. While sci-fi household names like Star Trek still feel like they are more about the universe than the people who inhabit it, this 2000s Syfy classic feels like the opposite, with a complex universe that remains focused on the people who inhabit it.

The original Battlestar Galactica series was created back in 1978 by Glen A. Larson as a sort of "response" to Star Wars, but it became truly great only decades later, when sci-fi TV veteran Ronald D. Moore gave it his own twist in 2003. Still reeling in the aftermath of 9/11, the new series draws from that paranoia and tells a story in which humanity fights for survival against an enemy of its own making. With so much at stake, it's hard not to engage with what is happening on screen.

In this world, humanity has expanded throughout the stars and settled on multiple planets, forming the Twelve Colonies of Kobol. However, this age of peace and prosperity abruptly ends with a surprise attack from the Cylons, a race of androids created by humans. From tens of billions, the human population is reduced to about 50,000 and forced to flee into deep space aboard a small fleet, searching for a fabled thirteenth colony where they could supposedly rebuild in peace: Earth.

This premise is a great setup for exploring many trademark sci-fi themes, but ​​​​in Battlestar Galactica, the causes and scale of an apocalypse aren't as interesting as how people respond to it. In the end, humanity is made of people, and, with so few left, it's how they face all this, both individually and together, that matters more. At that stage, even seemingly mundane problems such as dwindling food supplies, political disagreements, and interpersonal conflicts can become matters of life and death.

One of the reasons why some people don't really like sci-fi is the sheer number of different universes there are, and how, ironically, they all feel very similar for those not in the know. It's difficult to place certain concepts in their respective franchises; for example, people often confuse Star Wars and Star Trek, which are diametrical opposites. Battlestar Galactica also suffers from this, of course, but what sets it apart is how, upon a closer look, everything feels like it was built to work around the story and its characters, not the opposite.

Worldbuilding in Battlestar Galactica is as complex as it is in any other sci-fi franchise, but while there is inevitably some degree of exposition, we usually learn about things through the characters' experiences. The political structure, for instance, is a central source of conflict between Admiral William Adama (Edward James Olmos) and President Laura Roslin (Mary McDonnell), the same way military hierarchy and technological limitations shape the decisions and relationships of the pilots in the Colonial Fleet.

The limited setting is also a big factor in why Battlestar Galactica feels different among sci-fi franchises. There is no galaxy far, far away for anyone to boldly go to; in fact, there is nowhere to go, lest the fleet gets found by the Cylons. Character interaction and reaction to the surrounding universe is what drives everything forward, and it's what makes the world of the series feel actually lived-in instead of something that simply existed before the series began. Franchises like Star Trek eventually fixed this, but only after Battlestar led the way.

For sure, Battlestar Galactica has plenty of sci-fi elements to justify some confusion with other franchises, especially for people who see the genre simply as "small ships shooting lasers in space." However, while most other corners of the genre are usually as preoccupied with lore itself (alien species, institutions, regulations, etc.) as they are with characters, it's Battlestar's very premise that doesn't allow for that. There is no room for the so-called "technobabble," otherwise everything will inevitably grow too far beyond the scope of the story. Worldbuilding is important, of course, but never as much as what the characters are going through.

In the end, Battlestar Galactica succeeds because it doesn't feel obligated to make every episode about science fiction. Perhaps this is one of the reasons why it's so hard to bring it back for a revival — it would have to sacrifice too much of its identity to justify it with such a lean premise. Also, its real-world inspiration makes the series a true product of its time, so, unlike other franchises, there is no room for a reboot for the sake of it, just like there is no room for over-the-top self-referential lore. If anyone gets too caught up in that, they would become easy prey for the Cylons, after all.

Battlestar Galactica is available to stream on Paramount+.
