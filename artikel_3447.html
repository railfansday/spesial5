8 Near-Perfect Sci-Fi Miniseries that Hook You in the First Episode

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

Designed to be binge-watched over the weekend

The best science fiction stories have a habit of going on forever. Franchises like Star Wars, Star Trek, and even Alien enter the world with such incredible momentum that we're still making sequels and spin-offs all these decades later. After all, part of what makes sci-fi so great is the opportunity to imagine alternate entire worlds, galaxies, and universes, and then explore them indefinitely. But sometimes, you just want to watch something that can be finished in a single weekend. That's where miniseries come in.

Sci-fi stories are a dime a dozen, but very few genre stories manage to make an unforgettable impact with the miniseries format. This makes sense. Shows like The Expanse or Westworld need more space to flesh out their multi-season storylines. That said, some sci-fi miniseries accomplish a lot with less. Whether you’re looking for an action-packed show or one that is more philosophical in tone, these eight entries make for a near-perfect viewing experience that can be wrapped up in just a few days.

Stories set in a post-apocalyptic dystopia usually require some time to establish the rules of their worlds, but Station Eleven rewards us with 10 enthralling episodes that hit hard from the very start. Set 20 years after a flu pandemic that led to the collapse of human civilization, Station Eleven follows a group of survivors who make a living by performing as a theatre troupe. Instead of embracing nihilism, Station Eleven focuses on grief as a shared language between survivors, who try to heal through artistic endeavors. There’s also plenty of violent conflict, but the resolutions are satisfying and earned.

After serving as showrunner for Lost and The Leftovers, Damon Lindelof helmed Watchmen, penned as a sequel to Alan Moore and Dave Gibbons’ popular 1986 comic book. The miniseries draws from the source material, continuing the story of iconic heroes and villains like Doctor Manhattan and Ozymandias, while also introducing new characters to reflect how their world has evolved. In the show, a white supremacist group inspired by the Watchmen character Rorschach clashes with the Tulsa Police Department, who are forced to conceal their identities to avoid being targeted. Angela Abar (Regina King), also known as Sister Knight, is a detective investigating the murder of her friend, but she stumbles upon long-hidden conspiracies related to vigilantism, which has been outlawed in this reality. Watchmen excels in puzzle-box plotting, which is grounded in real-world historical trauma and a nuanced understanding of superhero mythos and how it evolves.

Time travel shows like Dark have elevated the genre like never before, but Bodies reinvigorates it with just eight episodes. Based on Si Spencer and Dean Ormston’s graphic novel, Bodies opens with the appearance of a corpse in the Whitechapel area of London. The same body, however, shows up at various points in time: 1890, 1941, 2023, and 2053. The four detectives involved with the case approach it differently, as their investigation is guided by the prevailing mood of the times. This is intriguing to watch, as a detective working during World War II sports a different sensibility than someone living in the Victorian era. Bodies makes good use of its talented ensemble cast, and it's a perfect binge-watch over your next rainy weekend.

You might know Russell T Davies as the driving force behind the Doctor Who revival, but he also wrote Years and Years, a dystopian sci-fi drama that’s quite emotionally devastating. Set between 2019 and 2034, the series follows the Lyon family, who witness the rise of a controversial politician named Vivienne Rook (Emma Thompson). This is a time of great political instability in Britain. Technological progress goes hand in hand with economic upheaval, affecting the everyday lives of the Lyons. Years and Years is perfect for anyone who likes realistic sci-fi that holds up a mirror to a possible future that is more bleak than hopeful. The plausibility of the premise makes it super effective, with Rook emerging as a chilling antagonist that evokes troubling parallels to real-world politicians of the current times.

The original six-episode anime FLCL is nothing short of a masterpiece. FLCL follows Naota Nandaba, a 12-year-old whose mundane suburban life is shattered by the arrival of Haruko Haruhara, an alien woman who runs him over with a Vespa and then hits him with a bass guitar. Soon, robots and weapons begin emerging from inside Naota’s head after the injury, which Haruko uses as a portal while carrying out an intergalactic mission. This coming-of-age adventure, produced by Gainax and Production I.G, sports a standout visual identity, thanks to a seamless blend of traditional cel animation and 3D CGI. FLCL works with flawed characters, creating a raw emotional resonance that feels special in this unique standalone tale.

Although Scavengers Reign had the misfortune of being canceled by HBO Max after one season, this animated sci-fi show retroactively works well as a brilliant miniseries with a tantalizing cliffhanger ending. The story is about the survivors of a damaged cargo ship stranded on an alien planet, whose vivid vegetation hides unpredictable dangers. Although the survivors are separated into three groups, all of them witness an odd symbiosis between the flora and fauna of the region, along with a complexity that has never been seen before. The planet feels like a character in its own right, forcing every survivor to closely study its bizarre characteristics and life cycles. Scavengers Reign leans into a 1990s aesthetic, inducing hypnotic beauty and environmental dread at the same time.

Alex Garland, best known for sci-fi movies like Ex Machina and Annihilation, created Devs, a sci-fi thriller that defies straightforward analysis. A cutting-edge tech company named Amaya offers a coveted position on its mysterious quantum computing team to Sergei (Karl Glusman). When tragedy strikes, Sergei's girlfriend Lily (Sonoya Mizuno), who also works as a software engineer for the same company, becomes involved in a mystery that starts feeling more surreal and abstract by the second. Devs is Garland at his surreal, unnerving best, and the show’s eight episodes take their sweet time to arrive at a complicated conclusion about determinism and free will. Despite its slow-burn, the show keeps you hooked with its intricate structure and compelling arguments about human nature.

Any sci-fi list is incomplete without an alien invasion story, and V fits perfectly into these expectations. Well, almost. Kenneth Johnson’s (Bionic Woman, The Incredible Hulk) two-part miniseries opens with extraterrestrial beings known as the Visitors extending an olive branch as they land on Earth. They seek aid for their dying world, offering knowledge about their advanced technology in return. After the government accepts this offer, a covert infiltration begins, with Visitors rapidly gaining power in political positions. V subverts expectations by turning its invasion premise into a grounded political allegory about fascism and resistance. Instead of leaning into the tropes of the time, this 1983 show uses documentary-style realism and nuanced drama to tell a story worth remembering.
