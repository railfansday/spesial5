We Read Novels To Recognise Ourselves

Such a hard decision to choose the next novel for this blog! But in this current dark world we all need some gentler moments, and Ann Patchett’s latest novel WHISTLER will give you many of those along with much food for reflection.

Patchett is one of America’s best known novelists and since 2004 when I read her memoir TRUTH AND BEAUTY, the story of her 20 year friendship with Lucy Grealy, whose incredible book AUTOBIOGRAPHY OF A FACE I read around the same time, I have read all of Patchett's novels. Lucy died of a heroin overdose when she was 39 after almost a lifetime of struggle with the aftermath of having a large part of her jaw removed when she was nine. I have early editions of both these beautiful memoirs on my bookshelf and although I haven’t read them for over twenty years I still remember their emotional impact.

Ann Patchett has continued to write memorable books, mostly novels, and now in her early 60s gives every indication that we will be able to look forward to many more. She is also well-known for her support of writers and books and is the co-founder of the bookstore Parnassus Books in Nashville where she lives. Her stance on social media is refreshing; she watches no television, does not have a cell phone and doesn’t participate in any form of social media. However, on her website you can find her interviewing authors and talking about books although she never watches those video clips! What she does do of course is read, write novels, and support all things books. Perhaps a recipe more of us should follow, at least occasionally!

Here is the back-of-the-book description of WHISTLER, published in June 2026. On Goodreads it currently has over 290,000 ratings and over 334,00 reviews with an average score of 4.28/5. So many of you have likely already read it!

The acclaimed, prize-winning #1 New York Times bestselling writer returns with a moving, luminous novel that reminds us of the sweetness and impermanence of life and the power of connection to defy time.When Daphne Fuller and her husband Jonathan visit the Metropolitan Museum of Art, they notice an older, white-haired gentleman following them. The man turns out to be Eddie Triplett, her former stepfather, who had been married to her mother for a little more than year when Daphne was nine. Now fifty-three, Daphne hasn’t seen Eddie for many years, not since the fateful event that changed the direction of both their lives. Meeting again, time falls away; while their relationship was brief, it had a profound impact on them both, and now that they are reunited, they have no intention of ever being separated again.Whistler is a story about two adults looking back over the choices they made, and the choices that were made for them. It’s a story about bravery, memory, the often small yet consequential moments that define our lives, and the endless stream of loss that in time comes for us all. Beautiful in its simplicity, it is ultimately about how love endures, and how the feeling of being known by one other person, even for a short period of time, can change everything.

A novel with a horse on the cover and perhaps with a description like this may seem like a novel for women. And of course it is, but I think both women and men could find this an immersive and so very pleasurable read. A story about aging and memory, and how our brains filter and rewrite our personal histories as the years roll by, it is also about those childhood memories we may have forgotten because they were so very fleeting.

The story revolves around the relationship between Daphne and Eddie; a relationship that occupied only one year of Daphne’s life when she was a child, and ended abruptly when she and Eddie, at that time her stepfather, both escaped death following a car accident. So when Daphne, now aged 53, saw Eddie again and they renewed their relationship, they both come to understand how influential their short relationship was on their lives. And as a reader you might find yourself thinking about brief relationships you had as a child that changed the trajectory of your life in ways that were profound but that perhaps you have not thought about very much. Of course some children are the victims of childhood trauma, but in this case Daphne and Eddie’s relationship changed both their lives and Daphne’s life in particular in positive ways.

So many news items are about traumatic and exploitative relationships, but what about the positive ones? The teacher who believed in you? The older cousin who took the time to listen? The best friend who you shared so much with but who you’ve not seen or contacted in thirty years? What are those memories? How has your mind stored them, preserved them, incorporated them into your life, your belief systems? Likely if you could go back and view them as they happened you would discover that they have been distorted over time, but perhaps this doesn’t matter, at least when you are content with the memory. In Whistler it becomes clear that Daphne and Eddie’s memories of the accident they were in are not the same, but that Eddie’s brief presence in Daphne’s life made a profound difference to the evolution of her resilience and bravery.

Often our favorite stories are about close family relationships, especially mothers and daughters, or sibling relationships. Whistler is about both of those, but also about the less-explored psychological dynamics of step-parenting and non-traditional family structures, and most especially good ones. The step-parent so often has that ‘wicked step mother’ or ‘abusive step father’ vibe but what about all the wonderful step parents?

And then there is Whistler, the horse. You will need to read the book to discover that story!
