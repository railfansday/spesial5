What Is "Emotional Regulation" for Parents?

Before I had children, I thought it would be hard and fun. I expected to be tired. I expected to have less time for myself. But I never expected to be so challenged by the emotions I had, like guilt and anger. And I was not equipped with the knowledge, skills, and tools I needed to navigate my emotions as a parent.

The emotional lives of parents are complex and important, especially for mental health. That’s because parenting evokes some of our biggest and most complicated emotions.

Having “negative” emotions towards children doesn’t mean we’re bad. It just means we have emotions, as all humans do. And contrary to what we may think, core emotions like anger, sadness, and fear are not under conscious control.

Emotional regulation refers to a calm and balanced nervous system. When we are regulated, we can experience our emotions, deal with them effectively, and relate in connected and attuned ways. When we are regulated, we don’t feel threatened by our children’s behaviors or emotions. Our capacity for compassion and curiosity is enabled—the qualities we need as parents.

Nevertheless, as parents, we are regularly thrown off balance because our children evoke strong feelings.

When I was young, I thought the only way to feel calmer was to get rid of my emotions by pushing them away or distracting myself. This is how many people think because we aren’t taught about emotions and how to be with them in healthy ways.

The goal isn’t to become a parent who never gets angry, frustrated, afraid, or overwhelmed. That’s simply not possible because emotions are hardwired into the brain and body and cannot be eradicated. Pushing emotions down dysregulates the nervous system, stressing the mind and body, which worsens anxiety and depression.

A better goal is to learn about emotions, develop emotional awareness, and ultimately learn how to process emotions. For example, being able to recognize and name the anger, fear, shame, sadness, or disappointment that you’re feeling begins the process of regulation, helping us feel and function better.

That’s a very different approach than ignoring your emotions and simply telling yourself to “calm down.”

One very effective way to understand and work with difficult emotions is through the Change Triangle, an emotional roadmap I adapted from AEDP psychotherapy and the academic literature on emotions.

Initially, the Change Triangle helps by teaching us the ABCs of emotions and how they work in the mind and body. As a result of this newfound emotional education, we can become less fearful of our emotions and more curious about what is happening inside us.

When something upsets you, you can turn inward and find where you are on the Change Triangle. You will find yourself in one of four places on the Change Triangle.

Protective defenses and inhibitory emotions obscure deeper core emotions such as anger, sadness, fear, disgust, or joy. Naming and validating our core emotions opens the door to regulation. The ultimate objective whenever we get dysregulated is to work our way back down the Change Triangle, moving from defenses back to the openhearted state of the authentic self, which is a regulated state.

This kind of curiosity creates space between feeling and action. And that space can change how we parent.

Imagine your eight-year-old refuses to turn off the television. You feel your body tighten. Your heart beats faster. You hear yourself thinking, Why won’t this kid ever listen to me?

When you listen to the anger and get curious about why you’re angry and what you need, you might notice something underneath the anger: I’m exhausted. I feel powerless. I need cooperation.

The situation hasn’t magically changed. Your child still needs to turn off the television. But now you’re responding from greater awareness rather than simply discharging the emotion.

You can set the same boundary without making your child the target of your unprocessed feelings. That’s emotional regulation.

Instead of yelling, gently take the remote from your child, turn off the TV, and tell your child calmly, “TV time is over.”

We spend enormous amounts of time teaching children about their emotions. We tell them to use their words. We teach them to take deep breaths. We encourage them to name their feelings and calm down. But parents need these skills, too. In fact, one of the most powerful ways to teach children emotional intelligence is to develop our own emotional intelligence. When parents learn to understand and process their emotions, they become better able to help their children do the same.
