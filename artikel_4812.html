Why You Get Annoyed, and What to Do About It

If you are annoyed, it’s not just about the annoying thing.

A few week ago, I was stuck in an airport trying to get to Washington, DC. It was Friday, and it had become one of those travel days that starts out inconvenient and then slowly turns into a nightmare: Two cancelled flights, and a long line of tired people trying to rebook.

I did not make it that night. I ended up taking a red-eye. But somewhere in the middle of this travel chaos, I was thinking about what word we should use in today's post. I jokingly thought: annoyance—and that is the one we landed on.

Annoyance is small. It is everyday. It is leaf blowers and people chewing loudly and your partner leaving his black socks on the floor again and your kid asking you where their shoes are while standing next to their shoes.

Annoyance is the emotional wallpaper of being human.

The same person can annoy you on one day and not annoy you on another.

Your mother can say the exact same thing on Monday and you laugh, and then on Thursday she says it again and you want to leave the room.

Usually two things: Your capacity, and your story.

Capacity is your bandwidth to be with life as it is, and is not just a mindset; it is biological. It’s impacted by heat, hunger, sleep, stress, hormones, and illness.

We joke about being "hangry," but it is real; there is research on hunger and irritability. When you are hungry, you are more likely to feel anger and irritation—and less likely to feel pleasure. Your body is saying, please feed me, and then your mind starts saying, everyone is terrible.

Sleep is another big one. Poor sleep changes your social perception. Couples that sleep poorly tend to have more conflict, less positive emotion, and less ability to accurately read each other.

Annoyance says, Check your capacity: Have I eaten? Am I too hot? Am I tired? Am I overloaded?

Because sometimes annoyance can be remedied with some protein or a good night’s rest.

An incident happens. Something small, like someone is late, doesn't respond to your text, or—if you are like me on that dreaded red-eye flight—you get stuck in the middle seat.

That is the incident. What fuels your annoyance is when your mind adds story to it. Your mind will pull in history and all the ways you have been disappointed before, the moments you didn't feel considered, and fuel the irritants like adding kindling to a fire.

Your mind can take an incident and turn it into a trend, an identity, or even a false truth: They never think about me. I am always the one who has to adjust. No one respects my time. I am trapped.

And then we are dealing with a whole elaborate story that feels real but is not true.

When we are annoyed, we separate. We separate from reality, the present moment, or even the person right in front of us. We also separate from the fact that other people are also tired, hot, delayed, worried, trying, failing, needing a break, needing a snack, orneeding a little mercy.

Annoyance is a beautiful teacher because it arises in ordinary life. This week, do not try to get rid of annoyance. Instead, explore it.

1. Check capacity. Get out of your head and into your body: Am I hungry? Am I tired? Am I overstimulated? Do I need water, food, movement, quiet, or a break?

Sometimes the most psychologically flexible thing you can do is eat lunch.

2. Name the incident. Get very concrete: What specific thing triggered your annoyance?

Naming the incident brings you back to the present moment, with just the facts.

3. Notice the story. Ask yourself: What is my mind adding?

You do not have to reframe the story. Just see it as a story.

4. Ask, What is actually threatened? Often annoyance is organized around a perceived threat to control, comfort, being respected or being seen, or to resources and time.

When you can name the threat, you tend directly to it.

5. Open the aperture. Let the annoying thing be there, but widen your attention. Feel your feet, notice the room, notice another person’s face.

Notice that there is more here than this one irritation.

6. Choose connection where possible. Connection is anti-inflammatory. Instead of blaming and fixing and complaining when you are annoyed, try making contact with another person.

Annoyance is a window that shows us where our capacity is low and where our mind is turning an incident into a story.

So this week, when annoyance shows up, see if you can pause before the complaint, check your body, and tend to your capacity. Then drop the story. So, you are stuck in an airport. What is the wise next best move?
