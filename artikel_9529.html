How to stop emotional flooding from disrupting your relationships

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

The argument started with the dishes. Now you're saying something you know you’ll regret. Two minutes ago, you were mildly annoyed. Now your heart is pounding, and you can barely hear your partner over the noise in your own head.

This is emotional flooding, and it can happen to anyone. It isn’t a character flaw, and it doesn’t mean you're bad at relationships. When emotions run hot, the level-headed version of you can seem to vanish.

Flooding tends to show up when you're close to someone and the stakes feel personal. Fortunately, if you spot the signs early, you can keep an argument from turning into a blowup. Ahead, we’ll explore the science behind emotional flooding and how to calm yourself when it hits.

Emotional flooding is a state of overwhelm during a heated moment, usually a conflict. The term comes from John Gottman, PhD, who describes flooding as the point when you’re so overloaded that you can't take in new information, solve the problem, or hear the person in front of you.

Gottman and his colleague Robert Levenson studied couples at the University of Washington. As partners argued, they tracked physical signals like heart rate. Couples whose bodies showed higher arousal during conflict tended to grow less satisfied with their relationship over the following years.

Flooding feels different from person to person, so you may notice some of these signs but not others. Here's what to look for:

Racing heart: Your pulse climbs, and you feel your heartbeat in your chest or throat.

Fast, shallow breathing: Your breaths come quick and tight, and a full inhale feels out of reach.

Heat and tension: Your face flushes, and your jaw or shoulders tighten.

Tunnel vision: Your thinking narrows, and the other person’s words get hard to follow.

Urge to fight or flee: You want to raise your voice or leave, and it feels difficult to hold back.

Numbness or blankness: Your thoughts freeze, and you feel disconnected from the conversation.

Emotional flooding involves the amygdala, the part of your brain built to detect danger and keep you safe. When it senses a threat, it triggers the stress response and releases a surge of hormones that put your body on high alert. Adrenaline and norepinephrine speed up your heart rate and pull your focus toward the threat. That narrowed focus is part of why listening gets so hard.

In an everyday disagreement, there’s usually no physical danger, but your brain can still treat a raised voice or a difference of opinion as a threat, setting off the same alarm and the same rush of anger.

In close relationships, flooding may get extra fuel. Arguments can touch things that matter deeply to you, like feeling respected or safe. Old wounds and fear of where the fight might lead can pile on top of the present moment.

A note on relationships that don’t feel safe: This article is about everyday arguments, not abuse. If a raised voice or a pattern of control leaves you afraid of your partner, that fear isn’t a false alarm. Self-soothing isn’t the tool for this, and your safety comes first. You can't always stop flooding before it starts, but you can help your body calm down once you realize it’s happening. Then, when you feel settled enough to think clearly again, you can gently return to the conversation. The tips below follow that order: calm your body first, then come back and repair. Try the ones that fit the moment, and skip any that don’t feel right for you.

The moment you notice your heart racing or your thoughts scrambling, tell yourself “this is flooding.” Naming it reminds you that the feeling is physical and temporary. That bit of awareness gives you room to choose your next move instead of reacting on autopilot.

When you're flooded, stepping away can help you collect yourself before you talk things through. Tell your partner you need a break and that you’ll come back, so they don’t feel abandoned mid-conflict.

According to Gottman's research, it can take at least 20 minutes for your body to calm back down, so give yourself enough time. If you can’t leave the room, even a few minutes of silence can help.

Your body often responds to physical cues faster than logic. Use your time-out to splash cold water on your face or step outside for a few minutes of fresh air. Either one can help slow your heart rate.

Emotional flooding is feeling so overwhelmed during a conflict or another intense moment that you can’t think clearly or listen. The term comes from psychologist John Gottman's research on couples.

A common example of emotional flooding is a tense meeting with your boss, when your heart starts pounding and your mind goes blank. It can also happen at home, like when a conversation with a family member or partner escalates.

Emotional flooding is a surge of intense emotion in a specific moment, while emotional dysregulation is ongoing difficulty managing emotions across a range of situations. Flooding can be one sign of dysregulation, but if it happens now and then, that doesn’t necessarily mean you have a pattern of dysregulation.

Emotional flooding can be more common and more intense for people with ADHD (attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder). Studies show that trouble managing emotions is a core part of ADHD in adults. With ADHD, you may flood more often or take longer to calm down.

If flooding feels constant or unmanageable, a doctor can help you figure out what’s behind it.

You can stop being emotionally flooded by giving your body time to settle before you continue the conversation. Take a break of at least 20 minutes, and try long exhales, cold water on your face, or a brisk walk. When you feel more grounded, give the conversation another try.

Mindfulness can help with emotional flooding because it trains you to notice early signs, such as a racing heart or a tight jaw, before they build. Over time, that gives you a chance to pause instead of react in hard moments.

Try to practice mindfulness when you’re calm, with short breathing exercises or guided meditation, so the skill is there when you need it.
