The Woman Who Said Yes While Shaking Her Head No

Posted November 8, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

Early on in my career, I was a research manager working on consumer health products for a pharmaceutical company. Part of my job was to develop ideas for new products by testing them in large quantitative surveys to forecast sales. We showed respondents product descriptions I had painstakingly written and asked how likely they would be to buy it.

We tabulated the results to the decimal point, giving the data an aura of accuracy. But there was one problem. What people said in surveys didn’t reliably predict what they bought in market.

I came across a clue as to why sitting behind a two-way mirror. The focus group moderator was reading a product concept we tested in an earlier survey. She asked: “If this was available, would you buy it?” Without hesitating, a woman across the table clearly said yes, while her head was already shaking no.

The woman herself and the moderator didn’t notice. And we all tallied a yes in the backroom.

But her words just didn’t match her gesture. This doesn’t mean a headshake is the same thing as a lie detector. I wasn’t sure what she might do later. But I knew at that time that her answer was less settled than she made it sound.

From then on, I would note verbal and nonverbal responses, and I kept seeing the same split. A yes would arrive with a wince, eye roll, or furrowed brow. Sometimes they would say deadpan they are excited to buy. I would ask them to write down their reasons. The written words seemed well-reasoned and coherent. Over time, the unspoken reactions helped explain what my self-reported surveys were missing. It’s not like the words were useless. But they weren’t the clear window into behavior that the market research industry was built on.

I also had been assuming that honest people could explain their answers. But I started doubting that, and it changed my career path. The pharmaceutical company offered me a paid executive MBA at Columbia. I asked if I could get a degree in psychology. They said no, so I said no. I left to work at a qualitative research firm conducting focus groups and in-depth interviews. The answers weren’t in my spreadsheets, so I’d rather be in the room with people, so I could see the real story show up before the words.

Years later, I found a different kind of research and an aha moment that helped explain the “say versus do gap” that had undermined my earlier self-reported quantitative forecasts.

Neuroscientist Michael Gazzaniga conducted fascinating studies on patients whose corpus callosum, the fibers connecting the two hemispheres of the brain, had been cut to control severe epilepsy. The surgery sharply limited communication between hemispheres, which allowed him to show patients information to one side of the brain without the other side seeing it.

A patient was shown the word “walk” to their right hemisphere and he got up and began walking and the experimenter asked him why. The left hemisphere, which controls speech, hadn’t seen the instruction, so the honest answer should have been "I don’t know." But instead, he said something completely sincere: "I was thirsty and wanted a drink."

Gazzaniga called this left hemisphere function the interpreter: a process that takes something we just did and creates the most coherent explanation based upon whatever information is available.

The woman behind the glass wasn’t a split-brain patient. Her hemispheres communicated normally. We can’t compare the two research studies literally. But what she did share with split-brain patients was the gap: a fluent verbal response that didn’t match her actions. People with intact brains base their explanations on much more information and they often get it right. But Gazzaniga argues that the interpreter is not just a quirk from surgery but something all our brains do as we attempt to make our experiences feel unified and our actions seem like our own doing. The surgery simply made the interpreter more visible; by cutting off information we would normally use.

You can see this in people with normal brains. Researchers at Lund University in Sweden conducted an experiment that let people in a supermarket taste jam. Shoppers were asked which jam they preferred and then asked to taste it a second time. But the experimenters secretly swapped out their preferred jam with another. Most of the participants didn’t even notice the swap, and when asked to describe why they preferred it, they easily explained why, even though they had just rejected it. Despite their brain being fully intact, their reasons seemed like honest reports when they may have been made up.

Have you ever bought something on a whim? You can probably think of a reason why easily. But here’s a harder question: How do you know that is the real reason? Maybe the answer arrived later based on what you know about the product, the moment you bought it, and the person you believe yourself to be?

Or how about bigger decisions, like the job that felt right, the person you just met that you trusted, or that comment from your loved one that made you snap, only to explain it after as: I was just having a bad day. I’m not saying your explanations are fictitious. But maybe it was the simplest story you could put together on such short notice, while leaving out a deeper feeling of being dismissed by them, or some other worry you felt but hadn’t yet named.

Your reasons are not necessarily lies. But alone they’re incomplete. Sometimes there’s a story, and then there’s the real story. When you close a conversation with a quick confident answer you miss an opportunity to dig deeper for a question that deserves to stay open a little longer.

If you ever find yourself doing something surprising, remember your first explanation is just the start not the verdict. Think about what was happening right before and try to parse out if you were moving away or toward something. We tend to avoid bad feelings rather than try to get what we want. We choose a more expensive bottle to avoid looking cheap, agree with someone to avoid a fight, or put off doing something because we fear what might happen. Those reasons are usually not the first things to come to mind. Make sure to check if your story fits everything, not just the things you noticed.

I never learned what that woman finally did. She said yes, but her body said no. And while both responses came from her, neither told the whole story.

Your explanation may be sincere, but it also might be incomplete.
