Money Launderers Need Your Bank Account – Never Move Money for Someone Else

Money laundering makes criminal money look legitimate, and criminals recruit ordinary people as money mules to move it through their own bank accounts. Never receive money and forward it for someone else, and never break up a cash deposit to avoid the report your bank files on cash over $10,000.

Say you’re hired online as a “payment processing assistant,” $4,000 lands in your checking account, and you’re told to buy gift cards with it and send the codes. Then your bank freezes the account, and you learn the money came from scam victims.

You’ve just been used as a money mule in a laundering scheme. Laundering runs in three stages: placement gets criminal money into the financial system, layering moves it around until its source is hidden, and integration spends it on things that look legitimate.

In the 1MDB case, the U.S. Justice Department says more than $4.5 billion taken from Malaysia’s state investment fund moved through banks worldwide. Some of it then bought luxury homes and a superyacht, and some went into the movie company behind The Wolf of Wall Street. Scams use money mules for the layering stage. Each one puts another person between the victims and the criminals, which the FBI says makes the money harder to trace.

Criminals often look for mules among students, job seekers, and people on dating sites, the FBI warns. The request might be a job that runs payments through your account or has you reship packages, an online partner you’ve never met who asks you to forward money, or a stranger’s deposit to send on. Not knowing won’t protect you. A mule can be prosecuted even without realizing it was a crime, held liable for repaying victims, and left with damaged credit, the FBI says.

A federal law aimed at laundering also makes banks report your cash transactions over $10,000 in a day, whatever the reason for the cash. Splitting deposits to avoid that report is called structuring, and it’s a federal crime even with legal money. The Treasury’s Financial Crimes Enforcement Network uses the example of a man who splits the cash from selling his truck into two deposits to dodge the report.

If money you didn’t earn lands in your account with instructions to send it on, don’t move it. Call your bank and report it to the FBI at ic3.gov.
