The core resists and initiates movement while preventing energy leakage between the upper and lower body. Training all its functions is vital for gains, but start by resisting movement.

Enter  the Cable Wide Stance Anti-Rotation Chop.

Unlike the standard cable chops, where your hips and torso move together, this chop variation challenges you to keep your lower body still as your arms move diagonally across your body. Your upper back rotates, while your obliques, abs, and hips work overtime to maintain stability.

If you want a stronger, more stable core without another set of side planks, the Cable Wide Stance Anti-Rotation Chop deserves a spot in your workout.

The Cable Wide Stance Anti-Rotation Chop is a standing core exercise that challenges you to resist unwanted rotation as you move your arms diagonally across your body. With your feet planted in a wide stance, you pull a cable across your body while keeping your pelvis and lower torso facing forward.

Sounds doable—until the cable starts pulling you toward the weight stack.

Unlike the standard cable chop, where your hips and torso rotate together, this variation requires you to keep your lower body quiet while rotating through your upper back. Your abs must resist the cable’s rotational pull while your shoulders and arms do the work.

The result is an exercise that challenges your ability to separate stability from movement.

The goal is to control what moves and what stays stable. It’s not just about moving the weight from point A to point B. It’s about maintaining control as the cable pulls you off center.

Like many core exercises, this one trains more muscle than meets the eye.

Internal and External Obliques: These are the main movers of this exercise because they resist unwanted core rotation and keep your lower torso facing forward.

Transverse Abdominis: Supports trunk stability and helps maintain abdominal tension throughout the movement.

Rectus Abdominis: Your six-pack muscle isn’t just for crunches; it helps keep your torso facing forward and prevents excessive lower-back arching.

Glutes: Stabilize your lower body while your upper body moves.

Hip Stabilizers: Your gluteus medius, the hip rotators, and the adductors help maintain pelvic position, control weight shifts, and keep your knees aligned over your feet.

Lats, Shoulders, and Upper Back: Maintain an upright position and help move the weight across your body.

The Cable Wide Stance Anti-Rotation Chop looks simple, but wait until you try it. Here are four common mistakes and how to fix them.

When your hips follow your arms across your body, you’re doing something closer to a standard cable chop than challenging your core to resist rotation.

The fix: Keep your feet planted and your belt buckle facing forward throughout the movement. Allow rotation in your upper back while keeping your hips still. If you cannot maintain this separation, reduce the resistance until you can.

Shifting your body away from it changes your leverage, gives you unnecessary assistance, and reduces the intended benefits.

The fix: Keep your rib cage stacked over your pelvis and distribute your weight evenly between both feet. If necessary, lighten the load and try again.

Your lower back isn’t built to move like your upper back. When you rotate through your lower back instead of your upper back, you create unnecessary stress, and the exercise benefits disappear.

The fix: Brace your abs before moving, and keep your ribs over your hips throughout the movement. If you cannot avoid twisting your lower back, shorten the range of motion.

If you don’t control the return, your torso and hips may rotate, and you’ll miss the chance to challenge your core as it resists the cable’s pull.

The fix: Slowly return along the same diagonal path while maintaining tension. If there’s a problem, reduce the load.

Most core exercises either create movement or resist it, but the Cable Wide Stance Anti-Rotation Chop does both.

Here are four more reasons to add it to your workout.

As you move the weight back and forth across your body, the resistance tries to rotate your torso toward the weight stack. Your obliques and other core muscles must resist that pull to keep your lower torso and hips facing forward, strengthening your core’s ability to resist unwanted rotation.

Separating upper-body form from lower-body movement is this exercise’s biggest challenge. Your thoracic spine rotates, while your hips stay facing forward. This requires coordination between the muscles that create movement and the ones that resist it.

As the cable moves across your body, your core must adjust to maintain position rather than brace against gravity in one position, like a side plank. This makes it a dynamic stability exercise because you train your core to stay in control while the rest of your body moves.

Your abdominal muscles work with your glutes and hip stabilizers to help prevent unwanted rotation and excessive side-to-side movement. If your hips turn every time you move the handle, you’ve lost that connection.

The Cable Wide Stance Anti-Rotation Chop works best as an accessory exercise. Because it is an anti-rotation exercise, quality repetitions always beat load.

Where It Fits: As part of your extended warm-up, after your strength exercises, or perform it in a core circuit like this:

1A. Cable Wide-Stance Anti-Rotation Chop: 8 reps per side

Sets, Reps, and Rest: 2-4 sets of 8 to 12 reps per side, depending on experience and strength level. Rest a minute between sets.

Progressions: Progressive overload matters, but adding weight isn’t the only way. Perform every inch of this exercise with control, and experiment with a narrower stance to increase the balance challenge.

Preventing movement is a big part of what the core does, but sometimes its most important job is allowing movement where you need it while preventing it where you don’t.

That’s what makes the Cable Wide Stance Anti-Rotation Chop a worthwhile addition to your workouts. It separates upper- and lower-body movement, improving movement in and out of the gym.
