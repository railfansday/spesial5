Tested: 2027 Mercedes-AMG GT 55 4-Door Proves EVs Can Be Engaging

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

This is an electric car that makes V-8 noises, goes like stink, and is loaded to the gills with Mercedes luxury.

Making a performance EV compelling enough to convince enthusiasts to jump on board has proven difficult, but that’s precisely what Mercedes-AMG is trying to do with the 2027 4-Door Coupe. Mercedes is going down the same path as other manufacturers and their acclaimed electric performance machines—such as the Hyundai Ioniq 5 N and Genesis GV60 Magma—by simulating the experience of a car equipped with an internal-combustion engine. It’s great fun, but the efforts would be for naught if the rest of the car didn’t live up to the experience. Thankfully, this electric AMG is a ripper from every angle. Except when viewed from the rear. Maybe avoid doing that.

It’s hardly lesser, but the “55” in the AMG GT 55 4-Door Coupe is empirically less mighty than the “63” in the GT 63. This EV features a tri-motor powertrain made up of three axial-flux motors engineered with YASA that combine for 805 hp and 1372 pound-feet of torque. That 805 hp figure is the peak, the lesser “continuous” rating coming in at 557 hp. But if you want all the power, all the time, that can be accomplished by putting the car in Race mode, or pulling both steering-wheel paddles to activate Boost mode, which lasts for 55 seconds. Mercedes claims a 2.7-second 0–60-mph time and top speed of 186 mph should you option the AMG Performance package.

Powertrain layout: 2 rear electric motors, 1 front

Slipping behind the wheel, the first thing that struck me about the GT 55 was the properly low seating position. Excellent work there, Mercedes. After fumbling about with the confusing start/stop button on the stalk (the car doesn’t really turn on until you flick it into Drive or Reverse), I started comfortably trundling along.

The AMG Active Ride Control air suspension did a standup job on the track in the GT 63, but it’s even more remarkable on the road in the 55. An air suspension with adaptive dampers is standard, but this optional hydraulically interconnected damper system is assuredly the one you want. Combined with the multilink suspension in the front and rear, an abundance of forged aluminum components on the front axle, and rear-axle steering up to six degrees, this chassis just oozes confidence right off the bat. There was no jittery stiffness or harshness to the ride in Comfort, and it was only moderately sharper on bumps in the punchier modes. You could spend every day behind the wheel thinking you’re in a regular Mercedes and not an AMG product.

But when I found some sweepers, the front end just begged for me to keep piling on speed thanks to how stable everything feels. At points, it was almost too confidence-inspiring, as the sophisticated chassis was writing checks the GT’s 5501-pound mass and Continental SportContact 7 summer tires struggled to cash on some of southern Spain’s steep downhill slaloms. The steering and body control say go, go, but the tires quickly say they need a break from the heat. Don’t mistake that as a braking system limitation either, because the optional carbon-ceramics hold up to mega abuse. I just wish the pedal feel wasn’t so mushy and inconsistent throughout the stroke.

Accelerating out of corners, the sensation was AMG at its best. With the ESP system in Sport mode, the rear tires surrendered grip progressively and tidily. It was so consistent and predictable that it almost felt calculated, and to a degree, it is. Mercedes let me play with the AMG Race Engineer system in the 63 model on the racetrack—that’s the three dials in the center console which let the driver customize how hairy or locked down they want the experience from a traction control and yaw agility perspective. The 55 has the exact same system, given you option it. It’s fun to toy with on the track but can just as easily change the character of the car on a fast canyon road to suit your mood.

And then there’s AMGForce Sport+, the system that simulates both a V-8 engine and a multi-clutch transmission system and that proved remarkably adept at convincing me I was piloting an internal-combustion-powered car. Mercedes mimics the soundtrack and other theatrics—engine braking, seat vibrations, and shifting—from the previous-gen AMG GT R Coupe. I found myself tapping up and down the paddles through the mountains with glee for miles on end. Yeah, it’s all fake, but the alternative is a pure electric car experience that I find gets a bit boring after a short period.

Would a proper V-8 and real transmission have been more involving and addicting to wind out? Absolutely. But even if you set aside the stagecraft, this AMG is a blast to drive. It toes the line between back-road barn burner and everyday luxury like the very best AMGs of recent history. Plus, the EV hardware backing everything up is elite.

A 106-kWh battery pack developed specifically for AMG performance cars can be charged at speeds over 600 kW, allowing you to go from 10 to 80 percent in only 11 minutes. No, 600-kW chargers don’t exist in the U.S. yet, but when they arrive, this car will be ready. The system can even switch from 800 to 400 volts by virtually “splitting” the pack in half for less compromised charging at 400-volt stations, which should help tremendously at Tesla Superchargers. Range figures aren’t out yet, but Mercedes is claiming up to 434 miles on the optimistic European WLTP test, so you can rest assured that EPA numbers will be well over 300 miles.

Mercedes has clearly done some learning from all the criticism leveled at its interiors over the past half-decade. The GT’s steering wheel has a proper scroll wheel for volume and a switch for cruise control. The door-positioned seat toggles physically move when you press them (finally), and those AMG Race Engineer controls in the center console are aptly the most satisfying knobs to turn in the whole car. The quilted leather on the doors themselves is spectacular, but don’t worry, there’s plenty to wrinkle a brow at too.

Adjusting the heated and ventilated seat controls requires multiple extremely precise stabs at the touchscreen. Trying to find your way around the unending drive mode and sound mode controls will require patience for even the most tech-savvy. And while the massive screens are of superb quality in terms of both picture and response, the sheer amount of real estate dedicated to them could easily be a turn-off. On the plus side, the dedicated tachometer that pops up in Sport+ mode is probably the best digital tach fitted to a Mercedes yet. Ironic that it arrives in an EV.

Practicality doesn’t take a back seat with this long, swoopy liftback. Despite its name, this is a four-door hatchback, and unlike the Porsche Taycan, it has enough space in the rear to seat medium-to-tall adults. It doesn’t completely suck to get in or out either. In another win for the AMG over Porsche’s sport sedan, the 14.7-cubic-foot cargo area seems positively massive. You can extend it further by putting the rear seats down, leaving an impressively large space to go road-tripping or move large items with. A carbon-fiber frunk exists for smaller items, too, and it’s here you’ll find the traditional signed AMG plaque, only this one is signed by any number of folks involved in the design or engineering of the vehicle.

If you found the Taycan a little too serious or not practical enough, the AMG GT 55 4-Door Coupe might be just the EV you’re looking for. It lands in between the Taycan 4S and GTS, but is quicker than both of those Taycan variants in a straight line. The Porsche is the more serious sports car, though practicality is firmly on Mercedes’s side. The debate between the 63 and 55 is where things get a little tricky with about a $30,000 gulf between the two—the 55 starts at $146,500, the 63 at $176,550.

Then there’s the forthcoming fully electric BMW M3 to consider, but we need to wait for pricing on that. But putting that and the GT together already feels as if it will be one of the benchmark tests of 2027.

I didn’t drive the GT 63 on the road, but it didn’t feel significantly quicker than the GT 55 in a straight line. Both have the same overengineered battery pack and tri-motor powertrain. The differences are largely in the suspension and software, like the AMG Race Engineer (again, optional on the 55) and pure luxury features like massage seats and an enormous HUD. That said, when you’re spending $145K and some change for options, what’s another $30,000 to get it all? If extra-dynamic driving isn’t in your future, perhaps a base 55 without all the go-fast goodies makes fiscal sense. But otherwise, it’s hard to imagine anyone who has sold themselves on the idea of the GT 4-Door not wanting the full 63 experience.

The active rear diffuser takes moving aero to a new level of complex and cool. Mercedes says it provides a boost to efficiency on the highway, but it also helps stability at high speeds. Watching it extend and retract on the move is quite the party trick. And thankfully, you can manually extend it to show off to your friends who are stuck with passive rear diffusers on their cars.
