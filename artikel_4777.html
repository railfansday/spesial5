Psychological Richness: The Third Dimension of a Good Life

Posted November 19, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

Imagine two people sitting at a quiet neighbourhood wine bar on a Tuesday night.

One has spent the last five years carefully pruning their life into a low-friction masterpiece. A dependable job with Sunday meal prep, and a tight circle of friends with minimal drama.

The other has spent those same five years changing industries, moving apartments twice, taking a solo trip where their luggage got lost in a country whose language they didn't speak, and surviving a breakup that rearranged their entire worldview.

If you asked someone coming from a traditional psychology background who is winning at life, they would likely give you two ways to score it. For over 40 years, researchers have treated a “good life” as a debate between two competing ideals.

On the one side is happiness (i.e., hedonic well-being): a life defined by comfort, stability, positive emotions, and a relative absence of stress. On the other is meaning (i.e., eudaimonic well-being): a life built on purpose, routine, coherence, and contributing to something larger than yourself.

For many, those two ingredients describe a lot. Plenty of people genuinely thrive on straightforward, comfortable lives anchored in meaningful contributions.

But think about the difference between someone who flourishes in the steady, familiar rhythm of a small town versus someone who only feels fully awake amid the unpredictable friction of a big city. If your primary goal is comfort and coherence, keeping your life simple works.

I have spent years trying to figure out why that two-door framework never fully made sense to me. Having things straightforward has never reliably led me to a long-term sense of “good.” Whenever my life becomes too predictable, too frictionless, or too cleanly resolved—when every week looks like a neatly organized calendar block—I don’t feel serene. I feel strangely flat.

It turns out there's a reason that type of restlessness doesn't fit neatly into the classic binary. Psychologist Shigehiro Oishi and colleagues have spent the past several years making the case that we have missed talking about a third dimension of a good life: psychological richness.

Oishi defines a psychologically rich life as one filled with diverse, interesting experiences that fundamentally shift one's perspective. Unlike happiness and meaning—which are predicted by personality traits like conscientiousness, agreeableness, and emotional stability, as well as a preference for familiarity and tradition—psychological richness is driven by openness to experience and curiosity. It’s why a bizarre detour on a whim or getting immersed in a dense literary novel can make some people feel deeply alive even if it has zero connection to a five-year plan.

In their paper, Oishi and Westgate refer to happiness, meaning, and psychological richness as three “paths” to a good life. Personally, I think calling them paths makes it sound like you have to stand at a fork in the road, pick one direction, and leave the others behind. To me, a more coherent way to understand how these three elements actually work together is through music.

If your life is an album unfolding over time, happiness is harmony. It’s the consonant, pleasant chords that feel warm and uplifting in the moment.

Meaning is the rhythm section. It’s the steady bass line and recurring beat that give pieces structure, direction, and momentum. If you only optimize for harmony and rhythm, you can write clean, catchy pop songs in 4/4 time and play them happily for decades.

But there’s also that third consideration of psychological richness: the structural complexity of the composition. It’s the odd time signature, the unexpected key change, the dissonant bridge that introduces tension and forces you to lean forward because the arrangement refuses to stay predictable.

Once you hear life as an album rather than a linear path, something counterintuitive clicks into place: miserable, frustrating, or unwanted experiences can still make a life deeply worthwhile.

In music, dissonance doesn’t have to be a mistake; it can be what gives a resolution its emotional weight. Research suggests that difficult or disruptive events—heavy workloads, culture shock, failed mountain-climbing attempts, or even recovering from a serious illness—routinely increase psychological richness even while actively tanking day-to-day happiness. When researchers analyzed New York Times obituaries, lives rated high in psychological richness were actually negatively correlated with perceived happiness. You don't have to sit with the ringing of a dissonant chord for it to make the rest of the track unforgettable.

An appetite for complexity doesn't just leave someone with interesting stories. The research suggests that people leading psychologically rich lives score significantly higher in cognitive complexity, specifically attributional complexity and holistic thinking, even after statistically controlling for baseline openness to experience.

Attributional complexity is the ability to hold multiple, competing explanations in your head at once, to see the invisible situational pressures, systemic context, and messy human contradictions driving someone’s behaviour. Holistic thinking is hearing how the entire arrangement fits together rather than isolating a single note.

That's why low-complexity thinkers aren't drawn to psychological richness. When someone cuts them off in traffic, or ghosts them after three great dates, or makes a baffling career pivot, a low-complexity thinker reaches for a tidy, single-cause label.

Straightforward lives rarely ask your brain to stretch. When everything resolves cleanly on the downbeat, your brain gets to run on autopilot, relying on familiar scripts and neat categories. You stay comfortable, you stay coherent, but your worldview stops expanding.

I used to think my dissatisfaction with simple, linear progress meant I was bad at being content—or worse, that I was needlessly complicating a perfectly good groove. Framing it through psychological richness changes how I am able to reckon with these feelings.

Some people are built for a clean, consonant melody. There’s real beauty in that. But if you, like me, are wired for cognitive complexity, a life without friction, surprise, and perspective shifts won't feel like peace. It will feel under-composed.

When harmony, rhythm, and complexity all show up in the same piece, it can feel like magic. But if your life feels less like a tidy, predictable tune and more like an intricate arrangement you’re still learning how to play, it doesn’t have to mean you’re failing at living the good life.

It just means that you’re writing a song that is deeply your own.
