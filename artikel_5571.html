Exposed to measles? 7 steps to take, from the 72-hour vaccine window to isolation

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Learn what to do if you were exposed to measles, from checking your MMR record and the 72-hour vaccine window to symptoms, testing and isolation

Measles exposure starts a clock. Some of the most useful protective steps are available for only a few days after contact. Decisions made in that short span can shape how the next three weeks unfold. Knowing what to do if you were exposed to measles means knowing which deadlines matter and who to call first.

Measles is highly contagious. Up to 90% of people without immunity who are close to an infected person become infected, according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). A single exposure at a clinic, school, airport or workplace therefore carries real risk for anyone who is not protected.

The first question is whether you are already immune. Vaccination and past infection are the usual routes to immunity. Confirming that status can settle the matter quickly. People who cannot document immunity face a different set of choices. Some of those choices depend on age, pregnancy or the state of the immune system.

This guide walks through seven steps in the order most people will need them. It starts with checking vaccination records and other evidence of immunity. It then covers the monitoring period after exposure and the early signs of illness. Those first signs can resemble a cold before any rash appears. The guide also explains why a phone call should come before any visit to a doctor's office or emergency room.

Two time-limited options get their own slides. A dose of the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine may offer some protection when given within 72 hours of first exposure. Immune globulin is a separate option that certain high-risk people can receive up to six days after exposure.

The final slide covers isolation. It explains when a sick person is contagious and when exposed people may be asked to stay home from school or work.

None of this replaces individual medical advice. A doctor can assess personal risk. Local and state health departments handle testing and contact tracing. They also set exclusion rules in their area. Anyone who learns of a possible exposure should contact one of them promptly, before the post-exposure windows close.

The first step after a measles exposure is to find out whether you already have presumptive evidence of immunity. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) considers people with that evidence presumed immune. For them, many of the usual post-exposure steps may not be needed.

The CDC accepts four kinds of evidence. The first is written documentation of measles vaccination. The second is a laboratory test showing immunity. The third is laboratory confirmation that you had measles in the past. The fourth is birth before 1957.

Written documentation means an actual record, not a memory of getting shots as a child. Common places to look include a childhood vaccination card, files kept by a pediatrician or family doctor and school or college health records. Many U.S. states also run immunization registries, and a doctor or health department can often search them.

How many documented doses count as enough depends on who you are. For preschool-age children and for adults who are not at high risk, one dose of the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine is considered adequate. School-age children, college students, health care workers and international travelers need two documented doses.

The gap between one and two doses shows up in protection. The CDC puts the effectiveness of two MMR doses against measles at about 97%. A single dose is about 93% effective. Neither figure reaches 100%. A small share of vaccinated people can still catch measles after an exposure.

The routine U.S. schedule gives the first MMR dose at 12 to 15 months of age and the second at four to six years. A child who followed that schedule and has the paperwork to show it meets the two-dose standard. A baby younger than 12 months has not yet reached the age for a routine first dose.

When records are missing or incomplete, a blood test can answer the question. A doctor can order a test for measles antibodies, and a result showing immunity counts as laboratory evidence. A past case of measles also counts, but only if a laboratory confirmed it. A remembered childhood illness without lab confirmation does not meet that standard.

The 1957 cutoff reflects how widely measles circulated before vaccination. People born before that year are presumed to have been infected as children.

Anyone unsure of their status should share whatever records they have with a doctor or the local health department. Either can judge whether the evidence is sufficient or whether further steps are needed.

Anyone without measles immunity who has been exposed should watch for symptoms through day 21 after their last contact with the virus. That 21-day period reflects the measles incubation period, the stretch between catching the virus and feeling sick.

For most people, symptoms begin seven to 14 days after exposure. The full range runs from seven to 21 days. A person who still feels fine at the end of the second week has not yet cleared the window. Watching through day 21 covers the late end of that range, so slower-developing cases are not missed.

The count starts from the last exposure, not the first. Someone who shared a home, classroom or office with an infected person over several days should date the watch period from the final day of contact. Day 21 then falls three weeks after that last contact. Writing that date down makes it easier to tell a doctor or health department exactly when the window closes.

In a household, each day spent near a sick person counts as a new exposure. The window keeps moving later until that contact ends.

Exposure does not require face-to-face contact. Measles spreads through the air when an infected person breathes, coughs or sneezes. The virus can remain in the air and on surfaces for up to two hours after that person has left a room. Someone who walked into a waiting room, store or airport gate after an infected person had gone may still have been exposed.

That airborne persistence is part of why measles spreads so efficiently among people who are not protected. For someone with no evidence of immunity, a confirmed exposure carries a high chance of infection.

People already protected by vaccination or past infection face a much lower risk. Anyone unsure of their status during the watch period should ask a doctor or the local health department how to proceed. That includes whether to limit contact with others before day 21 passes.

Measles typically begins with a high fever, a cough, a runny nose and red, watery eyes. The rash most people picture comes later, usually three to five days after those first symptoms appear.

That gap matters for anyone tracking their health after an exposure. A person waiting for spots on the skin may already be several days into the illness before the rash shows up.

The fever is often the most pronounced early sign. It can climb above 104°F. A fever that high, along with a cough and irritated, watery eyes, fits the typical early pattern of measles. For someone who shared space with a measles patient, that combination is a reason to watch closely for the next signs.

Koplik spots are a second clue, and they are easy to miss without looking for them. They are tiny white spots that form on the inside of the mouth. They can appear two to three days after the first symptoms begin. Because the skin rash usually comes later, Koplik spots can offer an earlier visible sign that the illness may be measles.

The rash follows a recognizable path. It starts on the face. From there, it spreads downward over the rest of the body.

Measles can also lead to serious complications. They include ear infections, diarrhea and pneumonia. In rare cases, the virus causes encephalitis, an inflammation of the brain.

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reports that about one in five unvaccinated people in the U.S. who get measles is hospitalized. The agency also reports that one to three of every 1,000 children who get measles die.

Knowing the order of symptoms helps an exposed person describe what is happening when they seek care. The sequence runs from fever, cough, runny nose and watery eyes, to white spots inside the mouth, to a rash that begins on the face. Anyone who notices these signs after a measles exposure should contact a doctor or the local health department for guidance on next steps.

Anyone who may have measles should phone the clinic, hospital or local health department before showing up for care. A call ahead gives staff time to prepare. That preparation helps keep the virus from reaching other patients in a waiting area.

Measles spreads through the air when an infected person breathes, coughs or sneezes. One unannounced visit to a crowded lobby can expose many other people. Clinics and emergency rooms often hold people who are already vulnerable, including babies, older adults and patients with weakened immune systems.

Calling first allows the facility to follow a U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) infection-control practice for suspected measles. Staff can place a mask on the patient as soon as they arrive. They can then move the patient into a separate room instead of the general waiting area.

The preferred space is an airborne infection isolation room. This type of room is built to keep air from flowing out into the rest of the building. Some facilities may also tell callers which entrance to use.

The phone call is also the time to share key details. A caller can describe where and when the possible exposure happened and which symptoms have appeared. It also helps to say when those symptoms started and whether there is a record of MMR vaccination. That information helps clinicians decide how quickly the person needs to be seen and where.

Local or state health departments direct measles testing and contact tracing. Measles is a reportable disease in the U.S. Clinicians who suspect a case are expected to notify public health officials. The health department can then identify other people who shared the same space and may need follow-up.

Testing usually involves two samples. One is a throat or nasal swab checked with PCR, a lab method that detects the genetic material of the measles virus. The other is a blood sample tested for antibodies to measles. Those antibodies show how the immune system is responding to the virus.

Results guide what happens next for both the patient and the people around them. A confirmed case can lead the health department to contact others who were at the same clinic, school, workplace or event. A negative result can help clinicians look for another cause of the symptoms.

Anyone unsure whom to call can start with a regular doctor's office or the local health department. Either one can explain where to go and how to arrive without putting others at risk.

People without measles immunity who get an MMR vaccine within 72 hours of their first exposure may gain some protection or have a milder illness. That is the guidance from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

MMR is the combined vaccine against measles, mumps and rubella. It is the same shot children receive on the routine schedule. Given after contact with the virus, it serves as a form of post-exposure prophylaxis.

The CDC describes two possible benefits. A dose given inside the window can provide some protection against infection. For people who still get sick after the shot, it can make the illness milder. Neither outcome is described as a guarantee.

The 72-hour limit is counted from the first exposure, not the most recent one. Someone who shared a household or classroom with an infected person over several days should count from the earliest contact.

Late notice also eats into the window. A person who learns of an exposure two days after it happened has about 24 hours left to get the dose.

The vaccine does no harm if the person was already infected at the time of the shot, according to the CDC. The dose can still be given when it is unclear whether the virus has already taken hold.

Infants can be part of this response. Babies aged six to 11 months can receive MMR as post-exposure prophylaxis, within the same 72-hour window that applies to older children and adults.

That early dose comes with a caveat for parents. A dose given before a child's first birthday does not count toward the routine two-dose MMR series. The child will still need both standard doses later, on the regular schedule.

A doctor or the local health department can arrange the dose. Calling as soon as an exposure becomes known leaves the most time inside the window. Health departments can also confirm whether an exposed person qualifies.

Some people face a high risk of severe measles but cannot safely receive the MMR vaccine or are too young for it. They may qualify for immune globulin, which can be given up to six days after exposure.

Immune globulin is a blood product made from the pooled plasma of many donors. It contains ready-made antibodies, including antibodies against measles. Those antibodies give the recipient temporary, passive protection. The body does not have to build its own immune response first. That lets the treatment help even after contact with the virus has already happened.

The CDC lists three groups to consider for immune globulin after an exposure. The first is infants younger than 12 months. The second is pregnant women without evidence of measles immunity. The third is people with severe immune suppression, such as some patients being treated for leukemia or recovering from a stem cell transplant.

None of the three groups can rely on the MMR vaccine in the usual way. Infants younger than 12 months have not yet reached the routine age for a first dose. MMR is a live-virus vaccine made with weakened forms of the measles, mumps and rubella viruses. It is not given during pregnancy. It is also withheld from people with severe immune suppression. In a body that cannot control it, even a weakened vaccine virus can cause serious illness.

Measles during pregnancy also carries its own risks, including miscarriage, preterm birth and low birth weight.

The type of immune globulin depends on the patient. Infants receive an intramuscular injection. Pregnant women and severely immunocompromised people receive the intravenous form, delivered through an IV line.

Immune globulin does not replace vaccination. Its protection fades as the donated antibodies break down, and it does not create lasting immunity. Those same antibodies can also blunt the response to a live vaccine given too soon afterward. A doctor decides when someone who received immune globulin can later get MMR, once they are eligible.

Anyone in these groups who learns of an exposure should contact a doctor or the local health department right away. Health officials can confirm eligibility, arrange the correct form and keep track of the days left in the six-day window.

A person with measles can spread the virus from four days before the rash appears until four days after it. Anyone who develops measles should stay home through the fourth day after the rash first shows up.

That contagious window opens before the illness is recognizable as measles. During those first days, an infected person can pass the virus to others before anyone suspects the cause. Health departments use the day the rash first appears as the reference point for timing isolation. Writing down that date makes it easier to work out when isolation ends.

For a person whose rash appears on a Monday, the contagious period after the rash runs through Friday. Staying home through that Friday covers the full four days after rash onset.

Staying home means skipping work, school, child care and other shared settings for the whole period. Inside the household, it means keeping apart from anyone who lacks evidence of immunity.

Anyone with measles should avoid contact with infants, pregnant people and people with weakened immune systems throughout the contagious period. Those three groups face a higher risk of severe illness from measles.

Exposed people who never get sick can still be asked to stay home. When the health department directs it, exposed people without immunity are generally kept out of school, child care and work from day five through day 21 after exposure. The exclusion covers people who feel well. An infection can still be developing during that stretch before any symptoms show.

A timely MMR dose can change that outcome. Exposed people who got the vaccine within the recommended window may be allowed to return to school or work. The health department makes that decision. The answer can differ from one jurisdiction to the next.

Questions about when isolation or exclusion ends belong with a doctor or the local health department. The health department sets the end dates and decides when a person can return to work, school or child care.
