Two LeetCode mediums in about 40 minutes. That is still the core of a Meta coding round, and most candidates who fail it run out of time rather than not knowing the pattern.

But since October 2025 the loop has picked up a second kind of coding round, one where you get an AI assistant and a multi-file project. A lot of the prep advice online hasn't caught up, so here's how I'd prepare in 2026, round by round.

The process usually runs four to eight weeks from the first recruiter call to a decision. Roughly:

It replaced one of the two traditional onsite coding interviews. You get 60 minutes in CoderPad with a chat panel, and instead of two unrelated puzzles you work through one extended problem with several stages, often starting with getting an existing codebase to run.

You can switch models mid-interview. Candidates have reported GPT-5, Claude Sonnet and Haiku, Gemini 2.5 Pro and Meta's own Llama 4 Maverick.

E3 and E4 candidates often get no design round at all. From E5 up, design carries as much weight as coding, and the behavioral round gets harder because it's partly deciding your level.

Meta's classic coding round is the most predictable interview in big tech, which makes it the most trainable. The question pool leans on a familiar set of topics:

Dynamic programming shows up less than at Google. Don't ignore it, but don't spend a week on it either.

Work through Meta-tagged problems on LeetCode. Premium is worth paying for a month or two, purely for the company tag and frequency sort.

Aim for under 20 minutes per medium, including talking. Many candidates report they couldn't run their code, so practice in a plain editor with no autocomplete, and dry-run your solution on a small input before you say you're done. Interviewers notice when you catch your own bug.

An opinion you can ignore: doing the top 150 Meta-tagged mediums twice beats grinding 300 problems that include hards. Meta rarely asks them, and the second pass is what builds speed.

This is where strong candidates get burned. They assume the round is easier because they have help, then spend 15 minutes reading generated code they don't trust.

What's being measured is still engineering. Can you read unfamiliar code, break the task into pieces, check output, and catch edge cases? Meta has said fairly directly that it's testing classic coding skills, with AI there to make the setting realistic.

The bar is also higher than in a normal coding round. Because you have an assistant, interviewers expect you to get further through the stages than you would alone.

Meta gives you a practice environment before the interview with a sample problem called "the puzzle." Do it. Then practice the shape of the round elsewhere: take a small open-source project, set a 60-minute timer, and add a feature with Cursor or Claude Code while narrating out loud. Hello Interview has a sandbox built around this format too.

Choose your model before the day. Some candidates found GPT-5 too slow under interview pressure and switched to Claude. Whatever you pick, know how it behaves before the clock starts.

Many candidates get to choose between system design and product architecture. Product architecture is about APIs, data models and client-server interaction; system design is about scale, storage and distribution. Backend and infra people should take system design. Product and full-stack engineers usually do better in product architecture.

Alex Xu's System Design Interview (both volumes) is still the standard starting point. After that, Hello Interview's write-ups of common questions like news feed, chat, Instagram and top-K.

Meta likes questions that look like its own products, so practicing "design the Facebook News Feed" is homework, not cheating.

Candidates who burn 20 minutes on requirements never reach that last step, and it's the part that separates E5 from E6.

It's easy to treat this round as a chat. It isn't. Meta scores it on specific signals, including handling conflict, driving work through ambiguity, learning from failure and the size of your impact, and at senior levels it feeds directly into leveling.

Write six to eight stories from the last three years. Each one should contain a number: latency cut from 800ms to 200ms, a launch that reached 2 million users, five engineers across two teams you got aligned. Map every story to two or three signals so you can reuse them.

Use STAR loosely. Interviewers interrupt with follow-ups, so know the details cold: who disagreed with you, what you'd do differently, what actually happened afterwards.

It's the most common mistake in this round. They want to know what you did.

Solo practice gets you to knowing the material. Mock interviews get you to performing it while someone watches, and that gap is where most people fail. You probably want a mix of AI, human and peer mocks.

These are the cheapest way to get volume:

The upside is that you can do one at 11pm on a Tuesday, and they're good at drilling the habit most people neglect, which is thinking out loud. The downside is that quality varies a lot and AI interviewers tend to be generous with hints and praise. Take their scores with a grain of salt. Also helps if you follow an interview guide for a Meta Engineer

A general assistant works surprisingly well. Give Claude or ChatGPT (voice mode is good for this) a prompt like:

You're a Meta E5 interviewer. Ask me one medium problem from Meta's usual topics, give no hints unless I ask, interrupt with follow-ups, and at the end grade me harshly on communication, correctness and time.

It's especially useful for behavioral practice if you tell it to dig into your stories the way a skeptical engineering manager would.

interviewing.io runs anonymous mocks with engineers from Meta and other large companies, and it's the closest you'll get to the real loop. Paid coaching usually costs $100 to $200 an hour.

Peer mocks through Discord groups, Reddit or a friend at another company are free and much better than nothing.

AI mocks for the first few weeks to build the narrating habit, then two or three human mocks in the last two weeks, at least one of them for design.

If you're working full time, give yourself six to eight weeks.

Ask your recruiter for more time if you need it. Meta recruiters push dates by a few weeks all the time, while a rejection usually means waiting six months to a year before you can reapply (one recent candidate was told a full year). Taking the interview before you're ready is the most expensive mistake in the whole process.
