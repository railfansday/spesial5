What The Telecom Bust Taught Me About AI’s Debt Boom

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

The financing of the AI buildout is shifting from equity to debt, and those who invested through the telecom bubble may recognize what that shift tends to mean.

There is an old story in Stephen Hawking’s “A Brief History of Time” about a scientist lecturing on the universe when a woman in the audience insists that the world rests on the back of a giant turtle. Asked what the turtle stands on, she replies that it’s “turtles all the way down.” Her response is what philosophers call infinite regress — a chain of claims in which each one rests on another of the same kind and solid ground never arrives.

I think of it when I look at the AI buildout because its logic has taken the same shape. Valuations support borrowing, and borrowing builds capacity. The capacity then becomes proof that the valuations were right, so each claim stands on the one beneath it.

Over the past year, more of the stack has started running on debt rather than equity, and in my experience, that crossing is the moment the telecom bubble becomes the relevant history given that debt may change how these stories end.

Until recently, the AI buildout was largely an equity-financed story, with venture investment reaching record levels.

Crunchbase data shows global venture funding totaled $679 billion through the first three quarters of 2026, the highest amount ever recorded for that period. AI companies attracted $102 billion in the third quarter alone, accounting for roughly 64% of global venture funding. A record 27 companies raised rounds of $1 billion or more during the quarter, collectively accounting for about a third of that total.

Equity financing carries significant risks, but unlike debt, it generally does not impose fixed repayment obligations. When investments underperform, equity investors typically absorb losses through declining valuations, while companies may retain greater flexibility to pursue their long-term strategies.

That debt is now arriving in size. Meta sold $30 billion of bonds last October in one of the largest corporate debt sales on record, then returned for $25 billion more in April, with investors reportedly demanding more compensation the second time. Amazon, Alphabet and Oracle have each borrowed tens of billions of their own, and Meta’s giant Louisiana data-center campus was financed with a record $27 billion bond raised by a joint-venture partner rather than by Meta itself. Morgan Stanley expects the big AI and cloud companies to spend about $3 trillion on infrastructure through 2028, with only about half of it expected to come from their own cash flow. The rest has to come from somewhere, and increasingly I think that somewhere is the bond market and private credit.

I watched the earlier version of this from a front-row seat, first as a venture capitalist in the late 1990s and since then as a lender to growth-stage technology companies. Telecom borrowed staggering sums against a demand forecast that was widely repeated and rarely checked, the famous claim that internet traffic was doubling every 100 days.

Traffic was actually doubling about once a year, spectacular growth but a fraction of what the borrowing assumed. By 2002 the telecom sector carried roughly $1 trillion in debt, much of which the chairman of the FCC told Congress would never be repaid, and WorldCom had entered what was then the largest bankruptcy in American history.

Much of the fiber the industry laid sat dark for years and was finally put to work by buyers who paid pennies on the dollar for it in bankruptcy, through a decade of restructurings and private mergers that rebuilt entire cap tables. Some companies survived that process, and many did not.

The echo that concerns me most is the demand narrative. Today’s version holds that data centers are effectively leased before they are built and that the only real mistake is building too little. That may prove correct, but it is repeated with the same uniformity the traffic forecast enjoyed in 1999, and much of it rests on projections supplied by the companies doing the building. What has changed in recent months is that investors have begun demanding an answer.

Anthropic is now public, and once companies of that scale report financial results every quarter, investors can check the demand story against audited numbers.

Some may argue, reasonably, that my comparison fails and that the companies doing the largest borrowing may be among the most profitable enterprises ever built, with cash flows the telecom carriers never approached. A few AI companies also appear to be reaching revenue scale faster than any businesses before them.

And the internet really did change everything, just as the traffic really did arrive. All of that may be true, and none of it answers the question I think a lender has to ask, which is whether the cash arrives on the schedule the debt requires. Equity can generally wait out a wrong timeline in a way debt cannot, and when borrowed money builds capacity faster than revenue arrives to service it, in my opinion, the correction tends to come through restructurings rather than quiet markdowns.

None of this is a prediction that the AI buildout must end the way telecom did. The most durable lesson of the telecom bust, in my view, concerned capital structure, which decided who was still standing when demand finally caught up with capacity, long after the technology itself had been vindicated. I have spent years arguing that growth companies fear debt more than they should, and I still believe it, but the argument has always been conditional.

Debt serves borrowers whose obligations are sized to revenue they can see and lenders who underwrite fundamentals instead of narratives, and it treats everyone else harshly. I believe the borrowing stacked beneath the AI boom will be judged by that standard. Every assumption in the stack may well hold, but what I learned the last time is that once investors begin asking what the turtles are standing on, they rarely stop until they reach something solid.
