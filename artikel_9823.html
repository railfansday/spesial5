Federated AI Still Has a Central Coordinator Problem

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Here's the problem federated learning is trying to solve: Most AI training starts with pooling data. That fails wherever the data is sensitive or legally stuck. In 2023, Samsung reportedly restricted employee use of ChatGPT after engineers pasted internal source code into it. In late 2024, Italy’s data protection authority fined OpenAI €15M over how personal data was handled in training. Hospitals hold exactly the data that could improve diagnostic models, but they can’t simply merge patient records.

Federated learning is the standard answer: instead of moving data to the model, move the model to the data. Each site trains locally and sends only model updates to a coordinator, which aggregates them. It’s not theoretical. Google has used it for on-device keyboard prediction in Gboard, and a multi-hospital study (EXAM) trained a COVID-19 oxygen-needs model across roughly 20 institutions without sharing patient records.

“The data never leaves” is not the same as “nothing leaks.” Model updates are computed from private data, and researchers have shown that training examples can sometimes be reconstructed from shared gradients (see Deep Leakage from Gradients, Zhu et al., 2019). Membership inference attacks can also reveal whether a specific record was in the training set.

Both help. Neither is free. Any claim of “private AI” should come with the accuracy cost attached.

This is the part the marketing skips. In most federated systems, one party runs the coordinator, owns the global model, and decides who participates. The data is distributed, but control isn’t. That’s centralized AI with extra steps.

If the goal is the open, user-owned AI that this contest is about, federation alone doesn’t deliver it. You also need verifiable records of what happened, independent compute, and governance people can contest.

Almost all federated learning is about prediction: classify this image, predict the next word. But decisions in medicine and policy depend on causal questions: does this treatment help, or do sicker patients just receive it more often?

Learning causal structure across silos is harder. Sites may measure different variables, confounders differ from site to site, and local graphs may disagree. Federated causal discovery work exists, but it’s far less mature than federated prediction. A practical starting point is to learn a causal graph locally at each site (for example with pgmpy), merge the graphs in an aggregation step, and test whether the merged structure stays consistent across sites.

A hospital in Delhi, one in Nairobi and one in São Paulo improve a shared model without any patient record leaving the building. The coordinator is a protocol anyone can audit. Every training round leaves a verifiable record of which sources, configuration and model version produced the result. Contributors are rewarded for useful updates instead of having their data harvested for free.
