Zwift+ Adds a Virtual Coach and Real Roads to Your Indoor Rides

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Zwift+ introduces a new subscription tier with an adaptive plan and real-road video. I tried both.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

Since its 2015 debut, Zwift has focused on indoor riding in a gamified virtual world. Now the company is nudging riders toward the real world, too. Zwift+ introduces a new subscription tier with two big features: a virtual coach that sometimes tells you to ride outdoors, and video routes that let your avatar ride real roads while you stay indoors.

I’ve got all the details, along with my first impressions of riding the new routes and working with Coach.

Let me guess: You heard “new subscription tier,” sucked your teeth, and thought, “Oh, great. Another subscription. *Sigh* How much?” Friend, I feel you.

In the U.S., Zwift+ costs $29.99 a month, $10 more than the standard Zwift membership. It includes all the standard Zwift membership features, plus Coach and Video.

You can’t add just Coach or Video separately: they are a package deal. But the company says you can move between the standard membership and Zwift+ easily and as often as you like.

The standard Zwift membership remains $19.99 a month or $199.99 a year.

First of the two new big features in Zwift+ is Coach. It’s an adaptable virtual coaching service that builds easy-to-understand training plans.

Zwift isn’t going after the TrainingPeaks power user or even the AI-powered CoachCat audience. It is aimed at Zwifters who ride about three to eight hours a week and want more structure and a personalized plan without hiring a personal coach.

Coach lives in the Companion app. To build a plan, you answer a few straightforward questions: your experience level (recreational, amateur, competitive, or elite); your goal (train for an event, build endurance or speed, burn calories, or improve climbing); plan length (four, eight, or 12 weeks); how many hours you can ride each week; which days you’re available; and which day you can set aside for a longer ride.

Then it crunches a few 1s and 0s and spits out a plan.

While the shorthand version is that AI generates the plans, Zwift describes the planning engine as machine learning, a subset of artificial intelligence. Zwift says the plan comes from a training model grounded in peer-reviewed sports science and uses anonymized power and heart-rate data from millions of Zwift riders. It then adjusts the result to your goal, recent fitness, schedule, and riding preferences. The company says the model was tested against more than 65 million Zwift rides from 2025 and considers dozens of possible sessions when building a plan.

“The plan itself follows clear rules, so we can trace recommendations back to the reasons behind them. That was deliberate so that you can know why Coach tells you to ride something,” Mark Cote, Zwift’s head of product, told me.

The plans are Zwift-forward, naturally. They prescribe Zwift’s workouts, routes, RoboPacers, and events, with the occasional outdoor ride mixed in. Zwift says 63 percent of its riders do a variety of activities, while only 13 percent stick exclusively to structured workouts. Coach embraces variety and isn’t just weeks of ERG grind sessions.

Once your plan is set, you can see the schedule in the Companion app and the main app’s home screen. After the ride, you get feedback in a new Coach Analysis tab on the activity report. You can also check it later in Companion.

You can make changes in Companion, too: schedule a different Zwift ride or event, add an outdoor ride, or log a rest day or time away. Based on your changes, Coach can then adapt the plan at your request.

It can also notice when your week goes differently than planned. For example, if you take a rest day instead of doing a workout, record an outdoor ride through a linked service such as Garmin, or go too hard during an event, Coach may prompt you to adapt the plan. If you accept, it adjusts the plan.

Zwift says recovery data from services such as WHOOP and Oura can shape the wording of Coach’s messages, but a low recovery score won’t automatically change your prescribed workout. Coach Chat, an AI chatbot that would let you ask questions about the plan conversationally, is a future feature. It won’t be available at launch.

One noteworthy shortcoming of Zwift Coach: You can’t push Zwift workouts to a GPS head unit to perform them outdoors. That said, there are fairly easy ways to take the bones of a specific Zwift workout and make something that can live on your Hammerhead or Garmin head unit.

I’ve been using a beta version of Coach for a few weeks now. I’ve found it admirably easy to set up, use, and adapt. It’s all very Zwifty, with lots of colors, iconography, and cheery, easy-to-digest language.

The plans and workouts (Zwift has recently updated its library with dozens of new workouts) seem well considered, but I don’t have enough time with Coach to know how effective it is at lifting my performance.

For someone taking their first steps toward structured training and feedback, I think Zwift Coach is a brilliant entry point, and I feel even more experienced cyclists looking to improve their performance will appreciate how easy and user-friendly Coach is.

The second highlight new feature is Zwift Video. It adds real roads to the game: Your avatar rides over footage recorded on actual routes, with familiar Zwift physics and game features layered on top.

At launch, 21 routes across six countries total 385 kilometers (239 miles) of roads. The routes range from 6.2 to 29 kilometers (about 3.9 to 18 miles) and include the French Alps, Mallorca, Tenerife, the Western Cape, Lofoten, southern Utah, Colorado, and the Scottish Highlands. Zwift says it will begin adding more routes regularly in the new year.

Zwift employees have recorded the footage specifically for Zwift’s video routes. Playback speeds up or slows down with your avatar, and like classic Zwift, you’ll still see other Zwifter avatars and find all the familiar Zwift features, including drafting, segment leaderboards, HoloReplay, PowerUps, Ride Ons, Drops, XP, and badges. Pack dynamics and road surfaces retain the same ride characteristics as in Zwift’s classic graphics-based worlds. Surfaces range from smooth pavement to broken pavement and gravel, with different rolling resistance.

The main restriction is that video routes are one-way. You can’t make a U-turn, and the roads don’t loop. Reach the end, and you are teleported back to the start where your ride continues uninterrupted.

I’ve ridden a few of the video routes so far. The rideplay is familiar Zwift, so it’s really just a change of scenery. And beautiful scenery it is. I’ve ridden a few of these routes in the real world, and while they can’t compare with the real thing, they are still often breathtaking in Zwift. There’s plenty to see, including quirky little details that I’m certain will become part of Zwift lore.

One welcome change is how Zwift marks the start of segments. Large chevrons “painted” on the road alert you to an approaching segment, and barriers and more chevrons clearly mark the start line. It’s a huge improvement over the sometimes easy-to-miss segment starts in Zwift’s classic worlds. I asked a Zwift representative whether classic-world segments might get similar treatment, and they said it was under consideration.

A couple of details take some getting used to. Cars and trucks appear in the footage. Zwift says it curates the footage to reduce traffic, but the roads aren’t traffic-free; however, the traffic doesn’t influence the game’s physics.

Climbing also looks different. I found that a 10 percent grade on a video route looked much less steep than the same grade in a classic Zwift world. A Zwift representative told me classic routes visually exaggerate grades, roughly doubling them, so the climbs read clearly on screen. Video footage doesn’t get that treatment, and the camera can flatten the terrain.

At launch, Video won’t have meetups or scheduled events, but Zwift says those will arrive in the coming months.

Now you might be thinking, wait, didn’t Zwift buy Rouvy, an indoor riding and training service built around real-world video routes? It did. But even though Zwift owns Rouvy, these aren’t Rouvy routes. Zwift representatives told me the video routes were filmed exclusively for Zwift and that the two services will continue independently, with their own content and features. They said there are no plans to bring Rouvy content to Zwift or fold Rouvy into the platform.

Zwift+ isn’t the only change coming. Zwift is also refreshing the Companion home screen, adding WHOOP and Oura connections, and introducing Watt Lock, an ERG-style control that lets you lock onto a target power during free rides.

Zwift also opened The Wild Isles, a new Watopia expansion, on October 26. Inspired by California’s coast and Catalina Island, it adds three islands and about 17 kilometers of flat roads between the Mayan Jungle and Jarvis Island. The new roads create a bypass around the Jungle and include nine cycling routes and three running routes, with ocean views and wildlife such as bison, whales, and egrets.
