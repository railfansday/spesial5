Your Brain May Be Creating ‘Psychic’ Moments—Meaning Your Subconscious Tunes Into Hidden Cues, a Study Finds

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

A 1966 mining disaster in Wales is famous for its alleged premonitions. New research suggests what those strange “gut feelings” could mean.

Here’s what you’ll learn when you read this story:

On the morning of October 21, 1966, the Welsh mining village of Aberfan was struck by one of Britain’s most harrowing peacetime disasters. After days of heavy rain, a slag heap collapsed off a colliery (a coal mine and its connected equipment) and tore down the hillside, submerging a school beneath a torrent of black sludge; 116 children died.

Warnings had come in more than one form. For years, villagers had raised concerns about the coal waste heaps perched above their homes. But some said the signs were stranger—uncanny, even. The day before the disaster, eight-year-old Paul Davies drew a picture of men digging into a hillside, with the words “The End” scrawled above the figures. That same week, ten-year-old Eryl Mai Jones told her mother she’d dreamt that “something black” had descended over the school.

Both children died the next day in the deluge.

A study by psychologist Olafur S. Palsson, PhD, suggests that so-called anomalous experiences—events that seem to go beyond what conventional science can explain—are not rare, but in fact quite common. Additionally, people who report experiencing premonitions, déjà vu, superstitious intuition, and out-of-body sensations often share distinct psychological attributes.

These “strange-but-true” perceptions correlate strongly with a cognitive trait Palsson terms subconscious connectedness: the degree of interaction between conscious awareness and subconscious thought. The research, published in the peer-reviewed journal Psychology of Consciousness: Theory, Research, and Practice in 2025, found that individuals high in subconscious connectedness were significantly more likely to report frequent anomalous experiences, including:

“Some people have a much closer connection with the non-conscious or subconscious parts of their mind,” said Palsson, professor emeritus at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. “Their conscious and subconscious mind influence each other to an unusually high degree in everyday life.”

Using a newly developed metric called the Thought Impact Scale, Palsson surveyed more than 2,000 adults in the U.S. to assess “interactions between conscious aware thinking and nonconscious mental functions.” Respondents rated how much they agree with statements (on a Likert scale) about their various mental experiences and psychological traits, including automatic thoughts, intuitive insight, emotional resonance, spontaneous mental imagery, as well as the frequency of unusual experiences such as telepathic impressions, precognitive dreams, and uncanny coincidences. A clear pattern emerged: individuals with high subconscious connectedness reported such experiences far more often than those with low scores.

Like extroversion or neuroticism, the trait varies from person to person, but stays relatively stable over time in each person. What changes, Palsson suggests, is how the trait manifests—the ways in which people interpret intuition, memory, or coincidence. For those with stronger subconscious input, the mind may simply register more of what others discard, Palsson suggests.

“Anomalous experiences are something most people have at least occasionally,” says Palsson. “Our studies showed that 84 percent to 86 percent of adults repeatedly have at least one type of such experience. Neuroscientists suggest that the brain’s default mode network—active during daydreaming and introspection—may play a role in generating these impressions. Heightened connectivity between regions of the brain responsible for memory, emotion, and prediction could explain the intensity or vividness of so-called premonitions.”

Might psychic foresight, hunches, or “gut feelings” confer some sort of evolutionary edge? Perhaps these anomalous experiences allow humans to make better sense of subtle patterns, according to Palsson. Or, perhaps premonitions and uncanny experiences point to something more banal: they might be explained by selective recall (remembering accurate predictions while ignoring those that never came true). Consider the confirmation bias and the “availability heuristic”—our tendency to remember emotionally charged events and forget the mundane. Most of our dreams are forgotten; the one that coincides with tragedy becomes unforgettable.

Premonition might be less about glimpsing the future than about perceiving—consciously or not—subtle cues the mind is already processing. And in that sense, the inexplicable becomes part of how we navigate the world, not what’s outside it.
