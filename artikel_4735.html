How to Know When It’s Time to End Therapy

"Got it. Best wishes for your continued personal development and your career.”

That was the message I received from my therapist of two years after I sent him a thoughtful email to terminate our work together.

After our last session didn’t go well, I questioned whether I wanted a termination session. Something inside me told me it was the last time I’d see him. I had brought up something he had done that didn’t feel good and had reenacted a feeling of abandonment.

After I left, I remember thinking, “He didn’t handle that well.”

I realize now that the part of me that didn’t want to have a final termination session in person had intuitively sensed his response. His email confirmed what I had felt, and I realized I was protecting myself from having to experience his coldness in person.

Being a therapist who is also looking for a therapist has given me a deeper appreciation for how important the therapeutic relationship is.

Shortly after, I consulted with another therapist. When he asked about my past therapy, I told him how it had ended. Rather than acknowledge how it might have felt to receive that response from a therapist I’d worked with for two years, he said he could understand my therapist’s response because “maybe he still had more work to do with me.”

Maybe he had more work to do with me, I thought, confused. It was my therapy, not my therapist’s. I was making a decision about what was best for me.

When clients are brave enough to recognize when something isn’t working and communicate that to their therapist, they may still question themselves, especially if they grew up in families where their feelings and needs weren’t acknowledged.

There were many times when I thought about terminating with my therapist, and a few times when I consulted with other therapists during our work together. I’ve come to recognize a pattern of staying in situations and relationships longer than I want to or should.

I think many of us have a hard time knowing when to end relationships, including our therapeutic relationships. We may question whether we’re being too sensitive, whether we’re expecting too much, or whether something that didn’t feel good was our own issue to work through.

If you’re in therapy and questioning whether you’re with the right therapist, it doesn’t automatically mean you’re not. It could be an opportunity for you and your therapist to check in about your goals and how things are going. I had done that with my own therapist, too. More than once, I told him I had a desire for him to pursue me more and ask questions about my past, but he didn’t seem to understand what I was trying to communicate. That was part of what contributed to my decision to leave.

Clients should be able to bring all of themselves into therapy and say how they feel, even when what they say might feel personal to the therapist. That’s part of our work as therapists. We have to be willing to examine our own reactions and understand how our expectations and unresolved feelings can influence the therapeutic relationship. In clinical terms, it’s working with transference and countertransference.

Our clients’ therapy is about them, not us.

Yes, therapy is a relationship, and we are part of that relationship. But when we aren’t aware of our own reactions, we can perpetuate the very wounds our clients are trying to repair.

Research supports the importance of paying attention to the therapeutic relationship, particularly when something goes wrong. A 2018 meta-analysis by Catherine Eubanks, J. Christopher Muran, and Jeremy Safran found a moderate association between successful rupture repair and positive psychotherapy outcomes.

The American Psychological Association also emphasizes that therapists should respond to signs of a rupture without becoming defensive, acknowledge their own contributions when appropriate, and examine their emotional reactions through self-awareness, consultation, and supervision.

A rupture doesn’t automatically mean therapy should end. Sometimes working through a difficult interaction can become an important part of the work. But repair requires a therapist who is willing to listen, take responsibility, and understand the impact of their behavior. Clients shouldn’t have to convince their therapist that their experience matters.

Therapy can offer us a reparative experience with endings. Many of us don’t know how to end relationships well. We may avoid difficult conversations, stay longer than we want, or leave without expressing what we feel. A thoughtful termination gives us an opportunity to reflect on the relationship, acknowledge what we’ve gained, express what didn’t work, and say goodbye.

For some clients, having a therapist who can make room for those conversations can be an important part of their healing.

When clients choose to end therapy, it’s our responsibility to support their decisions, even when we have our own feelings about the ending. A therapist might feel disappointed, sad, or hurt. Those feelings deserve attention, but they’re the therapist’s responsibility to work through, not the client’s.

If a therapist believes a client would benefit from another session or a different kind of ending, they can express that and invite a conversation. They also have to respect the client’s choice.

If you find yourself looking for another therapist while still working with your current one, it may be worth asking why. If you consistently feel missed, misunderstood, or as though your therapist is making assumptions about your experience, those are things worth bringing into the room. They may become important areas of exploration.

But if you can’t raise your concerns without offending your therapist, or repeated attempts to communicate what you need don’t lead to greater understanding, it may be time to consider ending the relationship.

You don’t need your therapist’s permission to end therapy or to prove your reasons are good enough. Therapy can help you develop a stronger relationship with yourself, including the ability to recognize when something isn’t working and make decisions that support your well-being.

Sometimes trusting yourself to know when to leave is part of the work.
