10 Fighting Game Remakes That Improved Everything From The Original Games

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Manuel Sifontes is a content strategist and multimedia journalist specializing in technology, culture, and storytelling. He crafts clear, engaging narratives and builds digital audience growth.

I still look back fondly on those afternoons at my grandmother's house, sitting in front of the TV with my cousins and firing up my PlayStation 1 for hours of fighting games like Tekken 3—a series I'm still playing with Tekken 8—and Mortal Kombat Trilogy, one of the best games with non-stop violence that ever existed.

Still, the genre hasn't always been perfect. Plenty of classics launched with serious balance issues, rough performance, or questionable mechanics that could easily sour the experience.

Fortunately, some of those games got a second chance through exceptional remakes that fixed every flaw from the originals. Here are 10 fighting games that completely reinvented themselves with new mechanics, features and definitive balancing.

As a longtime JoJo's Bizarre Adventure fan, I was excited when All-Star Battle got its Western PS3 release in 2014 (following its 2013 Japanese debut). That hype faded fast once I felt its 30 fps frame rate, slower combat pacing, and a Campaign mode weighed down by stamina timers and microtransactions.

In 2022, Bandai Namco released JoJo's Bizarre Adventure: All-Star Battle R on PS4, PS5, Xbox One, Xbox Series X/S, Switch, and PC. While the Switch version runs at 30 fps, the PlayStation, Xbox, and PC editions target 60 fps, immediately making combat feel snappier to me. It also added an Assist system for extending combos and expanded the roster across the series.

Best of all, it dropped the original Campaign's stamina timers and energy purchases—though the game still features paid DLC characters and costumes, and solo players lost the original Story and Campaign modes in favor of the panel-based All-Star Battle mode. For me, the faster gameplay made that trade-off worth it.

Not every necessary remake is modern. While the 1993 arcade Virtua Fighter was a landmark title, its 1994 Sega Saturn port was rough, with flat, untextured polygonal models and distracting polygon flickering.

Released in 1995 for arcades and the Saturn, Virtua Fighter Remix tackled those early-port issues head-on. Side by side on Saturn, the jump is clear: Remix introduced texture-mapped character models with far sharper facial and outfit detail. It wasn't a flawless fix—some polygon glitches remained, the lighting shifted, and load times increased—but for Saturn owners at the time, it was still a major visual upgrade that stood up much better alongside early Tekken.

Video game studios have done a great job of bringing retro fighting games to modern platforms, but a few are still trapped on antiquated systems.

After falling in love with Bloody Roar 2, playing Bloody Roar 3 on PS2 (following its 2000 arcade debut) left me wanting more. Its animal-transformation premise was still a blast, but to my eyes, the early-PS2 visuals looked rough, with jagged edges and coarse textures.

In 2002, Bloody Roar: Primal Fury launched on Nintendo GameCube (followed by Bloody Roar Extreme on Xbox in 2003) as an enhanced remake of Bloody Roar 3. Compared to the PS2 release, the GameCube version offered cleaner character models, upgraded lighting, animated endings for the cast, and newcomers like Cronos and Ganesha. Personally, it became my go-to way to play the game, making every match feel far more polished and satisfying.

I've loved Street Fighter for years, but jumping into specific Western arcade revisions of 1994's Super Street Fighter II Turbo—notably the World 940223 and USA 940323 builds—was a rude awakening. Both are notorious for a difficulty bug that makes the CPU unusually fierce regardless of the machine's setting, on top of lopsided matchups involving Old Sagat, Vega, and a tournament-banned Akuma.

When Super Street Fighter II Turbo HD Remix hit PS3 and Xbox 360 in 2008, I was immediately won over. Udon Entertainment redrew the sprites and stages in HD, and the game added a rebalanced mode to rein in dominant fighters while still letting purists play under the original rules. Paired with built-in GGPO rollback netcode, it became a much friendlier way to enjoy a classic.

The PS2 introduced me to countless classics, including Dead or Alive 2: Hardcore (an updated PS2 edition of the 1999 arcade and 2000 Dreamcast fighter). I loved its fast pace and multi-tiered stages, though its early-PS2 visuals aged quickly and its counter-heavy combat could feel unforgiving.

Dead or Alive Ultimate arrived on the original Xbox in 2004 with a ground-up remake of DOA2 built on an upgraded DOA3 engine. Compared to the PS2 version, the leap in lighting, textures, and stage detail was night and day. While reversals remained a defining—and sometimes divisive—part of the formula, Ultimate felt like the definitive package, bringing together fighters like Tengu and Bayman alongside DOA3's Hitomi and over 20 interactive stages.

At launch, it also stood out for its Xbox Live lobbies and spectator mode—a huge draw at the time, even though Microsoft officially shut down original Xbox Live servers in April 2010.

The PlayStation 2 brought some incredible fighting games during its lifetime. This list breaks down the 10 best to grace the PS2 system.

SNK's catalog deserves a spot in any fighting game discussion, including The King of Fighters 2002. This fantastic Neo Geo game had strong core gameplay, but its presentation felt more stripped-back to me than SNK's earlier classics, with a soundtrack that didn't hit as hard and several NESTS-saga favorites missing from the roster.

I also wasn't a fan of a meta dominated by Billy Kane, Athena, and Choi. That's why the 2009 remake, The King of Fighters 2002: Unlimited Match (originally on PS2, later coming to Xbox Live Arcade in 2010 and PC in 2015), felt like such a triumph.

Expanding the roster to 66 playable characters in its console and Xbox Live Arcade editions, it overhauled the stages (using 3D polygonal backgrounds on PS2 and 2D replacements on PC), added a new soundtrack, refined MAX Mode cancels, and introduced new moves across the cast—turning it into one of my favorite 2D fighters SNK has ever made.

The original Karateka launched in 1984 for the Apple II before receiving ports for Atari 8-bit systems, the NES/Famicom, and the Commodore 64. Its flaws stood out quickly, though: the controls were slow and stiff, there were no checkpoints—meaning a single lost fight sent you right back to the beginning—and the combat system was bare-bones.

In 2012, however, a remake arrived on Xbox 360 before making its way to PC, PS3, and iOS. This new version rebuilt the visuals from the ground up in cel-shaded 3D powered by Unreal Engine 3, and introduced a one-on-one combat system built around visual and audio cues for countering attacks. What really won me over was how it replaced the single-life structure with three consecutive suitors, each with different speeds and their own unique endings.

At launch, the original Fatal Fury: King of Fighters—released in 1991 for Neo Geo—drew plenty of criticism for offering only three playable characters: Andy Bogard, Terry Bogard, and Joe Higashi. On top of that, its gameplay lacked depth, relying on a simple three-button control scheme.

In 1999, Fatal Fury: Wild Ambition hit the Hyper Neo Geo 64 and PlayStation 1, expanding the roster to 12 fighters in arcades and 14 on PS1. It also introduced new defensive and meter-management mechanics, including the Heat Meter, Heat Blow, and Overdrive Power. It's worth noting, however, that Sony's console version took several noticeable graphical downgrades compared to the arcade release.

If you're a fan of weird and wonderful brawlers, here are several more obscure fighting games that are worth experiencing.

This title might not be as widely known as others on this list, but it had a strong following in Japan back in the day. Asuka 120% Burning Fest. originally launched in 1994 for the FM Towns, though several issues held the experience back—including a limited roster, a lack of defensive tools, and the fact that it was locked to a niche Japanese home computer.

Several years later, in 1999, Asuka 120% Final: Burning Fest. Final arrived on PS1. While it remained a Japan-only release, this version featured a completely rebuilt combat engine with a much faster pace and noticeably smoother sprite animations. It also expanded the character roster—adding secret fighters into the mix—and introduced deeper offensive and defensive mechanics, such as air recovery.

The original Eternal Champions launched on the Sega Genesis/Mega Drive in 1993 in North America, followed by Europe and Japan in 1994. Like many entries on this list, though, it suffered from frustrating issues—including punishing AI, clunky, unintuitive controls, and a small roster, among other flaws.

It wasn't until 1995 that its remake, Eternal Champions: Challenge from the Dark Side, which can be considered one of the best fighting games stuck on older systems, arrived on the Sega CD as an expanded overhaul of the original. It expanded the starting roster from nine to 13 fighters—alongside a large lineup of unlockables—introduced new FMV finishers called Cinekills, and improved the difficulty curve compared to the original game.
