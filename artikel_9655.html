Attributed Revenue Cannot Tell You What Your Ads Caused

A campaign report assigns revenue to an advertising channel. The budget meeting asks what would happen if the company stopped spending there.

The report describes credit under a measurement system. The budget decision requires an estimate of the difference the advertising made. A purchase can receive advertising credit even when the buyer might have purchased without that exposure.

My argument is not that attribution should be abandoned. It is that attribution and incrementality should be used for the questions they can answer, with neither quietly presented as a substitute for the other.

Attribution helps a business organize observed journeys according to a chosen model, identity system, and conversion definition. It can support troubleshooting, reporting, and comparisons within those boundaries.

Incrementality asks how outcomes differ because of an intervention. The missing outcome is what the same population would have done without it. A well-designed comparison attempts to estimate that counterfactual rather than assuming credited conversions are all additional.

Google Ads describes Conversion Lift as an incrementality tool using treatment and control groups. Access is not universal across accounts, so its existence should not be confused with automatic availability for every advertiser.

Do not compare the two outputs as if one must reconcile exactly to the other. They can use different populations, rules, windows, and estimation methods. The task is to explain those differences and choose the appropriate evidence for the decision.

An attribution report can be internally consistent while still being insufficient to justify a causal claim. That is a limitation of the question it answers, not necessarily an error in the report.

Before selecting a test, write the decision the result is meant to inform. Are you considering reducing an existing campaign, increasing spend, entering a new geography, or changing a creative strategy?

Each intervention has a different target. A test of one campaign at current spend does not automatically estimate the effect of doubling its budget. A regional experiment does not prove that the same outcome will occur in every market.

Specify the treatment in terms the delivery system can enforce. Include the campaigns, eligible population, duration, and planned exclusions. Identify other marketing changes that could interfere with the comparison.

Then define the business outcome. A form completion may be observable sooner than a qualified opportunity, but it is not the same result. If the decision concerns profit, a lift in raw leads may be inadequate without evidence about quality and economics.

Also define what evidence would change the decision. A test with no actionable threshold can consume time while leaving the original argument untouched. The threshold should reflect the business's economics and uncertainty tolerance, not a universal marketing benchmark.

Imagine a fictional B2B company testing an eligible advertising intervention. After the agreed observation period, the appropriately normalized treatment group has 120 qualifying outcomes and the comparison has 100 under the hypothetical design.

The illustrative difference is twenty outcomes. It is not automatically twenty new customers, twenty profitable customers, or proof that the effect will persist. The interpretation depends on the actual outcome definition and the uncertainty around the estimate.

Suppose the platform separately attributes eighty conversions to the campaign. Eighty and twenty do not represent competing counts of the same thing. One allocates credit; the other estimates a difference under the experimental design.

If group sizes differ, raw counts cannot simply be subtracted without the appropriate normalization. If the platform provides an estimate, use its documented method rather than reconstructing a casual spreadsheet formula from headline numbers.

The point of the example is conceptual. It is not a client result, benchmark, or recommended expectation. A business should not use the hypothetical twenty-outcome difference to forecast its own campaign.

Before launch, put the following on a one-page test card:

This is a planning artifact, not a substitute for statistical design or a sample-size calculation. If the available volume cannot support a useful conclusion, change the question or acknowledge that limitation before committing the spend. Do not wait until an inconclusive chart arrives to discover that the experiment could never resolve the proposed decision.

A holdout is useful only if the treatment distinction remains meaningful. If another campaign reaches the comparison group with the same message, the study may estimate a narrower difference than the team intended.

Document relevant concurrent activity. This may include brand campaigns, email promotions, sales outreach, price changes, and product availability. Not every concurrent event invalidates a study, but unexplained differences make interpretation harder.

For geographic tests, consider spillover and regional differences. Buyers may travel, media may cross boundaries, and sales territories may not align neatly with advertising regions. Comparable geography is a design problem, not a visual similarity on a map.

Google's geography-based Conversion Lift guidance describes a distinct approach from user-based studies. Do not borrow assumptions from one design and apply them to another without checking the method.

The same caution applies to account-based B2B campaigns. If several contacts from one company can fall into different groups, conversations inside the company may transmit the treatment. Depending on the experiment, the account rather than the contact may be the more appropriate unit of assignment.

Early results are tempting because they arrive while budget decisions are still being debated. Repeatedly inspecting them and stopping at the first attractive estimate can produce a misleading conclusion.

Google's user-based lift interpretation guide recommends waiting until the study ends for the most accurate results. The guide also documents modeled delayed conversions in a specific study context. A lift report should not be casually described as entirely observed events.

Agree on the study duration, analysis plan, and handling of delayed outcomes before reading the result. If the business must interrupt a test, record the interruption and narrow the claim accordingly.

Do not convert an inconclusive result into proof of no effect. The study may lack enough information to distinguish economically relevant possibilities. Conversely, an estimated positive effect can still be too uncertain or too small to justify the proposed investment.

Ask the analyst to explain the interval and the business decision together. Statistical evidence is useful when the team understands what range of outcomes remains plausible under the method, not merely whether a chart is green.

For businesses with long sales cycles, the earliest observable conversion may be a poor proxy for commercial value. A campaign could increase low-quality requests while leaving qualified opportunities unchanged.

Choose a primary outcome that can be measured consistently within the study's practical limits. Then define follow-up analyses for quality, progression, refunds, or realized value where those records are available and appropriate to use.

Do not quietly replace the primary outcome after seeing an unfavorable result. Secondary analyses can be useful, but they should remain labeled as such. A newly discovered promising segment is a hypothesis for another test, not automatically the original experiment's conclusion.

Preserve the cohort and observation window when joining CRM outcomes. Otherwise, late-arriving records, changed stage definitions, and retrospective contact associations can alter the apparent result without changing the underlying advertising effect.

Also distinguish incremental revenue from incremental profit. Delivery cost, discounts, service capacity, and customer retention may matter to the budget decision. A positive revenue estimate does not by itself establish that the proposed scale is economically attractive.

Google's user-based Conversion Lift setup guidance should be consulted for current eligibility and configuration rather than assuming that a standard campaign split creates an equivalent study.

Keep the experiment settings, campaign selection, outcome definitions, and analysis export in the review record. Note whether results contain modeled components and whether the study ended as planned.

If the required experiment is unavailable, do not rename an ordinary before-and-after report a lift test. Seasonality, demand changes, and other interventions can explain an observed difference. Such analysis can support a hypothesis while remaining observational.

A smaller business may need a simpler, carefully designed test or a decision made under acknowledged uncertainty. The absence of a sophisticated experiment does not make attribution useless. It makes the causal confidence more limited.

AI can help summarize a documented design and identify missing assumptions. It should not manufacture a control group, infer a statistically reliable effect from two totals, or describe a vendor's credited revenue as caused revenue.

Plan the handoff before the experiment begins. The person reading the result may not be the person who configured the campaigns. A short design record should explain the intended intervention and any operational constraints in language the budget owner can understand.

After the study, compare the delivered treatment with the planned treatment. If delivery was limited, the result may answer what happened under that limited execution rather than what would happen under a fully delivered campaign. That distinction belongs in the conclusion.

Also separate a valid result from a reusable policy. A study can be well executed and still become less relevant after a major pricing, product, or competitive change. Keep the date and scope attached to the finding when it is reused in future planning. Evidence has a context; it is not a permanent certificate for a channel.

A useful final memo states the intervention, population, outcome, timing, design, estimate, uncertainty, implementation deviations, and resulting decision. Include what the result does not support.

For example, a campaign-level finding may support maintaining current spend while leaving expansion uncertain. A regional result may justify another market-specific test. An inconclusive result may justify improving measurement rather than declaring the channel ineffective.

Record the difference between the experimental evidence and the attribution report without forcing either to impersonate the other. They can remain useful side by side.

The discipline is simple to state and demanding to practice: match the claim to the design. Attributed revenue tells you how a reporting system distributes credit. To decide what the ads caused, you need a defensible comparison and the restraint to stop where that comparison stops.
