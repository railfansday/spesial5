Massiv Trail: 224 Miles through Norway’s Wilderness

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

224 miles through some of Southern Norway’s wildest mountain landscapes: On the Massiv Trail, we hike through Breheimen, Jotunheimen, Skarvheimen, and across the Hardangervidda, navigating a seemingly endless and ever-changing combination of rocks, water, and mountains. Glaciers, countless lakes, waterfalls, and vast stretches of tundra accompany us on our journey from Sota Sæter to Haukeliseter. But the Massiv Trail doesn’t make things easy. Boulder fields, bogs, and barely visible trails slow our progress, while rain, fog, wind, and cold present the real challenges. The scenery is spectacular when the Norwegian weather isn’t hiding it behind thick clouds. We’re fortunate to experience many of its different faces during our 15 days on the trail.

Duration: 15–18 days Distance: 224 miles Starting Point: Sota Sæter, Norway Ending Point: Haukeliseter, Norway Total Elevation Gain: 47,570 feet Difficulty: Moderate to challenging; mostly mountain trails with some rocky and more demanding sections. Tip: There are no towns on the trail. Get a DNT key to unlock self-serviced huts to access facilities like the pantry to purchase basic resupply.

Sota Sæter to just before Lake Fivlemyrane 13 miles / 4,593 ft elevation gain / 8.5 hrs

At 8 am, the shuttle picks us up in Bismo and takes us to Sota Sæter, the starting point of our Massiv Trail adventure. It’s a chilly morning, and I’m bundled up in my down jacket, beanie, and leggings.

After weeks of checking the weather forecast and seeing little besides rain and low temperatures, we’re determined to make the most of today’s rare dry weather. But before setting off, we stop at the mountain lodge of Sota Sæter, where I get to pet a fluffy orange cat.

The trail climbs steadily from Sota Sæter, soon revealing beautiful views of Lake Liavatnet and the surrounding mountains. Along the way, a curious sheep approaches me, sniffs my hand, and even gives it a lick. Probably looking for some salt.

The Massiv Trail starts in Sota Sæter and the first climb gives us a nice view of Liavatnet

As we gain elevation, the wind grows colder. Layers that disappeared into our backpacks during the climb quickly come back out, followed by gloves. By the time we reach the first pass, clouds hang over the mountains, and the temperature has dropped considerably. The scenery, however, is spectacular, with the imposing rock and ice walls of Tundradalskyrkja rising above Lake Sottjønnin.

After crossing a few easy snowfields, we descend toward our first river crossing. We search upstream and downstream for a dry route across the rocks but eventually give up. I walk straight through the water in my trail runners, while Ryan takes his shoes off and wades barefoot.

Then we reach Lake Illvatnet, and the real challenge begins. The entire shoreline is one enormous boulder field. Red T-shaped trail markers painted on rocks and cairns guide the way, but following them is easier said than done. We constantly stop to locate the next marker while carefully balancing from rock to rock. I wonder what the T stands for. I decide it must mean “T-errific.” In reality, it stands for Turistforeningen, referring to the Norwegian Trekking Association (DNT).

Our pace drops to barely one mile per hour. Every step demands concentration, and the endless scrambling becomes as mentally exhausting as it is physically demanding. The lake seems to go on forever.

Navigating the boulder fields along the shore of Illvatnet

Ironically, the weather keeps improving. The clouds break apart, the sun comes out, and immediately it’s almost warm. The landscape is stunning, but I’m running out of energy to appreciate it. We try distracting ourselves with games before switching to an audiobook.

When the boulder field finally ends, I’m relieved. An actual dirt trail! Almost immediately, my energy returns. The next few miles make up for the struggle. We pass one sparkling mountain lake after another, occasionally navigating muddy and swampy sections that feel downright pleasant compared to the endless rocks.

Alpine Lakes on the first day on the Massiv Trail

The trail descends into a broad river valley, with glaciers clinging to the mountains across from us. A roaring meltwater river rushes through the valley, and we find a beautiful campsite beside it, still catching the last warm rays of sunshine.

The ground is covered in soft, springy white lichen. A few mosquitoes join us for dinner, but as soon as the sun disappears behind the mountains, so do they. Unfortunately, the warmth disappears just as quickly. Welcome to the Massiv Trail.

0.6 miles before Lake Fivlemyrane to Storevatnet 14.8 miles / 4,462 ft elevation gain / 8.5 hrs

The next morning, we continue through the valley until a detour around a hydroelectric power plant forces us off the trail and into swampy terrain. Far below us, a wide gravel road looks wonderfully smooth and easy. When we finally reach it, walking feels almost ridiculously effortless. No boulders to navigate, no mud to assess, no trail markers to search for. Just putting one foot in front of the other.

We arrive at Nørdstedalseter, a DNT mountain hut, where we treat ourselves to waffles and tea beside a warm fire. It’s hard to leave such a cozy place when it’s so cold outside, but eventually, we have to keep moving. The gravel road soon gives way to another muddy trail. At least Norway spoils us with three bridges today.

A light drizzle sets in, but we quickly warm up on the climb. Ryan even manages to sweat through his clothes despite the low temperatures. How he does it is beyond me. The man is a furnace. The fact that he’s the only person around wearing shorts might explain a thing or two.

As I trudge through yet another bog, I start contemplating the different varieties of mud. There’s the relatively firm kind, where your shoes sink only slightly. And then there’s the kind that looks perfectly innocent until your foot suddenly disappears ankle-deep. You never know which one you’re getting until you step on it. It’s basically postholing, but in mud. Mudholing?

During a short lunch break, we shelter behind a large boulder, but the cold quickly creeps back in whenever we stop moving. Shortly afterward, we meet two Dutch hikers, Lizette and Iris, who are also hiking the Massiv Trail. “It’s cold,” Ryan observes. “It’s Norway!” they reply matter-of-factly. Fair point.

The landscape becomes increasingly alpine, with beautiful mountain lakes, glaciers, and patches of lingering snow. One lake, backed by a partially glaciated mountain with small ice caves along its shore, becomes my favorite view of the day.

We’re soon back in another endless maze of boulders. Red T-shaped trail markers guide us through the rocks, although they’re not always particularly helpful. Sometimes they’re painted on top of enormous boulders for visibility, which doesn’t necessarily mean the trail actually goes over them. Occasionally, we discover this only after climbing.

Then the inevitable happens. We’d agreed that whoever slipped and fell in the mud first would earn the trail name “Muddy Butt” for the rest of this hike. Well, guess who wins.

At least our next river crossing goes better. We manage to keep our feet dry, although it requires a little bouldering. Meanwhile, tiny, hard snow pellets begin falling from the sky. It’s early September.

From a ridge, we look down toward Lake Storevatnet, surrounded by towering, glacier-covered peaks. A dusting of fresh snow covers the dark rocks like powdered sugar. Our destination looks tantalizingly close, but the trail takes a long, winding route around the landscape. My legs are getting heavy, and I switch to autopilot. From cairn to cairn, around puddles, through mud, and across rocks. I try not to lose the trail, but sometimes the trail loses me.

View of the high mountains of Jotunheimen

We reach the lake, find a flat spot for our tent near the dam, crawl into our warm sleeping bags, and enjoy a hot dinner. The day could end peacefully here. Instead, a loud “Pfffft!” suddenly comes from Ryan’s sleeping pad. There’s a large tear in it. He’s recently started using his food bag as a pillow to save weight, and something inside has punctured the pad. I’d actually pointed out that exact possibility earlier. Our exhaustion isn’t exactly helping us repair it efficiently, either: Our first attempt fails, but the second patch seems to hold for now.

Storevatnet to Skogadalsbøen 16 miles / 3,445 ft elevation gain / 9 hrs

We’re in a hurry this morning. Ahead lies an unusual section of the Massiv Trail: the official route crosses the Fannaråkbreen glacier. Crossing independently requires glacier travel experience and equipment such as ropes and crampons, which few hikers want to carry for a crossing that takes only about 45 minutes. For most Massiv Trail hikers, joining a guided glacier crossing is the obvious choice. There’s just one problem: we haven’t booked anything. I’d planned to take care of it yesterday evening, but we had no cell service at our campsite. Now we’re hoping to secure two spots on today’s tour when we reach Sognefjellshytta.

Fortunately, the trail is kind to us today. Small mountain lakes still dot the landscape, but the terrain is considerably easier than during the previous two days. No endless boulder fields, no constant balancing from rock to rock. We can finally make some decent progress.

At Sognefjellshytta, we’re back at a road after two days in the mountains. Inside, it’s warm and cozy, but our priority is finding out about the glacier crossing. The staff can’t book it for us directly, but they give us a phone number. One phone call later, we have our answer: We’re in!

There’s just one catch: the meeting point at the edge of Fannaråkbreen is still 3.7 miles away, and we have to be there by 12:30 pm. We’d love to sit down, warm up, and perhaps enjoy some waffles, but there’s no time. Instead, we leave the mountain lodge behind and hit the trail again.

Just before reaching Fannaråkbreen, it starts raining. We pull on our rain gear and continue until we spot a large blue barrel among the rocks. It contains the equipment for the glacier crossing and marks our meeting point. We arrive on time, along with Lizette and Iris, whom we’ve been running into since yesterday. Waiting in the cold isn’t particularly enjoyable, but soon our guides arrive and hand out harnesses and crampons. The rain has stopped, although the summit of Fannaråki remains hidden in the clouds.

Once everyone is roped together, we begin our ascent. One guide leads, another brings up the rear, and the six of us walk between them. The main challenge is maintaining a consistent pace so the rope stays neither too tight nor too loose. It’s Ryan’s first time on a glacier. After a few minutes, he announces that he’s now ready to climb Mount Everest. The ice beneath our feet glows pale blue, crisscrossed by tiny streams of meltwater. Elsewhere, its rough surface is streaked with dark rock dust. Above us, the black cliffs of Fannaråki disappear into the clouds, while behind us stretches a barren landscape of gray rocks and mountain lakes.

We also encounter numerous dead lemmings on the ice. Apparently, their journey across the glacier didn’t go particularly well. Our guide suspects they may have burrowed into the snow to spend the winter, unaware that they were on a glacier. A more likely explanation, however, is that they wandered onto the ice and couldn’t find their way back. With neither food nor shelter, the glacier is a dangerous place for a lemming. Norwegian lemmings are known to wander extensively during population booms, sometimes ending up in unsuitable habitats. Unfortunately, not all of them make it back.

After about 45 minutes, we reach the other side and remove our crampons.

From here, we could make a detour to the summit of Fannaråki, at 6,785 feet, the highest point on the Massiv Trail and home to Norway’s highest mountain hut. But the summit is completely hidden in clouds, and heavy rain is forecast for tonight and tomorrow. We decide to skip it and continue toward Skogadalsbøen.

First, though, lunch. Wraps with cheese and an outrageously expensive €18 salami. Worth every cent. For a brief moment, everything is right in this cold, inhospitable world. Lizette and Iris have booked a night at Fannaråkhytta and continue toward the summit. We say goodbye and begin our descent.

Soon, a beautiful valley opens up below us. The Gjertvassbreen glacier spills down between the mountains, with an enormous waterfall plunging into the valley beneath it. The sun even makes a five-minute appearance.

As we descend, trees begin to reappear for the first time in days. Unfortunately, so do mud and bogs. We cross a suspension bridge and follow a muddy forest trail, hopping between rocks to avoid the worst puddles. About 500 yards before Skogadalsbøen, I smell woodsmoke. A little closer, I smell food. Shut up and take my money!

We reach the mountain lodge, pay for a campsite, book dinner, and treat ourselves to soda. Even though we’re camping, we’re allowed to use the lodge’s facilities, including the showers. After three days of hiking, a hot shower feels incredible. Washing my socks is less rewarding. No matter how many times I rinse them, the water stays brown.

At dinner, we meet Mark, another German Massiv Trail hiker who’s traveling from hut to hut. His first two stages took around eleven hours each, but at least he gets a three-course meal every evening. Tonight, so do we.

And what a meal it is. Homemade sourdough bread with garlic butter, followed by a creamy leek-and-lemon soup so delicious I want to fill a bathtub with it and climb in. Then braised lamb ribs with roasted potatoes, vegetables, and lingonberries, followed by chocolate cake for dessert. I regret nothing.

By the time we return to our tent, the forecast heavy rain has arrived. We’re the only guests sleeping outside tonight, but we’re full, dry, and warm in our sleeping bags, listening to the rain drumming against the tent. Things could be considerably worse.

Evening view of Skogadalsbøen hut from our campsite

Skogadalsbøen to somewhere in Øvre Mjølkedalen 9.3 miles / 2,625 ft elevation gain / 5 hrs

Rain drums against our tent all night, but it’s our warmest night on the Massiv Trail so far. By morning, it’s still raining, and neither of us is particularly eager to get up. We keep falling back asleep and don’t pack up until around 10 am. We hang our wet tent in the drying room at Skogadalsbøen and retreat to the lodge for one more soda.

Morning at our campsite near Skogadalsbøen hut

Some Norwegians from last night’s dinner are there, too. They casually mention that they’ve already been swimming in the river this morning. Of course they have. Norwegians are built differently. Our enthusiasm for the cold rain and low-hanging clouds is considerably more limited.

Just, who works at the lodge, recommends taking an alternate through the valley rather than following the official Massiv Trail toward Fondsbu. The usual route involves extensive boulder fields, which are particularly slippery in this weather. His suggested alternate is slightly longer but easier to walk. There’s just one catch: parts of the trail are practically underwater. His advice is to just embrace the wet feet.

Eventually, we head outside. It’s still drizzling, but at least the rain isn’t particularly heavy. And Just wasn’t exaggerating. The trail alternates between bog, mud, and what might as well be a river. At first, we try to find dry spots but quickly give up. Our feet are soaked anyway, so we simply march straight through. It’s liberating and considerably faster; just need to watch out for frogs.

Wet bushes along the trail act like a car wash, ensuring that our legs stay just as soaked as our feet. Fortunately, it’s much warmer down here in the valley than up in the mountains. We follow the river through a lush green forest, surrounded by mosses, lichens, and patches of pink heather. Whenever the clouds briefly part, they reveal waterfalls cascading down the surrounding mountains. It’s beautiful and pretty miserable at the same time.

The trail begins climbing, and we leave the forest behind. The rain finally stops, and the landscape becomes increasingly rocky. In the distance, we spot a herd of reindeer – our first on the Massiv Trail! The clouds begin to lift, too. Instead of completely swallowing the mountains, they now swirl around the peaks. I hadn’t realized clouds could be so beautiful.

Reindeer herd in Jotunheimen National Park

We continue climbing past rivers and lakes, crossing countless streams along the way, and by now, we couldn’t care less about keeping our feet dry. The slippery rocks are a bigger concern. Ryan slips twice and takes a hard fall. I’m increasingly grateful that we followed Just’s advice and avoided the larger boulder fields.

Otherwise, the trail is a delight: well-marked, easy to follow, and fast. No constant GPS checks, no searching for the next red T. We’re making better progress than on any previous day. The downside of this weather is that breaks aren’t particularly enjoyable. Whenever we stop moving, the cold quickly creeps back in, so we barely stop at all.

Two hikers coming from Fondsbu warn us that good campsites are scarce in the rocky terrain ahead. Around 5:30 pm, we find a beautiful, flat spot for our tent. It’s still early, and part of me feels almost guilty about wasting a dry spell by stopping. But I’m tired, not so much physically as mentally. Besides, who knows when we’ll find another suitable campsite or when the rain will return?

We’ve done enough for today. And sure enough, just as we start preparing dinner, it begins raining again. Decision confirmed.

View from our campsite in Øvre Mjølkedalen

Øvre Mjølkedalen to 1.5 miles before Slettningsbu 20 miles / 3,346 ft elevation gain / 8.5 hrs

Strong gusts of wind shake our tent throughout the night, but thanks to earplugs, I sleep well. Just as we’re finishing packing in the morning, rain starts pounding against the tent. We wait for a break, only for it to start again right before we leave. Reluctantly, we pull on our rain pants.

Fortunately, the rain doesn’t last long. Soon, we’re peeling off our sweaty waterproof layers, and patches of blue sky begin appearing between the clouds. Mountain lakes surround us, glaciers shimmer in the distance, and occasionally, the sun makes an appearance.

The final six miles to Fondsbu take us through the beautiful Øvre Mjølkedalen valley. The trail is still muddy, and at one particularly slippery step, we both lose our footing at the same time. My arm ends up in the mud, Ryan’s knee. At least our feet stay relatively dry for once.

As we approach Fondsbu, the scenery becomes increasingly idyllic. Black wooden cabins with grass-covered roofs dot the landscape, cotton grass grows everywhere, and small waterfalls cascade down the hillsides.

When we reach the lodge, the sun comes out, transforming the entire landscape. We celebrate that with burgers and sodas. As usual, the lodge is so warm and homey that we could easily spend the rest of the day there. But sunshine is a rare commodity in Norway, and we’re determined to make the most of it.

Back on the trail, we follow an easy gravel road before turning onto a trail. Soon, we meet Rick and Nancy, two Americans hiking the Massiv Trail in the opposite direction. They recently retired and now live in Trondheim. They’ve hiked part of the Appalachian Trail, where they earned the trail names Big Red and Waffle. Naturally, we ask for some trail intel about what lies ahead. One piece of information immediately catches our attention: there’s fantastic pizza at a road crossing a few days away. “The reindeer pizza was bomb!” Nancy tells us, her eyes lighting up. Three consecutive days of good food? We can work with that.

A little later, we meet two Norwegian men training their dog for bird hunting. They’ve spent the day looking for ptarmigan without success. Their fishing efforts haven’t been any more productive. Ryan tries to cheer them up by pointing out that at least the weather is nice.

Enjoying the sunshine on the way from Fondsbu to Slettningsbu

Unfortunately, the trail becomes increasingly muddy. At first, I carefully probe the ground with my trekking pole, searching for hidden rocks or firmer patches that might support my weight. But then the ground becomes so swampy that there’s simply no way around it. We surrender and march straight through. No more detours, no more awkward balancing along the trail’s edges. If our feet are going to get wet anyway, they might as well get wet efficiently.

Later, we follow a road, discussing the possibility of buying one of the remote cabins out here and escaping to Norway to retreat from climate change. We climb away from the road and find a beautiful campsite sheltered among some rocks. Hopefully, it’ll offer better protection from the wind tonight.

Checking our mileage, we realize we reached the 20-mile mark for the first time on the Massiv Trail. It’s been our easiest day so far. Less climbing, easier trails, and a few stretches of road walking have helped us cover some good distance. But there’s another number that’s even more important: only eight miles until pizza.

1.5 miles before Slettningsbu to 2 miles past Sulebu 16 miles / 4,167 ft elevation gain / 7.5 hrs

Once again, we wake up to rain drumming against our tent. According to the forecast, it isn’t even supposed to be raining, but Norwegian weather has other plans. For the second morning in a row, we have to pull on cold, wet socks and shoes. There are more pleasant ways to start a hiking day.

Slippery rocks on the way to Slettningsbu in the morning

Things don’t improve when I step onto a rock covered in wet lichen and promptly land on my butt. My left butt cheek will probably remind me of this fall for a while. More importantly, I develop an immediate distrust of every lichen-covered rock on the trail.

As we climb through the cold, windy landscape, clouds hang low between the mountains, and the white tufts of cotton grass sway in the wind. Then we reach Slettningsbu, our first self-service DNT hut. These huts stock food that hikers can purchase and prepare on-site, so we investigate the pantry. Unlike many Massiv Trail hikers who travel from hut to hut, we’re carrying a tent and enough supplies to camp independently. The hut system certainly sounds comfortable, but I’d find it stressful to depend on reaching the next hut every day. Bad weather, an injury, or sheer exhaustion could easily throw off our plans. I much prefer the flexibility of stopping whenever we want to.

I’m feeling weak, tired, and unusually cold today. My body apparently hasn’t realized that we’re not starving. In fact, we’ve been feeding it an impressive amount of real food lately. Fortunately, the sun comes out, and the temperatures begin to rise. Then we reach the road and Kyrkjestølen, with a small café that has pizza. The legend is true.

We order two pizzas and sodas and even get to sit outside in the sunshine. Our shoes, socks, and tent are spread out to dry while we charge our electronics inside. A few days ago, we spotted two hikers carrying solar panels on their backpacks. Given the Norwegian weather we’ve experienced so far, I’m beginning to question the wisdom of that particular setup.

Soon, three other Massiv Trail hikers arrive: Sally from Oregon and Matthias and Mark from Germany. We’ve already heard about Sally from Lizette and Iris. At the beginning of her hike, she spent 36 hours trapped in her tent by relentless rain. Ever since, we’ve been calling her 36-Hour Sally. Naturally, all three order pizza, too. It turns out they heard the same glowing recommendation from Big Red and Waffle that we did yesterday. Apparently, the Massiv Trail grapevine works remarkably well.

We reluctantly leave our sunny spot and continue toward Sulebu, another self-service hut. The trail climbs through a short stretch of forest before returning to open mountain terrain dotted with lakes and rivers. Just before reaching the hut, we spot two reindeer beside a small lake: a mother and her calf. Their reflections shimmer in the still water as the youngster hops around. Their coats look as though someone sprinkled cocoa powder into a glass of milk and forgot to stir.

At Sulebu, we make ourselves some tea and sign the guestbook before heading out again. We want to cover another couple of miles before the next major climb, but finding a suitable campsite proves difficult. Strong winds sweep across the exposed landscape, and there’s little shelter.

Eventually, we find a spot among some rocks and pitch our tent. Unfortunately, the ground and limited space make it impossible to stake everything out properly. So we take it down again. Nearby, we find a second option. The ground is slightly boggy, but there’s more room and better protection from the wind. Whether this spot is actually any better remains to be seen. At least the view is spectacular. Lake Sulevatnet stretches out in front of us, with towering, snow-covered mountains rising in the distance.

2 miles past Sulebu to 1.6 miles before Bjordalsbu 16.2 miles / 4,101 ft elevation gain / 8 hrs

The day begins with an unfamiliar sensation: sunshine warming our tent. No rain, no wet tent, just blue skies. I could get used to this. Shortly after setting off, we spot a large herd of reindeer, around 50 animals moving together across the landscape. Later, we see a ptarmigan, too. It’s a magical morning.

After days of cold, gray, rainy weather, the landscape looks completely transformed. The sky is blue, the light is beautiful, and for once, we can actually see the mountains instead of watching them disappear into the clouds. We keep stopping to look around and simply marvel at the scenery. We’re not making particularly fast progress, but today, that doesn’t matter. Two more reindeer approach us, coming unusually close. For a while, we just stand there, regarding each other with equal curiosity.

The trail becomes increasingly overgrown, and we fight our way through the vegetation toward a road. There’s a lodge nearby, raising our hopes for another succulent meal, but a sign informs us that it’s only open to overnight guests. Disappointed, we continue. Apparently, we can’t rely on being fed every day on the Massiv Trail.

We follow a gravel road uphill before turning onto a steep hiking trail. Under the warm sun, we work up quite a sweat. By now, it’s becoming increasingly obvious that we could really use a shower.

As we climb along the edge of a gorge, three sheep watch us from the opposite side. The terrain levels out shortly before the top. By the time we reach the next lake, the sun has disappeared behind the clouds, and the difference is immediate. Just moments ago, we were hot. Now, we’re instantly cold. Any thoughts of taking a dip in one of the many lakes vanish instantly.

Then an old acquaintance returns: Boulder fields. Bjordalsbu is only about two miles away, but on this kind of terrain, two miles can feel like an eternity. At least there’s more wildlife to distract us. An entire group of ptarmigan, probably around ten birds, is wandering among the rocks.

By now, though, we’re tired. Our legs and feet hurt, and we’re hungry. When we discover an unexpected flat spot among the boulders, we don’t hesitate. Bjordalsbu can wait until tomorrow.

For once, we’ve managed to keep our feet relatively dry today. There were still a few boggy sections and river crossings, of course, but most of the time, we could hop from rock to rock without completely soaking our shoes. That doesn’t change the fact that our socks now smell like wet dogs. After being soaked and dried countless times over the past week, they’ve developed a personality of their own. The rest of us aren’t doing much better. My hair is tangled and matted, our clothes have seen better days, and we could both use a thorough cleaning.

Inside the tent, I give myself a quick wipe-down with a little water. That’s as good as hygiene gets today, but after a day like this, I can live with that. For the first time since starting the Massiv Trail, we’ve enjoyed an entire day of good weather. Blue skies, spectacular mountain scenery, reindeer, and ptarmigan. Skarvheimen is treating us well.

1.6 miles before Bjordalsbu to Austre Volavatnet (3.5 miles before Kongshelleren) 17.8 miles / 4,396 ft elevation gain / 8.5 hrs

We start the morning by navigating yet another boulder field. It’s much easier going than yesterday. Either the terrain has improved, or our legs are simply less tired.

The morning sunshine quickly warms us up as we climb toward Bjordalsbu. When we reach the hut, the smell of pancakes drifts through the windows. On the porch, we meet a Norwegian hiker. When Ryan mentions slipping twice on wet rocks a few days ago, the man informs us that hiking boots would obviously be better for that, eyeing our trail runners. We decide not to pursue the subject. Instead, we somehow end up discussing Henrik Ibsen. When I mention hiking the famous Besseggen ridge years ago, he tells us that its fame largely comes from Ibsen’s Peer Gynt, in which the title character claims to have ridden a reindeer buck along the ridge.

Leaving Bjordalsbu behind, we watch the sun disappear behind the clouds. Within minutes, the temperature drops dramatically. Sunshine and no sunshine feel like two entirely different worlds out here. Without it, the landscape becomes harsh and unwelcoming, and all we want to do is keep moving. When the sun returns, the lakes sparkle, the green hillsides glow, and we can’t stop admiring the scenery. Both have their own beauty, but only one makes us want to linger.

Today, everything seems to involve wool. White clouds drift overhead, cotton grass covers the landscape, and sheep graze between the rocks. We also spot another group of ptarmigan, wild geese flying overhead, and a bird of prey.

The trail gradually disappears among the rocks again, leaving me constantly scanning the landscape for the next red T. Eventually, we descend into a valley where an enormous waterfall tumbles down from a glacier. We follow the river past several lakes until we reach Iungsdalshytta.

Time for a late lunch! We order soda and open-faced sandwiches topped with reindeer patties. We also pick up chocolate and sour cream and lingonberry potato chips, a surprisingly delicious combination. Like every Norwegian mountain hut we’ve visited so far, Iungsdalshytta is incredibly cozy. Through the windows, we look out over the lake while cows graze peacefully along its shore. We also meet Jessi from Canada, who’s hiking the Massiv Trail with a tent, just like us.

We drag ourselves away from the warm hut and tackle the next climb, fueled by the power of reindeer. Then it starts raining, even turning into hail.  But we can see sunshine in the distance, so we push through the cold rain to make it through and walk ourselves dry afterward. It works. By the time we approach the top of the climb, the sun is shining again, illuminating tiny raindrops still hanging in the air. Then two rainbows appear over the mountains.

After another stretch of boulder hopping, we descend toward Lake Austre Volavatnet, where we find a suitable campsite. We’d expected it to be warmer down here, but somehow it feels even colder.

Not far from our tent, a group of sheep gathers for the night. Their bleating and the ringing of their bells provide our evening soundtrack. We’ve found our neighbors. And we’ve reached another milestone: we’re halfway through the Massiv Trail!

About 112 miles behind us, and roughly the same distance still ahead. Supposedly, the second half will be considerably easier and faster, especially once we reach the Hardangervidda. We’ll see about that.

Austre Volavatnet to Omnsvatnet 15.5 miles / 2,854 ft elevation gain / 7 hrs

Our sheep neighbors watch us pack up in the morning, chewing contentedly. It’s not quite as cold today, but crawling out of our sleeping bags exposes us to another unpleasant reality: our own smell. After nine days of sweating, getting soaked, and drying out again, our socks have practically developed the ability to stand on their own. Every night, we marinate in our juices inside our sleeping bags. We desperately need a shower.

First, though, there’s more hiking to do. Apparently, the DNT and I have slightly different definitions of “easy walking,” which is how this section was described. Around the self-service hut at Kongshelleren, the familiar boulder fields return. We balance across rocks and hop from boulder to boulder, making considerably slower progress than expected. At least the weather is beautiful. Big, fluffy clouds drift across the blue sky, reflecting in the mountain lakes.

In the afternoon, we reach Geiterygghytta and stop for a late lunch. Sandwiches, followed by Norwegian waffles with strawberry jam and sour cream. Outside the lodge, several chickens wander around. Their legs and feet are covered in thick feathers, making them look as though they’re wearing tiny pants. Watching them run is particularly entertaining.

Waffles and chicken with pants at Geiterygghytta

Leaving Geiterygghytta, we take advantage of the nearby lake for a much-needed wash. Mine is extremely brief because the water is freezing, but at least I feel slightly less offensive to the people around me. When you can no longer stand your own smell, it’s probably time to do something about it.

We’re approaching Finse and the railway line, which means we can take a train to Geilo, a proper town with supermarkets, showers, and all the other luxuries that are becoming increasingly tempting. Until now, we haven’t been sure whether we actually want to interrupt our hike. But this morning, my right knee and left foot made a pretty convincing case for a break. Combined with our questionable hygiene, the decision is easy. It’s time for a zero. Rain is also forecast for Friday, making the timing almost perfect. As soon as we have cell service, I book two nights in the ski resort town of Geilo.

We climb past waterfalls and mountain lakes, even spotting a small ice cave beside a river. By evening, we reach a large lake. The low sun sparkles on the water, and a few remnants of glaciers cling to the mountains on the opposite shore. We decide to stay.

Usually, our evenings follow a familiar routine: we hike until we’re tired, find a reasonably flat spot sheltered from the wind, and disappear into the tent as quickly as possible because it’s already cold. Tonight is different. For once, we’re choosing our campsite for more than practical reasons.

We cook dinner outside in the sunshine. Afterward, we simply sit there, eating hazelnut chocolate and gazing across the lake toward the mountains. My eyes keep returning to the small glaciers still clinging to the rocks high above us, and I can’t help wondering how much longer they’ll be there.

Tomorrow, we’ll reach Finse and take the train to Geilo for our zero before continuing across the Hardangervidda. The landscape is already beginning to change. Our daily elevation gain is decreasing, and the mountains around us are becoming gentler.

Omnsvatnet to Finse 6.2 miles / 1,378 ft elevation gain / 3 hrs

Last night, the stars sparkled above our tent, with the Milky Way stretching across the sky. This morning, the sky is still perfectly clear. The sun slowly rises above the mountains, there’s not a breath of wind, and the lake in front of us has turned into a giant mirror. It’s freezing cold but stunning.

We’re in no hurry today. Finse is only six miles away, so I crawl out of my sleeping bag to take a few photos of the perfectly still lake before retreating into the tent again. Meanwhile, Ryan is already working on our grocery list. After ten days on the trail without a proper resupply, we’re getting increasingly excited about our upcoming zero in Geilo. The snack list, in particular, keeps growing.

Eventually, it gets warm enough to start hiking. The sun warms my back as we tackle the first climb. Escaping the hot summer in southern Germany for Norway’s cool mountains seemed like a great idea, but after several days of barely seeing the sun, we’ve developed a whole new appreciation for it.

We pass another perfectly still mountain lake. Everything is quiet. No people, just the occasional sheep wandering among the mountains. Before descending to Finse, we decide to make a detour to the summit of Sankt Pål. We leave the trail and climb cross-country to the top, where we’re rewarded with a spectacular 360-degree panorama of glaciers, lakes, and mountains.

Back on the trail, we descend toward Klemsbu, where the landscape begins to change. On one side lies the barren, gray mountain terrain we’ve spent the past several days crossing. On the other, green hillsides covered in grass and moss begin to reappear.

As we approach Finse, the trail becomes increasingly busy. More and more hikers pass us, and the solitude of the past few days slowly fades away. Then Finse comes into view. We’ve reached the end of our journey through Skarvheimen. After ten days on the Massiv Trail, we board a train to Geilo for a well-deserved break.

The ride takes just 36 minutes, but the scenery changes rapidly outside the window. We pass lakes and rivers, trees become increasingly common, and with the sun shining, it instantly feels like summer again.

Our first stop in Geilo is a café. The focaccia sandwiches on display look almost absurdly delicious after ten days in the mountains. We order two. At the hotel, our next priority is laundry. We wash our clothes in the bathtub, although our mud-encrusted socks prove particularly challenging. I lose count of how many times we have to change the water before it finally starts running somewhat clear.

We spend the rest of the day and the following day mostly resting and eating. We barely manage a short walk around Geilo, and that’s perfectly fine.

Finse to Drageidfjorden 11.5 miles / 2,264 ft elevation gain / 4.5 hrs

After a relaxing zero in Geilo, we take the train back to Finse. It’s Saturday, the weather is good by Norwegian standards, and plenty of people are out enjoying it. The gravel road between Finse and Haugastøl seems particularly popular with cyclists, and we pass quite a few hikers, too.

We leave Finse behind and follow the lake, crossing a dam and several slightly wobbly suspension bridges. It feels good to be back out here. The landscape is vast, quiet, and peaceful. Cold, yes, but I’ll take that over the bustle of town any day.

We’re now on the Hardangervidda, Europe’s largest mountain plateau. The trail winds through a beautiful landscape dotted with lakes and is mostly easy walking. Only the occasional rocky section or short climb slows us down. After the challenging terrain of the past ten days, it’s a welcome change.

By evening, it starts raining. When we find a flat spot for our tent, we don’t hesitate to call it a day. Just before 8 pm, a Norwegian couple appears, hurrying down the hillside, shouting something back and forth in Norwegian. I don’t understand a word, but I imagine their conversation goes something like this:

Her, several yards ahead: “Hurry up slowpoke!” Him: “I’m doing my best!” Her: “Well, you need to do better if you don’t want us to miss dinner!”

Krækkja hut is still 3.4 miles away. Godspeed, my friends.

Drageidfjorden to 4.6 miles before Sandhaug 23.6 miles / 2,920 ft elevation gain / 9 hrs

After another starry night, we wake up to brilliant blue skies. The sun is shining, and Norway is being kind to us lately. We’re determined to make the most of it.

With every mile, the hiking gets easier. We take one last look back at the enormous Hardangerjøkulen glacier before the landscape gradually becomes flatter, greener, and less rocky. Exactly what we were promised about the Hardangervidda.

Along the way, we meet Guttorm, a 26-year-old Norwegian on his first long solo hike. He immediately wins Ryan over by offering us coffee from his espresso maker. That explains his enormous backpack, at least. It doesn’t seem to slow him down much. He’s recently been hiking around 25 miles a day and, despite starting three days after us, has caught up. Not bad.

We reach Stigstuv, where disaster strikes. A sign announces that the hut has been closed since August 16. We’d been dreaming of waffles, and now, our dreams are shattered. When I break the news to Guttorm, he initially thinks I’m joking, but I don’t joke about waffles.

We take a break together. Guttorm prepares a Norwegian-style meat-and-potato meal on his enormous stove while we settle for snacks. When we ask what kind of water-purification system he’s using, his answer is simple: “My stomach.” Fair enough. This is Norway, after all.

As we keep walking, several people coming from the opposite direction ask whether we’ve spotted any wild reindeer. We haven’t. Until now, we didn’t even realize this was a particularly good area for them. A little later, we encounter men carrying rifles and wearing hunting clothes. Evidently, reindeer hunting season has just begun. If I were a reindeer, I’d probably be hiding somewhere other than the wide-open Hardangervidda right now, too.

We reach a gravel road and a parking area near Lake Langhølen, and from here on the trail becomes ridiculously easy. Flat, smooth, and barely a rock in sight. After our first week on the Massiv Trail, it feels like a highway.  If you need or want to push your pace, this is it.

There’s just one problem with the wide-open landscape: the wind. With no trees or mountains to slow it down, it sweeps across the plateau completely unhindered. The scenery reminds me of the Great Divide Basin in Wyoming. The Hardangervidda certainly isn’t a desert, but the feeling is similar: endless open space, low vegetation, and nowhere to hide from the wind. In winter, this plateau actually becomes something of a white desert, with conditions so harsh that polar explorers like Roald Amundsen used it to prepare for their expeditions.

We get a small taste of that exposure when we start looking for a campsite. We investigate several spots behind large rocks, but no matter which side we stand on, the wind somehow finds us. Eventually, we give up and pitch the tent. There’s probably no escaping it out here anyway. That doesn’t stop Ryan from complaining extensively about the wind. Of course, he’ll be the first to put in his earplugs and fall peacefully asleep. And as it turns out, the wind actually dies down considerably. We end up having a remarkably quiet night.

Moonrise and campsite on the Hardangervidda

4.6 miles before Sandhaug to Holken (1.2 miles before Litlos) 19.1 miles / 2,559 ft elevation gain / 7.5 hrs

The Hardangervidda is shrouded in fog this morning, but the sun is already fighting its way through. Ahead of us, pale white arcs appear in the mist. Fogbows! They form much like rainbows when sunlight interacts with tiny water droplets. But because fog droplets are much smaller than raindrops, the colors overlap, and the resulting bow appears almost entirely white.

Countless tiny droplets cling to the grasses and low vegetation, sparkling like strings of pearls in the morning light. The landscape feels mystical, entirely different from anything we’ve seen on the Massiv Trail so far. I love it. As we climb higher, the sun gradually wins its battle against the fog. The mist disappears almost entirely, leaving another beautiful blue sky above us.

At Sandhaug, we stop for a break and finally get the waffles we were denied at Stigstuv yesterday. Justice has been served. Yesterday’s vast, flat plains give way to rolling hills and mountains. Nothing nearly as high or rugged as the terrain we encountered during our first week, but beautiful nonetheless.

And there are lakes everywhere. Beside one of them, we meet Emily from Massachusetts. A small trail community is beginning to form along the Massiv Trail. There aren’t many people hiking the entire route, yet somehow we’ve all heard about one another before we even meet. As we approach, Emily asks, “Are you the American?” She’s been talking to Guttorm, whom we’ve started calling Goose because Ryan can’t pronounce his name.

Emily has been living in Norway for six years and now calls Bergen home. She spent last night with a group of Norwegian reindeer hunters and learned quite a bit about their hunting practices. Apparently, some hunters even hire helicopters to transport their reindeer out of the Hardangervidda, at a cost of around 30,000 Norwegian kroner. Others use tractors, as we learned yesterday.

After Emily continues on, we take over her lakeside spot for lunch and jump into the water for a quick swim. It’s still cold, but today is the warmest day we’ve experienced on the Massiv Trail so far. For the rest of the afternoon, I can actually hike in shorts and a T-shirt. I even work up a sweat on the climbs. After the cold, wet beginning of our hike, this feels incredible.

We pass lake after lake, although the trail isn’t quite as easy as yesterday. After nearly 20 miles, our legs and feet are definitely starting to complain. Near the top of our final climb, just before the trail descends toward Litlos, we find a beautiful spot for our tent. Cotton grass grows beside us, the rocky summit of Holken rises nearby, and patches of snow still cling to the distant mountains. A few sheep wander past; the sun shines on our faces, and there’s nowhere I’d rather be.

The sun disappears behind the mountain. The sky turns peach-colored, the clouds glow pink, and the temperature slowly begins to drop. Another beautiful day on the Hardangervidda comes to an end.

Holken to Holmasjøen 18.5 miles / 3,215 ft elevation gain / 7.5 hrs

Another morning, another blue sky. Streams sparkle in the sunlight, and the fluffy white heads of cotton grass stand out against the green landscape. We’re slowly getting used to this glorious weather.

We descend toward Litlos, a mountain hut sitting right beside a large lake. Unfortunately, it’s still too early for waffles, so we keep moving. Before leaving, though, we spot Guttorm’s broken trekking poles and his makeshift wooden walking stick leaning against the hut. He must still be inside.

From Litlos, we follow the lakeshore, cross a slightly wobbly but very welcome bridge, and climb back into the mountains. The scenery is noticeably greener than it was a few days ago, but this part of the Hardangervidda is anything but flat or monotonous. The landscape remains wonderfully varied. Behind every hill, another lake appears, surrounded by green slopes and rocky mountains. We even get to scramble up a rocky gully filled with large boulders. Just in case we were getting bored.

Along the way, we meet two German hikers, giving Ryan an opportunity to practice his German. They’re doing a short loop from Litlos to Hellevassbu and back. Not long afterward, we reach Hellevassbu. It’s the last self-service hut on our route, beautifully situated beside yet another lake. We admire the setting but keep hiking.

Ryan takes full advantage of all this water, going for two swims in the freezing lakes. I manage one. Although calling it a swim is generous. At these temperatures, briefly getting in and splashing some water over myself is about all I can handle.

By evening, we find a campsite beside Holmasjøen, just 6.2 miles from Haukeliseter. Tomorrow will be our final day on the Massiv Trail. We have a bus to Oslo to catch at 1:15 pm, and there is only a short stretch of hiking left. After nearly two weeks in Norway’s mountains, we’re almost at the finish line.

Holmasjøen to Haukeliseter 6.6 miles / 984 ft elevation gain / 3 hrs

Just over six miles separate us from the end of the Massiv Trail, and Norway has saved some spectacular scenery for our final day. Another cloudless blue sky stretches above us, with lakes, mountains, and wide-open plateaus ahead. After the cold, rainy beginning of our hike, we could hardly have imagined such a beautiful farewell.

On our first climb, we look back toward Lake Holmasjøen. Small rocky islands dot the deep blue water, surrounded by mountains

Shortly afterward, we reach another large lake, where quite a few tents are scattered along the shore. Apparently, we’re early birds by Norwegian hiking standards. And that’s saying something, considering we didn’t even start hiking until around 8 am. When we pass the campsites an hour later, most people are still inside their tents.

After another climb, an enormous lake comes into view: Ståvatn. Somewhere along its shore lies Haukeliseter, the finish line of the Massiv Trail. We can’t see the lodge yet, though. Another 1.2 miles to go.

The trail now rolls gently across a plateau, passing the familiar combination of sheep, rocks, mountain lakes, and cotton grass. The landscape looks like perfect marmot habitat, which makes me wonder why Norway doesn’t have any. Apparently, marmots never made it back to Norway after the last Ice Age. Lemmings are among the few rodents we might encounter at these elevations.

Over the past two weeks, we’ve spotted reindeer, hares, falcons, and ptarmigan, along with those unfortunate dead lemmings on the Fannaråkbreen glacier. Norway’s mountains are also home to foxes and the elusive wolverine, while moose generally prefer forested areas. No marmots, though.

Then Haukeliseter appears below us. The large mountain lodge sits beside the road, and one last steep descent takes us down to the finish line. Looking at the slope, I can’t help thinking about the hikers starting the Massiv Trail in the opposite direction. That’s quite a climb to begin your adventure with.

And just like that, we’re done. 224 miles through Norway’s mountains. An almost endless combination of rocks, water, and mountains. Naturally, we celebrate with waffles and sodas. Then we discover the lunch buffet and pile our plates with meatballs, potatoes, and pea stew. I eat until my stomach hurts. At 1:15 pm, we board the bus to Oslo. And that’s a wrap on the Massiv Trail.
