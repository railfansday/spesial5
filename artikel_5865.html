It is now common to lament a crisis in the system of democracy. Confidence in public institutions has declined, experts are dismissed, and politics is becoming a contest over not only values and interests but reality itself.

These trends have come alongside a corresponding rise in attempts to explain this phenomenon, ranging from feckless leadership and rising inequality to globalization and economic shocks. While those are all factors, a more compelling explanation could lie in how the digital revolution has undermined trust in facts, governments, and democracy itself.

To be clear, falsehoods and disinformation are not new. Manipulating information has long been a tool of statecraft. The ancient Indian political treatise, the Arthashastra, advised rulers to spread rumors to control public opinion. But while modern media has vastly expanded the scale, scope, and reach of information, it has also turbocharged the dissemination of propaganda and misinformation.

This degradation of informational systems is precipitating a crisis of legitimacy in the core institutions of democracies. Meanwhile, there is a growing mismatch between inherited democratic institutions and our new social realities. In some countries, the central problem is the decay of once-strong mass intermediaries such as political parties, unions, a free press, and civic organizations. In others, mass intermediaries never achieved the authority imagined in the classic democratic model. In others, the state itself remains too uneven or predatory to serve as a reliable object of trust. But in all cases, democracies face a similar problem: how to make collective self-government credible when citizens no longer accept inherited intermediaries as authoritative or trustworthy.

What is the solution? There is no easy answer, but a path to restoring faith in democracy can only begin by making the democratic process more observable, relational, and capable. And this process must take place first at the local level, building from the bottom up—just as democracy was designed to do.

Trust—among citizens and between citizens and their governments—is the bedrock of democracy. Trust represents a pact between the trustor—the party extending the trust—and the trustee, or the party being trusted. Such a transaction requires a degree of vulnerability on the part of the trustor. Trust is a psychological condition that allows the trustor to set aside a purely rational calculus and make a leap of faith toward positive expectations about the trustee.

We can distinguish between two types of trust: cognition-based and affect-based. The former is an evidence-driven process by which individuals use updated information to judge the reliability, character, and ability of the trustee. Affect-based trust, in contrast, underpins social relationships and is based on emotions of care and concern for others.

In politics, cognitive trust is based on calculated assessments of the competence of politicians and governments based on tangible metrics such as economic growth, inflation, and crime. To feel this form of trust, voters need to have access to information they find reliable and the bandwidth to process it. This is decreasingly the case.

What is left is affective trust. This is driven by emotional bonds and shared identities and values. Affective judgments lead citizens to base their voting decisions on the person rather than the policies, on feelings rather than facts. This matters because new media—especially social media—is particularly adept at amplifying emotionally charged discourse. Such narratives are stickier than verifiable facts, and their participatory nature converts audiences from passive consumers to active co-authors churning out their own alternative narratives. These dynamics become even more febrile in an era of uncertainty. The result is emotionally driven discourse increasingly shaping public opinion, with facts becoming collateral damage.

Voters repose both cognition-based and affect-based trust in politicians and the state. But to the extent the information environment deteriorates—what Steve Bannon, U.S. President Donald Trump’s former advisor, once referred to as “flooding the zone”—the attention economy constricts cognitive bandwidth. The result, as Herbert Simon famously noted, is a “poverty of attention.” The arc of trust then bends toward affective trust. Emotional attachments to personalities acquire greater salience. This may explain, at least partly, the current wave of populism.

In 2023, 44 percent of citizens surveyed by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development reported no or low trust in their governments. In 2025, in the United States, trust in government fell to near its lowest recorded level, with just 17 percent of Americans saying they trusted the federal government to do what is right.

Democracy depends on trust at scale. A resident of New Jersey will rarely meet an Alaskan. In the modern era, their sense of collective fate is bound together by shared sources of authority, such as official documents, and means of disseminating commonly trusted information, such as news broadcasts. If achieving broad impersonal trust was one of the unsung accomplishments of the modern state, this was made possible by mass institutions such as unions, parties, and a reliable media ecosystem.

Both mass institutions and impersonal trust are deteriorating. Given the structure of the current information environment, these are unlikely to be revived. As mass trust has fragmented, particularized trust—in personalities, partisan leaders, or online communities—seems to be on the rise. There is no source of certification that is accepted at scale.

Misinformation is hard to defeat with truth alone; it requires truth to be tethered to a trusted authority transmitting the message. As trust has fragmented, attacking misinformation has become more complicated, requiring more than mere correction. When societies no longer trust a single common referee, it is hard to achieve broad acceptance of the contest they adjudicate.

Where do we go from here? We believe that rebuilding citizens’ trust in their governments will increasingly require the lived experience of national polities to occur at smaller scales where citizens can see public purpose enacted. Such public policy would be made not only in legislatures and ministries but also in the everyday discretion of frontline workers.

This is one reason that street-level bureaucracy matters so much. Citizens rarely encounter a national government. Instead, their primary encounters are with the local state—a teacher, a social worker, a police officer, a sanitation worker, a building inspector, a nurse, a benefits caseworker, a public meeting, a pothole crew, a school board, or a public transport employee. These encounters represent democracy’s front line. They are the key to a more capable state, which in turn boosts the state’s legitimacy and builds trust.

Smaller administrative units are more conducive for building trust because they can collectively draw on tacit knowledge and shared networks, repeat interactions, and direct observation. Engagement at this local level with the broader state can build relational state capacity—reciprocal relationships where both parties feel seen and understood by the other. Local units are primary sites of trust formation, handling decisions that depend heavily on contextual knowledge and observable accountability. Regional or national government can support the local, but in this model, trust ultimately flows up from the local rather than down from a pinnacle source, be that a Walter Cronkite, the United Nations, or a national leader.

Three principles are fundamental to creating this new reality. First, democracy must become more observable. Currently, citizens are asked to trust many things they cannot personally experience, such as central bank decisions, vaccine approvals, or election integrity. Some of this is unavoidable. A meeting to set a central bank’s interest rate will inevitably be an elite club. But many of these things don’t need to be opaque. Election infrastructure is needs greater transparency, incorporating community at the local level; many more opportunities for sustained citizen interaction, personal observation, and collective public judgment can and should be found. Citizens must be able to connect what they observe to public purpose. More quotidian, regular interaction with the state—not more online portals or structured public hearings—will help democracies build trust. Public work should be organized so that its progress, purpose, and challenges are intelligible to everyday citizens.

Second, democracy must become more relational. Trust will not emerge from public information messaging, but rather from repeated interactions where people can update their beliefs about one another. Mutual respect is key to interactions that build trust and expand the definition of community. Citizens do not need more exposure to opposing views; they need structured settings in which disagreement is part of a collective effort to constructively govern. Schools, juries, unions, and civic organizations show us the potential of these types of forums. Citizens must have the opportunity to assess whether officials listen, keep commitments, and exercise judgment with care. Officials, in turn, need to gain knowledge about citizens that administrative categories alone cannot supply. Building local trust requires rethinking how we create space for relationship building and empowerment of front-line workers. It also requires institutions that provide attentive treatment even to those who arrive without influence or connections.

Third, democracy must become more capable. The administrative state is the front door of democracy; it cannot be an afterthought. Simply put, the state must be able to deliver on its promises. Capability is not merely technical prowess, however. Complex collective problems often require multiple centers of decision-making, local knowledge, monitoring, and rule adaptation, as well as coproduction between the state and society. Front-line workers need not only have skills but also authority, organizational support, and flexibility for the state to become more capable.

It is tempting to respond to malfeasance and unequal treatment with layers of compliance such as rules, audits, targets, and procedural safeguards. Some are necessary. But when we set things up so bad that actions can never happen again, as contrite managers often say, we consign ourselves to a suboptimal, constrained state. Too much regulation makes public servants less able to solve problems or act in ways that citizens recognize as responsive. A state that exercises judgment capriciously and arbitrarily will not earn citizens’ trust, but neither will a state that cannot exercise judgment at all.

These principles are meant to complement rather than substitute for national and transnational units of government. The national unit cannot be a mere composite of local experience. States will need to adapt to the changing architecture of trust, which will require rethinking accountability. A focus on thin metrics will need to be replaced with a broader collective justification. That channel of accountability should, whenever possible, run from those engaging with citizens to those citizens themselves, rather than up the hierarchy to supervisors in a distant office. National institutions can rebuild trust through more reliable local encounters that their resources and coordination make possible.

However, these principles will have to confront the elephant in the room—technology. In recent years, new technologies, first digital and now artificial intelligence, have been fanning the fires of mistrust. National governments will especially need to play an active role in facilitating robust safety standards for new technologies. It will be exceedingly difficult to rebuild trust without first strengthening faith in these technologies themselves. Historically, technology standards have been built by professionals within the sector—not through government regulation—recognizing that guardrails are fundamental to building trust and thereby the long-term prospects of the technology. After the internet was established, the refusal of the tech giants to build strong universal safety standards sowed the very seeds of mistrust that are now coming back to haunt them. If this occurs with artificial intelligence, a much more consequential technology, the fallout will be even more dire.

Democracy first flourished in settings where citizens could assemble face to face. Mass institutions and new technologies allowed democracy to scale. One consequence was that the primacy of the local as the locus of trust, and ultimately where democracy thrived, withered. More recently, the deluge of information has also vastly increased the speed and scope of misinformation. But going forward, information will be increasingly produced—and consumed—by machines. The ructions from this shift will profoundly change the informational landscape and, with it, the cognitive foundations of democracy.

Rebuilding and nourishing the local roots of democracy should become an urgent imperative for societies, not simply in a geographically proximate sense, but incorporating any forum where members come to accept and trust one another. For governments, the lesson is not to control or dictate to these communities, but to scaffold and connect them. This, in turn, requires making the state more capable, more relational, and more observable. Governments will also have to coordinate to build stronger safety standards for the technologies that are enmeshed in the burgeoning labyrinths of mistrust.

Democracy will only be rebuilt if the corrosiveness of mistrust is reversed. We must accept what we cannot change—that the structure of information has drastically changed, and there will be no innovation that yields a rebirth of mass, impersonal democracy. The future of democratic trust depends not on our screens but on our neighborhoods. If we can no longer trust from the top down, then we must rebuild it from the bottom up.
