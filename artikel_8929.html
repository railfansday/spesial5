He Quietly Became America’s Third-Fastest Marathoner—Ever. Who the Heck Is Zouhair Talbi?

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

At the Houston Marathon, Talbi etched his name onto the U.S. all-time list. His wife, Elizabeth, had the race of her life, too.

Editor’s note: This story was originally published on January 16, 2026.

It’s been a long and winding journey for Zouhair Talbi to realize his American dream, but winning the Houston Marathon in 2:05.45 on January 11 as a U.S. citizen was only part of that dream coming true.

The 31-year-old Moroccan-born runner ran the third-fastest time ever by a U.S. runner on a record-eligible course—behind only American record holder Conner Mantz (2:04:43 in Chicago last fall) and Khalid Khannouchi (2:05:38 in London in 2002)—taking nearly a minute off his personal best set two years earlier in Houston when he ran under his Moroccan citizenship in his lead-up to the Paris Olympics.

But not long after he celebrated at the finish line with an American flag draped over his shoulders, Zouhair relished another milestone moment as his wife, Elizabeth Talbi, 23, also had a breakthrough result in the marathon, placing 10th among women in 2:35:26—a 15-minute personal best from her debut effort in 2024—just 10 months after giving birth to their son, Amir.

Moreover, they each surpassed the qualifying standards for the 2028 U.S. Olympic Trials Marathon, which means they’ll both be on the starting line of the races that will determine who will make the U.S. team for the Los Angeles Olympics. The race will either be in Phoenix or St. Louis, and it is tentatively scheduled for the weekend of March 25-26, 2028. In other words, after August 2027, when Zouhair becomes eligible to officially compete for the U.S. in international races and national team qualifying events.

Best of all, they were also able to celebrate their success with Amir, who was cared for during the race by Elizabeth’s sister, Kaitlyn, who lives in Houston.

“It was a special day,” Zouhair said. “It was the first time I was listed as a U.S. runner with the American flag next to my name. It was something I had always dreamed of when I thought about coming to America. And to have Elizabeth run so well, too, really made it a special day for both of us.”

Zouhair discovered his talent as a teen growing up in Tighassaline, Khenifra, Morocco, and emerged as one of the top high school runners in the country, eventually placing 14th in the U-20 race at the World Cross Country Championships in Bydgoszcz, Poland, as a 17-year-old in 2013. He continued on an upward trajectory as a college student, running 13:51 in the 5,000 meters as a 19-year-old and earning the silver medal in the event at the World University Games in Seoul, South Korea.

Despite his success, Zouhair says training and racing always seemed to be a constant struggle because of a lack of guidance and marginal support. He dreamed of running in the U.S. collegiate system, but it wasn’t until 2019, at the age of 23, that he enrolled at Florida State University.

That dream was temporarily derailed due to paperwork problems, but he eventually landed at Northwest Kansas Technical College (now Fort Hays Tech Northwest), a small junior college in Goodland, Kansas. Zouhair ran on the team there with some success, but then Covid hit in 2020, wiping out the spring track season and mandating all schoolwork to be done online.

After transferring to Oklahoma City University, things started to look up for Zouhair. Not only did he start running well again under energizing young coaches Matt Aguero and Frezer Legesse, but he also met Elizabeth Heffernan, a runner on the women’s team from Bentonville, Arkansas, who he immediately bonded with over a love of the sport.

Zouhair won the 5,000 at the NAIA indoor championships in 2021 and also anchored the school’s winning distance medley relay, and in the outdoor season lowered his 10,000 personal best to 27:20 by outrunning professional runner Emmauel Bor and top collegiate runners Joe Klecker and Mantz. Elizabeth ran well, too, earning All-American honors with a sixth-place finish in the 1,000 meters at the NAIA indoor championships.

As a senior, Zouhair made a clean sweep of the NAIA indoor championships, winning the mile (4:07.27), 3,000 (8:17.67) and 5,000 (14:28.39) on the same day, then parlayed that training success to a sixth-place finish in the 3,000 at the World Athletics Indoor Championships in Serbia, a result that helped earn him a professional contract with Asics International.

After some good track performances that summer—including a 20th place finish in the 10,000 at the world championships in Eugene, Oregon—Zouhair finished his season with a third-place showing (1:02:15) at the BAA Half Marathon in Boston, which got him thinking about the marathon. More importantly, he and Elizabeth eloped that year and began building a future together.

It just so happens that Zouhair’s coaches in Oklahoma City, Aguero and Legesse, were both former athletes of coach Scott Simmons, who, for the past decade, has headed up the successful American Distance Project (ADP), an elite Colorado Springs training group made up of U.S. runners with international backgrounds.

Many of the ADP runners ran collegiately in the U.S. and gained American citizenship through the U.S. Army, including Kenyan-born Paul Chelimo, a two-time Olympic medalist for the U.S. in the 5,000, Lenny Korir, a 2024 U.S. Olympic marathoner, and Hillary Bor, a 2016 U.S. Olympian in the 3,000-meter steeplechase who made his marathon debut in New York in 2025.

At the recommendation of Aguero and Legesse, Zouhair and his wife moved to Colorado Springs in 2023 and joined the ADP program. Training with so many elite-level runners had an immediate impact, as Zouhair turned in a sterling effort in his debut marathon in Boston that spring, placing fifth in 2:08:35.

Under Simmons, Zouhair began running 100 to 120 miles per week during peak training for the first time, but his quick progression also came from the fast-paced 30K to 40K tempo runs the group does on a weekly basis, Simmons said.

“Those are not long, slow runs,” Simmons said. “They’re marathon specific runs that are quite fast, particularly toward the end, where, in the last 10K or so, they’re really pushing the pace. And that’s all worked out well for Zou. He’s adapted well from the training, and I think he still has a ways to go.”

Continuing his development as a marathoner under Simmons and the ADP, Zouhair ran his way to a 2:06:39 breakthrough win in Houston in January 2024 and another strong showing in Boston (10th, 2:10:45). He competed for Morocco in the marathon at the Paris Olympics, placing 35th overall in 2:11:51, and finished that year with a 2:07:17, third-place effort at the Dongying Marathon in China.

Zouhair admits training with the ADP has made a huge impact, not only in his improvement as a marathoner but also because of the camaraderie among runners who have already realized their dream of running for the U.S.

“I am very, very grateful to have these guys to train with,” Zouhair said from his home in Colorado Springs. “They inspire me, and I think I inspire them as well. We always talk about training as 2:05, 2:04 guys, and we just need the perfect race to do it. I think that brings belief to the whole group. We’re all up to something big, but I can’t do it without them. It requires a whole team working every week with certain goals to hit in workouts, the splits, the mileage, all of that.”

As much as Zouhair has started to become the runner he always dreamed of becoming, running has galvanized his family life, too. After completing her collegiate running career, Elizabeth, with guidance and encouragement from Zouhair, also began training for longer distances and joined him at the race in China, where she placed 11th in 2:50:30 in her marathon debut.

In a case of everything, everywhere all at once, Zouhair began the process of gaining U.S. citizenship in late 2024 through the U.S. Army Reserve program. He completed basic training at Fort Jackson in Columbia, South Carolina, in early 2025 and was sworn in as a U.S. citizen just a few weeks before Elizabeth gave birth to Amir in March.

After six weeks of postpartum rest and recovery, Elizabeth, inspired anew by everything blossoming in their lives, began running again, and then in early May, Zouhair ran his first race as a U.S. citizen and placed fourth in the USATF 25K Championships in Michigan.

With several months of consistent training under her belt, Elizabeth placed third in a half marathon in Boulder in September, just two seconds off her PR. After three months of high-mileage running, Zouhair knew she would have her own breakthrough in Houston, and she did, too.

“I feel like being a mom and being a runner is really hard, but it was the push I needed to become a better runner,” Elizabeth said. “Before that, I liked running and loved that I got to go races and cheer on Zouhair. But something about having my son just kind of changed my mindset. I was like, ‘I gave birth to a whole new life, so maybe I can do what I’ve always wanted to do, which is be an elite athlete.’ And so I just told him pretty quickly after having the baby that I was going to qualify for the Olympic Trials. And he was like, ‘I’ll train you for it. Let's go!’ And that’s pretty much just how that worked.”

The U.S. Olympic Trials are still two years away, but Zouhair and Elizabeth are both inspired like never before. They admit they have their hands full with Amir, but it’s a balancing act they know they can handle, especially with running as a key element to it all. They’ve talked about running a half marathon together in Denver in May as an intermediary goal.

“I think that we just make sacrifices, that’s our life,” Elizabeth said. “I always take the backseat in the training. If Zouhair has a workout plan, I let him do that first thing in the morning, and I watch Amir. And then when he’s done and has time to watch the baby, I do my workout. So I did a lot of solo runs and, if it was dark outside, I did a lot of late night treadmill workouts. I would just put on a playlist and get it done.”

Zouhair said Elizabeth’s motivation and training, which sometimes included doing workouts with the ADP team, as well as the excitement about their young son, was an inspiring part of his buildup to Houston, and made him want to dream bigger.

World Athletics requires a three-year waiting period for transfer of national allegiance, regardless of when citizenship is granted, so Zouhair will officially become eligible to compete for the U.S. in August of next year. That would mean he would be eligible to represent the U.S. at the World Athletics Championships in Beijing, if chosen for the team.

In the meantime, agent Hawi Keflezighi is eyeing a big spring marathon for Zouhair, as well as a new sponsorship, as his Asics International deal expired at the end of 2025. But running sub-2:06 should bring new opportunities for racing and financial support.

“You know everything went really well in Houston,” Zouhair said. “I’ve checked a lot of boxes, for sure, and now I can just focus on the one thing I know, which is training hard and performing at races. The only thing left now is to, as Elizabeth has said, put a big goal out there like she did and to really go for it. I want to put a really big goal out there for me—to become a medalist in the Olympics—and shoot for that and see what that can bring.”

Brian Metzler is a Boulder, Colorado, writer and editor whose work has appeared in Runner’s World, Sports Illustrated, ESPN, Outside, Trail Runner, The Chicago Tribune, and Red Bulletin. He’s a former walk-on college middle-distance runner who has transitioned to trail running and pack burro racing in Colorado.
