Raiders Mailbag: How will Fernando Mendoza look in 2nd career start against Denver Broncos’ defense?

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Fernando Mendoza had an up-and-down performance in his first career start against the Seattle Seahawks last week, and his second start comes against the Denver Broncos' defense, which led the NFL in sacks during the 2024 and 2025 campaigns. So, there's a lot of talk about the Las Vegas Raiders' Week 11 matchup and the rookie quarterback in this week's mailbag. Let's get to it!

A: The Broncos' defense definitely isn't as dominant as we've seen over the last two years, ranking 27th in yards per game allowed (355.0) and 13th in EPA per play (-0.05) allowed, per Sumer Sports. Granted, the latter is just outside the top third of the league, but that's below the standard Denver has set recently. So, I think Mendoza can have more success and should have an easier time moving the ball this Sunday compared to last week.

The biggest area he needs to improve on is handling pressure. Broncos defensive coordinator Vance Joseph is different from Seahawks head coach/defensive play caller Mike Macdonald, in that Macdonald leans more on creating confusion through varying pre-snap looks and simulated pressures (showing blitz but rushing four).

Joseph will line up and play more, letting Denver's excellent pass-rushers pin their ears back and do their jobs. But he isn't afraid to bring the heat either, ranking 11th with a 33.7 percent blitz rate, according to Sumer. Especially after watching last week, I'd expect Joseph to dive deep into the playbook and continue to throw the kitchen sink at the rookie quarterback making his second career start.

So, it's going to be important for Mendoza to stay poised under pressure and be faster at finding his answers/check downs. I have confidence that he'll be able to make an adjustment; poise and toughness in the pocket were two of his biggest strengths last year at Indiana. However, of course, it's a whole different ball game at this level, and he needs to prove it on the field.

The good news is that it should be all downhill from here as far as facing strong defenses with very good pass-rushers for the rest of the season. Well, until the Raiders play the Broncos again in a few weeks, LOL!

A: I'm definitely on board with you that Klint Kubiak has to reverse script and use the passing game to set up the rushing attack. Also, I think you're onto something about that being the strategy last week, where the first two plays of the game were play-action bootlegs. The next four plays were runs (one three-yarder and three four-yarders) to keep the offense on schedule, and then another play-action bootleg.

As you mentioned, Brock Bowers going down changed the strategy, or at least the effectiveness of it. Also, Macdonald started giving Mendoza the confusing pressure looks toward the end of the second quarter, making the problem even worse.

It sounds like Bowers will at least be out there on Sunday (he's currently listed as questionable) to make the Broncos account for him, so I'd expect Kubiak to use a similar strategy. Hopefully, the 'football robot from heaven' is healthy enough to be effective and can stay on the field to give us a full game to see how using the pass to set up the run works with Mendoza behind center.

Q: When you think about QB skill sets and what can or can't be developed effectively, where does pocket presence fall? Not going to lie, Mendoza's pocket presence troubles me a bit going back to Indiana.

A: Mendoza's feel for pressure was/is definitely one of my biggest concerns about his transition to the NFL. Where it can get complicated is that it's tied to his fearlessness under pressure. He's tough as hell and has no problem standing tall and making throws while taking a hit in the pocket. But the downside is that he will stay in the pocket too long and take sacks, some of those being big hits from free rushers. I flinched on both of Devon Witherspoon's sacks last week. LOL!

So, it's one of those things where Fernando is going to have to find the balance between one of his biggest strengths and one of his biggest weaknesses. You don't want him to become timid and turn into a 'Checkdown Charlie'. However, six sacks a game will lead to a short career.

Ultimately, the only way to find the balance is to get live reps in games since the defense isn't hitting the quarterback in practice. Jordan Love had a similar problem when he first became the Packers' starter, taking 29 sacks with a 15.2 percent pressure-to-sack ratio, per Pro Football Focus. In year two, Love's figures went down to 14 and 8.9 percent, and then 21 and 10.9 percent last season.

Granted, this season he's back up to 25 and 15.8 percent, but 12 sacks came in two games against the Vikings. That's just par for the course against Brian Flores, especially with how that defense has been playing this year. I'd be willing to bet Love's pressure-to-sack percentage comes down by the end of the year.

In other words, it's something that can be improved. Mendoza just has to get more reps and make an adjustment to have a better feel for pressure. Personally, I think it's easier to coach a quarterback to get the ball out faster than it is to force a scared quarterback to be braver in the pocket. One is a timing issue, and the other requires a rewiring of the brain, so to speak.

Q: It might be way too early for this question: Xavier Gilliam, Zereoue Williams, Jackson Harris, and Will Echoles are there for a first-round pick. What's your choice? The team needs WR and IDL talent badly, but may also need to look at a future replacement for Kolton Miller soon.

A: You're scratching the surface on an offseason topic that will likely be heavily talked about: Is it time to move on from Miller?

I wouldn't say he's been bad by any means, but the 31-year-old has certainly regressed this season to the point where finding a long-term blind-side protector will be on the table. So, someone like Williams or Jordan Seaton (if the Raiders are picking high enough) could be an option in the first round. However, right now, I'm willing to give Miller the rest of the campaign before closing the book on him and creating a new need in Las Vegas' trenches.

On that note, the Raiders need to get better on both sides of the trenches before worrying about adding a wide receiver, in my opinion. The only caveat to that would be if they're in range for Jeremiah Smith (highly unlikely) or Charlie Becker (fairly unlikely). Outside of those two, there isn't a receiver in this class who stands out enough to push me away from a lineman. I think there should be good/better value at wide receiver in the second round this year.

So, it comes down to Gilliam or Echoles. To be honest, I'd be pretty happy with either, but I lean toward Echoles. Gilliam has undoubtedly been the better player this year and has the potential to be an impact player against the run and as an interior pass-rusher. However, he is a one-year wonder, so Echoles has the bigger body of work, and I trust him against the run more.

The Ole Miss product definitely has to be more consistent as a pass-rusher, but the flashes are there to show some potential to grow in that department. But, again, I'd be pretty happy if the Raiders managed to land either of those defensive tackles.

Q: What is the difference between this year's Ashton Jeanty and last year's Ashton Jeanty?

A: To me, Jeanty doesn't have the same lateral movement skills and shiftiness that he showed as a rookie. Granted, those were never his biggest strengths, but I feel like his jump cuts and ability to make defenders miss have regressed this season. The straight-line speed and burst are still there, as seen in last week's 38-yard catch. However, Pro Football Focus had him with 61 missed tackles forced in 17 games last year (about 3.6 a game), but that's down to 11 MTFs in 10 games (1.1 per game) in 2026.

I think another good example is that Jeanty's yards per carry on outside zone runs has dipped from 4.0 (109 carries for 438 yards) to 3.2 ypc (29 carries for 92 yards), per Sumer. That's where being able to cut and move laterally really comes into play, and his effectiveness/efficiency has taken a hit this season. Maybe that's tied to the late-training camp ankle injury and it never fully healed, but running backs are never going to be fully healthy in the NFL. So, this is something to monitor over the next couple of months.

Q: What do you think is the main problem with the running game?

A: It's still the offensive line and, more specifically, the guard spots. Trey Zuhn III has been solid and looks like a good fit in Klint Kubiak's wide zone scheme. However, he only has 66 run-blocking snaps in the three games since becoming a starter, per PFF, so it's still too small of a sample size to definitively say the rookie can be a long-term option.

Meanwhile, I'm going to be pretty upset if Spencer Burford gets re-signed in the offseason. Take this for what it's worth; PFF has Miller (80.5), Tyler Linderbaum (77.7), DJ Glaze (72.6) and Zuhn (70.8) graded in the 70s or higher as zone run blockers, and Burford is sitting at 58.9. That's not the end-all-be-all and, again, it's a small sample size from Zuhn. But it's pretty telling to me that the same grading/evaluation system has four out of five linemen executing Kubiak's system at a high level and one guy who isn't.

Looking at the big picture, something that worries me is that the Raiders might have a zone-blocking offensive line and gap-running backs. I think Jeanty can execute zone runs and was decently effective at executing them last year, all things considered. However, that hasn't been the case in 2026, and Mike Washington Jr. doesn't have the vision and agility to be a consistent zone runner, in my opinion.

Kubiak's offense can still work where Washington is the change-of-pace back, but the team is going to need at least one back who can execute zone runs at a high level. Hopefully, that's Jeanty, and we'll see how this plays out down the stretch, but this could be another storyline heading into the offseason.

A: I think I've gotten this question (or some variation of it) in each of the last three weeks, and it's completely justified! To be honest, I don't think there is a "valid" reason, other than the coaching staff — Kubiak, more specifically — is attached to Burford and thinks he's playing better than what everyone else perceives. Meanwhile, it feels like they've soured on Jackson Powers-Johnson. It was pretty telling that when Zuhn got a concussion in San Francisco, Caleb Rogers was the one to replace him.

To be fair, JPJ is a questionable fit in a zone-running scheme. I thought he could make it work because he showed impressive athleticism at Oregon despite how big he is, but that just hasn't been the case this season. For perspective, he's the only lineman who has a worse PFF run-blocking grade on zone runs (50.5) than Burford does.

I'm with ya in that I think Powers-Johnson should get a shot at left guard; I just don't think we'll see it happen. Hopefully, I'm wrong, and something changes in the next few weeks.

Koonce is on a one-year contract and, as it currently stands, I don't think it would make sense to bring him back in the offseason. Then again, I said the same thing last year…

Might as well give the most talented one of the bunch a shot down the stretch.

Q: I've seen an uptick in criticism of GM Jon Spytek regarding his failures in both the draft and free agency during his tenure. Jack Bech in round 2 and the Kwity Paye contract are borderline criminal, IMO. Do you believe year 3 for him is make it or break it?

A: I dove into this more in depth a few weeks ago, and my opinion hasn't changed since then. So if you're looking for a more detailed answer, click on this hyperlink.

At a high level, I definitely think the pressure is going to be on Spytek this offseason/next season. I'm not in favor of moving on from him at the end of the year, but I'm also not going to sit here and say that the returns he's gotten on his investments so far are acceptable.

Spytek definitely leans toward taking gambles on draft prospects who are more projects than complete players, which can be frustrating because it takes patience as those players develop. However, that also means next year will be a make-or-break type of campaign for the 2025 draft class, and simultaneously, for the general manager who drafted those players.

A: Absolutely. When you're a head coach, you shoulder blame for any issue the team has. Kubiak may not be the primary reason for the defense's struggles, but ultimately, he's the one who hired Rob Leonard, and I think you're correct that Kubiak's idea was to replicate Seattle's defense without Seattle's personnel. That isn't to say he should be fired over Leonard, but it's on the head coach to make a change in one form or another to get better results.

Maybe that's moving on from Leonard and/or changing up the scheme, or getting with Spytek in the offseason to figure out what players they need to bring in to execute the system the way it's supposed to be run. Regardless, barring a dramatic turnaround over the next couple of months, Kubiak has to make an adjustment this spring.

One of the big differences between being a coordinator and a head coach is that you can no longer wash your hands clean when one side of the ball isn't playing up to par.
