What’s the Minimum Amount of Core Work Runners Need Each Week?

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

A powerful stride depends on having a strong core. It’s basically the glue that holds your running form together and will make every mile more efficient, helping you hit faster speeds.

But between fitting in runs, setting aside time for recovery, fueling, and living life outside of running shoes, it’s hard to know if you’re dedicating enough time to strengthening this key muscle group. So when your calendar is already packed, what’s the minimum number of core workouts you need to do to reap the benefits?

We asked experts for that magic minimum number, so you can boost your performance without carving a ton of time out of your busy schedule.

Simply put, every runner should aim to get at least two 10- to 15-minute core sessions done per week, says Yusuf Jeffers, NYC-based strength and run coach, Hyrox race director, and Nike Well Collective trainer. This is an adequate number to support your running form, build more strength and power, and improve overall running efficiency.

However, several factors can adjust this number, including your running goals and the season you’re in, says Holly Dickinson, UESCA-certified run coach, NSCA-certified strength and conditioning coach, and owner of StrideHer run coaching. For example, if building strength is your offseason goal and you’re not running as much mileage as you would during race training, you might increase the minimum amount of core work you do to three or four sessions per week. During a racing season, though, you might stick to two sessions per week.

As for the exercises you need, you can skip moves like crunches, which can stress your back and neck muscles and isolate just a few ab muscles (namely, the rectus abdominis or those six-pack abs) and instead, go for moves like planks, bird dogs, Copenhagen side planks, and glute bridges. These moves help with spinal and pelvic stability and core muscular endurance, and mimic running form—plus, they work 360 degrees of your core.

Jeffers recommends spending at least 30 seconds on each move, completing your core work in a circuit-style fashion (which means completing each move back-to-back with minimal rest in between) and repeating the circuit for at least two sets.

If you strength train regularly, you probably don’t have to do any additional core workouts because many programs often require you to brace your midsection and keep your spine neutral in order to do the exercise properly, Jeffers adds, which is basically like built-in core work. Think of weighted back squats, deadlifts, and bench presses, for example. You need to engage your core to maintain a stable spine through all of these exercises.

Many people tend to think of the core as just ab muscles, but your core is literally everything that’s not your arms and legs, says Jeffers. That’s essentially everything from your abdominal muscles to your glutes, pelvic floor, and back. All of these muscles work together to keep you stable on your runs and prevent form breakdown.

“The stronger your core is, the better the transfer of power as you’re running,” Jeffers explains. “That can keep your form better over time and also improve running economy.” For example, marathoners who tend to slouch or lean too far forward in the final miles of a marathon may lack the strength and resilience in their core to keep their posture upright, which can then make running feel more difficult and lead to a slowdown, he explains.

You should consciously focus on your posture and form during moves like planks and glute bridges, Jeffers says. He urges runners to avoid compensating with other parts of your body in order to hold a given movement. For example, if you’re arching your back or sagging your hips in a plank, or you’re feeling the burn in your lower back instead of your glutes when you’re doing a glute bridge, these are signs you need to brace your core more and focus on a straight spine.

Additionally, avoid using momentum or trying to go through an active exercise with speed. “The second you feel like you have to use momentum or use other body parts to either hold a movement or to do the movement, that’s your cue that you’re not holding onto the form,” Jeffers adds. In that case, slow down your movement and focus on form rather than trying to reach a certain rhythm or number of reps.

“The convenient thing about core work is that it’s often done without any equipment,” Jeffers says, making it easy to fit into your schedule.

For example, you can turn to bodyweight core exercises as part of a run warmup. Or you can add core moves to a strength session, like performing Copenhagen side planks between heavy squat sets. You could also save dedicated core work for the end of a heavy lifting session or for after an interval workout.

The key to reaping the benefits of a twice-weekly basic core workout is actually doing it regularly.

Oftentimes, you might look for a quick fix to a strong core, Dickinson says, but keeping up with simple, frequent sessions is more effective long term. “Putting in the hard work is going to pay off, and it doesn't have to look extreme or crazy hard,” she says. “It’s about that slow, consistent work.”

Experts agree that planks, glute bridges, and Copenhagen side planks are some of the best moves for runners. Incorporate these three moves into a quick routine two times a week to improve deep core strength and stabilization for faster, more efficient running.

Why it works: Planks train full‑body tension and teach your core to support the spine, just as you need on the run. By adding in variations like leg raises or shoulder taps, you can challenge your core strength even more and make planks extra functional, Dickinson told Runner’s World.

Why it works: Glute bridges allow for full hip extension, which leads to less strain on your back on the run and more glute activation, which can help improve—and add power to—your stride.

Why it works: This move works the obliques and hip adductors, or the muscles on the inside of the thighs that help draw the leg in toward the center of your body, allowing for better knee alignment on the run. Plus, they help with pelvic stabilization, which is crucial solid landings and forward propulsion.
