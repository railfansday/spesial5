‘The Mosquito Bowl’ Producer Alex Gayner Thought He’d Solved Every Problem a Set Could Throw at Him. Then Came a Runaway Parrot.

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Ten seconds of footage ended up being the most nerve-wracking part of the shoot for the movie veteran who has been on sets since the '90s.

Filmmakers often thrive on controlled chaos. Alex Gayner has spent his career orchestrating that madness.

Long before producing The Mosquito Bowl for Peter Berg, Gayner cut his teeth doing odd jobs on ’90s direct-to-video actioners — the kind of movies you’d stumble across on a pay-cable channel at 1 AM during a bout of insomnia. Then, a second assistant director was let go before lunch, and Gayner found himself promoted on the spot. It was an unlikely break that set the course for the rest of his career.

He gradually climbed the ranks to first assistant director, eventually trading B-movies for some of the biggest studio productions around, including Guillermo del Toro’s Pacific Rim and Gareth Edwards’ Godzilla. He also developed a reputation as a closer, overseeing reshoots for Suicide Squad (2016), Birds of Prey, Zero Dark Thirty and Mulan, among others. Another turning point arrived in the late 2010s, when the native New Yorker decided to pivot to producing. If the first AD is the on-set quarterback who executes the plays on the field, then Gayner wanted to become a full-time offensive coordinator, the person drawing up the plays and mapping out the production behind the scenes.

He credits Nicolas Winding Refn with giving him his big break as a producer on the 2019 limited series Too Old to Die Young, an opportunity that paved the way for him to produce the first season of Beef and win an Emmy for his work. (Gayner and Refn are now assembling the latter’s long-gestating Maniac Cop reimagining, which recently received approval for a California tax credit.)

After wrapping Beef, Netflix pointed Gayner toward Berg’s 19th-century Western limited series American Primeval. But first, the two had to bridge the gap between the producer Berg expected and the one Gayner turned out to be.

“When I met Pete, I don’t think he really knew what kind of producer I was,” Gayner tells The Hollywood Reporter. “Naturally, he didn’t know if I was going to come in to watch the money or try to make the show on soundstages. We had to figure each other out.”

To keep the football metaphors going, Berg likes to run his sets with a no-huddle offense, frequently calling audibles along the way. He might improvise dialogue, change camera positions or throw out the playbook altogether. Most of all, he has a strict devotion to immersive, practical filmmaking. Fortunately, Gayner has been adapting on the fly ever since that lunchtime promotion more than 30 years ago. He also shares Berg’s preference for capturing as much as possible in camera.

Thus, they quickly got on the same page for American Primeval, setting the stage for two features, The Mosquito Bowl and the highly anticipated Call of Duty video game adaptation.

“That [adherence to practical] really solidified our relationship as filmmaking partners. He knew I wasn’t afraid to go back to the old-school way of how we learned to do things,” Gayner says. “It’s a little uncomfortable to get that realism and that immersive, visceral experience on film, but it’s worth pushing the envelope.”

The Mosquito Bowl, based on a novel by Berg’s cousin Buzz Bissinger, chronicles four real-life college football players who enlisted in the U.S. Marine Corps during World War II and played in a fabled 1944 football game known as the Mosquito Bowl alongside 60 other former collegiate players turned soldiers.

Berg and Gayner threw Nicholas Galitzine, Bill Skarsgård, Ray Nicholson and Tom Francis into football crash courses with help from San Francisco 49ers All-Pro running back Christian McCaffrey, the New York Giants and two of the industry’s best on-set football coordinators. Then they dropped the actors into the ocean and jungle of Queensland, Australia, doubling for Japan, while surrounding them with tanks, explosions, flamethrowers and blank gunfire.

Berg wanted to honor one specific character detail that belonged to Nicholson’s Tony Butkovich, the former Purdue University fullback who was drafted in the first round by the NFL’s Cleveland Rams prior to enlistment. Bissinger wrote about how Butkovich sent photos home of himself with a parrot on his shoulder, so Gayner received the unenviable task of tracking down what would become the one-and-only available parrot on the island continent. But the bird was still young, and its wings had to be clipped to prevent catastrophe during filming.

After months of planning and training, cameras finally rolled on Nicholson and the supposedly grounded parrot. Within seconds, the parrot flew off into the 100-foot-tall trees. Its four wranglers panicked, as did the rest of the stunned cast and crew. Gayner, meanwhile, tried to counter Berg’s fatalism with optimism.

“Pete looked at me and said, ‘I know exactly what’s going to happen. The headline of every entertainment paper in L.A. is going to be, “Peter Berg Kills Parrot During Filming of The Mosquito Bowl.”’ And I said, ‘No, Pete, we’re going to find the parrot.’”

Hours later, they managed to retrieve the parrot from a tree and tie a string to its leg as an added precaution until they got the necessary shots. The trial amounted to roughly ten seconds of footage in the final cut, but it’s a stark reminder of how much labor goes into even the briefest moments on screen.

“We can control actors, special effects and stunts. But when the only parrot in Australia that’s available for our movie flies away, you’re stuck,” Gayner says. “We didn’t want to use a CG parrot. We’d made that commitment.”

Below, in a conversation with THR, Gayner elaborates on training two British actors and a Swedish actor to play a sport they’d never played before, let alone understood.

Your filmography is overwhelming. You’ve seen some things, haven’t you?

(Laughs.) I’ve seen a few things, yeah. I’ve been around the block. I’ve gone from tiny little things to big giant things — and everything in between.

I can only imagine the war stories you must have, especially as first assistant director. That’s widely considered the most stressful job on set. First ADs are often regarded as the quarterback to the director’s head coach. Did you have any kind of support group with your fellow first ADs?

Not really, because first ADs never work with other first ADs. You can talk to each other, share experiences and discuss your approaches to working with directors, but you never really get to spend time with them, especially on a movie set, unless there’s a first and second unit. Everybody has such different styles. But it is a stressful job. It’s a heavy-lifting job.

I started making films in Hollywood when I was 20 years old, and I worked for a very low-budget film company that made straight-to-video action movies. They filmed one movie every three weeks. So we would do 18-day shoots — three consecutive six-day weeks — with the exact same crew. We’d wrap a movie on a Friday, and then we’d start a new movie on a Monday. You’d get the script midway through one movie, and everybody would just roll onto the next film. I did that for three years, and I made 30 films in three years.

I did everything from driving the grip truck to learning how to load film, because we were still shooting film at the time, to being a boom person and working with the grips and the electric. So it really was my film school, and it taught me so much about what everybody does on a film set. Then I just happened to fall into the AD department because a second AD got let go. At lunch, they were like, “Alright, you’re now the second 2nd.” And I was like, “Okay, I’ll figure out how to do that.” Eventually, that happened with the [first] AD.

Why do you think these assistant director roles fit you so well?

I have a knack for communicating with all the departments and rallying everybody to have a common vision. And as I started to become a first AD on smaller movies before eventually doing some second-unit action sequences and working my way up to the bigger studio films, I made it less stressful for myself. I really understood what everybody was up against and what everybody was doing. I didn’t want to live under that constant pressure of only being focused on, How are we going to make our day? How are we going to get the schedule done? We have to just keep going.

That is part of the job, but really, I’m a filmmaker. I want to participate creatively in supporting the story and the characters. And as a first AD, if you can figure out how to do your job and motivate the crew — while working in a respectful environment with a couple hundred people, keeping them on the same page and being a good creative partner to the director, the producer and the studio — it takes a lot of the pressure off. You’re not being this machine butting up against an artist trying to birth something that’s in their brain.

So, as a first AD, I was always a creative first, and I figured out how to translate that into the schedule, the days, the shot lists and the time that the DP had to light. Integrating myself as a creative partner to everybody made it less stressful and more enjoyable.

Another pattern in your work is that you tend to assistant-direct a lot of additional photography. Examples include: Suicide Squad, Birds of Prey, Zero Dark Thirty, Mulan and Total Recall. That’s a fraught period of time since the production is spending the last of its money, and there’s a lot riding on the additions and/or revisions. Was there a reason why your number kept getting called for reshoots? Are you the Winston Wolf of reshoots?

(Laughs.) I don’t really know the answer. Part of it was schedule and circumstance. Another part of it was word of mouth. I really took pride in what I did as a first AD, and I’m a very even, mild-mannered first AD. There are some who are not. Everybody has their own style, but I always was very balanced and very calm in the pocket, so to speak, to go back to your quarterback reference. So I think that reputation just got around town.

Also, if a director’s usual first AD wasn’t available for reshoots, I tended to be on the short list to be called. It was very flattering. It was a really great way to build relationships with other directors without having to commit a year or two to the overall process, and it expanded my résumé quite a bit.

You’d produced here and there prior to 2023, but did your Emmy for producing Beef season one lead you to produce full-time? It looks like you stopped assistant-directing that same year.

I’d been going back and forth before producing Beef. The last movie I AD’d was Spiderhead with Joe Kosinski during COVID. It was a very hard transition. I’d worked so long to be successful as an AD, and then I looked in the mirror and went, “Alright, I’m going to stop doing this and make the transition to producer.” With a family and a mortgage, it was a big commitment to step into a new role, essentially starting at the bottom. But I knew that it was the natural next step, something I had to do and really wanted to do. The project that got me really confident as a producer was a limited series called Too Old to Die Young that Nicolas Winding Refn directed.

Big fan. Every time I see Nell Tiger Free, I remind her how haunting her beach scene with Miles Teller is.

I absolutely remember that day. We went up to the [Mugu] rock at the end of Malibu, right on the turn there. It was a wild show, and I loved making it. Nick originally called me to AD the series, and I turned him down because I was really committed to producing. But he kept calling me and calling me. And I said, “Nick, look, I really appreciate it, but I have to stick to this. I have to make this transition. This is what I’m going to do.” In 2017, limited series really weren’t that big a thing yet, and Amazon, under Roy Price, was also still finding its legs. This was long before Jen Salke came in.

Then Nick said, “I’ll tell you what, I’m going to direct this entire series by myself, which I’ve never done before. If you AD the first episode of the show for me, I’d love for you to produce the series. Once you get me on my feet, then you can produce the rest of the episodes. I just need you to prep for me and help me with casting and finding locations.”

So we figured out what would work for him and what would work for me, and we made a deal. I AD’d the first three weeks or so, and then I stepped off the set. Other ADs then came in, and I produced the rest of the show. Nick wrote the series with Ed Brubaker, and besides directing all the episodes, he was editing every night. So he was at it seven days a week, 20 hours a day.

It was a fantastic experience and one of the most creative things I’ve ever been a part of. It was such a specific vision that he had. We shot most of it in L.A. over a hundred days. It was a very small-budget show, and we stretched every dollar. That was really the beginning of producing for me.

Do you think Maniac Cop is ever going to happen?

I’m as sure as I can be. It just qualified for the California Tax Credit. It got approved. He’s making it as a film instead of a series. We tried to make it as a series for HBO. We were very close to making it, but once COVID hit, it was just impossible to make that kind of show at the time.

You wrapped Beef season one in 2022. In 2023, you EP’d American Primeval for Pete Berg. Did you hit it off to such a degree that he wanted you to be his boots-on-the-ground producer and UPM for The Mosquito Bowl?

American Primeval was a very complicated show to make out in the elements for a hundred-plus days. I was asked to come in as a non-writing EP. Pete and I had never worked together. I’d never even met him. I had previously worked with the other executive producer, Eric Newman, and I had a longstanding relationship with a lot of people at Netflix.

When I met Pete, I don’t think he really knew what kind of producer I was. He’s worked with a lot of the same people over the years, and he has this very raw, immersive, visceral vision of whatever he’s making. So, naturally, he didn’t know if I was going to come in to watch the money or try to make the show on soundstages. We had to figure each other out.

But as soon as we hit the ground in New Mexico, he learned that I have the same desire to make films and television as practically as he does. There are definitely benefits to going on stage and shooting blue screen for certain pieces, but for a huge sequence at the end that takes place on top of a snowy mountain, there was talk of going to a quarry-type setting and making fake snow in a very controlled environment. As soon as I started talking creatively with Pete about it, I knew there was no way this was going to work for what he was trying to do visually and what Mark L. Smith wrote.

My first reaction and suggestion to that proposed plan was, “I don’t want to do that. I want to gather a lean crew with as little gear as possible, and climb up a mountain in the real snow. We can build a shack and do it for real, while having access and keeping everybody as safe and comfortable as humanly possible.” To fabricate all that in the New Mexico summer and then add visual effects, it would’ve felt too different. Paper snow doesn’t react to horses the same way as real snow, and that went for every element of that sequence.

So that [adherence to practical filmmaking] really solidified our relationship as filmmaking partners. He knew I wasn’t afraid to go back to the old-school way of how we learned to do things. It’s a little uncomfortable to get that realism and that immersive, visceral experience on film, but it’s worth pushing the envelope.

Pete is known for having a very vocal, energetic and off-the-cuff directing style. He’ll throw out alternate dialogue mid-take without cutting. How did you adapt your own process to his “no-huddle” manner of shooting a movie?

I didn’t have to adapt that much. Working with Pete as a producer, it’s like creating an action-packed play. He’s very improvisational. If something comes to mind on set, he goes for it. Being a First AD for so many different kinds of directors, as well as shooting additional photography, really served me as a producer. I learned how to step into a preexisting structure, whether for a couple of scenes or a whole sequence, and adapt to a crew that’s already been through battle together. As a producer, I try to be as responsible for the active environment I’m creating as I am for the story.

Pete especially likes a 360-degree environment, and that’s how I approach every project. There’s no cheating. You can’t go out to a location with all your trucks lined up behind you and say, “Okay, you can only look west.” That 360 approach really slimmed everything down, allowing us to be nimble and giving Pete the freedom to say, “Okay, I want to turn around and look the opposite way.” It helped us discover some magic on American Primeval and The Mosquito Bowl. Organic pieces of the puzzle fell into place. The actors appreciated it, too.

Mosquito Bowl has numerous war and football sequences, which must have been a logistical nightmare. You weren’t the First AD on this set, but that experience must have also served you well here.

100 percent. Every challenging project I’ve produced has been made better by my experience and the tools I gained as a first AD. The Mosquito Bowl wasn’t a logistical nightmare, but it was very challenging logistically. There were so many challenges to figure out on a daily basis that I had to use every tool in my toolbox.

When Pete started writing and talking about The Mosquito Bowl while we were shooting American Primeval, we agreed that if we were going to make a war movie, we had to do something different. So many amazing World War II films have already been made. We had to take responsibility for trying to capture even 0.1 percent of what these soldiers went through emotionally. Obviously, we would never compare what we do as filmmakers to what any soldier experienced in war, but we had to deliver something immersive, violent and shocking for the audience while remaining true to what really happened.

That meant putting the actors in the ocean instead of in a water tank or a pool and using real explosions and blank gunfire instead of visual effects. We had to find running tanks and develop hundred-foot flamethrowers. As a producer, I was never going to let “it’s impossible” come out of my mouth. There was always going to be a way to make it as real as possible, and that became our mission statement from the beginning.

With every person we interviewed and every department we approached about joining The Mosquito Bowl, we said, “This is how we want to do it. We want to make this as real as possible. Can you do this? Are you up for it? And what are your ideas to help us get there?”

Did San Francisco 49ers running back Christian McCaffrey consult you guys as far as how to train the actors to look like capable football players in just four weeks?

He absolutely did. Christian McCaffrey helped us build the foundation for the actors to become football players. Pete, who’s friendly with Christian, gave him a call once we had Nicholas Galatzine, Bill Skarsgård, Tom Francis and Ray Nicholson on board. Christian is a beast. He’s one of the most amazing football players there is, and he’s such a gracious guy. He does so much work with veterans, and he’s involved with a nonprofit, so he was immediately willing to donate his time. He was even coming back from an injury at the time, but two days after Pete called him, he was on the field in Westlake with Ray, running plays, teaching him moves and consulting as a running back. Additionally, we sent Tom Francis to the New York Giants because he was doing Sunset Boulevard on Broadway, so he got to train with them.

We had two Brits, a Swede and one American, and with Ray being the American, he was the only one who had ever really played football. He played high school football and knew a lot about the game, but Nick, Tom and Bill didn’t know much about it. So they worked tirelessly with our football coordinators, Mike Fisher and Don Powers, who have worked on a lot of football movies. They came down to Australia and ran a four-week camp with the actors, bringing in a ton of Aussie rules football and rugby players, as well as a few college football players from the States to fill in at quarterback.

For all the football and battle sequences, ten seconds of a parrot interacting with Ray’s character was the task that put you through your paces the most?

(Laughs.) That put Pete through his paces more than anything else on the movie. I don’t know about myself. Every single day, my brain was working at a hundred miles an hour trying to solve problems. Birds and animals in Australia are different from those in other parts of the world. You have some of the deadliest snakes and spiders, as well as bull sharks. Everywhere you go, you have to be aware of nature’s pitfalls. So finding movie animals there was difficult, and Australia doesn’t have every breed you might find in North America or elsewhere.

Ray’s character, Tony Butkovich, really had a parrot as a pet during World War II. That was based on real information. But finding a movie-trained parrot that looked the way we wanted was nearly impossible. We worked with animal wranglers who eventually found one, but it was very young. They had to train it so we could feed it.

They also clipped its swings so it wouldn’t fly away. We had to shoot the scene within a specific window before the parrot got old enough to learn how to fly. So we were up against the deadline of the parrot instinctively knowing how to fly. We also shot the whole movie in continuity, so our first AD, Andrew Ward, had to figure out how to fit the scene into that window.

There had been months of logistical planning and training, and Ray had to understand what he could and couldn’t do with it. Eventually, the day came when we had to work with the parrot. We were in the jungle, surrounded by smoke. Everyone was carrying guns and wearing all this gear, but everything had to be silent and still.

Gosh, all this for ten seconds of footage.

Yeah, when Ray sat down to do the scene, the set had to be cleared. Pete couldn’t call action, and nobody could say a word. The animal wranglers put the parrot down next to Ray and made hand signals for the cameras to start rolling. Ray started feeding the parrot, and within two seconds, it flew off into the jungle. (Laughs.) The parrot that wasn’t supposed to be able to fly with its clipped wings disappeared up into the trees.

The animal wranglers freaked out, and we all had to stop and look up. We were looking for a tiny parrot in the Australian jungle, surrounded by hundred-foot trees and dense foliage. The animal wranglers then crawled through the jungle, making parrot noises and trying to call it back.

Pete looked at me and said, “I know exactly what’s going to happen. The headline of every entertainment paper in L.A is going to be, ‘Peter Berg Kills Parrot During Filming of The Mosquito Bowl.’” And I said, “No, Pete, we’re going to find the parrot.” So the search went on for hours, and the animal wranglers stacked ladders and held pool cues with baskets on the ends. Eventually, the parrot came back and we got the shots, but it was one of the most stressful ordeals.

We can control actors, special effects and stunts. We can have that kind of input. But when the only parrot in Australia that’s available for our movie flies away, you’re stuck. We didn’t want to use a CG parrot. We’d made that commitment.

You just proved why I’m endlessly fascinated by behind-the-scenes stories. Of all the projects you’ve been a part of, which one deserves the I Play Rocky treatment?

It’s between American Primeval and Too Old to Die Young, but it’s hard to pick one or the other because they’re very different.

American Primeval was logistically the hardest project I’ve ever produced and made. We did it for real in the New Mexico desert through winter, spring, summer and fall, with no cover sets and no place to go, with horses and 1800s footwear and clothing, a writers’ strike and a SAG strike. Taylor Kitsch, our lead actor, even broke his foot, and Betty Gilpin, our lead actress, got pregnant during the strikes before having to come back to finish the show. We went from shooting in one winter season with the heaviest snow in New Mexico to finishing the show the next winter season, and it was New Mexico’s warmest winter with no snow.

Too Old to Die Young was the most eccentric creative experience I’ve ever produced. Nick shot 10-minute-long takes where the camera didn’t move. We drove around Hollywood at three in the morning with John Hawkes and Miles Teller. They drove a police car by themselves up the 405 while I was in the backseat with a camera in my hand. A CHP officer followed us and allowed us to do that night after night. It was the most surreal experience.

***The Mosquito Bowl is now streaming on Netflix.
