A year ago, most AI video clips lasted a few seconds, had no sound and drifted off-model by the final frame. Today several tools produce 1080p or even 4K clips with synchronized audio, camera control and reference-based consistency. The market also moves quickly: OpenAI announced it was shutting down Sora in March 2026, so the list of tools worth using has changed.

This guide compares six AI video generators that developers, designers and marketers can use today. For each one you will see what it does well, where it falls short and who it suits. Plans, credits and model names change often, so confirm details on each vendor’s site before you commit.

OpenArt’s AI video generator puts a range of video models behind one interface. Its page lists models such as Veo 3.1, Kling, Seedance, Wan, Hailuo and PixVerse, so you can run the same prompt on different engines and compare the results without managing separate accounts.

Beyond text-to-video and image-to-video, it offers start and end frame control, video-to-video restyling, motion transfer from a reference clip, a character builder for consistency, lip sync and conversational editing for tasks such as swapping a background or relighting a scene. Resolution goes up to 4K on many models, with 1080p as the default, and clips are usually up to 15 seconds, with longer limits on a few models.

Strengths: model variety, a single credit pool, editing tools alongside generation and a shared library for your clips.

Limits: credit use varies by model, so it is worth testing short clips first. Commercial-use rights depend on your plan and on the model you pick, so check the pricing and terms pages.

Best for: creators and small teams that want to compare models quickly instead of committing to one.

Veo is Google DeepMind’s video model. Veo 3.1 generates sound effects, ambient noise and dialogue together with the video, supports image-to-video, first and last frame transitions, reference images for characters or objects, and outputs 1080p and 4K. You can reach it through the Gemini app, Google Flow and the Gemini API.

Strengths: audio generated with the picture, strong prompt adherence and an API that fits into developer workflows.

Limits: base clips are short, around 8 seconds, so longer scenes rely on scene extension. Dialogue quality in short segments is still being refined, and access and quotas depend on your Google plan.

Best for: developers and marketers who want audio and video from one prompt, or who plan to automate generation through an API.

Runway has been a staple of AI filmmaking since its early Gen models. Its pricing page lists Gen-4.5 and Aleph alongside partner models, and the paid plans include all video and image models, 4K upscaling and parallel generations. Higher tiers add features such as voice clones, brand kits and HDR or ProRes output.

Strengths: polished editing tools, video-to-video workflows and a professional output pipeline that suits post-production teams.

Limits: credits are consumed quickly on premium models, and on the lower paid plans they do not roll over from month to month.

Best for: video editors and agencies that need control over shots and want AI to fit into an existing editing process.

Kling from Kuaishou has built a reputation for natural motion and expressive characters. The current release, Kling 4.0, sits alongside tools such as Motion Control, a canvas workspace, an agent and MCP or CLI batch generation for teams that want to automate production.

Strengths: realistic movement, motion transfer and batch tools for producing many short clips.

Limits: the main site does not spell out all clip-length and resolution limits for each model, so check the model page you plan to use. Pricing and free credits vary by region and plan.

Best for: social video, product shots and character-driven clips where motion quality is the priority.

Luma’s Dream Machine is built on the Ray model family. Its current page lists Ray3.2 and Ray3.14, along with a “Modify video” tool that restyles footage, extends duration and reframes aspect ratios. It also lists camera-motion controls and high-quality EXR export for professional pipelines.

Strengths: reshooting or restyling existing footage, camera-motion control and pipeline-friendly export.

Limits: paid plans start at around $30 per month (or less when billed yearly), and heavy use pushes you into the higher tiers.

Best for: filmmakers and designers who want to modify and extend footage rather than only generate new clips.

Adobe Firefly offers text-to-video and image-to-video with Adobe’s own Firefly Video Model. It also provides partner models, including Google Veo 3.1, Luma Ray3, Kling and Runway Gen-4.5, so you can compare outputs from inside one product. An Assembler and AI video editor let you trim, reorder, extend and add voiceovers, music and sound effects.

Adobe states that the Firefly Video Model is trained on licensed content such as Adobe Stock and public domain material, and describes its output as safe for commercial use. Partner models may carry different terms, so read the details for whichever model you choose.

Strengths: a clear licensing story for the native model, integration with Adobe’s creative apps and an included editor.

Limits: most clips run up to 8 seconds, with some models reaching 15. Credits are allocated monthly, and premium partner models can use them faster.

Best for: brand and in-house teams that care about provenance and already work in Adobe tools.

Credit counts are hard to compare, so convert them to clips. Runway’s pricing page lists Gen-4.5 at 60 credits per 5-second clip, which means the 625 monthly credits on the Standard plan cover roughly ten clips. Adobe says its Standard plan’s 2,000 credits equal up to 40 five-second videos, and OpenArt’s Starter plan lists about 50 videos for 4,000 credits. Premium models cost more: the same Runway page lists Aleph 2.0 at 140 credits per 5 seconds.

Plan for waste. If you keep one usable clip out of every three attempts, your real cost per finished shot is about three times the sticker price. Test with short, low-resolution drafts, then render the winner at full quality.

Because every tool outputs short clips, the real work is assembly. A typical workflow is: write a shot list, generate two or three takes per shot, pick the best, normalize them to one format, join them and add captions and audio. FFmpeg handles the joining step from the command line:

Matching resolution, frame rate and codec before joining avoids glitches at the cut points. For character consistency across shots, reuse the same reference image in every prompt and keep descriptions of the subject identical.

If the clip ends up on your own site, treat it like any other media. Provide a poster image, load metadata only until the visitor presses play, and include captions so deaf and hard-of-hearing users, and anyone watching without sound, can follow along. Also write a text description or transcript near the video. Search engines and screen reader users cannot see the footage, so the surrounding text is what explains it.

Marketing demos show the best of a tool, so run your own test. Use the same prompt, duration and aspect ratio on each tool, generate a few takes and score them against a short checklist. Keep notes on how many credits each usable clip costs.

Run the same test again with an image-to-video input if your workflow starts from product photos or illustrations. That often exposes differences in consistency that text prompts hide.

Which AI video generator is best? There is no single winner. Veo is strong for audio, Runway for editing, Kling for motion, Luma for modifying footage and Firefly for licensing clarity. A multi-model tool such as OpenArt helps you compare them.

Can AI video generators make long videos? Not in one pass. Most models create short clips, so longer videos are built by extending or stitching clips together.

Is Sora still available? OpenAI announced in March 2026 that it was shutting down the Sora app and API, so it is not a safe choice for new projects.

Can I use AI videos commercially? Often, but it depends on the plan and the underlying model. Read the current terms before publishing.

How do I keep a character consistent across clips? Reuse the same reference image, keep the subject description identical in every prompt, and use character or reference tools where the platform offers them.

Should I add captions to AI-generated video? Yes. Captions help accessibility and viewers watching without sound, and they give search engines text to index.

Do I need a powerful computer? No. These services run in the cloud, so a browser and a stable connection are enough.

The best AI video generator depends on what you make. Start with a short test prompt, compare a few tools, and keep the one that gives you usable clips at a sensible credit cost. If you are not sure where to begin, a multi-model option such as OpenArt lets you try several engines before you settle on one.
