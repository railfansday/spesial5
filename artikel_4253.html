Murals Preview: Frantic Fairy Tale Fun

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Murals is taking old fairy tales and filtering them through an action RPG lens, while injecting the stories with a fresh dose of British humor.

Fairy tales have been deconstructed and shown through a modern lens so many times that subversions are almost as common as straight tellings of the original stories. Murals is an upcoming action RPG that takes a comedic, subversive look at classic kids' stories, wrapped in an addictive gameplay loop familiar to fans of the big hits of the past few years.

Roguelike action RPGs have exploded in popularity, especially on PC, with gamers becoming hooked on brutally difficult gameplay merged with random elements that offer near-infinite replayability. On the surface, Murals seems cut from the same cloth as something like Hades, being an action RPG with an isometric viewpoint, where a long hero dashes around, avoiding attacks, while unleashing an increasingly larger arsenal at their foes.

DualShockers visited developer Dlala Studios at their home base for a hands-on preview of Murals. This consisted of playing the first two chapters and experiencing an in-development build of the game that is still being tweaked and worked on. What’s here is impressive, especially in terms of the aesthetics, with gorgeous visuals on display for Murals’ fairy-tale world.

The Murals preview I played featured two stages: Little Briar Rose (the story that formed the basis for Sleeping Beauty), and Rapunzel. The story starts with the protagonist, whose appearance can best be described as “Player 2 April O’Neil,” waking up in a mysterious new world with no memory of how they got there.

The protagonist of Murals doesn't talk much, but when they do, they have branching dialogue options. Sadly, the game immediately says that these have no bearing on the gameplay and are just for flavor, so the choices may as well not exist. While I'm not expecting some Mass Effect-style consequences for every dialogue choice, it'd be nice if some of the responses had some kind of bearing on the gameplay.

The protagonist doesn't talk much because they're soon joined by Loaf, the game's mascot character. Loaf is a poorly drawn doodle that resembles an eraser with lizard-like plates on its back. Loaf quickly sets the comedic tone of the story, with Monty Python-esque retorts to the silliness of their situation, before the two are joined by Slash Gordon, the player’s trusty blade.

The chapters of Murals I experienced involved solving the locals' problems, such as helping an insomniac Sleeping Beauty or freeing a trapped Rapunzel by climbing her hair up the tower. There was also an overarching story involving a mysterious being known as The Creator, who looks like a smug art critic and seems to pull the strings from afar, guiding the protagonist along.

One reliable ally the player has between battles is Magic Dave, voiced by legendary UK comedian Bob Mortimer. He’s the Resident Evil 4 Merchant of the game, providing upgrades (called Eeries) between stages, while exchanging witty banter with Toast.

The comedy in Murals is going to be love it or hate it. Saying it’s steeped in British irreverence is an understatement, and that sentence alone might be off-putting to some. A great comparison is the works of the late great Terry Pratchett: that provides a metric of Britishness the player should expect.

Dlala Studios is no stranger to pretty 2D games, having previously created the modern Battletoads game, as well as Disney Illusion Island. Murals marks the studio's departure from established intellectual properties and into publishing, which is why fairy tales were the company's next stop, since they're in the public domain.

Easily the high point of Murals is its visuals. Each level in Murals is a stunning, hand-drawn piece of artwork, where every room appears lovingly crafted and filled with little touches to admire while exploring.

The same is true of the character artwork, with everything polished to perfection. The level of detail is especially impressive, considering Murals is still being developed, yet the team already has the aesthetic nailed down.

What once started out as a subgenre of NES classics has now become a typically hand-drawn set of video games that never fail to impress.

The only exception to the pretty visuals is the enemies in Murals, which are 3D models and nowhere near as visually impressive as the stages or NPCs. The monsters are quite generic and don't feel like they match the fairy-tale world they exist in, with designs that could fit into pretty much any action RPG.

The boss monsters are similarly whelming, with the giant frog faced in the Little Briar Rose stage doing the same jumping attack over and over again, only adding mobs to his move pool. In contrast, the final encounter of the Rapunzel stage is just waves of enemies, with no big boss in sight.

Fairy tales are full of creative villains who could be repurposed for use in video games. Just look at The Wolf Among Us for only one example among many. The enemies in Murals need an injection of creativity in both their design and battle strategy if the game wants to keep the player's interest.

It's impossible to play Murals and not compare it to Hades or its sequel. Just looking at a gameplay video on the Steam page, the comparisons are easy to see. It’s impossible to deny that Murals has Hades DNA, with the same basic combat loop, even though it takes a drastically different approach.

In Murals, the player has a choice of melee weapons (the sword named Slash Gordon and the hammer called the Wolfhammer in the demo), a ranged weapon with recharging ammo, and a super move that builds up over the battle, all of which are used to destroy foes. The player can dash to avoid enemy attacks and must survive waves of enemies through chains of rooms, with liberal health drops between areas.

As someone who was brutalized by Hades 2 in the past, one mechanic in Murals' combat immediately stuck out. When swinging a weapon or using a ranged attack, it's possible to perform a dash and gain invincibility frames. This makes the combat significantly easier than in similar games, as the player doesn't have to commit to attacks in the same way as they would in, say, a FromSoftware game, leaving them locked into a position where they can be countered.

The quick dash is more effective when using Slash Gordon, as it synergizes well with a swift weapon. This isn't to say that the Wolfhammer isn't also broken in its own way, as it has a breath weapon-style attack that can be merged with a Frost Rune to perform multiple hits that each have a chance of inflicting the Freeze status effect on an enemy, drastically increasing the chance of paralyzing them.

Murals is still a work-in-progress, so there’s plenty of time for developers to tweak the gameplay balance. What’s here is considerably easier than many of the action RPGs on the market, but the reason for that could be related to the fact that it’s a mostly linear, story-based game, with few roguelike elements.

The levels in Murals might look like the randomly connected rooms of countless roguelikes, but the stages have been crafted and designed to tell a story, including NPC encounters, puzzles, and even side quests. While there are some random elements, such as how the upgrades/Eeries are handled, the bulk of the game is a planned affair, which explains why it’s easier than its contemporaries.

It's a bold move to go for a linear approach in a market so consumed with randomness, especially when Murals uses a gameplay style and overall aesthetic that’s tied to roguelikes. That being said, there’s plenty of room for a more focused and narrative-driven title that uses the gameplay loop of familiar titles, especially when the levels look this good.

Murals has a ton of potential and an amazing pedigree behind it. Dlala Studios has produced some amazing titles in the past, and the fact that the game is so polished and impressive at this point in development is a feat unto itself. The hard part is going to be tweaking the combat to make it satisfying for everyone, meaning some strict difficulty modifiers could be the answer.

The comedic writing and tone in Murals are trickier, as they're so baked into the game’s identity, yet also have the potential to miss the mark. It’s the kind of thing that someone will know they’re going to enjoy within a short period of time, and hopefully, Mural’s subversive take on fairy tales is as appealing to the audience as its combat.
