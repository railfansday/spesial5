Which vaccines do you need to travel abroad? 7 shots and their timing

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Vaccines to get before holiday travel include MMR, hepatitis A and yellow fever, and this guide explains seven shots, their dose schedules and how early to book

Travel vaccines work on a schedule, and that schedule starts long before the flight. Many shots need a series of doses spread over days, weeks or months. Others need time after the injection before the body builds protection. A traveler who visits a clinic a few days before a December flight may find some vaccines cannot take full effect in time.

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) generally advises travelers to see a health care provider four to six weeks before departure. That window gives multidose schedules time to begin and protection time to develop. Some shots also have limited availability. In the U.S., the yellow fever vaccine is given only at authorized yellow fever vaccination centers.

This guide covers seven vaccines to get before holiday travel. It is a selection, not a complete checklist. Some entries are routine shots that many adults need to update, such as the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine. Others depend on destination. Yellow fever occurs in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and tropical South America, while typhoid risk is highest in South Asia. One is tied to a specific journey. Saudi Arabia requires meningococcal ACWY vaccination for Hajj and Umrah pilgrims. Holiday travel often means crowded airports and aircraft, which is why the seasonal flu shot is on the list.

Each entry explains what the vaccine protects against, how many doses it takes and how far ahead it needs to be given. Those details answer two common questions: which vaccines do I need to travel abroad, and how early should I get them?

Recommendations also differ from one traveler to the next. Age, vaccination history, health conditions and itinerary all shape what a provider suggests. Close contact with animals or long stretches far from medical care, for example, can make rabies pre-exposure vaccination worth discussing.

Vaccine schedules and entry rules can change over time. Before a clinic visit, check the CDC and World Health Organization (WHO) destination pages for the countries on the itinerary. Confirm entry requirements with each destination's official sources as well. A provider can then match that guidance to a specific trip and health history.

Measles is a risk on international trips, and the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine protects against it. Two doses of MMR give full protection.

CDC guidance for adult travelers is specific. An adult who travels abroad without evidence of immunity should get two doses. Those doses should be given at least 28 days apart.

That interval sets the planning timeline. A traveler who starts the series less than 28 days before departure cannot finish both doses before leaving. Booking the first appointment well ahead of the trip leaves room to complete the series before the flight. A single dose before departure falls short of the two doses CDC advises for adults without evidence of immunity.

MMR is a routine vaccine, not one tied to a particular destination. That makes the same check relevant before any trip abroad, from a short hop across a border to a long-haul holiday journey. The deciding factor is whether the traveler already has evidence of immunity.

Infants follow a separate rule. A baby aged six to 11 months who is traveling internationally can receive one early dose of MMR before departure. It is meant to cover the child during the trip itself.

That early dose does not count toward the child's routine series. The child still needs two more doses, starting at 12 months of age, to finish the standard schedule.

An infant vaccinated early for a trip ends up with three doses in all: one for travel and two for the routine schedule. A written record of the travel dose lets the child's later appointments account for it.

Parents can raise the early dose with a pediatrician before a holiday trip abroad. Adults unsure of their own status can review vaccination records with a provider before booking travel. Finding a gap early leaves time to complete both doses before departure.

The hepatitis A vaccine is given as two doses, six months apart, and the first dose on its own provides strong protection. A traveler short on time can get that first dose shortly before departure and finish the series later.

Hepatitis A spreads through contaminated food and water. The disease is common in many parts of the world where sanitation is limited.

That route of transmission shapes the risk for travelers. Exposure does not require unusual activities. It can come through ordinary meals and drinks in places where food and water handling fall short. Ice made from contaminated water or produce rinsed in it travels the same route.

The two doses do different jobs. The first shot delivers strong protection on its own. The second turns that early protection into long-lasting protection. That matters for anyone who expects to travel again.

The six-month gap has a practical consequence for holiday planning. A traveler who gets the first shot just before a December trip would be due for the second around June. That appointment falls months after the trip ends. It needs to go on the calendar separately.

Skipping the second dose leaves the series incomplete. The long-lasting protection depends on finishing it. Keeping a record of the first dose makes the follow-up visit simpler, especially if it happens at a different clinic.

This flexibility sets hepatitis A apart from vaccines that must be given by a firm deadline before departure. A late clinic visit still leaves time for the first dose to help. For a traveler booking a last-minute holiday trip, hepatitis A is one shot that does not have to be ruled out for lack of time.

Whether hepatitis A vaccination is recommended depends on the destination. Current destination guidance from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the World Health Organization (WHO) shows where vaccination is advised. A health care provider can confirm the dose timing for each traveler and each trip.

Typhoid vaccine comes in two forms: a injectable Vi polysaccharide shot given at least two weeks before travel, or four oral Ty21a capsules finished at least one week before departure.

Typhoid spreads through contaminated food and water. Risk is highest in South Asia.

The Vi polysaccharide vaccine needs only one injection. That means one clinic visit covers it, as long as the visit falls early enough.

Protection from the Vi shot does not last indefinitely. Travelers $TRV who face continued risk need a booster every two years. Someone who had the injection before an earlier trip should check the date of that dose. If it was given more than two years ago, a booster is due before another trip to an area with typhoid risk.

The Ty21a vaccine is taken by mouth. One capsule is taken every other day rather than daily, for a total of four capsules.

That schedule sets a start date well ahead of departure. Four capsules taken every other day stretch across six days from the first to the last. Adding the one-week buffer means the first capsule should be taken no later than 13 days before the trip begins. A late start can push the final capsule past that window.

The two options need similar lead time. The injection must be given at least two weeks before travel, and the oral course must begin no later than 13 days before. The difference lies in format: one dose at a clinic versus four capsules spread across six days.

Neither vaccine is fully protective. A traveler who has had the shot or finished the capsules can still be infected. Food and water precautions still apply. They target the same route the disease uses to spread. Vaccination and careful choices about what to eat and drink work as two separate layers of protection.

The yellow fever vaccine is a single shot, and the WHO says one dose gives lifetime protection. The shot must be given at least 10 days before arrival for the international certificate of vaccination to be valid.

Yellow fever is a virus spread by mosquitoes. It occurs in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and tropical South America. A holiday itinerary that includes either region is the usual reason to ask a provider about this vaccine.

The vaccine does two separate jobs. It protects the traveler against a mosquito-borne virus, and it produces a document some governments check. A trip can call for one without the other. A traveler visiting an area where yellow fever occurs may want the protection even if no border agent asks for proof.

Some countries require the certificate for entry. The document serves as proof of vaccination at the border. Entry rules differ from country to country. The destination's official requirements are the place to confirm whether a certificate is needed.

The 10-day rule shapes the travel calendar. A shot given fewer than 10 days before arrival leaves the certificate invalid when the traveler lands. The count runs to the arrival date in the country that requires proof, not to the day the trip begins. Travelers $TRV heading to such a country need to book the appointment with that window in mind.

Since July 2016, the international certificate of vaccination has been valid for life. The change matches the WHO's position that one dose protects for life. A traveler who already holds a certificate from an earlier vaccination can present that same document on later trips. For that traveler, the 10-day wait no longer applies, because the original shot already meets it.

In the U.S., the vaccine is not offered at every clinic. Only authorized yellow fever vaccination centers can give the shot. A provider who handles other travel vaccines may not be one of those centers. Finding an authorized center ahead of time keeps the 10-day deadline within reach.

Saudi Arabia requires pilgrims performing Hajj or Umrah to show proof of quadrivalent meningococcal ACWY vaccination. The same vaccine is also relevant for trips to the meningitis belt of sub-Saharan Africa, which is an area of higher risk for the disease.

Meningococcal disease is a serious bacterial infection. It can cause meningitis. It can also cause an infection of the bloodstream. The ACWY vaccine is intended to protect against this infection.

The vaccine's name describes its coverage. "Quadrivalent" means the shot targets four groups of meningococcal bacteria. Those groups are labeled A, C, W and Y.

For pilgrims, the shot is an entry matter as well as a health one. Saudi Arabia requires proof of vaccination, not only the vaccination itself. A traveler should keep the record from the clinic visit and pack it with passports and other travel papers.

Saudi authorities set the timing rules for that proof. The window in which a dose is accepted for entry should be confirmed against the official requirements before travel. Pilgrims vaccinated for an earlier trip should check whether that dose still meets the current rule.

Travel to the meningitis belt raises a different question. The case for the vaccine there rests on the higher risk of disease in the region. A traveler heading to sub-Saharan Africa can raise the ACWY vaccine with a provider at a pre-travel appointment.

Official sources settle the details for each itinerary. Current CDC and WHO destination guidance, along with each country's own entry requirements, shows what applies to a specific trip.

Rabies pre-exposure vaccination is meant for travelers who may have close contact with animals or who will be far from medical care. In 2022, the U.S. Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) recommended a two-dose schedule, with the second dose given seven days after the first.

That schedule fits the whole pre-exposure series inside a single week. A traveler who sees a provider in the weeks before departure can finish both doses before leaving. The short gap also leaves room for holiday plans that come together late, as long as both appointments fall before the flight.

The two groups face different versions of the same problem. Close contact with animals raises the chance of a bite. Distance from medical care makes it harder to reach treatment quickly after one.

The vaccine does not remove the need for medical care after an animal bite. A vaccinated traveler who is bitten still needs to be seen by a medical provider. The earlier shots change the treatment that follows, not whether treatment is needed.

That treatment changes in two ways. Prior vaccination reduces the number of post-exposure doses a bitten traveler has to receive. It also removes the need for rabies immune globulin. A traveler who skipped pre-exposure vaccination may need both rabies immune globulin and a longer series of post-exposure shots.

For a traveler far from medical care, that difference carries practical weight. A shorter post-exposure course means fewer return visits to a provider after a bite. Skipping rabies immune globulin removes one product from the list of what a clinic must supply.

The decision rests on the itinerary. A provider can weigh planned activities and the distance to medical care against the time needed for the two-dose schedule. Recommendations can change. Before booking, current CDC and WHO destination guidance shows what applies to a given trip.

Holiday travelers need a current-season flu shot at least two weeks before departure. Protection takes about two weeks to develop after vaccination.

That two-week lag sets the deadline. A shot given a few days before a flight will not have finished building protection by the time the traveler boards. Someone leaving in late December needs the vaccine by mid-December at the latest.

Flu seasons do not line up around the world. The Northern Hemisphere season generally runs from October to May. That window takes in the year-end holidays across the U.S., Canada, Europe and Japan.

The Southern Hemisphere season generally runs from April to September. A December trip to Australia, Argentina or South Africa falls outside that usual window. A mid-year trip from the U.S. to the same places reverses the picture. Home is between seasons, while the destination is in the middle of one.

The tropics follow a different pattern. Flu can circulate there year-round. Destinations near the equator therefore have no dependable off-season. A trip to Southeast Asia, Central America or the Caribbean can overlap with flu activity in any month.

Holiday travel puts large numbers of people in the same spaces. Trips at this time of year often run through crowded airports and onto full aircraft. A shot timed to take effect before departure means protection is in place for the journey itself, not only for the destination.

The flu vaccine formulation changes every year. A shot from a previous season does not carry over to the new one. A traveler vaccinated last year, or earlier, still needs a new dose before this year's trip. Checking the date of the most recent shot is a quick way to confirm whether it belongs to the current season.

Vaccine recommendations can change. Travelers $TRV should check current guidance from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the World Health Organization before booking. A provider can advise on timing, particularly for trips that cross hemispheres.
