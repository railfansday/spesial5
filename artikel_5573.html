I’m taking a (very basic, 101) biochemistry course, and I spent much of the weekend trying to memorize the steps of glycolysis and the citric acid cycle. Metabolism is a beast, and I welcome your mnemonics!

In exchange, I offer a glimpse inside the two-day meeting of the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force, which hasn’t met in more than a year. Members (many of them new and appointed by health secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.) grappled with how to apply the Trump administration’s DEI rules to recommendations on cervical cancer screening and perinatal depression, among other things. Read on.

In its first meeting since March 2025, an influential panel of doctors that evaluates preventive medical care grappled with the ways Kennedy has upended science and medicine.

Last week, the 16-member panel voted on recommendations on screening for cervical cancer, perinatal depression, and unhealthy alcohol use by teens and adults. The recommendations have been awaiting completion for more than a year while Kennedy repeatedly canceled meetings and largely remade the panel’s membership.

The discussion was livestreamed to the public for the first time, though the votes were held in private. Throughout the session, longtime and new members debated a Trump administration policy requiring the removal of language on “diversity, equity, and inclusion,” after Roger Klein, director of the USPSTF’s parent organization, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, cut all sub-population data from recommendation language.

“I worry that having edits from political leadership on the topline may lead to the perception that the task force recommendations are no longer scientifically independent,” Alicia Fernandez, a member of the panel since 2025, said as members discussed cervical cancer screenings.

At another point, some members expressed concern about bias in the presentations from subject matter experts. Members also asked how they might accurately and transparently explain when and how artificial intelligence is used in their data and recommendations.

“Should our documentation say ‘supported by AI?’” new member Ronald Karlsberg asked. — Chelsea Cirruzzo

Over the last several months, the American Diabetes Association has been … a real mess. There have been recriminations, a raft of resignations, and a 1,221-member boycott. Who’s the loser in all this high drama?

Experts say the impact of the ADA’s crisis won’t be felt immediately. But a dysfunctional professional organization could stymie advances in diabetes care for years to come. That’s because the ADA plays a key role in setting guidelines and sharing research in journals and at wide-ranging meetings that attract thousands.

“All of this has coalesced to make everyone anxious, uncertain about the future of diabetes research,” one expert told STAT. “And the ADA should at this point be standing up and being something that we can all rally behind. Instead it is being fractured by its own actions.” Read more.

If you know the MAHA Institute, a think tank launched to support Kennedy’s agenda, it’s probably because of its roundtables.

Events, STAT’s Isa Cueto writes, are the organization’s “bread and butter.” They were also a money pit — one “Friends of MAHA” fundraiser cost $230,000 to put on and netted just $55,000 from three contributors.

That might help explain the group’s current financial troubles. As Isa reports, the Institute started 2025 with nearly $1.3 million in its coffers, and ended the year with $171,000, per its latest IRS return, filed last week. That 86% drop was hardly offset by the group’s revenue, which was just shy of $67,000 for the year. Read more, and also check out her companion piece on the MAHA PAC’s very odd spending habits.

When Eva Yi Xie moved to the U.S. from China in 2016, she spoke so little English she told her teachers she “probably won’t understand what you will talk about.”

But her biology teacher helped her bone up, giving her vocabulary lists and translated tests. By 2020, Xie was a Regeneron Science Talent Search finalist on her way to MIT. She has since become a rising star in science circles. As a graduate student at Princeton, Xie is combining neuroscience and AI to understand how different portions of the brain work together to make decisions.

Xie was detained by Immigration and Customs Enforcement last week while traveling home from a neuroscience conference in Washington, D.C. While her visa had expired, Xie’s lawyers said Xie has a pending asylum application and is in the U.S. legally.

The news has shaken academics across the country, who wonder if it’s safe for international scholars to travel to present their research. “We are very privileged as a country to have people like Eva continue to pursue academia at the highest level, and to share that with other people,” one said. “That’s literally the nature and reason why we go to conferences to present stuff. It is appalling to me that she cannot do that with reasonable certainty that she can’t be detained.” Read more.

Halfway through Friday’s meeting of the federal autism coordinating committee, where members discussed the most important scientific papers about autism in the last two years, Ginger Taylor was “very angry.”

“Committee members have been robbed of their statutory duties in having limitations on what can be submitted,” said Taylor, a parent of an autistic child and the former director of the Maine Coalition for Vaccine Choice. She wanted to add more than 200 articles from her personal archives that investigate whether vaccines can cause autism. (There’s no evidence they do.)

As they had during the previous meeting of the committee, which coordinates federal agency efforts to address autism and advises RFK Jr., Taylor and several other Interagency Autism Coordinating Committee members intermittently hijacked the gathering. They suggested the group, handpicked by RFK Jr., should include research on “immunological triggers” in its annual survey of important autism research.

If you want to read the more than 500 papers submitted by the public and committee members, you can find them here. Befitting the wide spectrum of autism, the papers diverge in topic and tone. The group will vote on which papers to include in coming weeks. These votes and the group’s strategic plan will act as guides for Congress as it allocates federal autism research funding. — O. Rose Broderick

Over the last decade, the NIH has received more than 13,300 requests for grant proposals, which “has significant negative ramifications on our society,” Duncan writes. “Scientific grants contain unpublished scientific data and sensitive intellectual property.” Read what happened next, and why Duncan believes we need more protections for unpublished scientific research.
