Wine, beer, or spirits? Massive study reveals a surprising health divide

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

A massive study involving more than 340,000 adults has uncovered a surprising difference in the health risks associated with wine, beer, cider, and spirits. Although heavy drinking was linked to worse health outcomes regardless of the beverage, the picture was more complicated among people who consumed smaller amounts of alcohol.

Researchers found that low to moderate wine consumption was associated with a lower risk of death, particularly from cardiovascular disease. In contrast, similar amounts of beer, cider, and spirits were linked to a higher risk. The findings, presented at the American College of Cardiology's Annual Scientific Session (ACC.26), suggest that both the type and quantity of alcohol may influence its relationship with long-term health.

The results also reinforce a broader conclusion from previous research: drinking less alcohol is generally better for health. Importantly, the new findings do not establish that wine itself protects against disease.

"These results come from the general population, and in certain high-risk groups, such as people with chronic diseases or cardiovascular conditions, the risks could be even higher," said Zhangling Chen, MD, PhD, a professor at the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University in China and the study's senior author.

How a Study of 340,000 Adults Measured Alcohol Risk

To investigate the relationship between drinking habits and longevity, scientists examined data from 340,924 adults enrolled in the UK Biobank between 2006 and 2022. The UK Biobank is a large research resource that collects health and lifestyle information to help scientists investigate factors associated with disease and mortality.

Participants reported their alcohol consumption through dietary questionnaires when they joined the study. Researchers then classified them into four groups based on how much pure alcohol they typically consumed each day or week, rather than simply counting drinks.

This distinction matters because beer, wine, and spirits contain different concentrations of alcohol. For comparison, a 12-ounce can of beer, a 5-ounce glass of wine, and a 1.5-ounce shot of spirits each provide approximately 14 grams of pure alcohol.

People consuming fewer than 20 g (about 1.5 standard drinks) per week were placed in the never or occasional drinking group. Low consumption was defined as 20 g per week up to 20 g per day for men, and 20 g per week up to 10 g per day for women.

Moderate drinking was defined as 20 g to 40 g (about 1.5 to three standard drinks) daily for men and 10 g to 20 g daily for women. High consumption meant more than 40 g (about three drinks) per day for men or more than 20 g (about 1.5 drinks) per day for women.

Researchers followed participants' health outcomes for an average of more than 13 years, allowing them to examine how drinking patterns were associated with deaths from different causes.

Wine, Beer, and Spirits Show Different Heart Health Risks

The clearest finding concerned heavy alcohol consumption. Compared with people who never drank or consumed alcohol only occasionally, heavy drinkers had a 24% higher risk of dying from any cause. Their risk of dying from cancer was 36% higher, while their risk of dying from heart disease was 14% higher.

These increased risks were associated with heavy drinking regardless of whether participants preferred wine, beer, cider, or spirits.

However, the results became more surprising when researchers examined low and moderate alcohol consumption. At these levels, the type of beverage was associated with substantially different outcomes.

People who consumed wine in low to moderate amounts had a significantly lower risk of death, while those who consumed comparable amounts of beer, cider, or spirits had a significantly higher risk.

The contrast was especially striking for cardiovascular disease, a broad category of conditions affecting the heart and blood vessels. Moderate wine drinkers had a 21% lower risk of dying from cardiovascular disease than people who never or only occasionally drank alcohol.

By comparison, even low consumption of beer, cider, or spirits was associated with a 9% higher risk of cardiovascular death.

These percentages describe relative differences in risk between groups, not the absolute probability that an individual will die from cardiovascular disease. Because the research was observational, the findings also cannot demonstrate that choosing one alcoholic beverage over another directly changes a person's risk.

"Our findings help clarify previously mixed evidence on low to moderate alcohol consumption," Chen said. "These findings can help refine guidance, emphasizing that the health risks of alcohol depend not only on the amount of alcohol consumed, but also on the type of beverage. Even low to moderate intake of spirits, beer, or cider is linked to higher mortality, while low to moderate intake of wine may carry lower risk."

Why Might Wine Be Linked to Lower Heart Risks?

Researchers identified several possible explanations for the differences, including the chemical composition of wine and the habits of people who drink it.

Red wine contains polyphenols, naturally occurring compounds found in plants that can have antioxidant properties. Antioxidants help counter certain chemical reactions involving unstable molecules that can damage cells. Some polyphenols have been investigated for potential benefits to blood vessels and cardiovascular function.

These compounds could contribute to the associations observed in wine drinkers, although the study did not establish that they were responsible for the lower mortality risk.

Another possible explanation involves how and when people drink.

Wine is more commonly consumed alongside meals, and wine drinkers often report healthier diets and lifestyle habits. Drinking wine may therefore be associated with other behaviors that support cardiovascular health.

Beer, cider, and spirits, on the other hand, are more frequently consumed outside of meals. In this study, people who favored these beverages also tended to have poorer overall diet quality and other lifestyle factors associated with increased health risks.

Such differences make it difficult to separate the effects of the alcohol itself from the characteristics and habits of the people consuming it.

"Taken together, these factors suggest that the type of alcohol, how it is consumed, and the associated lifestyle behaviors all contribute to the observed differences in mortality risk," Chen said.

What the Findings Mean for Alcohol and Health

To make their comparisons more reliable, the researchers statistically adjusted their analyses for numerous factors that might influence mortality. These included age and other demographic characteristics, socioeconomic circumstances, lifestyle behaviors, and cardiometabolic health.

Cardiometabolic factors include conditions and measurements related to heart and metabolic health, such as blood pressure, blood sugar regulation, and cholesterol levels. The analyses also accounted for family histories of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer.

Even with these adjustments, the researchers emphasized several important limitations.

Because the study was observational, it could identify associations between alcohol consumption and mortality but could not prove that particular beverages caused better or worse outcomes. Other differences between participants may have influenced the findings despite the statistical adjustments.

Alcohol intake was also measured through questionnaires completed when participants first entered the study. That information depended on participants accurately reporting their own drinking habits, and it did not capture changes in consumption during the years that followed.

Another consideration is that UK Biobank participants tend to be healthier than the general population. This could limit how well the findings apply to other groups of people.

The researchers suggested that high-quality randomized trials could provide stronger evidence about the health effects of different drinking patterns. Such studies would help clarify whether the observed differences reflect the beverages themselves or other factors associated with their consumption.

Nevertheless, the enormous study population and more than 13 years of average follow-up provide considerable statistical strength. By examining specific types of alcohol, different levels of consumption, and multiple causes of death, the research offers a more detailed picture of alcohol's potential health risks than many earlier investigations.

The findings highlight an important distinction: while heavy drinking is consistently associated with harmful health outcomes, the relationships observed at lower consumption levels may depend on beverage choice, drinking habits, and broader lifestyle patterns.

For now, the apparent advantage among wine drinkers should not be interpreted as a reason to start drinking wine for heart health. The evidence points to a complex relationship between alcohol and mortality, rather than proving that any alcoholic drink offers protection.

Ziyue Li presented the study, "Alcohol Use at Mid-Life and All-Cause and Cause-Specific Mortality," at ACC.26.
