Even Without Warren Buffett as CEO, Berkshire Hathaway Is Outperforming the S&P 500, Nasdaq, and Dow by the Widest Margin Since 2022. History Says This Is What Happens Next.

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Berkshire remains an excellent value stock to build a portfolio around.

By Daniel Foelber – Nov 25, 2026 at 9:30AM EST

10 stocks we like better than Berkshire Hathaway ›

As of market close on Nov. 20, Berkshire Hathaway (BRKA +0.15%) (BRKB +0.11%) is up 13.4% year to date, while the S&P 500 (^GSPC -2.60%) is up just 1%, the Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI -1.35%) is clinging desperately to the green at 0.2% year to date, and the Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC -4.02%) is down 2.7%.

The reversal is nothing short of jaw-dropping. Consider that, as of Sept. 30, Berkshire had a negative 0.9% year-to-date return while the Nasdaq was up 15.6%. Now, Berkshire is outperforming the major benchmarks by its widest margin since 2022.

Here's why investors are flocking to Berkshire amid market volatility and what history says could happen next.

Warren Buffett. Image source: The Motley Fool.

After roughly six decades as CEO of Berkshire Hathaway, Warren Buffett stepped down and passed the baton to Greg Abel on Jan. 1, 2026. Abel's capital allocation principles are closely aligned with Buffett’s. In his Feb. 28 letter to shareholders, Abel summed up those principles as follows:

As CEO, Abel has eliminated or drastically reduced many of Berkshire's smaller positions and pole-vaulted Alphabet (GOOGL -5.06%)(GOOG -4.69%) from a new position in the third quarter of 2025 to Berkshire's fourth-largest holding behind Apple (AAPL -1.74%), American Express (AXP -1.09%), and Coca-Cola (KO +0.52%). However, he has also discussed his intention to maintain core sizable positions in Apple, American Express, Coca-Cola, and Moody's (MCO -0.86%) because these are companies that Berkshire understands well, has faith in their management teams, and expects to compound over the long term. In 2025, Berkshire collected a combined $1.668 billion in dividends from these four stocks, and their combined value closed the year at $158.617 billion. Berkshire's cost basis for the four positions combined is just $9.089 billion – a testament to the benefits of buying and holding quality businesses over an extended period.

Berkshire's steady approach can seem dull when the market is being driven higher by red-hot artificial intelligence (AI) stocks. But during times of intense volatility and crashing growth stock prices, Berkshire is a safe haven because of its disciplined capital allocation and fierce preservation of its reputation.

In 2022, the S&P 500, Nasdaq, and Dow all produced negative total returns (gains plus dividends reinvested), while Berkshire actually gained slightly on the year. Berkshire has posted double-digit returns every year since and is currently outperforming the major indexes by its widest margin since 2022. Over the last five years, Berkshire has exactly doubled as of market close on Nov. 20, easily outperforming the three major indexes. However, over the last decade, it is closer to in line with the S&P 500.

History shows that Berkshire tends to have its greatest periods of outperformance when value stocks outperform growth stocks. Berkshire's public equity portfolio is chock-full of value stocks and dividend-growth stocks. Today, Apple resembles a modern-day consumer-staples giant more than a tech company, especially given its steady cash flows and lower AI capital expenditures. Alphabet is ramping up AI spending, but it has historically generated very high free cash flow (FCF) thanks to steady ad revenue from Google Search and YouTube. Alphabet will likely return to positive FCF once it begins converting AI spending into profits.

Outside of its public equity portfolio, many of Berkshire's controlled businesses are steady cash cows. Berkshire generated $33.9 billion in net cash flows from operating activities in the first nine months of 2026. Its property and casualty insurance businesses, BNSF railroad, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, and ownership of several manufacturing, service, and retail businesses are worth far more combined than its public equity portfolio -- given that Berkshire's market cap is $1.21 trillion while its equity portfolio is worth $346.1 billion.

As of Sept. 30, Berkshire had a staggering $372.77 billion in cash, cash equivalents, and short-term investments in U.S. Treasury bills (T-bills). This is especially appealing to value investors given interest rates on T-bills are at multiyear highs. With a cash reserve that's worth more than its entire stock portfolio, Berkshire has ample dry powder to buy excellent companies if their valuations become too cheap to ignore.

There are timeless lessons in how Berkshire manages its operations and investment portfolio. Berkshire doesn't chase hot stocks or tether its performance to the benchmarks over the short term. Rather, it goes with its best ideas and lets compounding do most of the work.

Similarly, history says that buying Berkshire and holding it over the long term has proven to be an excellent investment. And there's every reason to believe that will continue under Abel. In fact, I could see Berkshire's returns improving if Abel casts a wider net and identifies more tech-focused companies, such as Alphabet, that have the potential to be excellent multidecade compounders.
