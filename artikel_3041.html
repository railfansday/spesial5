Zelda Producers: Ocarina of Time Remake Is a Bridge to the Future

Nintendo’s execs on how young designers reinterpreted a classic game, maximized the Switch 2’s graphics capabilities and briefly considered turning the Joy-Con into an ocarina.

Over the last month, I fell in love with a new game that’s actually old. It’s happened to me before, but Nintendo’s Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time remake drove that feeling home in a surprising way. How did Nintendo get me to fall so deeply in love again with a game I’ve already played? And why is it still winning me over, even with Grand Theft Auto 6 here?

I’m still thinking about (and playing) Ocarina of Time on the Switch 2, even with GTA 6 loaded up at home. GTA’s universe overwhelms me, but it also makes me appreciate Ocarina’s focus and clarity. And it’s also another incredible graphics showcase for what the Switch 2 is capable of. Yes, GTA 6 is jaw-dropping. But so are Ocarina’s 60fps vistas and details. Zelda pioneered 3D gaming before GTA ever went 3D.

I also can’t stop thinking about what a game like this means for Nintendo’s next wave of Switch 2 games, and a future Zelda game. Zelda’s previous games became increasingly open world, full of multiplying tasks and larger maps. It can get overwhelming. Ocarina of Time’s focus is smaller, but still tangled and immersive. Can the two concepts feed off each other? Will Ocarina help rethink where the next Zeldas are heading? And, maybe, where other open-world games are heading, too?

At Ocarina’s launch, I met in person with Nintendo’s producers for the remake, Eiji Aonuma and Hidemaro Fujibayashi, to discuss what it all meant and how the remake bridged the gap between the “old Zelda” and “new Zelda.” Aonuma has been a producer for Zelda games since the original Ocarina of Time, and Fujibayashi directed both Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom.

For this project, they brought on staffers who’d worked on Tears of the Kingdom, many of them much younger developers.

“In fact, some of them hadn’t even played the original Ocarina of Time on Nintendo 64,” says Aonuma. “We also saw this as a really interesting opportunity for this younger staff to make discoveries on the course of this project that they could bring forward on future Zelda projects that they might work on as well.”

There are a lot more subtle visual clues, I found, even than what I remembered in Nintendo’s last two Zelda games.

“When we were working on Tears of the Kingdom, we had this very high capacity to express all kinds of details and this feeling of immersion in the world, and that’s something I think was a big influence on the remake,” says Aonuma. “Whether it’s a visual detail that you might spot from a distance that you may not have in the original, that could be something that draws the player in and suddenly gives them the motivation to explore.”

What worked wonders, I found, was the quiet, subtle immersion, especially when exploring dungeons or figuring out solutions I’d forgotten decades ago. I had to study the environment for clues.

That’s the magic ingredient: a throughline in the Zelda games that Fujibayashi says is “the idea of exploration and discovery.”

Fujibayashi appreciates how the visual tools have improved, even since Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom on the original Switch.

“A system that lets you share a high, dense volume of information visually provides new ways of giving clues to the player: They can use their sight to gather the information they need to do some of the puzzle solving,” he says. “Even if you compare the graphics to Breath of the Wild, it looks slightly more realistic now. And that impacts how we approach puzzle solving and puzzle creation.”

I think about Grand Theft Auto as the new benchmark for immersion and graphic detail, but I’m also finding that a lot of GTA’s discoveries are made through pop-up interaction graphics that let you know when something can be activated. Ocarina of Time doesn’t always do this. And I also can’t help but wonder: Could the Switch 2 achieve a way to run a new wave of impressive games using optimized graphics, even something on a level like Grand Theft Auto’s latest?

Or, rather, what if Nintendo aims for a new level of hyper-immersion in its games going forward, learning from Ocarina of Time?

Speaking of immersion, I kind of wish I could hold my Joy-Con up and use it like an ocarina in the new game. You can’t, although you can use the controllers to play notes or hum or play a tune into the built-in microphone using your own voice or instrument — something lots of people have been showing off online. But the Joy-Con-as-ocarina idea almost became a reality.

“To be honest, we did briefly think about doing exactly that, but then we also considered how difficult it is to turn the Joy-Con into something that’s going to make a sound that is faithful, and then also how to develop that into the application inside of the game as well,” says Aonuma. “What we ultimately decided to do instead was to have this audio recognition feature in the software where you can use any instrument that you would like, whether it’s analog or digital, and you can play these themes and have the effect in the game and experience that way.”

What I’m really hoping for is a Majora’s Mask remake, the second N64 Zelda game that built off Ocarina’s graphics engine. Nintendo could do the same here, although Nintendo wouldn’t answer my questions about Majora’s Mask during my interview.

I’m still unlocking all the little secrets in Ocarina of Time, sinking into the world as I go. And when I play the notes on my Switch, I imagine I’m playing that ocarina. After playing this Zelda game, I wonder what Nintendo could do with that same level of detail for Mario’s return on the Switch 2, almost 10 years after the last new 3D Mario game on the Switch. We might know the answer to that soon enough.
