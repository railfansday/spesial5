You Don’t Need ‘Pervert Glasses’ to Remember Your Hike

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

A woman is holding a pair of smart glasses from Ray-Ban Meta in her hand at an eyewear store. Because these glasses allow users to take photos and videos, some outdoor and indoor swimming pools are considering banning them. (Photo: Christoph Schmidt/picture alliance via Getty Images)

In the annals of uncool accessories—the pocket protector, the cell phone holster, cargo shorts—smart glasses have already earned a place of special scorn. Unlike those utilitarian pieces, the backlash against the high-tech glasses isn’t about what they look like, though their oversized temples and chunky frames do give them a distinct Spirit Halloween look. It’s what they do: Their ability to record anyone the user looks at has led governments from Norway to New York to consider restrictions and businesses like theaters and bars to ban them. After several well-publicized incidents of users deploying smart glasses to covertly film women on the street, some detractors have labeled the wearables “pervert glasses.”

Despite that stigma, Meta, the company behind both Facebook and the most popular models of smart glasses, reportedly sold 7 million pairs of them in 2025 alone, and the eyewear has become a regular sight everywhere from bike paths to restaurants. Now, Meta wants you to take them on your next hike.

In a patent application published on October 15, Meta outlined a software update for its product that would use contextual clues like audio, speech, and data from wearables to automatically edit video captured by the glasses. The first use case the company offered: Creating a highlight reel from a recording of a hike. For example, if the glasses record the sound of a waterfall or the user saying “Look at that bird,” they could tag the footage from that moment as significant; likewise if, for example, a connected smartwatch registers your heart rate spiking. While those automatic features are still pending, hiking footage shot on the Meta glasses abounds on YouTube.

Exactly how much of the internet that search would have scoured is an open question: The company reportedly contemplated both limiting it to a user’s friends and allowing it to search everyone with a profile on Meta platforms like Instagram, Facebook, and Threads. While Meta quietly removed the software after Wired’s report, it’s been mum on its future plans for the technology, and third-party researchers have demonstrated how easy it is to livestream footage from the glasses into existing facial recognition engines. It’s easy to imagine a worst-case scenario where a stranger passing by could learn an uncomfortable amount about your life without ever saying a word. (While a built-in LED signals when the glasses are recording, some regulators have noted that it is not easily visible in bright sunlight, and models from many lesser-known manufacturers don’t have any kind of indicator.)

One might ask: Is any of this worth it? Meta has spent tens of millions of dollars lobbying the government not to regulate it. Is letting it ride shotgun on your hike like some real-life episode of Black Mirror a good idea? Will anyone actually look at the hundreds of thousands of hours of video these glasses capture, or will it just gather dust in a data center somewhere like most of the footage people shoot on their phones? (To be fair, I’ll also note here that smart glasses have been a game-changing tool for their small population of blind and low-vision users—about 8 percent of buyers cite accessibility as their main reason, according to a report by Security.org—though they don’t seem to be the demographic Meta is serving with these proposed software updates.) But I’m not here to judge how other hikers use tech. I’m more concerned with how it affects everyone else.

The backcountry means something different to every hiker, but to a lot of us, it’s an escape—from responsibilities, from city noise, and, increasingly, from being watched. We live today in a world that’s more monitored and recorded than ever before: Between automatic license plate readers, Ring doorbells, dash cams, and commercial security systems, an average urban dweller might feasibly spend their entire day hopping from one camera’s field of view to another. The backcountry is one of the last unsurveilled environments in North America; with the glasses’ new capabilities, Meta proposes to introduce what’s rapidly becoming a face-mounted Flock camera into it.

Whether we’re talking about smart glasses, stealth camping, or hiking with off-leash dogs, discussions about how we behave in the backcountry often devolve into arguments about individual freedom and responsibility. That misses the point: There are some behaviors that affect the experience of everyone around us. The person who decides to film their hike with a wearable that monitors everything they see and hear reaps all the benefits of that decision; it’s other backpackers that I foresee bearing the cost. Nor are those costs evenly distributed: A YouGov poll found that roughly two-thirds of people interested in buying smart glasses are men, while the Security study found that women are consistently more likely to report concern about the glasses’ cameras and potential facial recognition tech.

Someone will likely read this and ask what the big deal is; anyone with a phone, after all, can already record anyone they please. But there’s a big difference between pointing a camera at another person and being able to record anyone you look at, let alone run them through face ID. The trail is where backpackers feel the most unburdened—the least obligated to perform the roles we inhabit in our everyday lives. When anyone with a pair of shades could be recording you, living freely may start to feel a lot less sustainable.
