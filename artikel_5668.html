Groups Respond to Army’s Plan to Proceed with Lead Azide Detonation in Iowa

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Army says it plans to detonate degraded lead in Iowa town no later than Nov. 24, despite court stay.

Des Moines, IOWA — The Iowa Environmental Council, Sierra Club, and Earthjustice will continue to advocate for accountability from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and the U.S. Army in the planned detonation of lead azide in Middletown, Iowa. Early Sunday morning, the groups filed declarations with the court that call out the Army’s inadequate public notice and information, and question the Army’s and EPA’s claims that all alternatives were given appropriate consideration.

The U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) filed a statement with the court late on Saturday, Nov. 21, indicating that the Army intends to invoke emergency provisions of the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) to detonate 9,773.5 pounds of lead azide stored at the Iowa Army Ammunitions Plant, without a permit from the EPA. According to the DOJ’s court filings, the Army intends to do so no later than Nov. 24. On Nov. 20, the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals granted Earthjustice’s request to put on hold a permit allowing the Army to detonate.

“People across numerous impacted communities speaking up and demanding accountability is the only reason there was an opportunity to pause and think about better ways to approach the lead azide problem,” said Earthjustice Attorney Thien Chau. “Yet despite claiming public safety is its top priority and public communication is essential to trust, the Army seemed more concerned over the weekend with finding a way to sidestep the judicial process than on meeting the public’s demands for greater transparency, notice to affected communities, and far stronger protection of people, property, and the environment from serious harm.”

This emergency is of the Army’s own making. The Army has not publicly provided details about why the lead azide was not disposed of before it became so dangerous. The Army’s own storage and inspection practices allowed a primary explosive to sit until it reportedly degraded to the point that any alternative disposal method is now deemed too dangerous. The lack of transparency raises real questions about the scope and extent of potential impacts.

“The new information from the DOJ indicates that the problem is significantly worse than the Army originally shared. It appears that detonation may be, at this point, the only feasible option due to how long this problem was allowed to persist. The public deserves to know immediately which communities will be impacted and how residents can most effectively protect themselves and mitigate impacts on their health and homes,” said Michael Schmidt, General Counsel for the Iowa Environmental Council.

The Army knew about this stockpile of dangerous munitions waste for years, and it knew since at least March that the material was becoming unstable. Yet, just one week ago, most Iowans — including those directly in the path of a toxic lead plume, the Iowa governor, and key state agencies and officials — were unaware the Army intended to detonate nearly five tons of lead azide.

The environmental groups argue the EPA and Army permitting documents have not demonstrated, nor provided appropriate documentation that all other safer alternatives to mitigate the material were thoroughly explored.

“The Army has clearly mishandled this situation, and Iowans and residents in our neighboring states deserve a full review of all of the missteps that led us to this point. However, if detonation is now the only option, it is imperative that everyone impacted has access to the full range of testing, mitigation, and remediation resources,” said Wally Taylor, Attorney and Legal Chair for the Sierra Club Iowa Chapter.

As of 11 a.m. CST on Sunday, Nov. 22, the Army has failed to provide the public with updated weather modeling and mapping showing the path of the lead dust plume, which means people impacted have little time to take precautions to protect their families and homes. The wind patterns have shifted significantly, which means that potential community exposures have likely changed without visible effort by the Army to provide updated information to the public.
