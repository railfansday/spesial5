When Intelligence Becomes Abundant, What Is Left to Lead?

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Posted on Nov 24 • Originally published at ganeshraju.hashnode.dev

A CTO and CAIO's perspective on leadership in the age of AI agents: what changes for the CTO, how to grant autonomy without losing accountability, and an 18-month roadmap for the autonomous enterprise. Originally published on my blog and LinkedIn.

Picture a Tuesday night. Nobody is in the office.

An AI agent has spent the evening doing what used to take a team of five a full sprint. It read forty thousand lines of legacy code, mapped the dependencies, wrote a migration plan, generated the tests and opened the pull requests. By morning there is a crisp executive summary waiting in your inbox. It recommends retiring a platform that, as it happens, one of your plants still quietly depends on.

The plan is well argued. The numbers look right. The diagram is clean.

It is wrong in exactly one place, and that place matters.

The agent didn't fail for lack of intelligence. It failed for lack of institutional context that nobody had ever written down.

And that points to a problem far bigger than one flawed migration plan. For decades we built enterprises on the assumption that people did the work and technology supported them. We are now entering an era in which technology increasingly does the work while people remain accountable for its consequences. We are scaling execution faster than we are scaling understanding.

I keep coming back to this scenario because it captures the question I now hear, in one form or another, from almost every leadership team I sit with. It isn't "Can AI do this work?" We know it can. The real question is harder and more uncomfortable:

Does anyone in the organization still know enough to notice?

I have spent more than fifteen years building data and AI systems, contributing to open source and leading technology teams across energy, manufacturing, healthcare and enterprise software. Today I work with executives who are trying to move from AI pilots to AI-operated work. Along the way I have become convinced that most of us are framing the leadership question too narrowly.

We ask how much work we can hand to machines. I think the better question is this: when intelligence is no longer scarce, what is left to lead?

My short answer, and the thread that runs through everything that follows: when intelligence is no longer scarce, the scarce resource becomes the capacity to notice what is wrong, decide what matters and remain accountable for the outcome. As AI takes on more of the execution, leadership doesn't shrink. It moves upstream into purpose and downstream into judgment, accountability and consequences.

This piece is my attempt at an answer. It is written for CEOs, boards and technology leaders, with a particular focus on the role I believe is changing fastest and least visibly: the CTO.

For a century, we built organizations around three kinds of scarcity.

Information was scarce, so senior people knew things junior people didn't. Expertise was scarce, so it took a decade to make an architect, a controller or a plant engineer. And execution was scarce, so every analysis, every line of code and every report cost someone's hours.

The org chart, the approval chain, the annual plan and the corner office are all, in their way, monuments to those scarcities. Managers existed partly because coordination was expensive. Leaders were trusted partly because they knew more.

AI is eroding all three at once. An analyst can now model a dozen scenarios before lunch. A product manager can prototype an application without waiting for a development cycle. A small engineering team working with agents can ship what once took a department.

And that brings us to the paradox at the heart of this whole conversation. As the cost of producing answers falls toward zero, the value of knowing which answers deserve to be acted on goes up sharply. The better AI gets at sounding right, the more expensive it becomes to accept the times it is wrong. And as we automate the very work through which people used to build expertise, we risk weakening the judgment we need to supervise these increasingly capable systems.

The danger isn't that AI fails loudly. It is that an enterprise becomes remarkably productive at generating output while slowly losing its ability to understand that output.

The early evidence points the same way. In one of the largest field experiments on knowledge work, several hundred strategy consultants using AI finished more tasks, faster and at noticeably higher quality, as long as the work sat inside the model's capabilities. On a task deliberately placed just outside those capabilities, the AI-assisted consultants were less likely to reach the right answer than colleagues working without it, and their wrong answers were more convincing. Separate evaluations of models on open-ended business problems tell a similar story: answers that score well under lenient grading often fall short once every requirement is checked.

Polished is not the same as complete. Fluent is not the same as right. For leaders, that gap is where the real work now lives.

There is a quieter, human side to this. AI sounds just as confident when it is wrong as when it is right. After months of answers that turned out fine, people naturally stop checking, and review slowly becomes a rubber stamp. The errors that slip through rarely stay small. They resurface weeks later as a customer escalation, an audit finding or a question from the board, and by the time they reach leadership they have usually been compounding for a while.

If you feel as though your organization is running two operating systems at once, you're not imagining it. Most enterprises I see are caught between the model they built and the one that is emerging, and the in-between is the most uncomfortable place to lead from.

On one side sits the operating model that served us for decades. Annual budgets. Approval chains designed for people. Functional silos connected by layers of managers who relay context up and decisions down. Capacity measured in headcount. Expertise earned by doing the junior work for years. Technology delivered to the business as a series of projects.

On the other side, a new model is taking shape. Capacity gets reallocated continuously rather than once a year. Decisions are tiered by risk, with agents acting inside clear limits and named people owning the outcome. Small teams of people and agents form around outcomes instead of functions. Capacity is measured in verified results, not bodies. Expertise has to be built on purpose, because the old apprenticeship is disappearing. And technology becomes a platform the whole business builds on.

Caught between two operating models. Progress is rarely even, and the spread between your most and least advanced lines is where the friction lives.

Try placing your own organization on each line. Most leadership teams I ask find the same shape: technology has raced ahead, while the way they plan, count capacity, develop people and measure value is still anchored in the old model. That unevenness is where the friction shows up, and you can usually spot it from the symptoms. Pilots that impress in the demo and then never scale. Two sets of metrics, one for the AI program and one for everything else, that never quite reconcile. Shadow AI, because employees found useful tools faster than procurement did. Governance that lags months behind what teams are already doing. And a middle layer of managers who are anxious, overloaded and unsure whether they are being asked to lead the change or survive it.

None of this is a sign of failure. Every major platform shift has had an awkward middle. What is different this time is the speed, and the fact that the technology keeps changing while we are still reorganizing around the last version of it.

When I ask leadership teams why they aren't further along, the honest answers are rarely about the models. They are about the organization.

The forces on the transition. Most of what holds us back is organizational, not technical.

The forces pushing us forward are real. Intelligence keeps getting cheaper. Competitors are moving. Customers expect speed. Talented people expect modern tools. Boards are asking pointed questions. But the forces holding us back are just as real, and in my experience they are where leaders should spend most of their energy.

Here is the leadership insight I would underline. You rarely move the line by adding more pressure. Mandates, targets and hackathons push harder on the forward side. The faster path is usually to weaken the restraints: fix the data that blocks a high-value workflow, write the decision rights, change one incentive, give middle managers real air cover. That is unglamorous work. It is also where most of the value is hiding.

There was a time when innovation could be treated as an event: an annual strategy offsite, a stage-gate process, a new product every few years. That time is over for most industries. Product cycles are shorter, competitors appear from adjacent markets, and customer expectations reset every time a better experience shows up anywhere in their lives.

When innovation has to be continuous, the constraint stops being ideas. Most organizations have plenty of ideas. The constraint becomes the cost and speed of finding out which ideas are any good. And that is precisely where AI changes the economics.

Innovation as a system, not an event. AI accelerates most of the loop; people still own the two moments that matter most.

Think of innovation as a loop: sense what is changing, frame the problem worth solving, prototype, test, decide, then scale and learn. AI can now compress almost every step of that loop.

The goal, put simply, is to move from idea to evidence in days rather than quarters. In a world where innovation never stops, cycle time is strategy.

But two parts of the loop stay stubbornly human, and leaders should guard them. The first is framing: deciding which problems are worth solving and for whom. The second is the decision: having the courage to kill a project that looks good on paper, or to back one that the data cannot yet justify.

There is a quieter risk too. If every company uses similar models trained on similar data, ideas start to converge. Research has already observed AI-assisted work becoming more alike. The organizations that stand out will be the ones that protect originality: real customer insight, proprietary context, diverse teams and leaders with taste. AI can generate a hundred options. Knowing which one is distinctive is still a human skill.

For the CTO, this has a practical implication. Your job is no longer to approve innovation projects one by one. It is to build the system that lets the whole organization experiment safely, quickly and cheaply, and to fund that system rather than individual bets.

Any meaningful piece of knowledge work follows a chain: choose the problem, frame it, execute, evaluate, decide and own the result, then learn.

For most of business history, the middle of that chain absorbed the hours, the headcount and the management attention. Juniors executed, managers supervised execution and leaders reviewed it. AI is compressing that middle: unevenly across occupations, industries and risk environments, but unmistakably. What remains, and what increasingly becomes the job, are the two ends: deciding which questions matter and supplying the context a model doesn't have, then judging whether the output is complete, fits the real situation and deserves to be acted on.

The leadership value chain. Execution compresses; framing and judgment become the work.

This quietly rewrites the job description of every leader. Authority that came from knowing more, executing better or controlling the flow of information is eroding. Authority that comes from asking sharper questions, setting clear constraints and making accountable calls under uncertainty is about to become far more valuable.

It should also change what we measure. AI makes it trivially easy to produce more decks, more code and more tickets closed. A team producing twice as many strategy presentations has not doubled the quality of its strategy. An engineering organization producing twice as much code may simply be producing twice as much future maintenance. I encourage leadership teams to separate four questions: How much did we produce? At what cost? Did it solve the actual problem? And did it improve margin, customer trust, resilience or our strategic position? Only the last two are value.

Leaders have traditionally drawn their influence from experience, privileged information, technical expertise and control over resources. AI redistributes most of those advantages. A junior employee with strong domain context and good AI tools can now challenge a senior executive's assumptions with real analysis. A small team can produce work that once needed a department. Analytical firepower that used to live in specialist functions is available to almost everyone.

That changes the leadership contract. Authority moves from controlling information to setting direction, from approving activity to defining decision rights, and from demonstrating expertise to building an environment where expertise can be challenged constructively.

The next generation of leaders may have less informational advantage over their teams than any generation before them. Their legitimacy will come from the quality of their judgment, not the exclusivity of their knowledge.

For CEOs and CHROs, this is as much a question of culture and talent as of technology. Leaders who feel threatened by being challenged will slow the organization down. Leaders who invite it will find their teams, working with AI, smarter than any hierarchy could make them.

For three decades, the CTO has answered one question: how can technology make the enterprise more capable? The internet, the cloud, mobile, data platforms and automation each made the role bigger, but they all rested on the same assumption. Technology enabled people to do the work.

AI breaks that assumption. Technology now reasons, writes software, coordinates activity and takes actions across enterprise systems. The CTO is no longer just responsible for the tools people use. The CTO now has to help design an enterprise where people and intelligent systems share the work while people and institutions keep the accountability.

That raises questions no earlier wave forced us to confront. What happens to hierarchy when intelligence can be provisioned on demand, like compute? What happens to technical expertise when machines produce sophisticated solutions faster than most specialists? Who becomes the next generation of architects if AI does the foundational work they used to learn from? And what does executive accountability mean when an agent can take a thousand consequential actions before anyone reviews one?

The emerging CTO mandate, from technology stewardship to intelligence stewardship. Responsibilities accumulate rather than replace one another.

I find it helpful to think of the role in three layers. Technology, the work of building and operating, never goes away. On top of it sits intelligence: coordinating and governing models, agents, data, context and decision systems. And above that sits the enterprise: transforming how the business creates value, develops talent and holds itself accountable.

The distinction that matters most is between technology leadership and intelligence leadership. The first asks whether systems work as designed. The second asks whether the combined system of people, data, models and agents makes better decisions. A technically flawless AI deployment can still be a leadership failure if it erodes customer trust, creates risk nobody can see or weakens capabilities the business will need in a crisis.

The reframe I find most useful with boards is this. For thirty years we justified technology on technology ROI: costs avoided, uptime gained, projects delivered. In an AI-operated enterprise, the unit of value becomes the decision. The CTO's mandate shifts to decision ROI, which means balancing human judgment with autonomous execution, scaling decision-making without costs growing in step, and building governance into how systems actually run rather than bolting it on afterward.

A simple example makes the difference concrete. The traditional business case for an invoice-processing system promises a lower cost per invoice processed. A decision-ROI business case for an agentic version asks different questions. What share of exceptions are resolved correctly the first time? How many payment errors reach suppliers? How quickly do disputes close? How much working capital is released by paying on the right terms rather than the earliest ones? And who is accountable when the agent gets one wrong? The first case is about doing the same work more cheaply. The second is about making better decisions at scale, and it is the one a board should want to see.

That also means technology architecture and organizational architecture can no longer be designed separately. The CTO's work now runs straight through the CEO's strategy, the CFO's economics, the CHRO's workforce agenda and the CISO's risk mandate.

Where does that leave the CTO alongside the CIO, the Chief AI Officer, the CISO and the CHRO? Titles and splits vary, but a clean division looks like this. The CTO owns the architecture and the platform that agents run on. The CIO, where the role is separate, owns enterprise systems and data operations. A Chief AI Officer, where there is one, owns the AI portfolio, its value and the standards for responsible use. The CISO owns the security boundaries around autonomous action. The CHRO owns roles, skills and careers. The org chart matters less than two rules: every autonomous workflow has one named business owner, and these leaders design together instead of handing off to each other.

From the traditional CTO to the emerging CTO:

A conventional application follows a designed process. An agent interprets an objective, chooses its tools, retrieves information and decides its own sequence of steps. That changes what architecture has to answer.

Take a finance close. One agent finds reconciliation exceptions and proposes adjustments. Another checks policy compliance. A third drafts the management commentary. Now ask the questions most integration diagrams never address. Who authorized each agent, and to do what? Which data could it see? Could it actually post a journal entry? Who evaluated its reasoning? What happens when two agents disagree? And how would we catch an error before it became a material misstatement?

A reference architecture for accountable autonomy, from business intent and decision rights down to systems of record, with observability and assurance alongside.

Two principles separate serious enterprises from impressive demos. First, a semantic layer is a control, not a convenience. When an agent reports revenue, margin or inventory, the number has to come from governed definitions, not the model's improvisation over inconsistent systems. Second, the agent harness has to enforce permissions and limits regardless of what the model intends. Accountability belongs in the operating system of the enterprise, not in a polite line of a prompt.

Here is a boundary every architecture should get right. An agent investigating a supplier invoice discrepancy may need to pull the purchase order, check goods receipts, compare contract terms and draft a resolution. The right to investigate does not imply the right to change payment details or release funds. That line has to be enforced by design. The aim isn't to automate every decision. It is to make autonomy proportional to demonstrated reliability and to the cost of being wrong.

Getting that boundary right comes down to four architectural disciplines.

Intent is not authority. The agent reasons; the enterprise decides what it may do.

An AI system's ability to decide what it wants to do must never be confused with its authority to do it.

Imagine an agent notices that a critical material shipment will arrive four days late, threatening next week's production schedule. Within minutes it can analyze inventory, supplier commitments, production dependencies and alternative sources, and propose either an expedited order from a second supplier or a resequenced production plan.

But this isn't just a scheduling problem. The choice touches supplier relationships, customer commitments, product quality, contractual obligations and working capital. So the CTO makes sure the agent has reliable context and scoped authority. Operations defines the acceptable trade-offs. Finance sets the spending limits. Procurement governs what the agent may say to suppliers. And a named person is reachable for anything above the threshold.

The intelligence to optimize the process may sit in the agent. The authority to decide which business consequences are acceptable still sits with the organization.

First move: map the five agentic workflows that matter most and, for each, write down what the agent may investigate, what it may recommend and what it may actually do.

If generating software becomes nearly free, what becomes the bottleneck? Not typing. The constraints move to requirements, architecture, integration, security, operability and, above all, the ability to understand what has been built.

The early signals are sobering, if still incomplete. In one careful study run in early 2025, experienced open-source developers working on codebases they knew well were measurably slower with the AI tools of the time, yet believed afterward that they had been about twenty percent faster. One study of one generation of tools shouldn't be stretched to cover today's coding agents, and measuring agent-assisted work has become harder, not easier. Meanwhile, engineering teams with heavy AI adoption report merging far more pull requests while review time climbs. Activity looks great on the dashboard while the cost of verification quietly rises.

Velocity is no longer limited by how fast people write code. It is limited by verification capacity, observability and the organization's ability to assign ownership when no single person wrote the line. The questions a CTO should ask are changing accordingly: how much verified capability did we ship, how much rework did we create, how quickly do we catch and fix failures, and can we still understand and maintain what we built?

First move: put verification capacity and rework on the engineering dashboard next to throughput, and review them together every month.

If one engineer directs ten agents, is that engineer ten times more productive? Not necessarily. That engineer has also inherited ten streams of output to verify, ten sources of error and a coordination load nobody budgeted for.

The span-of-control illusion. Agents multiply output; verified outcomes are the scarce resource.

The metric that matters is not how many agents a person supervises. It is how many reliable outcomes that person can meaningfully stand behind. The strongest technology managers of the next decade will be the ones who can break a problem down, write a brief an agent can act on, define what good looks like and, critically, recognize when an autonomous process should stop. There is encouraging early research here: the people who lead human teams well tend to lead AI agents well too. Orchestration turns out to be a leadership skill.

The structure around those people is changing as well. When agents can coordinate work and information, the layers built mainly to relay context and approvals compress. That isn't a story of replacement. It is a redesign.

From hierarchy to orchestration. Pods organized around outcomes, with explicit human roles beside agents.

The strongest teams I see organize into small pods around outcomes, with clearly named human roles beside the agents: a workflow owner accountable for the result, a domain expert who supplies context, a reviewer who verifies, and an exception handler who steps in when the agent reaches its limits. Two cautions. Prefer the simplest design that does the job, because a multi-agent architecture you cannot observe or recover is a liability, not an achievement. And bring the CHRO in from day one, because pod design and career paths are workforce decisions as much as technical ones.

First move: pick one value stream, stand up one outcome pod with those four named roles, and compare it with the old team on verified outcomes rather than output.

An agent can have perfectly legitimate access and still do something unacceptable. It can misread an instruction, act on poisoned content it retrieved, misuse an authorized tool or chain together individually permitted steps that add up to a bad outcome. Security now has to govern not just who can access a system, but what an intelligent system is allowed to decide and do: agent identity, least privilege, policy-enforced tool gateways, transaction limits, approvals, behavioral monitoring and tested recovery. Security stops being a perimeter around applications and becomes a set of enforceable boundaries around behavior.

First move: inventory every agent that can touch a production system, and give each one its own identity, owner and limits within the quarter.

Every CTO knows technical debt. AI adds new forms that build up underneath systems that appear to be working fine.

The intelligence-debt iceberg: context, evaluation, governance, integration and cognitive debt below the dashboard.

Context debt accumulates when agents rely on stale or contradictory enterprise knowledge. Evaluation debt builds when systems ship without real test sets or ongoing measurement. Governance debt grows when autonomy expands faster than permissions and ownership. Integration debt appears when agent solutions multiply without a common architecture. And cognitive debt sets in when people stop keeping enough independent understanding to intervene. These debts compound. A company deploys agents on inconsistent master data, compensates with ever more elaborate prompts and gradually loses track of which business rules are really being applied. Everything looks fine until one significant exception exposes the fragility.

Here is how they combine in practice. Go back to the finance-close agents. The reconciliation agent relies on a chart of accounts that was restructured last year, but half the mapping still lives in an analyst's spreadsheet: context debt. Nobody built a test set of tricky reconciliations, so no one notices accuracy slipping after a model update: evaluation debt. And the two analysts who used to do the work by hand have moved on, so the people reviewing the output no longer know what a wrong answer looks like: cognitive debt. Each debt alone is manageable. Together they produce a misstatement that everyone signed off on.

First move: run an intelligence-debt review on your three most important agentic workflows, with an owner and a paydown date for every debt you find.

This may be the defining leadership challenge of the decade, so it gets its own section below.

CTOs were once asked how technology could support the business model. Increasingly, they have to help answer whether AI makes that model obsolete. If software delivery becomes dramatically faster, what happens to pricing built on effort and hours? If agents can do much of a standard business analysis, where does the value of advisory work move? If customers start interacting through their own AI assistants, who owns the relationship?

The greatest threat may not be that competitors adopt AI faster. It may be that AI changes what customers are willing to pay for.

The CTO has to be willing to challenge not just legacy architecture, but legacy economics.

First move: ask the leadership team to name the three things customers pay you for today that an AI-native competitor could offer for a fraction of the price, and what you would do about each.

Watch people work with AI and you see three distinct styles. Some work like a centaur, dividing tasks cleanly between what they do and what the machine does. Some work like a cyborg, weaving human and machine contributions together so tightly the seams disappear. And some quietly hand over the whole task and accept what comes back. The first two styles tend to build capability. The third tends to erode it, and it is the one most organizations accidentally reward, because it looks like the most efficient.

The practical leadership question isn't whether to use AI. It is how much autonomy each piece of work deserves. I ask four questions. How reversible is the action? How large is the impact if it goes wrong? How well can we measure whether the output is right? And how much context does the task need that the system doesn't have?

The autonomy allocation matrix. Autonomy is a dial, not a switch, and grows with verifiability and lower consequence of error.

A useful rule of thumb: default to the lower level of autonomy whenever any one of the four factors (reversibility, impact, measurability or context dependence) is unfavorable. Autonomy should be promoted on evidence, never assumed.

Here is the uncomfortable truth. Autonomy is rising quickly. Accountability structures are not. Every month, agents get the ability to do more, while the questions of who approved this, who owns the outcome and who can stop it are still answered the way they were when software only did what it was told.

The autonomy-accountability gap. The risk lives in the widening space between machine-speed execution and human-speed oversight.

That gap is where the next generation of serious incidents will come from, including the Tuesday-night kind. Closing it doesn't require exotic technology. It requires the discipline of naming people. I find it helps to adapt a familiar tool into what I call a RACI-O model for agentic workflows, with two important distinctions. First, the agent is the execution mechanism, not the responsible party: a named person or team stays responsible for operational control. Second, Override has to be technical, not just organizational. The person named must be able to actually stop or contain the workflow, not merely ask someone else to.

Override doesn't always mean reversal. A completed payment, a disclosure of confidential information or an action in the physical world may not be undoable. For workflows like these, the controls have to move earlier: prevention, approval gates, containment and compensating actions. If you cannot name the Override, or the action cannot be undone and nothing stops it beforehand, the workflow isn't ready for autonomy.

This is the challenge I think about most, because its consequences arrive slowly and then all at once.

Picture an engineer who joins your company in 2030. From her first week, agents write most of the code she ships. Her reviews are clean, her output is impressive and her velocity numbers are the best in her cohort. Then, three years in, a production incident hits a system none of the agents can explain. Her manager turns to her. And she realizes she has never debugged anything truly hard on her own, because she never had to.

Expertise has always been built through the work AI is now absorbing: the tedious first drafts, the failed builds, the reconciliations that didn't tie out. That struggle was the curriculum. If we remove it without replacing it, we will produce people who are fluent at supervising machines and unable to step in when the machines fail. And senior judgment only comes from people who were once juniors.

The research here genuinely cuts both ways, which is exactly why design matters. In some settings, AI assistance has helped the least experienced workers improve fastest, by passing on the patterns of top performers. In others, people who learned with unrestricted AI help performed worse once the help was taken away than people who learned without it. The tool doesn't decide the outcome. The way the learning is designed does.

Only after an oral walkthrough with a senior controller do they get production access to the agents. It costs a few weeks of throughput. The apprenticeship problem has an organizational twin. As AI generates more of an enterprise's code, analysis and decisions, the share of that work anyone fully understands shrinks. Systems keep running. Reports keep arriving. And the organization slowly loses the ability to explain itself.

Any answer of "no" or "not sure" becomes a work item with an owner, not a footnote.

The last question is the real test, and it is the Tuesday-night question in another form: does anyone still know enough to notice, and to step in? Resilience is not the absence of failure. It is the capacity to recover independently when failure comes.

Organizations that need to innovate continuously face a genuine tension. Lock AI down and the best ideas move to shadow tools. Let every team build freely and you get a thousand agents, inconsistent data and risk nobody can see.

The pattern that resolves it is familiar from the best platform organizations: federate the innovation, centralize the controls.

Federated innovation on a central control plane. Business teams build freely on shared rails.

Business teams own their use cases, their domain context and their outcomes. They are closest to the problem and should move fast. The center provides what no single team should build alone: identity and permissions for agents, the semantic layer, evaluation and observability, model access and cost controls, and the guardrails that make speed safe. Done well, the control plane is what makes it possible for teams to say yes quickly. Governance becomes a service, not a gate.

At Elixir Global, we work with enterprises in manufacturing, energy, life sciences, utilities, consumer goods and the public sector as they move from AI pilots to AI-operated work. The challenges are remarkably consistent. Institutional context is scattered across ERP platforms, legacy systems and people's heads. Governance often arrives after the agents do. And the hardest part is rarely the model. It is redesigning the workflow, the decision rights and the roles around it.

What encourages me is how quickly that changes once leaders treat it as an operating-model question instead of a technology rollout. When governance and evaluation are designed in from day one, when proven agents are reused rather than rebuilt for every team, and when the people closest to the work help shape it, organizations move faster and safer. The in-between is real, but it is very much a passage, not a destination.

When answers become cheap, the quality of a leader's questions becomes the quality of the organization's thinking. AI will answer whatever it is asked, fluently and quickly. Whether the organization is asking the right things is now the leader's job.

Every leadership team will face a choice it may not frame explicitly. When AI frees up capacity, what do we do with it?

The productivity dividend. Released capacity can be harvested as efficiency or reinvested in growth, resilience and capability.

One path harvests the dividend as cost: fewer people, the same ambitions, a better quarter. The other reinvests it: more experiments, faster cycles, new products, deeper customer work, and the deliberate rebuilding of skills. Most organizations will do some of both, and that's reasonable. But a company that only ever harvests eventually discovers it has optimized itself into a smaller version of the business it already was. In a world that demands constant innovation, the reinvestment path is the one that compounds.

If you measure only efficiency, you will get efficiency and slowly lose everything else. I suggest a balanced view across three tracks.

One scorecard, three enterprise outcomes (value, trust, capability), with agent, human and system diagnostics underneath.

Value covers cost per verified outcome, decision cycle time, revenue and margin effects, and benefit realized against what was promised. Trust covers policy violations, consequential error rates, incident severity and recovery time, and whether overrides actually work when they are used. Capability covers independent problem-solving, coverage of critical skills, successful AI-off recovery drills and bench depth in key roles.

Underneath sit the diagnostics: agent measures such as task success and drift, human measures such as decision quality and errors caught, and system measures such as rework and recovery time. They don't replace the three outcomes. They explain why one of them moved. Growth on the value track while the other two decline is not success. It is debt.

If I had to write the commitments a CTO now makes to the CEO, the board and the people in the organization, they would be these.

None of this happens in a single program. I find it helps to think in four overlapping phases.

An 18-month roadmap for the CTO. Four phases, Frame, Prove, Scale and Compound, each with an exit gate.

Treat the dates as targets, not entitlements. A phase ends when its evidence is in.

These are my perspective, not settled findings. They are meant to start arguments in the boardroom.

By the end of the decade, I expect the strongest enterprises to look different in ways that are already visible at the edges. Smaller teams accountable for larger outcomes. Agents handling most routine coordination and execution. Leaders spending less time supervising work and more time setting direction, developing people and making the calls that can't be delegated. And a new kind of technology leader at the center of it, less a builder of systems than an architect of how a company thinks.

The transition we are in is uncomfortable precisely because it asks us to let go of the ways we learned to lead before the new ways are fully formed. That discomfort is not a sign we are doing it wrong. It is the sign that it is real.

Go back to that Tuesday night, but make it 2030, in an organization that did the work.

The agent still reads the code, maps the dependencies and drafts the migration plan overnight. This time it knows about the plant, because someone made sure that knowledge lived in the context it draws on. The plan is flagged before it goes anywhere, because retiring a production dependency sits above the agent's authority. And the engineer who reviews it in the morning knows exactly what to look for, because she was trained to find what the machine misses.

Nothing about that morning is dramatic. That is the point.

For decades, a CTO's success was measured by the systems the organization could build, the platforms it could run and the technology it could deploy. The next decade asks for a different standard. Can the organization make better decisions? Can it move faster without surrendering accountability? Can it develop people who understand the systems they supervise? Can it recover when intelligent systems fail? And can it create value that competitors cannot copy simply by buying the same models?

The CTO of the past architected technology for the enterprise. The CTO of the future has to help architect the intelligence, judgment and accountability of the enterprise itself.

So, when intelligence becomes abundant, what is left to lead?

Everything that makes intelligence worth having.

I'd genuinely like to hear from you: where is your organization most caught between the old way of working and the new one? Is it the architecture, the decision rights, the talent model or the way you measure value?

The CTO's mandate is expanding from building and running technology to architecting enterprise intelligence and autonomy. That means deciding what to build, buy, generate or delegate to agents, governing agent identities, tools and actions, orchestrating human-AI teams and measuring outcomes, correctness and trust rather than velocity alone.

Accountable autonomy is letting AI agents act on the enterprise's behalf only within limits enforced by the platform: verified agent identity, policy checks outside the model, risk-tiered approvals, observability, and a named human owner who can stop the workflow. Autonomy grows with demonstrated reliability, not enthusiasm.

Use two questions: how costly is an error, and how cheaply can the output be verified? Low-consequence, easily checked work can run with sample audits; high-consequence or hard-to-verify work should stay advisory or supervised. Turn the dial down when actions are irreversible, the task builds important expertise, key context is tacit, or feedback is slow.

Intelligence debt is the hidden cost that builds up under fast AI adoption: context debt from stale knowledge, evaluation debt from untested agents, governance debt when autonomy outpaces permissions, integration debt from unmanaged agent sprawl, and cognitive debt when people lose the understanding needed to intervene.

When AI does the entry-level work, junior people lose the repetitions that used to build judgment, yet the enterprise needs more people who can tell when the machine is wrong. The answer is to design apprenticeship deliberately, from AI-off fundamentals to AI-augmented work to orchestration.
