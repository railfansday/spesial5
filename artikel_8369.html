I never once mentioned the term 'repo rate' except in response to your confusing source. I'll just lay it out one more time for future reference of people looking at the thread.

The index call price is per B-S formula N(d1)*S*e^-(d*t)-N(d2)*K*e^-(r*t)
'r' is often called 'the risk free rate' but is actually the rate at which the 'market clearing participant' can finance the physical stock basket of stocks using them as collateral. So it's somewhat specific to that underlying (the SPX in this case) and market. It doesn't need to be equal to what individual investors are (naturally) used to thinking of as the 'risk free rate', the T-bill yield, nor somewhat equivalent rates they're less familiar with (again for good reason) like the SOFR swap rate. 'r' for box spread 'is what it is' and we just need to evaluate it if we're borrowing (no credit risk to us) compared to other alternatives, also considering tax (the 'interest' will manifest as a Section 1256 mark to market capital loss, again for SPX options, 60% long term/40% short term regardless of holding period, plus whatever state tax). If instead we're investing via box spread, we need to consider the actual credit risk we're taking (risk of collapse of the broker dealer *and* the options exchange) v how much higher the rate is than T-bill (again after tax, T-bill ordinary income Fed but no state tax).

'd' is the dividend yield of the index.

The word 'repo' hasn't appeared in this or my other direct explanations and needn't. Again your source IMO is inherently confusing in adopting this term as a general monicker for the rate discounting the underlying, because the term 'repo rate' much more often refers to the rate at which *US treasuries* can be financed using those treasuries as collateral which has nothing directly to do with prices of SPX box spreads. And in the SPX case again, the discount rate attached to 'S' is the *dividend yield*, there's no reason to call it by any other more general name.
I'm sorry that you find my posts confusing. I carefully read dozens of quantitative finance sources that are linked from the Google results. I stand by my interpretation, which is also very explicitly stated in the screenshots, the actual quant finance sources of which I read and verified:
The expected dividend d, often designated as q, along with other cash flows associated with holding the underlying, go into the term that contains d, q, rrepo, or rf - rrepo (depending on the formulation). The inputs in this term represent the net cost of holding the underlying in the delta hedged portfolio that is used in the derivation of the Black-Scholes formula. This term cancels out it the case of box spreads.
The risk-free rate associated with the dollar value of the options spread is in the term that contains rf only, and represents the financing rate of dollars borrowed or invested.

In addition to what is explicitly said in the screenshots, and in all the sources that I read and verified, this also makes intuitively sense (to me at least):
Only the former term, that contains the underlying process (S), depends explicitly on the delta exposure of the option, while the latter depends heavily on the strike prices selected (K), and therefore on the dollars borrowed or invested.
Furthermore, the cashflows arising from holding the underlying (as part of a delta hedging portfolio) are interchangeable, regardless of whether they represent continuous dividends, the repo rate of the underlying, or any other cash flows: only the net cash flow from holding the underlying matters. This makes also intuitively sense to me.
