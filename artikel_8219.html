Des Linden’s Simple (and Somewhat Shocking) 100-Mile Fueling Plan

Boston Marathon champion Des Linden will toe the line at her first 100-miler on October 31, 2026, at the Javelina Jundred near Fountain Hills, Arizona. To prepare for the distance and hot desert conditions, she worked with a team of sports scientists, including Olympic-marathoner-turned-ultrarunner-champion Magda Boulet, to dial in her metabolic needs and a fueling plan. She will primarily rely on liquid calories, with the addition of some more out-of-the-box products like “cooling” menthol gels, while understanding that mid-race adjustments will be key.

For Boston Marathon champion-turned-competitive ultrarunner Des Linden, one question keeps driving her forward: “Can I do this?”

After years of racing some of the world’s most prestigious road marathons, the two-time Olympian is shifting her focus to off-road competitions. On October 31, she’ll attempt her most ambitious athletic endeavor yet when she toes the start line of the Javelina Jundred, a 100-mile trail race through Arizona’s Sonoran Desert that also serves as a Golden Ticket race into the 2027 Western States 100.

“I feel like part of me is just missing being afraid of something; the unknown,” Linden tells Trail Runner. “What is this going to pull out of me? What is this going to ask of me? That’s all really fascinating.”

In addition to the distance—nearly 40 miles more than Linden has ever covered in one go–Javelina presents a slew of unfamiliar challenges. One of the biggest is fueling and hydration over the course of 13-plus hours in desert conditions. Linden got a small taste, literally, at the Black Canyon 50K in February. It was her first ultramarathon and she acknowledged after the race that all of those years of fueling on the roads was only so helpful.

“I went into Black Canyon 50k with a loose fueling plan—to have a Gu at each aid station and sip on an electrolyte beverage between stations,” Linden said. “I wasn’t diligent about it during the race and certainly didn’t account for the time required for trail miles versus road miles. After the race, I realized it was a very easy place to make improvements, and getting it dialed before MDS would be important.”

It’s a relatable learning curve that Linden has taken seriously in the eight months since. Together with a team of sports scientists, including Olympic-marathoner-turned ultrarunning-champion Magda Boulet, Linden has tested her metabolic needs under hot ultramarathon conditions to develop a (somewhat surprising) fueling plan for October 31.

Linden retired from professional road running in 2025 after an illustrious career that included two Olympic Games, two runner-up finishes at both the Chicago and Boston Marathons, and an iconic win at the 2018 Boston Marathon—becoming the first American woman to do so in 33 years. Rather than hanging up her running shoes, Linden is targeting some of the world’s most prestigious trail races.

“I think a lot of this part of my career is getting away from comparing myself to my former self. So this is uncharted territory for me,” Linden says.

This past April, Linden, 43, competed in only her second ultra-trail event, finishing third at Marathon des Sables (MDS), a six-day stage race in the southern Moroccan Sahara often referred to as the “Toughest Footrace on Earth.” While Linden has spent her career refining how she fuels for peak performance and recovery, her MDS experience reinforced just how critical nutrition is to sustaining her endurance and rebounding after hours on her feet. She adds that ultrarunning has pushed her to reevaluate her approach to nutrition as she continues to take on longer and more demanding challenges.

“The distance itself makes nutrition so much more important,” Linden says. “You can’t cover that distance if you’re skimping or shortcutting getting the calories in throughout the day.”

The Javelina Jundred is an open and exposed course. The race is defined by heat: the average high is around 82 degrees Fahrenheit and the average low is roughly 58 degrees. Managing more time on her feet also creates more opportunities for things to go awry. Research has long suggested that heat stress can reduce appetite: A 2018 study in Nature found that exercise suppresses appetite by heating up a region of the brain responsible for regulating eating. Of course, this appetite suppression is counterproductive since you are expending lots of extra energy while running, and create a negative feedback loop that self-perpetuates.

Along with dehydration, racing in the heat creates a unique set of challenges. Linden has been working hard to prepare for them.

To prepare for the challenge, Linden enlisted experts from Gu Energy Labs, based in Berkeley, California, including Roxanne Vogel, Ph.D., nutrition scientist, exercise physiologist, and director of research and education, and Magda Boulet, Gu Energy Labs president and one of the world’s most accomplished ultrarunners. Boulet’s own evolution from Olympic marathoner to Western States 100, Leadville 100, and Marathon des Sables champion has made her a valuable resource for Linden’s own ultramarathon journey.

“I imagine most ultrarunners will look and be like ‘you’re not ready at all.’ But they don’t necessarily know that behind the scenes I have the ultrarunner,” Linden says.

Ahead of MDS this spring, Linden underwent laboratory testing at Gu’s Performance Lab with the goal of understanding her energy expenditure, hydration needs, and fueling tolerance during ultramarathon efforts. As part of the assessment, Vogel set out to measure Linden’s sweat rate and sodium losses under heat stress, her caloric demands at race pace, and how her body uses carbohydrate versus fat in simulated desert conditions.

The data helped Vogel develop an individualized metabolic and hydration profile for Linden. Vogel discovered that Linden isn’t a very salty sweater, and she also learned that Linden needs approximately 10 grams of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight per day. (For a 120-pound person, that’s roughly 550 grams of carbs.)

Going into MDS, the team developed a precise nutrition strategy for Linden, which included Gu Roctane Drink Mix along with gels and chews for her on-course fueling. After each stage, Linden leaned on nut butter and Roctane Protein Recovery Drink Mix to supply the necessary carbohydrates, electrolytes, and 20 grams of whey protein. Her dinner included a savory dehydrated potato mash with added protein and sodium. Linden also integrated Gu Roctane BCAA capsules before and after the stages to support muscle repair and reduce soreness.

Vogel says those core principles will be part of Linden’s approach at Javelina, but the demands of a single day 100-mile race in the Arizona desert will require some adjustments. For Javelina, liquid calories will be the backbone of her nutrition strategy. The target is to consume approximately 16 ounces (500 milliliters) of Roctane Drink Mix per hour. Per 16-ounce serving, it offers 250 calories, 60 grams of carbs, and 320 milligrams of sodium. Linden also plans to integrate some products that remain under development, Vogel says, including Roctane MC-318, a patented menthol-based energy gel designed to help athletes feel perceptually cooler. The product is not yet commercially available, but has already been tested by athletes at races such as Badwater 135, Western States 100, and Marathon des Sables.

The Javelina course consists of five loops along rolling desert singletrack. It’s a format that allows for both Linden and her crew to accurately anticipate her arrival at aid stations, where she’ll be able to replenish supplies and, crucially, will allow for crew to frequently assess how she’s managing the nutrition and hydration plan.

The terrain will help determine when Linden takes in different forms of fuel. Uphill sections, when she’s moving more slowly, provide an opportunity to take gels or chews. On downhills, when she wants to let her legs unwind and rip down as fast as possible, she’ll focus more on liquids. Her approach also reflects a shift from road marathon racing, where fueling opportunities are often more structured and brief.

“In the marathon, [every] 5K you grab the bottle, you want eight to 10 ounces and you want to get rid of the bottle as quickly as possible. We have the luxury of carrying it and sipping on it, and also strategizing,” Linden says.

Temperature will be one of the most significant variables. Research has found that during prolonged exercise in the heat, gastrointestinal symptoms are greater.

“People think it has to be really hot for them to need to make changes to their nutrition or their electrolyte plan,” Vogel says. “The cutoff is somewhere around 75 degrees Fahrenheit. Above that, you’re certainly going to be at an increased level of needs in terms of your hydration and electrolytes, plus also potentially your carbohydrate needs. Even in somewhat moderately warm temperatures, athletes can be needing more than what they would normally take in.”

As the temperature rises throughout the day over the course of the race, Linden anticipates leaning more heavily on liquids. The drink mix can provide fluid and carbohydrates while helping her meet her electrolyte needs, and gels with water will fill any gaps.

“She’s never done a [single day] race this long. The longer you’re out there, the more likely your gut is going to have some issues,” Vogel says. “When you’re exercising, blood flow is going to your working muscles. Less of it is going to your digestive track. So that’s one thing that’s putting stress on the system that makes it harder to digest and absorb [nutrition]. Hence the whole reason for things like gels. They’re easy to digest.”

During the hottest part of the day, Vogel says the team may decrease Linden’s carbohydrate intake to account for changes in digestive tolerance. But as temperatures fall into the night, they’ll look to increase her carbs. Vogel says caffeine will require similar consideration.

“During very hot weather events, caffeine can actually be triggering to the gut in a bad way,” Vogel says. “Rather than following a fixed schedule regardless of conditions, we might actually pull back on caffeine during the heat of the day and start to strategically add it back in as it cools off later in the day.”

Another factor to consider: flavor fatigue, when sensory receptors on your tongue that detect flavor become less responsive with repeated consumption.

“Flavor fatigue is something we know and understand. In hot environments, you don’t generally want to have things that are what we call indulgent—chocolates and caramels and vanillas that are going to stay heavy on the palate. We want more things like citrus and light fruity or even sour, tart flavors that are going to clear the palate or stimulate some thirst,” Vogel says.

Ultimately, the goal is to make the strategy simple to execute, Vogel says. Linden’s success will depend not only on having a precise nutrition plan, but on adjusting on the fly when fatigue and discomfort begin to accumulate.

“I feel like it’s a race within itself. Did I try to execute as well as possible? Did I follow the plan? Did I make good decisions?” Linden says. “I want to finish, and I think there’s so many things that can go in so many different directions in that amount of time and that distance. So if I fueled enough to get myself through 100 miles, that’s a victory.”
