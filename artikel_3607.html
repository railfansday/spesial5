Exclusive: ‘Breaking Bad’ Star RJ Mitte, in His Own Words

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

For those of us who managed to land a role on Breaking Bad, the show is a formative part of our lives. It represented a chance to work on what is now considered one of the best shows TV has ever seen, and the cast and crew forged an unbreakable bond. RJ Mitte was part of the show’s heartbeat. When I spoke to him for this book, we reflected on our days shooting in New Mexico fondly.

I’ll always remember him as a young kid—bright, talented, and a real sweetheart—who was so thrilled to be on set with us that it reminded us how lucky we all were. We'll all miss him dearly. Rest in peace, RJ. —Dean Norris

The last person to join the White family was RJ Mitte, cast in the role of Walt Jr., aka “Flynn.” RJ, who was only fourteen years old at the time, joined the project after we had already begun filming the pilot, following multiple rounds of auditions stretching out over several months. You might wonder why casting Walt Jr., a comparatively minor character among the core cast, took so long. Well, it was about verisimilitude. As written, Walt Jr. had cerebral palsy, albeit a relatively minor case. It was a fundamental part of the character, and Vince was adamant that the role not be played by a nondisabled actor.

VINCE GILLIGAN, creator: I was worried at the beginning that people wouldn’t connect with Walt. They’d say, Why is this guy doing this terrible thing? And so I kind of loaded him up for bear in terms of, you know, he had a son with cerebral palsy. You felt bad for him. People bullied him.

SHARON BIALY, casting director: We had a lot of autonomy with casting, once we knew what Vince was looking for, once we all agreed that, aesthetically, we were talking about the same type of actor. A case in point is RJ Mitte’s role. Vince only wanted actors who had CP. So, we conducted a nationwide search.

RJ MITTE, Walt Jr.: Walt Jr. is actually based on a real person, a friend of Vince’s from college who had cerebral palsy. So, Vince was really particular about the role of Walt Jr. and who was going to play him.

Experienced actors who also have mild CP is not the deepest pool, of course, but there are more candidates than you might expect. And RJ Mitte’s casting has to be considered something of a surprise, given his relative lack of experience and a variety of other challenges he faced while growing up.

RJ MITTE: We were living in Houston when my sister got discovered at a water park, and that’s what brought us to LA. My sister got booked on a job at Universal Studios for an ad campaign. She was one and a half at the time. We really didn’t have a lot going on. Things were tough. My mom was a single mom, and she had recently been partially paralyzed in a car accident, and my grandparents, who I was very close to, were both sick and dying, so when we got this offer to go to LA, we took it. It was a paid trip. They put us up in a motel in Hollywood—there was a strip club on one side, and a Ralphs and a library on the other side. That’s LA.

Everything was covered for six months, so I just started acting to meet kids my own age and have a little extra cash while we were there. And it ended up turning into a career for me. I started working at a young age, not in the acting industry, but with my uncle and family jobs and stuff to make money as a young kid. I would like to think I grew up with a strong work ethic. So, when we got to LA, I hit the ground running. Within the first six months, I was on shows like Hannah Montana, Everybody Hates Chris, Weeds, Seventh Heaven, Drillbit Taylor, Epic Movie—­ all as an extra. I think that really helped prepare me for Breaking Bad, just with the quality of work and the type of professionalism that the set had. I’m very grateful, because if I did not get Breaking Bad, I wouldn’t have an acting career.

At any number of points along the journey, RJ’s dream could have died. It’s hard enough for a known actor to land a role in a show of this quality, but for a newcomer, it must have been daunting, although you never would have known it by the way he carried himself on set.

RJ MITTE: Funny story: My very first audition ever in my career was an anti­ meth campaign for the state of California. It was a wild PSA, and I ended up not getting it. A year or so later, I started auditioning for Breaking Bad. The description of the role literally said, “dark hair, big eyebrows, and mild cerebral palsy.” And I was like, that’s me!

Sharon Bialy called me in, and I already had been on quite a few auditions by that point, and I didn’t book any of them, so I knew what rejection felt like. I had a sense of, Don’t hold your breath. But I’m a firm believer that you go in owning it and when you walk out, don’t look back. Every callback was a little more like, Wait, I’m actually still in the running.

Walt Jr. had a somewhat more severe case of CP than RJ, who did not use assistive devices for mobility in real life.

RJ MITTE: When I went in for my first audition, Sharon was there, and she had hurt her foot. So, she actually handed me her crutches that she was using for the audition. Six months went by, four callbacks, four auditions, and in that final one, they’re like, Okay, we’re sending you. You’re going to New Mexico. The next day I was on a 6 a.m. flight to audition for Vince and the team. Then he excused me for a moment and brought me back in to do my test screening with Anna and Bryan.

Think about that. RJ was fourteen years old and trying to land a job that would change his life, a job that would help him support his entire family. Talk about pressure.

RJ MITTE: I think in life I’ve always had pressure. It really didn’t stand out to me too much. I can only do what I can do. And so as long as I’m doing my best, that’s what matters. But it always kind of felt like I was part of the team to some degree, even when I wasn’t. And so when it came to doing the scene with Anna and Bryan, it really felt like a family. It was like, Oh, yeah, this feels right. Apparently, they must have had the same feeling because they hired me.

Like everyone else, RJ made the most of his opportunity and crafted a fully formed portrait of an adolescent at the center of a family storm; one whose disability may have helped shape him but did not define who he was.

RJ MITTE: This was the first time I think people really saw a character with a disability who wasn’t disabled. There’s nothing he couldn’t do that other kids were doing. It wasn’t about the disability, and that’s something that I think Vince really stressed: it’s not about his disability; it’s about being a part of the family and living with it.

Five seasons in, Breaking Bad reached its apotheosis with the monumental episode “Ozymandias,” in which Walter White’s house of cards comes crashing down. It’s also the episode where Flynn learns the truth about his father’s double life, and that Walt has led my character—his brother-in-law, Hank—to his death. The episode builds to a climactic moment where Flynn’s mother, Skyler, grabs a knife and brandishes it at her husband.

RIAN JOHNSON, director, “Ozymandias”: The actual fight itself, I remember being surprisingly fun to shoot—­just making sure to give Anna and Bryan the space that they needed to find it. I think RJ also did a terrific job in that moment—­ he’s kind of the linchpin of the whole thing.

It is indeed Flynn who rises to the occasion. When Walt wrestles the knife away from Skyler, his son jumps on top of him and throws him across the room. He’s a big kid, strong enough to handle Walt despite his disability; strong enough to protect his mother.

RJ MITTE: It was very intense to shoot. At the same time, we’re professionals. Everything was very choreographed. There was one moment when we first started, I got so heated, and so into it, that I kind of accidentally grabbed Bryan the wrong way and kind of like tossed him . . . wrong. And everyone was like, “Whoa, RJ! You can’t throw him like that.” And I was like, “Oh, I’m so sorry. I just got caught up in the moment.”

RIAN JOHNSON: When you see Walt Jr. turn, then you know it’s truly over. And Bryan’s reaction to that just breaks my heart every time I see it.

In the series’s penultimate episode, Walt clandestinely calls Flynn at school. A package is on its way, he says, to be delivered to the home of one of Flynn’s friends. The package contains money, one hundred thousand dollars—­the most he could stuff into one box. “I wanted to give you so much more, but this is all I could do,” Walt explains, leaning into the pay phone as Flynn listens, wordlessly, on the other end.

RJ MITTE: I remember at the time we were shooting that scene it was in this school-slash-doctor’s-office that we were using. Bryan was in the room with me when we did this part. It definitely was heartbreaking to have to feel that way about someone that I admire. And I think that’s what Junior felt as well. I’m a believer that in life, there’s always a moment where you have to choose to either be an adult or to stay a child. This moment comes in many different forms, but it comes down to that moment where you say, Am I going to accept this and give them what they want and be submissive, and still have that child aspect of who I am? Or am I going to take charge and become the person that I need to be—­ the man, the adult?

RJ does some terrific wordless acting in this scene, wrestling with this decision. Then he speaks: “Just shut up! Just stop it. Stop it. I don’t want anything from you.”

“You need this money,” Walt says. “Your mother needs this money. It can’t all be for nothing.”

Yes, it can, as Flynn makes abundantly clear.

RJ MITTE: A lot of the decisions I made for Junior were based on my own experiences as a young adult. At the time I was still a child, but I utilized those moments from my own life of having to stand my ground and make that decision—­ realizing who I am and what I need to do to take care and provide for my family. In the show, Walt was no longer doing that. He was actually creating more danger for his family.

I think it’s very powerful that Walt Jr. had the ability to stand his ground, and to put those boundaries in place: “You’re out. This is my family now, and you’re not a part of it.” I find a lot of young men and women have to take that stance at some point, especially with parents or family figures that live in a volatile world, a world that is corrupt.

Later, in the series finale, Walt is standing over a crib, caressing the hair of baby Holly as she naps. He leaves and then watches from a distance as Flynn gets off a school bus and walks into the apartment.

VINCE GILLIGAN: With Walt, you want him to have a goodbye scene with his wife. We were sad we couldn’t have a goodbye scene with his son, Walt Jr., because RJ is a wonderful actor. We just didn’t have the room.

For those of us who were fortunate enough to be a part of it, Breaking Bad remains a seminal event in our lives, both personally and professionally—­ an opportunity to work on one of the greatest television shows in history, alongside people who became lifelong friends.

BRYAN CRANSTON: I knew when it ended that this was the role of my life.

AARON PAUL: I think I can speak for everyone involved in the show: It changed all of our lives.

RJ MITTE: I will be eighty years old in a nursing home and there will probably be someone looking at me through a window, saying, “There’s Walt Jr.” And I’m okay with that.
