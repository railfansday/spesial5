A troubling reality hangs over the United Nations climate conference (COP31) opening in Turkey today: overshoot. For the first time, the annual meeting of the world’s countries to hash out action on climate change must face the fact that it is now inevitable for global temperatures to exceed the 1.5 °C warming limit pledged in the Paris Agreement. Call it ‘the overshoot COP’. “We need to first recognize that global policy has failed. This is a moment of self-critique,” says climate scientist Johan Rockström.

Environmental journalist Fiona Harvey, who has been to almost every UN climate conference for more than 20 years, asks: what’s the point of COP? “The convention that all resolutions must pass by consensus has given bad actors free rein,” she writes. The Paris agreement itself has “reached an impasse”, not least because of the withdrawal of the United States. But China — the world’s biggest carbon emitter — is more engaged in multinational negotiations than ever, partly because of its huge stake in green technologies such as solar panels. And the impact of the UN process is everywhere, from the rise of electric cars to the energy transformation. “Any time you have a consensus decision, it sends a signal,” says Sue Biniaz, a former US climate negotiator. “These days with the geopolitics of the world — if something can be agreed by the whole world, it’s impactful.”

A new artificial-intelligence system enables researchers to enhance the performance of robotic equipment in their labs. In the process, scientists describe their research programme and lab set-up to the AI agents, which generate step-by-step experimental protocols for the lab’s robots. The system can also generate a virtual version of the lab in which to test its suggestions, and even devise a new floor plan that optimizes the placement of lab robots and equipment. The “very cool” concept could drastically reduce the effort required to set up automated labs, says bioengineer Evan Collins.

People seem to find conversations more enjoyable if they contain more pink noise — sound in which there is equal energy per octave of frequency. Researchers analysed 1,000 conversations between strangers and found that participants felt a stronger sense of connection with their partner when their chats had featured more pink noise. The team also found that chats between podcast hosts comprise more pink noise as the series continues, which suggests that making a conversation ‘pinker’ is a skill that can be honed, says psychologist and study co-author Kathryn O’Nell.

A separate research team found that pink noise can also help to kickstart the brain’s ‘wash cycle’ during sleep. They found that timing bursts of pink noise to ‘slow’ brain waves can encourage cerebrospinal fluid to flow through the brain, clearing out any harmful particles that have accumulated during waking hours. The team says that the pink pulses might activate a particular kind of slow wave that silences distractions.

In his new book, On the Equality of All Things, physicist Carlo Rovelli writes that fundamental physics is overrated. “It’s great science,” he tells Nature. “But stop thinking that it is the root of everything else.” Physics also has a hubris problem, he says, which can lead to wild goose chases for evidence of phenomena that aren’t real. Discoveries in quantum mechanics and general relativity have brought us to the cusp of fresh conceptual breakthroughs, but it might take some time for a coherent picture to emerge from the noise, he says.

Compared to the dangers of extreme weather, “little attention has been given to what happens when El Niño disrupts food supplies, affects what people eat and potentially changes their exposure to infectious disease,” argue economist Eoin McLaughlin and management scholar Matthias Beck. “One advantage with El Niño is that there is plenty of warning,” they note, urging governments and agencies to prepare for the weather pattern’s possible effects on food and health systems.

Some men looking for emotional support are turning to commercial chatbots and specially designed AI apps, partly because of the stigma around traditional mental health treatment. “You don’t have to make an appointment. You don’t have to go sit in a waiting room. You don’t have to even really explicitly take that initial step to ask for help,” notes psychiatrist Michael Heinz. But is therapy-like discussion with an AI system better than no therapy at all? “I think [AI app] founders have to realize therapy does have a rate of harm,” says psychiatrist John Torous, who specializes in digital mental health. “You need some rules of the road.”
