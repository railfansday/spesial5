MIT Review Acclaims zk-SNARKs, but zk-STARKs May Steal the Show

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Updated look back: this article revisits a 2018 story with what has happened since.

The short version: STARKs took a large share of the stage, SNARKs adapted, and Bulletproofs found a narrower job. Here is how the three compare and how the story played out.

A zero-knowledge proof lets you prove that a statement is true without showing the data behind it. MIT's examples were proving you are over 18 without giving your birth date, or proving you can afford a payment without showing your balance.

The idea is old. According to ethereum.org, it first appeared in a 1985 paper. What changed was that it became usable. MIT's entry, written by Mike Orcutt, credited much of that progress to Zcash, a currency launched in late 2016, and listed Zcash, JPMorgan Chase and ING as key players.

The same entry named two drawbacks. zk-SNARKs were slow and heavy to compute, and they needed a "trusted setup". It added that researchers were already looking for alternatives without those problems, but did not name any.

zk-SNARK stands for zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive argument of knowledge. "Succinct" is the selling point. Zcash's documentation says a proof is only a few hundred bytes and can be checked in a few milliseconds, even for a very large computation.

That is what makes shielded Zcash transactions possible. The transaction is encrypted on the blockchain, yet the network can still confirm that it follows the rules.

The cost is the setup. Early SNARKs needed a one-time ceremony that produced public parameters. The ceremony also produced secret randomness, and Zcash's own documentation says anyone who kept it could forge proofs and create counterfeit coins. Zcash ran two multi-party ceremonies, one for its Sprout version and one for Sapling, so that no single person held the secret. Users still had to trust that at least one participant destroyed their share.

The STARK paper, "Scalable, transparent, and post-quantum secure computational integrity", was submitted to the IACR ePrint archive on January 10, 2018 by Eli Ben-Sasson, Iddo Bentov, Yinon Horesh and Michael Riabzev. Its title states the three goals.

Transparent means no trusted setup. STARKs use public randomness, so there is no secret to leak.

Post-quantum refers to the underlying math. Ethereum.org explains that SNARKs depend on elliptic curve cryptography, which a future quantum computer may break, while STARKs rely only on collision-resistant hash functions.

Scalable means the work of checking a proof grows far more slowly than the computation being proved.

The trade-off is size. STARK proofs are larger, and ethereum.org notes that this makes them more expensive to verify on Ethereum. Figures quoted in the Ethereum Stack Exchange thread that cites this article give a sense of scale: about 288 bytes for a SNARK against about 45,000 bytes for a STARK in one comparison, and "hundreds of kilobytes" in one developer's real project. Those numbers depend heavily on the computation, so treat them as orders of magnitude.

Bulletproofs came out of Stanford in 2017, from Benedikt Bünz, Jonathan Bootle, Dan Boneh, Andrew Poelstra, Pieter Wuille and Greg Maxwell. Like STARKs, they need no trusted setup.

They were designed for one job: proving that a hidden amount in a confidential transaction falls within a valid range. The Stanford project page says they cut that proof from over 10 kB to under 1 kB. It also says a Bulletproof takes longer to verify than a SNARK, which is why they are not used for proving large computations.

Monero added Bulletproofs in its October 2018 network upgrade. Its documentation reports that average transaction size fell by at least 80 percent.

Partly. Three things happened after 2018.

STARKs reached production. Eli Ben-Sasson, the paper's first author, co-founded StarkWare with Alessandro Chiesa and Uri Kolodny. The company used STARKs first in StarkEx, a scaling service for individual clients, and then in Starknet, which launched its Alpha on Ethereum mainnet in November 2021. Starknet's documentation describes it as a permissionless validity rollup that settles on Ethereum using STARK proofs.

SNARKs fixed their own weakness. PLONK, published in August 2019 by Ariel Gabizon, Zachary Williamson and Oana Ciobotaru, is a universal SNARK: one setup can serve many different programs instead of one ceremony per program. Halo went further. Zcash says Halo removes the trusted setup entirely, and its Orchard shielded protocol, activated with the NU5 upgrade in May 2022, runs on the Halo 2 proving system.

That last point matters for the old "SNARKs need a trusted setup" comparison. It was accurate in 2018. It no longer holds for every SNARK.

The fair verdict: STARKs did not push SNARKs aside, but they did prove that transparency and hash-based security work at scale. The choice today is less about which family wins and more about what you need: the smallest proof, no setup, or protection against quantum attacks.
