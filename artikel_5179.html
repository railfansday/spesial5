DBS and JPMorgan Launch 24/7 Tokenized Dollar Transfers Across Borders

DBS and JPMorgan’s Kinexys have launched a service connecting tokenized dollar deposits in Singapore and the United States. The November 16 announcement moves a cross-bank project into commercial use, with Ant International as its first customer. The practical question is how much flexibility a treasury team gains when money can move between its banks outside the usual working day.

The DBS announcement describes a connection between permissioned blockchain accounts, using Partior for settlement orchestration. That creates a route between two banking environments rather than requiring a company to keep every participating account with the same provider.

For a business with separate regional operations, that distinction can be useful. Cash held for one subsidiary may be unavailable to another at the moment it needs to pay a supplier. A dependable interbank connection could reduce the need to position money in several places long before it is needed. The benefit would depend on the company’s access, account structure and funding arrangements.

The banks’ November 2025 framework announcement discussed interoperability across public and permissioned blockchains. It included the possibility of exchanging a JPMorgan deposit token on Base for equivalent value through DBS. Monday’s launch concerns the specified permissioned account networks; the broader examples in the earlier plan should not be treated as features all delivered at once.

Readers following JPM Coin’s separate expansion to Ethereum will recognise the distinction. A bank can support several digital-money products, with different eligible users and transfer rules. Connecting one pair of services does not automatically connect every token carrying a related brand.

DBS’ Token Services documentation places programmable payments and liquidity management within the same product family. Its Treasury Tokens service is designed to move value between group entities across markets. The new interbank link adds another possible route for those kinds of treasury workflows.

Consider a company that receives customer payments in one location while its operating expenses fall due elsewhere. It may otherwise keep a buffer in both places, even when its total cash position is comfortable. If transfers become reliable outside local banking hours, it could adjust those buffers more frequently. That is an operational possibility, not a savings figure established by the launch.

The same timing issue appears in Hong Kong’s work on continuous tokenized settlement. Extending a trading or business window is useful only if usable money can reach it. Treasury teams would still need to compare the full journey, including conversion, approvals, reconciliation and access to their ordinary bank accounts.

DBS identifies one limitation: moving funds between legacy demand-deposit accounts and Kinexys blockchain accounts has a Saturday downtime from 3 p.m. to 6 p.m. Eastern time. Transfers inside the blockchain account network remain available around the clock.

That qualification is especially relevant for a customer planning weekend funding. Money already positioned in the appropriate account can have a different availability profile from money that first needs to enter it. An internal payment policy would have to reflect both routes rather than rely on a single headline about continuous service.

A named customer and a live connection give this project a firmer footing than an exploratory agreement. The commercial assessment now turns on repeated transfers: whether treasury teams can access the balances they need, account for them correctly and complete a payment at a predictable total cost.
