In Security, Words Matter as Much as Tools — Maybe More

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

From talking cloud security to using AI, cyber leaders are finding that clear, disciplined communication is now a front-line defense capability across the enterprise.

The best tool cyber teams can wield to defend their organizations has nothing to do with tech; it's how they talk. Whether the audience is the C-suite, the development team, or the sheepish user who just fell for a phish, communications savvy is increasingly mission-critical.

During a Dark Reading Virtual Event panel I recently put together to talk tech, tools, integration, and frameworks, called "Key Tools & Technologies for Locking Down Your Cloud," SANS instructor Ahmed AbuGharbia urged cybersecurity teams to "learn the language of the cloud" to build credibility with IT, and to do the same for developers. We discussed technology and products, too, but I was surprised at the amount of time that was spent on stressing the importance of communication and language when it comes to having effective conversations with vendors, executives, and users.

My other panelist, Theresa Lanowitz, a principal analyst at Omdia, detailed the distinction between security "of" the cloud and security "in" the cloud, noting that under existing shared responsibility models, cloud service providers assume responsibility "of" the cloud itself, rather than what organizations decide to put "in" the cloud.

This wasn't the first time I had noticed a shift in the way security practitioners focus on a commitment to using effective language.

A couple of weeks ago I sat in several sessions at the ISC2 Strategic Conference in Denver that spent a remarkable amount of time on strategies for clearly communicating risk and documenting those exchanges in much the same way as they would any other log, piece of evidence, or asset. In the past, these conversations would have been more focused on translating tech talk into corporate speak. CISOs have worked for a long time on how to talk to leadership about risk and budget; it's been an ongoing conversation for several years. However, emphasis on how cyber teams can learn to ask the right questions not just of leadership, but pertinent people both inside and outside the business, feels like a fresh effort. Using precise language and presenting strategic (not just technical and tactical) concepts effectively is no longer being discussed as a nice thing for security pros to be able to do, but critical to fundamental enterprise defense.

For example, CISO and expert Laura Vaughn has cleverly broken down threat modeling into 4 Simple Questions on a White Board. Her strategy distills years of experience and technical expertise into plain language anyone can understand, like, "What [cyber incident] would hurt most on Monday morning?" That honed language helps get teams to the heart of the threats, and helps them understand the work that needs to happen next. That all saves time and money, and eliminates the scariest risks first. Other deceptively simple questions she suggests asking include, "Who hacks organizations like ours and how?" And, "What is the cheapest control?" Smart comms informs and focuses the technical work.

Likewise, technologist and attorney Daniel B. Garrie, CEO of Law and Forensics, presents language as a frontline defense, and coaches CISOs and cyber leaders on the finer points of contract negotiations. He advocates rigorous documentation of communications on risk and defense decisions, so those decisions would be defensible later, and so that transparency can help drive cybersecurity across the organization.

The introduction of AI is also requiring cybersecurity teams to pick their words carefully. AI has put a language interface between the user and compute operations that benefits those who can ask the best questions. Likewise, experts across the cyber sector urge caution around using humanizing language to describe AI agents or attribute human motivation to their actions.

Being an expert communicator is no longer a skill that's nice to have in cybersecurity; it's fundamental to enterprise defense. Be precise in the words you choose, and meet your audiences where they are. You might be surprised to find it's becoming the most powerful tool you or your organization could ever deploy against the next threat.
