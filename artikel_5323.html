Companies want AI-ready workers. Are they willing to adapt, too?

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

by Lisa Stiffler on Nov 27, 2026 at 1:00 pmNovember 27, 2026 at 11:48 am

Companies want workers who can adapt to AI. Arif Gursel has a question for those employers: Are they willing to change, too?

Preparing people for technological change cannot fall entirely on students, universities or employees trying to keep their skills relevant, said Gursel, a serial entrepreneur who runs training programs in coding, AI and other fields.

“A skilled and adaptable workforce also needs a skilled and adaptable workplace. Those things have to be in balance,” he said, speaking at a recent Technology Alliance event focused on building talent in Washington state.

A study released Monday by the Schultz Family Foundation points to a related disconnect. Based on nearly 3,000 interviews with young adults, employers and others, it found that 79% of Washington businesses want new entry-level workers to have mid-level AI skills or better. Slightly less than half of the young adults surveyed felt their schooling cleared that bar.

The study didn’t examine whether employers are changing how they train workers. It did find that more than 80% of employers are interested in internships, apprenticeships and similar programs, but 77% say they lack the staff time to make that happen.

Gursel said the challenge extends to experienced workers, too. He called on employers to help design training, connect it to actual jobs and make the workplace somewhere people can develop new capabilities, rather than simply replacing those whose skills no longer match the task.

He offered an example from a past coding training program he helped build with Microsoft, a company where he worked for more than a decade early in his career. Gursel, founder of the corporate strategy firm 8genC, partnered with a Microsoft hiring manager and together they developed a curriculum and the manager committed two open positions to graduates.

Gursel argued that AI tools present an opportunity to build those kinds of partnerships again, with companies helping community colleges and nonprofits prepare people for work rather than leaving educators to guess what employers will need.

Ingrid Stegemoeller, of the Washington Roundtable’s Partnership for Learning, echoed some of those ideas in a statement about the Schultz family study.

“If we make it simpler for employers to share clear signals about the jobs and skills they need with schools, colleges and training programs, we can turn their participation into coursework and work-based learning that prepare young people for those jobs,” she said.

Coleman Bryant, a technology innovation master’s candidate at the University of Washington, also spoke on the panel. He raised concerns about AI and education: Students, he said, can end up “offloading all of the critical thinking to the AI” instead of working out solutions on their own.

He called for hands-on, interdisciplinary education that requires people to connect technical knowledge with business, design and collaboration.

The study indicated schools may be sending mixed signals on AI. About as many young adults said their institution discouraged AI use (36%) as said it helped them learn to use it (34%).

There’s also the fundamental issue of access to AI tools, Gursel said.

“There are underserved and under-resourced and marginalized communities all across Washington state, especially as we think about our tribal communities, our indigenous communities, our Black and Brown communities,” he said.

“How do we build the bridge for that digital divide that is just widening as a result of AI?” he asked.
