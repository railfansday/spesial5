Big Sean and Jalen Rose made it big. Now they’re making sure they keep showing up for the city that raised them

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

For the 13th draft pick Jalen Rose, loving Detroit means showing up for the city on the good days, the bad days, and all the ordinary ones in between. The former NBA star—whose mother created the name Jalen—has spent years giving back to the place that raised him. And along the way, he’s learned what separates those who lift Detroit from those who only show up during election time.

“You show up during a holiday or election, that type of thing, but you’re not here on a Wednesday when it’s the middle of January and it’s freezing outside,” Rose said Tuesday at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum in Detroit.

Sitting beside him was multi-platinum rapper Big Sean, whose real name is Sean Michael Leonard Anderson, who also grew up in Detroit and has turned his success into opportunities for young people back home. Between Rose’s tuition-free high school, Big Sean’s recording studios, and their growing entertainment ventures, the two are investing in Detroit’s next generation while trying to make the city a place where young talent can build careers of their own.

Rose understands firsthand what it means to grow up without much. Some days, his family didn’t have heat or hot water, so they relied on kerosene heaters and boiled water to wash.

“My motivation was growing up poor and watching, you know, my mother go to work,” he said.

As a kid, he promised his mother he’d make it to the NBA and get their family out of poverty. He even started writing “Dr. J” on his school papers, borrowing the nickname of the basketball legend Julius Erving.

“I’m going to the league. You know, I’m gonna get us out the hood,” he recalled telling her. He kept that promise, playing 13 seasons in the NBA and never forgetting the people who helped him get there.

Now, his Jalen Rose Leadership Academy is completing its 16th school year, serving 460 high school students and supporting roughly 1,100 graduates pursuing education after high school, Rose said. And the work is paying off, as Rose noted that the school has Detroit’s highest student index, a distinction he takes immense pride in.

And Big Sean has been in Rose’s corner since long before they shared a stage Tuesday, attending the academy’s first graduation back in 2015. More than a decade later, the students keep Rose motivated.

“You know, our young people, the way they receive it just makes me want to work harder for them because they deserve the opportunities,” Rose said.

Giving back started long before Big Sean went platinum with hits like “Blessings” and “Bounce Back.”

His grandmother served as a captain in the Six Triple Eight, the all-Black, all-female Army unit that sorted military mail during World War II. Growing up, he watched her make helping others a family tradition, no matter how much the family had.

“No matter how good or bad the years were,” Big Sean recalled, his grandmother made sure the family donated clothes and supported people in need—a lesson that stayed with him as his music career took him around the world.

The Sean Anderson Foundation is run by his mother, who was backstage Tuesday while his father and brother cheered from the audience. Through it, he has built recording studios at Boys & Girls Clubs, giving young people a chance to learn not only how to perform but also how to become sound engineers and work behind the scenes.

The first of those studios opened at his alma mater, Cass Technical High School, where Big Sean once risked suspension for selling his own CDs. Years later, he had his own kind of “Bounce Back” moment, returning to the school not to sell music, but to help the next generation make it.

“It was cool to see a full-circle 180 moment,” he said.

The work has since expanded to the new Boys & Girls Club at Detroit’s restored Michigan Central Station, where he and Usher backed the $1 million Detroit Entertainment Innovation Incubator, which opened in February, to give young Detroiters access to creative and technical training.

There, young people can explore everything from engineering and film makeup to sneaker resale and tech. Big Sean said seeing young people from those programs land jobs has been one of the most rewarding experiences of his career.

“It’s the first time that I’ve seen all the hard work of giving back starting to pay off,” he said.

Both men are also thinking beyond philanthropy, working to make Detroit a place where creative talent can build careers—not just a place artists leave once they get noticed.

Rose launched Same Page Entertainment in February with Detroit Pistons owner Tom Gores, aiming to produce films, documentaries, television shows, and other media from Detroit.

He said the goal is to create the kind of entertainment hub Tyler Perry built in Atlanta, giving Detroiters opportunities both in front of and behind the camera. For years, Rose said, pursuing a media career meant leaving Detroit for Los Angeles or New York. He wants to change that.

Big Sean, who also sees some NBA crossover serving as the Pistons’ creative director of global experience, has been using the team’s platform to spotlight local artists. He even teased a major entertainment project that he said Michigan Gov.-elect Jocelyn Benson knows about and wants to support, though he wasn’t ready to announce details just yet.

“Detroit has something magical here, and it’s up to us to activate the magic,” Big Sean said.

And even as his career has taken him around the world, he hasn’t forgotten where it all started. He sees his success as a chance to make the road a little easier for those coming after him.

“Wherever we go, you always leave breadcrumbs behind,” he said, explaining that those breadcrumbs aren’t just for finding his own way home, but for helping someone else follow the path.

Tatiana Sataua is a News Fellow at Fortune covering business, technology, AI, and consumer culture.
