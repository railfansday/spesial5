How Women Weavers Are Preserving Indigenous Amazigh Culture in Morocco

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Malika Oubzou is sitting on a red and blue handwoven rug on the floor of her childhood home in Tamda Noumercid, a small village near Azilal in Morocco’s Atlas Mountains. The 36-year-old weaving artisan is enjoying a glass of milk and a plump, juicy date from a large plate she’s set out for guests—myself among them—when she looks up to see even more people arriving. To accommodate the new visitors, she finishes her milk, leaves the room, and returns with a low table. Then another. And another after that.

Soon enough, more food comes out: a steaming clay pot of tajine maazi (goat tagine) with potatoes and carrots, fresh tomato salad, and fluffy Tafarnout bread, still warm from the clay oven. Malika shifts the tables around to make room for the additional food, and her brother, Mohammed, rearranges the pillows that line the rug-filled floors of the space. It continues like this for the next couple of hours: A table appears here, a pillow moves there. Food comes in and food goes out. The only thing that stays put? The rugs themselves.

Malika Oubzou greets her neighbors at the door of her home in Azilal. In Morocco, Friday is celebrated as "Couscous Day," a cherished tradition where families gather for a communal lunch, usually after the weekly congregational prayer.

“In a typical rural Moroccan household, everything is mobile except the rugs,” Jihane Lahbibi, the Moroccan co-founder of the Moroccan rug brand Casa Amar, tells me as we dunk our Tafarnout into the tagine juices. “The pillows, tables, mattresses…they are all roving. But the rugs are the constant. Symbolically, they are the pillars. They anchor the room. They are the room.” In fact, as Malika echoes later through a translator, rugs are the most sacred part of the home. “When you welcome guests,” she says, “the rug is everything.”

Malika was the first weaver Jihane and her Italian husband Matteo met when they began looking for artisans to make custom rugs for Casa Amar back in 2021. Today, they partner with women in five communities across Morocco to create and source rugs for clients across the world, and were even part of Milan Design Week last year. As interest in Moroccan craftsmanship and design continues to soar, I’ve come to spend some time with a few of the artisans Jihane and Matteo work with to learn more about the Amazigh weaving culture behind the craft.

This scene with the roving tables is a very good start. It’s impossible to miss the deep significance of rugs in everyday Moroccan life once you experience their starring role in every room, after all. But as the days go by, I realize rugs have taken on even more meaning in recent decades, too: They’ve become a way to keep Amazigh culture alive in the face of modernization.

“Younger generations aren’t making as many rugs these days, so when the older women weavers eventually die, the younger ones won’t know how to make them—and that makes me really, really sad,” Malika tells me after we’ve finished our tagine and have moved on to mint tea and a bowl of grapes for dessert. “So now, when I’m weaving, I think about my culture. I also think about the satisfaction of making something good, and the money I will get from making it. All of those things are important. But rugs are objects that last, and because they last, they are one of the best ways to preserve the culture.”

Like most Indigenous traditions around the world, Moroccan rug weaving dates back thousands of years. Amazigh women (also known as Berber) in the Atlas Mountains originally crafted handknotted rugs from local sheep’s wool to protect themselves and their families from the elements: harsh sun during the day, and freezing mountain temperatures at night. Over time, rug weaving morphed into an art form as well, one filled with all sorts of storytelling and symbolism. Some rug patterns represent universal emotions like love and hope, while others are more like talismans, meant to protect against evil and infertility. No matter the pattern, though, the weaving tradition itself has never been written down anywhere. It’s passed down from mother to daughter simply by watching.

Nejma Oubzou prepares freshly harvested dwarf palm by cutting the thorns off and separating the fan-like leaves in order to dry them out. The fibers are used to create woven items such as baskets, mats, and home decor.

“Amazigh women know how to make rugs the same way many other people around the world know how to make bread…it’s just a part of the culture from a very young age,” Jihane, who grew up in Casablanca, tells me one afternoon as we’re driving through the mountains. “It’s not like someone sits you down on the loom and says, ‘This is how you do it.’ It’s just that you learn the same way you learn how to walk: No one tells you, you just learn it by seeing.” When she started Casa Amar in 2021 after leaving her job as a business lawyer, she used to ask the artisans she met when, exactly, they learned to weave for the first time. But now she’s come to understand that seeking out that information was always a fool’s errand. “I realize now that it was never so specific,” she reflects. “It was always: They’re around, they see, and they try.”

That’s exactly what happened with Malika. She wove her first rug at age 15 after years of watching her mom weave, and then watching her weave some more. But that first rug she ultimately created wasn’t just a result of growing up around looms; it was a way for her to care for her own mind. When she was 10, her older brother asked her to drop out of school. Their sister had just gotten married, their parents worked outside the home in agriculture, and he and his younger brother wanted to stay in school—so he decided Malika would be the one to stay home and handle the household chores. Weaving became her creative outlet as a result. “What happened was that after I finished my household work, I had this emptiness that I wanted to fill, and weaving was how I filled it,” she tells me. “I didn’t go to school and I didn’t work, so weaving was how I took care of my brain. I used it to fill a void.”

Today, Malika continues to weave to fulfill her creative dreams, and to make money by doing so. She works independently in the home, not as part of one of the thousands of weaving cooperatives scattered throughout Morocco. When she’s weaving a custom rug for Casa Amar, she follows the design given to her by their clients. But when she’s weaving just for herself or her family (which is increasingly rare due to her growing amount of paid work), her patterns are instinctive and intuitive, usually based on how she is feeling in the moment. “There’s no particular reasoning behind so many of my designs; it’s just what I like and what my mind tells me to do,” she explains. “It’s a very free process. It’s not like if you have green, you have to put yellow. You can do whatever you want. It’s all in the mind when you’re making a rug for yourself.”

But aside from earning a living and expressing her creativity, there’s a third reason Malika weaves: a desire to preserve her craft in the face of modernization. This is where things get slightly complicated because, in some ways, the tradition is thriving. As any design aficionado knows, the allure of Moroccan textiles is increasingly global and well-documented, everywhere from Instagram to the pages of this very magazine. According to the international research firm 6W, the Moroccan rug market is projected to reach $485 million in the US by 2032, up from $371 million in 2025.

Every step of the wool preparation process is done by hand. After the wool is sheared from the animal, the artisans wash, brush, and spin it into yarn.

New York-based interior designer Sarah Lederman believes this uptick in interest could be a response to, take a guess, AI. “People are craving more handmade items in their homes now because they’re the opposite of uniform,” she says, “and Moroccan rugs bear the mark of the person who made them.” They’re a reminder, in other words, of what it means to be human.

Khadija Rhandouss in the carpet section of the weekly souk in Khmisset. Rhandouss is a local businesswoman who trades in Moroccan rugs. Every Tuesday she attends the weekly souk in Khmisset where all the best rug deals happen before sunrise.

At the same time, there are threats to the longevity of traditional rugmaking. For one thing, as Malika mentioned at multiple points throughout my visit, fewer young women are actually learning the traditional weaving techniques required to make said rugs. The work is time-consuming, after all, and the money isn’t always great. And now that there are more profitable career options available elsewhere, many younger women are choosing to leave their villages in the mountains, making weaving less of an obvious path than it once was.

Nejma Oubzou spins wool into yarn at her home in the Atlas Mountains in Morocco.

That’s not to mention the modern technology that has changed the game even more. In the early 2000s, some Moroccan small producers started to buy machines from China that allowed them to process bad-quality wool (known locally as “dead wool”) to make carpets. While starting a new rug used to begin with cutting pure wool from sheep, these machines—which are still used by many today—make it easy to cut out that step entirely. This eliminates a huge and significant part of the process. “A lot of people think that making a carpet is just about weaving on a loom, but actually, making the raw material—i.e., transforming it from sheep to the wool that allows you to start weaving—is just as important,” Ibrahim Noubl, a longtime Moroccan rug dealer, told me in Marrakech at the beginning of my trip. He owns Soufny Art Gallery on the edge of the medina, and often collaborates with the beloved, art-filled hotel El Fenn just a two-minute walk down the road. “In Morocco, we are very sentimental people. We believe in lots of non-material ideas, like the thought that wool is blessed,” he explained. “When blessed wool is inside your home, it will bring you comfort and joy—but all of that disappears when it’s not alive in the first place.”

Mimouna Fadil attaches the wool to a wooden plank to start a new rug at her home in Khmisset.

Artisans, particularly from the Amazigh communities, hand-dye the wool using traditional techniques, often boiling it with ingredients to create long-lasting, eco-friendly colors. Red wool in Moroccan rug making is traditionally achieved using natural dyes derived from plants, minerals, and insects to produce vibrant, lasting colors. Earthy reds often come from henna or madder root, while brighter shades are created with pomegranate peels or cochineal insects.

With that in mind, vintage rugs are one of the best bets for high-quality wool, Ibrahim advises, because the product has already survived a few decades—so you know the wool is good. (Carpets made from “dead wool” often start to smell after a few years.) But to keep the ancient traditions alive in a competitive marketplace, companies like Casa Amar, Salam Hello, and the ever-popular Beni Rugs also sell new rugs and put traditional, sustainable craftsmanship front and center. Not only do they use high-grade natural wool (no “dead wool” to be found here), but they also work directly with artisans like Malika, helping provide an income that allows them to continue their traditional and beloved craft.

Nejma Oubzou, mother of Malika, in her bedroom in her home in the Atlas Mountains. Her name means “stars.”

Many of the artisans even pray before they sit down at the loom because it’s such a sacred act. They start with the Arabic phrase “Bismillah,” which means “in the name of God,” then follow it up with a prayer for peace and serenity during their time on the loom, often including phrases like nsaliw l mensej f salam ou l hna: “We started with peace; may we finish with peace.” After all, as Jihane tells me at various points over the course of our week together, a rug can be both sacred and a source of income. The two are not mutually exclusive. “To buyers, rugs may be just random objects, but people pray before making them, even for people they don’t know,” she says one Friday over Friday couscous, a longtime weekly tradition in Morocco. “I never want to romanticize weaving, because at the end of the day, artisans make the rugs to survive. But both can be true. Just because you have to earn money doesn’t mean you don’t make it respectfully the way your elders did it. It’s something you do with dignity and pride…something to carry the legacy.”

For the last leg of my Morocco trip, Jihane takes me to Khemisset, a small market town about an hour’s drive inland from the capital Rabat, where she lives with Matteo and their two (adorable) young sons. Khemisset is known for its surrounding countryside—think rolling wheat fields, fragrant eucalyptus trees, and grazing sheep—plus its Amazigh weaving traditions, particularly its regionally specific palm mats. Not many artisans make these mats anymore except those in the Khemisset countryside and a few others scattered throughout the Atlas Mountains, and they are sadly going extinct as a result—so I wanted to see them before it’s too late. Jihane and Matteo are also trying to help preserve the mats by spreading the word to their customers. “It’s simple: If people know about them and buy them, artisans will keep making them,” she tells me in the car on the drive from Rabat.

Unlike handknotted rugs, these palm mats are woven from dried palm leaves (not wool) and embroidered with colorful wool patterns on top. They typically cost much less than wool rugs because the raw material is cheaper, but they’re equally difficult to make—and a bit harder on the hands. The leaves tend to dry out during the day, even after the artisans boil them for softness and place them in sachets. As a result, the six women I meet—all of whom collaborate with Jihane and Matteo—often weave late into the night when it’s cooler, or in the morning before the sun becomes too strong. Despite these challenging conditions, they love what they do and are determined to keep the craft alive. (Not entirely unrelated, they are also determined to stuff me and Jihane with more dates, milk, Tafarnout bread, olive oil, and honey—the welcome spread of choice throughout our journey.)

Nora Rhandouss, Khadija Rhandouss, and Jihane share mint tea in Khadija’s salon in Khmisset.

“The techniques to make these mats are very old and very ancestral,” 60-year-old Fatiha Amalou, one of the artisans, tells me as we snack on a bowl of particularly glistening dates. We are sitting on a green and white patterned rug on the floor of another artisan’s house, where we’ve all gathered to chat. Not surprisingly, we’re also circled around a low table that, wouldn’t you know, magically appeared when we arrived. “The mats are so sacred that back in the old days, when people would die, we would wrap them up in them to take them away for burial,” Fatiha continues. “They were like a cape.”

Today, she is dedicated to preserving that sacred weaving tradition. But she also cares deeply about the craftsmanship itself. “Of course I want the legacy to continue; everyone wants the legacy to continue,” she tells me. “But really, perhaps more than anything else, I want to make something that makes me proud. We always say: If you make a good thing, it means you have a good head.” Another artisan, 51-year-old Khadija Rhandouss, nods in agreement. “When I make something good, I feel at peace,” she says. I tell them all that, as a bit of a perfectionist myself, I can relate to that sentiment wholeheartedly. Fatiha just laughs and smiles. “Yes, weaving is what we need to do to make money,” she says, “but it’s also part of what we do to feel good. It’s part of who we are.”
