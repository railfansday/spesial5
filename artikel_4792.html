Meta's Billions Won't Fix Youth Mental Health on Its Own

If I had $17 billion dollars from Meta in the settlement of one social media addiction case, I know exactly what I would not do with it. I would not spend it the way states have been spending their opioid settlement money.

Over the past several years, tens of billions of dollars have flowed to states from opioid settlements meant to save lives through prevention, treatment, and recovery. While opioid-related deaths have declined, how much of that decline is attributable to settlement-funded initiatives is unclear. This is a fact researchers partly trace to the glacial pace of spending, fragmented efforts, and a lack of transparency.

Now, states face a smaller-scale version of the same test, this time over youth and social media. In late August, Meta agreed to pay $12.1 billion to resolve a lawsuit brought by 47 states and the District of Columbia that claimed Facebook and Instagram were designed to hook young users at the expense of their mental health. A chunk of that money is earmarked for youth online safety and mental health initiatives and will be paid out over the next ten years, pending court approval. Meta is dangling an additional $5 billion if other major platforms match the restrictions in the settlement.

Meta agreed to numerous product changes, including stronger age verification, parental controls, and an end to overnight use. But few states are relying on that alone, taking matters into their own hands. California, for example, passed bipartisan legislation on September 20th, 2026, banning infinite scrolling and personalized algorithmic feeds for youth under 16.

That said, as a clinical psychologist, Yale professor, and youth mental health expert, I know the devil is in the details, not the celebratory headlines. What gets done with this money, and how quickly, is still an open question. But if states take a few real notes from the opioid settlement, they can handle this one differently.

First, we have to get real. The Meta settlement was an important exercise in accountability for technology companies, but this money will not solve the youth mental health crisis. It can't. Social media is only one thread of a much larger knot of problems affecting youth mental health. While Meta plays a role here, it is our collective responsibility to improve equity in mental health support availability, access, and quality.

Second, one of the clearest critiques of the opioid settlements is the absence of any national requirement that states report where they spend the money. At a minimum, every state receiving Meta settlement funds should publicly share what it funds, what it's learning, and what isn't working, on a recurring timeline. Minnesota runs a public dashboard breaking down settlement spending, and North Carolina requires local governments to report spending annually through a public portal. Beyond spending transparency, with different amounts per state, implementation and outcomes transparency is also key. California, which helped lead the case, stands to receive the largest final payout, in excess of $2 billion. That puts California and other large recipient states in a position to share their implementation strategies, pilot data, and model resources that other states with smaller amounts can use.

States must also be disciplined. With healthcare budgets under pressure from federal Medicaid changes, especially for access to mental health services, the temptation to backfill budget holes is understandable. But this approach is unlikely to produce sustainable change. Even the roughly $56 billion states and localities receiving over 18 years in opioid settlements doesn't come close to covering what's been lost elsewhere, according to published analyses from the Bipartisan Policy Center’s Opioid Crisis Task Force. This money was never going to replace existing investment, only add to it. That's why at least a dozen states have written explicit restrictions into law against using opioid settlement funds to backfill existing budgets, and states handling the Meta settlement should adopt that restriction from day one.

Finally (for now), young people must be included in whatever gets built on their behalf from these dollars. Their lived experience is vital expertise, and its absence is a documented missed opportunity in the opioid settlements. According to reports, only about 7 percent of people on opioid settlement advisory committees nationally had lived experience for the crisis they were advising on. Alternatively, Washington state required at least four members with lived experience to be appointed members to the settlement committee and compensated for their work. States building out Meta settlement advisory structures should build from this model. Implementation of any solutions is certain to be more successful when young people, their communities, and priorities are centered.

I don't have $12 billion settlement dollars, and neither does any single state on its own. But every state that's about to receive a piece of this Meta settlement has more control over what happens next than the opioid settlements ever gave states credit for exercising. The difference this time isn't the dollar amount. It's whether we treat the last decade of settlement spending as a cautionary tale or as a blueprint we already have in hand.
