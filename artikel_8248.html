We are well into deer season now, and bucks are definitely feeling the pressure. If you’re like the rest of us, you’re probably seeing daylight movement start to decrease on your trail cameras, or maybe it’s already dropped off. I’m right there with you. The places that I hunt see plenty of pressure, even though they’re on private ground. This week on the podcast, I’m talking to our gear editor, Scott Einsmann, who just came back from an incredibly unique experience: He was hunting almost completely unpressured mature bucks in the Midwest. Scott shares what that experience was like, what he learned from it, and how it can help him — and you — back in the real world of deer hunting. —Alex Robinson, editor-in-chief

Listen to this episode on Apple, Spotify, or wherever you get your podcasts.

Alex Robinson: Hey, Scott, thanks for joining the Outdoor Life Podcast. I want to talk to you about your favorite thing in the world, which is bowhunting for whitetail deer.

Scott Einsmann: Awesome. Let’s do it. I just came off the most amazing week of bowhunting for whitetails.

Alex: Yeah. We’re coming up on the holiday season, and you have a good redemption story, and everybody loves a good redemption story around the holidays. So to start this off, explain the property that you’re able to hunt in Illinois. What’s it like, and how did you weasel your way into getting bowhunting access to this place?

Scott: It’s 800 acres in southern Illinois, and what makes it a really good 800 acres is, one, it has topography, and it also has large wood blocks in it, and it’s surrounded by big ag fields. So once all the crops are cut, all the deer get sucked right into this property. For that reason, it’s just insane hunting. There are really good deer. There’s not a 200-incher on it, but there are a ton of 130- to 150-inch deer, and a ton of really good 8-points — some really cool deer. Last year, some big 6-points were killed, which is always fun. And maybe 300 does just running around this place. I mean, it’s crazy.

Alex: So talk about whose property it is and how you’re able to get on it, because you’re not a multimillionaire property owner.

Alex: A lot of these properties are either owned or leased by — you know, it’s the stuff that everybody dreams about hunting, but not everybody really gets access to.

Scott: Yes. And I did inquire with the farm manager there: How does someone get a lease in this area? You really just have to know the local farmers, because a lot of them have been burned by people leasing their property and not treating it right. So you really have to have someone to vouch for you and then be part of the community.

Luckily, I know someone. Levi Morgan and Andy Morgan have the lease for this property, and they have several leases in the area now, and they’re all pretty incredible. But this one they’ve had the longest — about three years now — and they’re really starting to dial it in and understand how the deer use it. They’re connected with our friends at Tactacam Reveal, and part of being an outdoor writer is you get to go on press trips.

Press trips vary in quality and fun. But this one is by far the best I’ve ever been on, because it’s not a guided hunt. You’re not just being driven around by an outfitter and sitting over a corn pile somewhere. We go out there in the summer, we hang stands, we hang cameras, get to learn the property a little bit, and then we have access to those cameras all season long — which for me is great, because my home lease is not that exciting by comparison.

Scott: So just being able to scroll and see cool deer after cool deer — and they have a ton of turkeys as well, and I have some good news there. So we have access to all that, and then we come back in November and hunt the place during the peak of the rut.

And it is so much fun to be in a place where you get to see whitetail deer acting like whitetail deer, because it gets hunted two weeks out of the year, by bowhunters only. There’s no gun hunting on it. So it’s a big property with very limited pressure, and these deer act like deer. They’re out in the middle of the day. There’s this one field right by the hog barn, and there’s a little strip of beans in it, but the rest is wheat or rye or something like that. It’s a huge field. At 1 o’clock in the afternoon on any given day, there’s a dozen does out there, and there’s probably going to be a buck checking every one of those does. It’s a really hard place to kill a deer, but it’s a fun place to watch deer.

It’s just an example. There are just deer out like that. In my area, in the Southeast, you would never see that, because someone’s going to take a shot at those deer from the road.

Alex: Yeah, I mean, even my area of Wisconsin and Minnesota — you’ve got a couple people hunting every, say, 80 acres, right? If you split up all the farmland around my area of western Wisconsin, there’s probably one hunter per 80 acres hunting pretty much every weekend. Some of those guys are very good hunters and they’re holding out for big bucks. Other folks are just out there for fun and they’re going to shoot whatever. There’s a lot in between, but there is hunting pressure basically from October through the heart of the gun season, which we’re in right now.

We have private property in north-central Wisconsin that my family always hunts, and opening day of gun season, it’s like a war zone. You just hear gunfire from all directions echoing through the morning, basically that entire first day. You can see the neighbors on their properties. Some folks will sit right on the property line. And even though we’re hunting private land, the deer know that they’re being hunted, and it’s a high-pressure situation. And that’s not even considering public land, right? So you have this view into a world that very few —

Alex: Very few places even exist like this, where deer can just be deer throughout basically the fall cycle.

Scott: Yeah, pretty much. And I’m lucky to be at a place in my hunting career where I really, really appreciate it. I know how good it is and how lucky I am.

Alex: So this is your second crack at it this season. And not to dredge up the dark times, but I feel like enough time has passed now: You hunted out there last season. Talk about what went down.

Scott: So last season, one, we didn’t hit the chase period — the rut — as well as we did this year. So we weren’t really seeing a ton of deer, by the standards of this place in Illinois.

Scott: This was maybe day three or so. I had sat a stand on this cool little food plot that we have down there, and some guy just ends up walking in on the setup around 8 or 9 o’clock in the morning.

Scott: Yes, trespassing into it. I took a picture of him real quick and shot a text to Levi and Andy, who had already killed their deer, so they were just helping everybody else kill. They said they would be there lickety-split. We said hi to the guy, and he looked up like a doe who just realized that you were there. Just busted. And he said, “Oh, sorry,” and then he left. I was like, “That was so weird. I don’t know who that was or what they were doing here.”

And it turns out that in Andy and Levi’s truck was the farm manager, John, who is a character all of his own. We should do a documentary about him. He’s such an interesting individual. So they’re pulling in, and they see this guy leaving where we’re at. Before the truck even stops, John is out of the truck running up to this guy, and he just unloads a verbal assault on him. Andy and Levi are in the truck in shock, like, “Oh my God, is there about to be a huge fight?” But this guy understood that he was in the wrong, and he backed off, apologized and left. Turns out he was living next door, and they believe he was being evicted, and they think he was coming to look for something to steal before he left.

Alex: So, like, maybe trying to grab some trail cameras or —

Scott: Yeah, trail cameras or something, whatever. Not the most upstanding citizen of McLeansboro, Illinois.

Alex: OK. All right. So we’ve got this going on in the middle of your hunt.

Scott: Yeah. So that spot is blown, but I’m watching trail camera feeds nonstop. And that morning, there was a good deer in this food plot just up the hill from where we were sitting, and we had seen him from that spot. They were like, “He might come back there this evening. Let’s try to get into that stand.” So we get up there. It’s called Liquid Death, and it’s right next to a plot called Andy’s Plot. So you have these two food plots that are right next to each other, and right behind the stand you have a really nice watering hole.

We see some does throughout the day into the evening, and then it’s the last hour of daylight and we see a buck way off. There are some does right up on the hill that this food plot leads up to, and he bumps one of those does down. She comes down into the food plot pretty close to us, like 30-ish yards. And then he’s up there on this hill, sun going down, perfect golden-hour lighting, just looking unbelievably majestic. Oh, and by the way, I have a camera guy over my shoulder filming all this.

So then he follows that doe right down into the plot. I had ranged little empty spots in the food plot, or little shadows, earlier in the day, but now it’s last light. There are no shadows.

Scott: But also my brain was not working. I don’t know exactly what my thought process was, but I was at full draw, and I dropped my 35 pin behind the shoulder and shot. As soon as the arrow hit, it was like, ugh, because it did not penetrate even a little bit. You could just see it hanging out. And he bounds off and stands at 60 yards.

Scott: Yeah, a high shoulder hit. And he’s standing there right at 60, looking around, not sure what just happened. He stood there for a while, and then he just walks off into the woods. And I was just like, “What just happened?” I think everyone knows that thick feeling when you know you just screwed up big time — especially because it was a great deer, a really nice, tall 8-point.

So what had happened is I shot him for 35, and he was 30. And looking back at the slow-motion footage, he dropped a few inches. Also, his leg that was closest to me was back, and that arrow hit the upper portion of the scapula — there’s that hard ridge right there. The arrow hit that bone square. So it didn’t even hit the soft part of the scapula, where you might have a chance. It hit right on that bone. I would imagine that deer survived. We looked all the next day, and we didn’t find anything but a few drops of blood.

Alex: Yeah, that’s a nonlethal hit with archery equipment pretty much every time. You’re just kind of screwed. That’s not where you want to shoot a deer, you know?

Scott: No. If you’re trying to kill one, that’s not the spot to hit them.

Scott: And to make matters worse, I’ve been a fan of archery my whole life, and Levi Morgan is the guy in 3D archery — definitely the greatest of the current generation, and in the conversation for greatest 3D archer of all time. And he’s across the field watching this with some other guys, and their binoculars all go down. Oh, no. So someone you look up to is watching you screw up. Even worse.

Alex: Yeah. And he has you on his property to hunt these big deer. And you’re like, “I’m an Outdoor Life archery guy. I can do this. I’ve bowhunted my entire life. This is my thing.”

Scott: Yeah. And there were a lot of things I did wrong there, obviously. I think the moment was so big — this really cool sequence of events — and I just didn’t take the second to range. My rangefinder was right there. I could’ve just clicked and confirmed, but in the moment I was like, “Yep, that’s 35, maybe.” That little short circuit is ultimately what it was, because the shot-execution part was fine.

Alex: It’s not like you hit far back. You basically hit where you were aiming, right? You were just aiming with the wrong pin, or you were not holding low enough.

Scott: Yeah. It’s the dumbest of all mistakes to make.

Alex: Going back to the unpressured deer, one thing that I have noticed — especially with more mature bucks in the rut — is you can get away with so much more than we often think. Especially if you’re used to hunting deer earlier in the year, or shooting does, where any little movement or any little mistake and their attention is on you. There have been times where I’ve tried to stop a buck with the classic “meh” to get them to stop walking so I can shoot, and they’re 20 yards away and they don’t stop. I’ve had to yell to get their attention, because they’re just in that rut mode.

And I guess my point is I’ve made mistakes of being in the tree and thinking, “OK, I can’t range. I need to just shoot, because if I try to range and then draw, he’s going to see me draw, and I just have to make this shot.” Oftentimes you can range. Even if the buck’s in the open, you can draw, and maybe they see you and stop and then you can shoot them, or maybe they don’t even see you.

Scott: Yeah. And it was also an open field, so it’s not like you could range some trees and have real landmarks — which I guess I’m not very used to. I don’t hunt a lot of open fields. But talking to Levi this year about what he does in the tree when a buck is coming in, he has his rangefinder in his hand and is ranging the deer as it’s coming in, thinking about all the possible places where that buck could end up in front of him. He’s one of the best people in the world at judging distance, and he’s still ranging the deer as it comes in until he gets a shot.

Alex: Yeah. A world-champion 3D archer is doing this.

Scott: Yes. And he said he wants to know the exact distance that deer is at before he pulls back his bow.

Alex: On my tree harness, I have a little clip that I clip my rangefinder to, so I can range and then just drop it, and it stays on my harness. I don’t have to shove it back in my pocket or take it out. Honestly, I started doing that after a similar situation to yours. I shot a buck that was closer than I thought, and because of that, I didn’t hit him well and didn’t get him. And I was like, “OK, now I need to range every shot, unless it’s obviously 20 yards or closer.”

Scott: Yeah. And I’ve also started aiming a little lower. I was aiming mid-body behind the shoulder, but now I’m aiming at the top of the heart, especially on those closer shots.

Alex: OK. So let’s move to this year. What was your hunt like this year? How’d that play out?

Scott: So this year, we hit a cold front just as we were arriving. It was pretty incredible, actually, to have that timing — perfect weather, ideal conditions. The deer were chasing. You could see it all on the trail cameras from 10 a.m. until 3 p.m. Bucks everywhere.

Scott: The day that we arrived, we were planning on trying to get out in the stand. We all got there around noon, but we were like, “We can’t, because there are deer everywhere and we’ll just end up bumping them. It’s better to sneak in in the morning and then hunt.” And so that’s what we did.

One really cool thing that I’ve learned from Levi and Andy is how to hunt deer without them knowing that they’re being hunted. They are very particular about their entry and exit routes into the stands — getting in clean, getting out clean, in terms of wind direction, ground scent and just not bumping deer on your way to a stand location. So they have some really cool, sneaky spots where you can very easily get in and get out.

The one I was hunting, there are these train tracks that run through a portion of the property, and they have three stands right off the train tracks. You drive a side-by-side down the train tracks, go maybe 50 yards into the woods, and you’re up a tree. So it’s real easy, with no chance of really bumping anything, and you just sit there all day. For the most part, they’re on pinch points between food and bedding, or on natural travel corridors. I mean, there isn’t a bad spot on this property, but these ones in particular are very, very cool.

Alex: I have a quick question on that. You have all this camera activity and you’re getting pictures of all these bucks, and obviously some places are going to be a little harder to access than others. And then you have these train-track stands that are super sneaky to get in and out of. I feel like I know the answer to this, but if there’s one camera that has a giant buck on it, but there’s a probability of spooking deer on that access, versus these other cameras where you know you can get in clean and out clean — which one are they going to send you to?

Scott: The ones you can get in and out clean.

Alex: Even though you only have a few days to hunt, they’re like, “No matter what, we’re not going to spook deer.”

Scott: Pretty much. For example, that stand I hunted last year — this year, Levi shot a really nice buck out of that same stand. And right after that, there were more big bucks on that same food plot every single day. But nobody hunted it, because you can’t get in there in the morning without bumping them. It’s a food plot, and the only access is to walk down the edge of the woods. The other problem is the wind direction ended up switching. So you’d have to try to get in there in the middle of the day, but the deer were so active in the middle of the day that that whole really awesome spot with good deer on it didn’t end up being huntable.

Another part of this approach is they’re really hunting the outer perimeter of the property and not going right into the middle. Last year we had trail cameras down in these creek bottoms, and it was like a deer highway, especially in the middle of the day — big bucks just cruising up and down. But we didn’t hunt them, because there’s too great a chance of getting busted in there. When the thermals switch, the wind has to be so perfect that it’s not worth the risk of going right into the middle of the property and trying to kill a deer, because you’re going to blow the whole property out on that one attempt. Maybe if it was just your property you could do whatever you want, but there are other people trying to kill bucks at the same time.

Alex: So the reason the deer are the way they are is, one, because it’s only hunted two weeks out of the year. But two, the way that it’s hunted, the deer don’t even feel the pressure that they have on them, because they’re just not bumping into humans or human scent.

Scott: Right. And in the places where they are — I mean, it’s an active farm. They’re used to seeing people on the farm roads and stuff.

Scott: And just to hit that point a little bit more: When you do bump a deer going in, if you’re accessing through an area where they’re used to seeing people, those deer will be right back.

Alex: Yeah. It’s not like you’re bumping them out of their bedding area or their main feeding area. Got it. All right, go on with your hunt for this year.

Scott: So back to the redemption day. I’m in this stand. Another thing about hunting with Levi and Andy is they like to hang their stands very high. One of the guys got into one and ranged it, and it was 12 or 13 yards to the bottom of the tree. So like 36 feet in the air. They’re not all that high, but they’re all at least 20 feet up.

Alex: Why do they do that? For scent, or so you don’t get seen, or they just like it?

Scott: A little bit of both. I talked to Levi about this last year. It’s just how he grew up hunting. His dad always hung the stands really high, because he’s from North Carolina, where deer actually do look up into trees as they walk. So this is what he’s used to and what he’s always done. And Levi’s also really tall. He’s 6-foot-4. So if you’re shorter than 6-foot-4, you might have to make some jumps as you climb up the sticks, because of the gap between the sticks. Luckily I didn’t have that problem.

Alex: OK. So somehow you made it way high up there.

Scott: I’m high up in the tree. I’ve got a camera guy with me, and the stand looks higher than it is because it’s up on a hill, and you’re looking down at a couple trails that are at the bottom of this hill. So it’s a pretty steep downward angle.

The morning was pretty quiet for the most part. That first hour of daylight, I saw some deer, nothing crazy. Saw a coyote. Heard the most amazing turkey vocalizations of all time — a huge group of hens behind us, everything in the book. It was nuts, and loud. So that was cool for me. And then we had an 8-point come in at about 15 yards — a really nice, maybe 3-year-old typical 8-point. But the camera guy couldn’t get on him. And I mean, it’s the first morning.

Scott: Yeah. I’m not used to passing a deer like that, but I did. It felt weird. And the morning just goes on. We hear some deer fighting behind us. I see some bucks cruising the ridge ahead of us, about 100 yards out. And then it goes quiet, and I start eating every snack and sandwich I packed. Getting a little sleepy — it’s 12 o’clock, the sun’s on me. I’d packed a heavier jacket, so I throw it on, and I’m kind of nodding off, not really paying attention.

And then all of a sudden, the woods erupt in front of us, and two does come barreling down into this bottom. I’m trying to get my stuff together and stand up out of this — I was in a Millennium stand, so I was very comfortable — trying to sit up and grab my bow off the hanger. And then I’m looking for whatever buck is going to be coming in after them.

Alex: Can I ask you a quick question? When you have deer come in and you’re pretty sure it’s a buck, do you stand up or do you stay sitting down?

Scott: In this stand, because of the shot angle, you definitely had to be standing to bend over for that shot. But the year before, there was a limb that came up right where my top cam was, so I had to sit and shoot, which I’m not used to.

So I get stood up. There are some deer coming off to the left now. I see another doe come running in, and I’m looking for the buck that’s chasing these. And then I see this tall 8-point that has tines that almost come in and touch. He’s the one that I had seen on the trail cameras leading up to this trip. I’d actually talked to Levi about him the day before, as we were discussing all the cool deer: “Man, I really, really like that unique 8-point look.” And it’s him — on the complete opposite side of the farm from where I had seen him the day before.

Scott: Yep. So he’s coming in. I had ranged some trees ahead of time, and there are pretty much two trails he could have walked in on: one that goes from 30 to 20 yards in front of the stand, and one that goes right under the stand. He’s standing right by the one that goes from 30 to 20, and I know I’m going to shoot him with either my top pin or my second pin. So I come to full draw, thinking he’s going to step out and turn broadside any second. And he’s just standing there, and I’m at full draw thinking, “What is this dude going to do?” And he turns and walks to the closer trail, which none of the does had walked in on.

Now he’s going to be coming in about 10 yards from me. I’m at full draw for what felt like forever, but it was totally fine, with a giant buck in front of me. He’s walking, and I give him the bleat, and he does not stop. I do a louder one, and then he stops, and I drop my top pin just above the midline on his body, since it’s such a steep angle. And the shot goes off. I was shooting a resistance release, and it went off very easily. And I see about half an arrow’s worth of penetration, and I’m like, “Not again.”

Alex: Oh, no. Did it feel like you hit where you aimed?

Scott: I honestly don’t remember. I’m just glad it was 10 yards. Maybe we got lucky there. The deer runs off, and then we hear him crash. I’m looking at the camera guy, and I’m not ready to celebrate, because I’m not feeling 100 percent confident in the shot. Typically I’m used to passing through deer, with either compounds or recurves, especially at that distance. But we heard him crash, so I felt pretty good about it.

It takes me a while to settle down, and we climb down. I don’t find any blood where I shot him, but we start walking in the direction we heard him crash, and there he was, lying there 40 yards away — dead as a doornail.

Scott: Basically what happened is the arrow came in high, right behind the shoulder, and then exited at the armpit on the offside and hit the elbow bone on that offside shoulder.

Scott: Yeah. The broadhead was just poking through the skin on the offside. So luckily he crashed close, because that would’ve been a tough one to track.

Alex: So basically two years of hunting this place, and you walk up on this deer. You made your shot. How does that feel?

Scott: It took me a while for it to fully sink in, because hunting in the rut, things happen so quickly. I was about to take a nap, and two minutes later this deer is there. It was a huge weight off my shoulders. I got to have a drama-free experience of finding the deer. Everything went great on that end. And then Levi and Andy came in the side-by-side, we picked him up, and it was just like, “Man, what a relief.” But at the same time, I was like, “I really want to spend more time in this stand.”

The day after, I couldn’t get into the stand to try to fill my doe tag, but I ended up hanging stands with Levi and Andy on one of their new leases, and that was awesome. We found such a cool spot, and I got to watch how they approach picking a tree and that on-the-run scouting. I mean, that was worth the price of not going hunting that day. I would much rather do that.

Alex: What was your biggest takeaway from watching those guys? They know an area’s good, but what’s the next step for narrowing it down to, “OK, this tree is where we’re going to hang it”?

Scott: Andy calls it structure, because he’s a pro bass fisherman, so it’s terminology he’s familiar with. Basically what they’re looking for is little things that will funnel deer movement closer to your stand location, but also give you a backstop for your wind direction — like, deer won’t be coming from here, so we can hunt it on this wind. The way he puts it is you’re always giving up something. There’s not one perfect wind direction. You always have that risk, but you’re trying to minimize it and at the same time funnel deer past your stand location.

In this case, there’s an ag field maybe 60 yards in front of the stand. Then in the woods behind you, there’s a really thick brier patch that deer won’t walk through. But there’s an opening in the brier patch, and an old two-track that comes in also. You could just see the deer trails funneling right through this spot that they found. Then you find the right tree to hang it in. It was cool. We saw a couple of really good deer while we were hanging that stand, so it’ll be a good one for next year. But those guys are always hanging more stands. I think they must have hung a few while we were there — more wind-direction options.

Alex: You had this whitetail wonderland experience, but what do you think your main takeaways are coming back to the real world and hunting regular places like the rest of us? What’s applicable?

Scott: I mean, the entry and exit thing is something that I’ve applied to how I hunt my lease. I wish my lease neighbor did the same. But just being mindful of the pressure you’re putting on it going in, going out, and while you’re in the stand — where your scent cone is blowing.

I still need to get better at staying present and task-focused when a big buck comes barreling in on you. Hunting during the rut can definitely leave you a little flat-footed sometimes. I think that’s just more experience than anything else, because I’m more used to hunting deer over food sources, where you have a little more time to get warmed up to the idea of killing this deer.

Alex: Right. So many times in the rut, you hear a buck coming, then you see him for a few seconds, and then you have to shoot. You have to decide that he’s a buck you want to shoot, and you have to decide how you’re going to shoot him and where, all in a matter of a few seconds. And like you said, you could’ve been about to take a nap or eat a sandwich 30 seconds before that, you know?

Scott: Yeah, for sure. And I got pictures last night of a really great deer on my lease. I mean, it was 9:30 at night, but I still have another month to find that deer and get him killed.

Alex: All right, Scott. Well, thank you for the insight and for sharing this little window into the perfect corner of whitetail hunting country. We can all live vicariously through you.

Scott: Yes. If I never get invited back, I won’t be too sad.
