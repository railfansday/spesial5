Ai2 gets $5M from Google to turn AI weather model into a tool for projecting climate change

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

by Todd Bishop on Nov 4, 2026 at 8:00 amNovember 5, 2026 at 2:27 pm

Google is giving Seattle’s Allen Institute for AI about $5 million in funding, plus another $1.8 million in cloud credits, to turn an AI weather model developed by Google Research into a tool for projecting climate change decades into the future.

The money will help Ai2 build an open-source model designed to give scientists, water agencies and other planners a faster, more accurate look at how rainfall, temperatures and water supplies could change over the next five to 50 years.

Announced Wednesday morning, the three-year project pairs funding from Google.org, the company’s philanthropic arm, with technical support from Google Research. Ai2 will add four people to its eight-person climate modeling team to do the work.

Google’s work on the model centers on weather, looking ahead a year at most. It has less interest and expertise in climate projections that run decades into the future, said Oliver Watt-Meyer, Ai2’s director of research for AI climate modeling.

“Climate modeling is a bit more of a public good,” he said. “It is information that people need to know, but it’s often more government agencies that are thinking about this.”

Starting with Google’s model: Ai2’s model, NeuralGCM+, will build on Google’s NeuralGCM, which mixes traditional physics equations with AI. Watt-Meyer, who wrote about NeuralGCM for the journal Nature when Google published its research in 2024, said the model does well at forecasting weather and at recreating past climate.

In that piece, he also pointed out its limits: some of its long runs broke down, and when researchers simulated ocean temperatures about 7 degrees Fahrenheit warmer than today, its predictions for the atmosphere didn’t make physical sense.

Under the new project, Ai2 will try to fix both problems. It will connect NeuralGCM+ to an ocean and sea ice model, with the goal of running stable simulations that span decades. It will also build more established physics into the model, then check its predictions for a warmer world against results from traditional climate models.

Ai2’s climate team is made up largely of atmospheric scientists, with the engineers and computing power of an AI institute behind them.

Ai2 is “uniquely positioned to extend Google’s NeuralGCM model into the next generation of long-term climate projections,” said Alex Diaz, head of applied science at Google.org, in a statement, citing the institute’s climate science expertise and commitment to open research.

Where the idea started: The team’s origins go back to the late Microsoft co-founder Paul Allen, who took an interest in climate modeling before his death in 2018. His company, then known as Vulcan, launched the climate modeling project in 2019, and the group moved to Ai2 in 2021. Allen had founded the nonprofit AI institute in 2014.

The original plan was to combine physics and AI. The group set that aside because the AI couldn’t be trained well inside older climate models written in Fortran, a decades-old programming language. Google wrote NeuralGCM in modern code that solves that problem.

In that way, Watt-Meyer said, “This is actually kind of coming full circle for us.”

Ai2 already has its own family of AI climate models, called the Ai2 Climate Emulator, or ACE. These models rely entirely on AI and run much faster than NeuralGCM. But versions trained only on historical data struggle to project a climate the planet hasn’t experienced yet.

Watt-Meyer said NeuralGCM’s design could hold up better as the world warms, because the laws of physics built into it won’t change.

The timeline: The first step is releasing the code for training NeuralGCM, which has been tied to Google’s internal systems. Making it public will let other researchers train their own versions, including on common GPUs rather than only Google’s own chips. Ai2 expects to release it within the first year, with work on the ocean model running in parallel.

Making the model reliable in a warmer world is the hardest part, and it could take longer than three years. “We don’t know if it’ll work necessarily,” Watt-Meyer said.

He said AI coding agents are changing what his team thinks it can get done in that time. The tools are well suited to translating old code into modern programming languages. In one case, an outside group converted a well-known ocean model from Fortran into the same language NeuralGCM uses, so Ai2 won’t have to build its own.

Watt-Meyer called the tools “a real game changer” for simulation. “It’s kind of a golden era for this kind of scientific computing,” he said.

Google Research will keep developing NeuralGCM for forecasts of a few weeks to a year, and the two teams will share training code and data. One of Google’s lead NeuralGCM researchers is spending three days at Ai2 this week.

Who would use the model: Watt-Meyer pointed to questions such as how rainfall could change in the Southwest, a key issue for water planning around Lake Powell on the Colorado River. Insurance and reinsurance companies already use Ai2’s ACE models in their research.

Asked whether data center developers could use these kinds of models, Watt-Meyer said they could help answer questions about cooling, access to renewable energy and risks such as extreme weather and rising seas. But it isn’t clear if the industry is paying attention.

“There’s so much frantic energy to build these things as fast as possible,” he said. “I’m not sure people are really seriously considering this kind of climate data, though maybe they should be a bit more.”

Correction: An earlier version of this story gave an incorrect date for the start of the climate modeling effort at Vulcan. It started in 2019.
