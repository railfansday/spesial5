How boards handle a CEO scandal: 7 moves they make first

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Free daily briefing on global business news.

How boards handle a CEO scandal often starts with seven early moves, from independent investigations and evidence holds to disclosure filings, pay clawbacks and succession plans

When a CEO is accused of misconduct, the board faces a structural problem. It has to investigate the person who normally runs the company and often helps set the board's own agenda. That tension shapes almost every decision directors make in the first days of a scandal.

Those early days carry outsized weight. Employees want to know who is in charge. Investors want to know whether the problem touches the financials. Regulators, lenders and customers watch for signs that the company is hiding something or moving too slowly. A board that stumbles at the start can turn one executive's conduct into a governance crisis for the whole organization.

Understanding how boards handle a CEO scandal means looking past the headlines about resignations and firings. Much of the work happens out of public view. Directors decide who will oversee the inquiry and who will conduct it. They lock down records before anything disappears. They check their insurance policies, review their disclosure obligations and pull out the CEO's employment contract. That contract shows what the company owes and what it might recover.

This slideshow covers seven moves boards commonly make early in a CEO scandal. It is a selection, not a complete checklist, and the moves do not follow a fixed order. In practice, several run at the same time. A special committee may hire investigators on the same day lawyers issue a legal hold. A board may weigh an interim leader while counsel drafts a securities filing.

The specifics vary by company, jurisdiction and the nature of the allegation. A financial fraud raises different questions from a workplace relationship that broke company policy. A U.S.-listed company faces filing deadlines that a private firm does not. The same broad concerns recur, though: independence, credibility, evidence, leadership, disclosure, pay and communication.

Each slide below takes one of those moves and explains what it involves, why boards use it and where its limits lie. Each one can be read on its own.

Boards facing allegations against a CEO usually move oversight to a special committee of independent directors — members with no personal, financial or business ties to the executive. This keeps the accused leader, and anyone close to them, out of decisions about their own conduct.

Without that step, the executive under scrutiny could steer the inquiry. A CEO usually controls the flow of information from management to directors. Many CEOs also hold a seat on the board. Either position could influence an investigation into the CEO's own behavior. Handing the matter to a separate group of directors removes that influence before the first interview takes place.

Independence in this setting goes beyond the formal label. NYSE and Nasdaq $NDAQ listing standards generally require listed companies to have a majority of independent directors. A director who qualifies on paper can still be conflicted in a specific case. Examples include a longtime friendship with the CEO, a business deal with a company the CEO controls or a seat the CEO personally recruited them to fill. Directors with ties like these typically step back from the committee.

The committee then takes charge of the CEO misconduct investigation. It decides the scope of the inquiry, controls who receives updates and sets how often it reports to the full board. Management stays out of those reporting lines. The committee also decides which documents and witnesses matter. It holds the authority to recommend outcomes ranging from no action to dismissal.

Courts pay attention to this structure. In Delaware, where many U.S. public companies are incorporated, judges examine whether committee members were independent when weighing their decisions in shareholder litigation. The Delaware Supreme Court set out that kind of review for special litigation committees in Zapata Corp. v. Maldonado in 1981. A committee seen as conflicted gives plaintiffs an opening to challenge its conclusions.

Nestlé offers a recent case. In September 2025, the Swiss food company dismissed CEO Laurent Freixe over an undisclosed romantic relationship with a direct subordinate. Chair Paul Bulcke and lead independent director Pablo Isla oversaw the investigation that preceded his exit.

The Nestlé case also points to the role of a lead independent director. Many boards create that position to give independent members a leader of their own, separate from management. In a CEO scandal, the lead independent director can serve as a point of contact for the committee's work and for questions from investors.

Boards typically retain an outside law firm to investigate a CEO scandal, and that firm reports to the independent directors rather than to management. The goal is findings that regulators, investors and courts will treat as credible.

The reporting line is central to that credibility. If investigators answer to the CEO, the general counsel or anyone else under scrutiny, their conclusions can be dismissed as compromised. When the firm takes direction from a committee of independent directors, those directors control the scope, the budget and the final report.

Many boards also pass over the company's regular outside counsel. A firm that has advised management for years may have close working relationships with the executives involved. It may also have signed off on decisions that are now being questioned. Hiring a firm with no prior ties removes that objection at the start.

The work usually combines document review with interviews. Lawyers collect emails, text messages, expense records and calendars, often with help from e-discovery vendors. When the allegations involve money, forensic accountants may trace payments and reimbursements. Interviews generally move from peripheral witnesses toward the executive at the center of the inquiry.

Privilege shapes much of how the investigation is run. Communications between a company's lawyers and its employees can be protected by attorney-client privilege. The U.S. Supreme Court addressed that protection in Upjohn Co. v. United States in 1981. Investigators typically open interviews with what is known as an Upjohn warning. It tells the employee that the lawyer represents the company, not the individual. It also explains that the company may later decide to share what was said.

Those protections have limits. Handing findings to regulators or prosecutors can waive privilege, and that can open the material to plaintiffs in later lawsuits. Boards therefore weigh whether to receive a full written report or an oral briefing. They also decide whether to publish a summary of the conclusions.

Cooperation with regulators is part of the calculation. The SEC's 2001 Seaboard Report sets out the factors the agency considers when deciding how much credit to give a company for cooperating. Those factors include whether the company investigated the conduct itself and shared what it learned. They also include who conducted the review and how independent that reviewer was from management.

Counsel normally issues a legal hold early in a CEO scandal, ordering that emails, messages and financial records tied to the matter be kept rather than deleted. The board also checks whether its directors-and-officers liability insurance requires prompt notice of a potential claim.

A legal hold, also called a litigation hold, suspends a company's routine deletion practices for anyone whose records may be relevant. Those people are known as custodians. In a CEO case, the list usually starts with the chief executive. It then extends to assistants, direct reports and anyone named in the allegations.

The hold has to reach the places where records actually live. That includes corporate email, chat platforms such as Slack $WORK and Microsoft $MSFT Teams, text messages, calendars, expense systems and shared drives. It can also cover personal phones and messaging apps like WhatsApp or Signal if they were used for company business. Automatic deletion settings on those tools are typically switched off for the custodians involved.

The duty to preserve generally begins once litigation or an investigation is reasonably anticipated, not when a lawsuit is filed. In U.S. federal courts, Rule 37(e) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure covers lost electronically stored information. It lets judges impose remedies when a party loses that data by failing to take reasonable steps to keep it. The harshest sanctions require a finding that the party intended to deprive the other side of the information. One such sanction is an instruction that the jury may presume the lost evidence was unfavorable.

Destroying records to obstruct a federal inquiry can also be a crime. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act created a federal offense covering the destruction or falsification of records with intent to impede an investigation. Boards often have forensic specialists collect and copy devices rather than relying on the executive under scrutiny to hand material over.

Insurance notice runs on a separate track. Most D&O policies are written on a claims-made basis. They respond to claims made during the policy period, and they set conditions on how and when the insurer must be told. Many also allow a notice of circumstances. That lets a company report facts that could lead to a claim. A lawsuit filed later can then attach to the current policy.

Late or incomplete notice can give an insurer grounds to contest coverage. Policies may also require the insurer's consent before the company settles or commits to certain defense costs. Public-company D&O programs usually have three parts. Side A covers individual directors and officers when the company cannot indemnify them. Side B reimburses the company when it does indemnify them. Side C covers the company itself, typically for securities claims.

Many policies also contain conduct exclusions for fraud or intentional wrongdoing. These often apply only after a final, nonappealable adjudication. Until then, an accused executive may still have defense costs covered. Where allegations involve harassment or other workplace conduct, the company may also need to notify the carrier of its employment practices liability policy.

One of the first calls a board makes is whether the CEO keeps running the company while the facts are gathered. The choices are to leave the executive in place, put them on leave or suspend them. If the CEO steps aside, the board often names an interim leader to run the business in the meantime.

Keeping the CEO in the role signals that the board sees no immediate reason to act. It also leaves the person under scrutiny in charge of the staff, records and budgets an investigation may need to examine. That tension is why boards often look for a step short of removal.

Leave is the lighter of those steps. It takes the executive out of daily operations without a finding of wrongdoing. That keeps the board from prejudging the outcome. It also preserves the option of a return if the allegations are not substantiated.

Suspension carries more weight. It usually signals that the board considers the conduct serious enough to bar the CEO from acting on the company's behalf. The executive's employment contract shapes what the board can do here. Some agreements spell out when a suspension is permitted and whether pay continues during it.

When the CEO steps aside in either form, someone has to run the company. An interim CEO may come from inside, such as a chief operating officer, a chief financial officer or another senior executive. A board may instead ask a director, sometimes the chair, to take on the role temporarily. The mandate is usually narrow. The interim leader keeps operations stable, reassures staff and avoids major strategic shifts until the board settles the CEO's status.

The data software company Astronomer faced this decision in 2025. A video of CEO Andy Byron with a colleague at a Coldplay concert spread online. The company placed Byron on leave. He later resigned.

That sequence shows what leave can offer a board. It buys time to review the facts before committing to an outcome. It also lets a company show it has responded without announcing a conclusion it has not yet reached.

The choice also has consequences beyond the executive suite. Employees, customers and investors read the board's decision as an early signal of how seriously it is treating the matter. A CEO who stays at work during an inquiry may make staff less willing to come forward with information.

For a U.S.-listed company, a CEO's departure triggers a Form 8-K filing under Item 5.02. That report is due within four business days of the event. The board also has to decide whether the conduct behind the departure is material enough to disclose sooner.

Form 8-K is the Securities and Exchange Commission's current report. It exists so investors learn about significant corporate events between quarterly and annual filings. Item 5.02 covers the departure and appointment of certain senior officers and directors, a group that includes the principal executive officer. If the board names a replacement, including an interim chief, that appointment is also reported under the same item.

The four-business-day clock starts from the triggering event, not from the date a press release goes out. That timing forces boards to settle basic questions quickly. They need to know whether the CEO resigned, was terminated or remains in place on leave. Each answer affects whether a filing is required and what it must say.

The harder call concerns the misconduct itself. A scandal can become a disclosure issue well before anyone leaves. The test for materiality in U.S. securities law traces to the Supreme Court's 1976 decision in TSC Industries v. Northway. Under that standard, information is material if there is a substantial likelihood that a reasonable shareholder would consider it important. Allegations involving financial reporting, related-party dealings or misuse of company funds tend to raise that question more directly than purely personal conduct.

Outside counsel and the audit committee usually guide this judgment. The audit committee oversees financial reporting and the company's relationship with its outside auditor. If the conduct touches the books, the auditor may need to be told. The company's internal controls may also come under review.

Disclosure rules also constrain what executives say elsewhere. The SEC's Regulation FD bars public companies from sharing material nonpublic information selectively with analysts or large investors before telling the wider market. A private call to a major shareholder about the investigation can create a separate compliance problem.

Companies listed outside the U.S. face their own regimes. In the European Union, the Market Abuse Regulation requires issuers to disclose inside information as soon as possible. The U.K. applies its own retained version of that regulation. Under both, delay is permitted only on limited grounds.

Whether a CEO leaves "for cause" or on negotiated terms largely decides what the executive takes on the way out, including severance and unvested equity. Clawback rights can let a company go further and recover pay it has already awarded.

Most CEO employment agreements define what counts as cause. Typical definitions include serious misconduct, a breach of company policy or a violation of law. A for-cause termination usually cuts off severance. It can also mean forfeiting stock awards that have not yet vested. A departure without cause, or a negotiated resignation, often triggers the payouts written into the contract.

That makes the label a financial decision as well as a disciplinary one. The board's compensation committee typically handles it alongside counsel. The investigation's findings carry weight here. A for-cause call has to rest on what the facts show. A CEO who disputes it can challenge it in court.

Clawbacks deal with pay that has already been handed over. The Securities and Exchange Commission adopted Rule 10D-1 under the Dodd-Frank Act. It requires companies listed on U.S. exchanges to adopt written clawback policies covering their executive officers. The exchange listing standards that carry out the rule became effective on Oct. 2, 2023.

The rule has a narrow trigger. It covers incentive-based pay awarded on the basis of financial results that were later corrected through an accounting restatement. It reaches back over the three completed fiscal years before the restatement. It applies whether or not the executive was at fault. A scandal about personal conduct, with no restatement, does not bring the mandatory policy into play.

Some companies write broader clawback provisions into their own policies or employment contracts. Those can reach misconduct that the federal rule does not cover. Whether a board can use them depends on the language it adopted before the scandal began.

McDonald's $MCD offers a case in point. The company fired CEO Steve Easterbrook in November 2019 over a relationship with an employee. It later sued to recover pay after finding further misconduct. The two sides settled the dispute.

A separation agreement signed early sets terms based on what the board knows at that moment. If more misconduct surfaces afterward, getting money back can require a lawsuit, as it did at McDonald's.

A board facing a CEO scandal speaks through one consistent message, telling employees, shareholders, customers and regulators what it knows and what happens next. At the same stage, it starts or speeds up the search for a permanent chief executive.

Message discipline sits at the center of this step. The board typically designates one spokesperson, often the chair or lead independent director. Counsel usually reviews every statement before release. Mixed signals from different executives can suggest the board has lost control of the situation. Conflicting versions of events can also create legal exposure.

The message itself tends to follow a narrow pattern. It states the facts the board has confirmed and describes the process under way. It explains who is running the business day to day. It avoids speculating about findings that are not yet complete. A statement that later proves wrong may need a public correction. That correction can reopen the story.

Employees usually hear from the company first or at the same moment as the public. Staff who learn about a leadership crisis from news coverage or social media may fill the gap with rumor. Internal memos and town-hall meetings let senior leaders explain what changes for teams and what stays the same. Managers often receive talking points so they can answer questions in the same terms.

External audiences have different concerns. Large shareholders want to know whether strategy and financial guidance still hold. Customers and suppliers want assurance that contracts, deliveries and service will continue. In heavily regulated industries such as banking or health care, regulators may expect direct contact beyond any public statement.

The permanent CEO search runs alongside this outreach. Boards with a standing succession plan can draw on internal candidates who have already been assessed. Boards without one often retain an executive search firm and weigh outside candidates against internal ones. The nominating or governance committee usually leads that work and reports to the full board.

The choice of successor also carries a message. An internal pick can signal continuity in strategy and operations. An outside hire can signal a break from the previous leadership and its culture. In either case, the board's explanation of why it chose that person becomes part of the public record of how it handled the scandal.
