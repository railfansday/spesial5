Inside the dismal final days of Jensen Huang: a broken man heard saying things like ‘whatever’ and ‘I don’t care’

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

We’re getting some extraordinary reporting on what it has been like inside Nvidia during Huang’s last days. Before his forced resignation, Huang had given up, Bloomberg says, and was heard saying things like “whatever,” “I don’t care,” and referring to himself as a “piece of sh--.”

That, of course, was in stark contrast to how Huang historically ran the company. Huang had “imperial control,” and ran it like a “feudal system,” according to the FT. “I can’t tell you what a cult this place is,” a source told the paper. General counsel Tim Teter wore a leather jacket just like Huang’s, the source said: “Jensen is a god, and that’s how he’s worshipped.”

Noteworthy: In addition to having no board chair, the company also did not have a compliance officer, the FT says.

Interim CEO Colette Kress and whoever replaces her as permanent CEO will have messy office politics to deal with, according to the FT. Huang’s daughter Madison Huang is a senior director of marketing at the company, but she acts like her father’s de facto chief of staff, the FT says. Nvidia also employs her husband Nico Caprez and Huang’s son Spencer.

Kress, meanwhile, sometimes did not get Huang’s respect, the WSJ reported. He once called her “a deer in the headlights” and a “reporting CFO” (as opposed to a strategic leader) during a meeting in which they were discussing Nvidia’s agreements “to provide billions of dollars of guarantees to companies that couldn’t afford its chips,” the WSJ said.

Kress struggled to rein in Caprez, who had made multiple offers of financing to cloud providers and data-center builders in return for shares of revenue.

Lenders relied on the Huang family's backing for AI financing when structuring deals with AI providers. Without Huang at the top, “that Nvidia guarantee is not worth what it was worth a week ago, that puts those neocloud businesses at risk,” Joe Kaiser of Switchyard Partners, an AI financing company, told Fortune’s Catherina Gioino.

Ex-Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang and commerce secretary Howard Lutnick have been called by Sen. Tim Scott (R-South Carolina) to testify in front of the Senate Banking Committee, the NYT reports. The hearing will be conducted next month. “Recent reports raise serious concerns about our national security and the protection of sensitive U.S. technology,” Scott said.

That’s a reference to Huang’s relationship with a suspected Chinese spy who ran a company that bought Nvidia chips and is suspected of smuggling them into China in violation of U.S. export controls.

As for Lutnick, Republicans are furious at what they perceive was the special treatment he gave Nvidia in terms of its access to the Chinese market and in his foot-dragging on the enforcement of export bans to China, the FT says.

Lutnick shielded Nvidia from the White House’s attempts to restrict export of the U.S.’s best technology to China, the NYT reported.

All matters related to Nvidia were routed through a deputy to Lutnick, Jeff Kessler, who rarely took action without Lutnick’s approval, the NYT said.

Inside the White House, senior Trump administration figures largely blame Lutnick for being asleep at the wheel in terms of the threat Nvidia posed to national security, given its ties to Chinese importers, the WSJ reported.

​​Alice Huang: Those concerns will get extra momentum on the news that Jensen Huang’s affair partner, suspected spy Alice Huang (no relation) of Megaspeed, had a relationship with Huawei, China’s biggest competitor in China, according to CNBC.

Alice Huang allegedly ran a complicated network of companies to do deals with Nvidia that resulted in its chips ending up at Chinese companies, per Bloomberg.

For decades, the markers of extreme wealth were easy to spot: the second home, the fancy cars, the Ivy League education. New analysis of Federal Reserve data points to a subtler one: a third child.

Mike Konczal, vice president of policy and research at the Economic Security Project and a former White House economist, studied households headed by 45- to 64-year-olds in his analysis of the Fed’s Survey of Consumer Finances, pooling five surveys from 2013 through 2025. He found that 49% of top-1% earners have three or more children, compared with about 40% of the rest of the top 10%.

Families with four or more kids are about as common in both groups, so the gap comes down to the third child: 34% of the 1% stop at exactly three, versus 24% of the merely affluent.
