How to be more productive: 6 tips to help you get things done

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

You start your day with a plan: clear your to-do list, get ahead, maybe even sign off early. Instead, your attention gets pulled in every direction. Back-to-back calls, a stream of emails, countless tabs open at once. Next thing you know, the day's almost over, your list is still full, and you're left wondering how you’re supposed to get anything done.

Busyness and productivity can look identical from the outside, but they’re not the same. You can stay busy all day tackling small, urgent-feeling tasks without ever touching the work that really counts. Being productive means doing the tasks that matter, which requires staying intentional about where your time and energy go.

So what's getting in the way of your productivity, and what can you do about it? Here’s a closer look at the everyday friction that can slow you down, plus a few shifts to help you make the most of each day.

Most productivity drains are minor, routine points of friction that are easy to miss, yet they add up fast. A few usual suspects:

Context switching: Research shows that every jump between tasks costs you the time it takes to mentally reset, even when it feels instant.

Constant interruptions: Responding to notifications and drop-in requests can feel productive, but it turns your attention to other people's priorities.

Unclear priorities: When everything feels urgent, you often default to whatever's quickest instead of what matters most.

Running on empty: Skipping breaks seems like it saves time, but it slows you down later and wears down your focus.

Procrastination: When a task feels overwhelming or unclear, it’s tempting to reach for something more manageable instead of starting the job you need to do.

Good news: you can reduce these points of friction without turning your whole day upside down. The tips below aren’t a set system, just a few ideas you can implement on your own terms. Start with one or two, and notice what feels different.

Decide what would make today count before you open your inbox. Not five things, just one that matters most. Write it down somewhere visible, then dedicate the first stretch of your day to that task before anything else pulls your focus away.

Multitasking may feel efficient, but your brain can’t really do two things at once. It's switching fast between them, and each switch costs you a little focus and energy. Over a full day, those costs add up.

Try working in single-task blocks. Close the extra tabs, silence your phone, and stay with one task until it's done or you hit a natural stopping point. You'll often finish faster than if you’d juggled three tasks at once.

The apps and platforms you use are built to stretch your attention in a dozen directions. Every ping invites you to switch tasks, and you’re driven to take the bait without thinking twice.

You don't have to go off-grid to take back control. Check your email at two or three set times instead of leaving it open. Turn off notifications you don't need, and put your phone in another room during focused work. If one site keeps tempting you, a browser blocker can hold the line.

Your focus isn't a tap you can leave running. If you push too long without pausing, your attention frays, mistakes creep in, and even small tasks can start to feel hard.

Try working in stretches of about 25 minutes, then step away to stretch, breathe, or look out a window. It’s also worth protecting your downtime in the evenings and on weekends, so you're not completely drained by mid-week.

Your to-do list is probably a mix of very different kinds of work. A report needs deep focus. A stack of emails or calls may not need any. Some work requires creativity, while some mainly requires attention to detail. Tackling tasks in a random order means starting cold, over and over.

But if you batch similar tasks, you can move through them while you're already warmed up for that type of work. Keep your quick replies to a couple of set windows instead of scattered across the day, and stack your calls so they run one after another. Once you're in the groove of one kind of work, staying there beats starting over.

A task tends to stretch to fill whatever time you give it. Timeboxing flips that. You pick a task, decide up front how long it gets—say 30 minutes—then stop when the time runs out, whether you're finished or not. That cap keeps one email from swallowing a whole morning, and stops you from polishing something long past the point of diminishing returns.

It also takes the guesswork out of what comes next, since you've chosen in advance. Block the slot on your calendar and set a timer. When the box closes, decide consciously whether the task gets another round.

You can train yourself to be more productive by gradually incorporating new helpful habits, like choosing a top priority for the day, single-tasking, and reducing digital distractions. Start with one small change and repeat it until it feels natural. One well-known study found that a new behavior took an average of 66 days to feel automatic—ranging from 18–254 days depending on the person—so give yourself time to adjust.

Yes, people with ADHD often struggle with productivity, and it’s not about effort or willpower. ADHD involves differences in executive function, the mental skills you use to start tasks, plan, and hold attention. This is why even starting tasks you want to do can feel difficult. The strategies in this article are still useful, though you may need to adapt them to what works for your brain.

Productivity peacocking is when you put more energy into looking busy than into doing meaningful work. Think replying to messages instantly instead of finishing the task at hand, or reorganizing your to-do list for the third time instead of crossing items off.

There's no single best time of day to work, since it depends on your body clock and when your energy naturally rises. Instead of trying to follow someone else's schedule, notice when you feel most alert, and dedicate that window to your most demanding work.

Yes, it's possible to be more productive without working longer hours, since a lot of time gets lost to switching tasks and reacting to interruptions. Try protecting focused blocks, sticking to one task at a time, and taking proper breaks, since a tired brain works slowly and rest can help you get more done.

Stress affects productivity by making it harder to focus, plan, and think clearly. When you're stressed, your brain pulls resources away from the areas that handle focus and memory. Bring your stress down and calm your mind with a few minutes of slow breathing.

One of the most effective ways to stop getting distracted while working is to remove the distraction before you start. Silence notifications, close the extra tabs, and place your phone out of reach so you're not relying on willpower in the moment. When your mind wanders anyway, and it will, bring it back without giving yourself a hard time.

Yes, meditation can help you be more productive, though the effect is indirect. In one randomized study, a two-week mindfulness course improved people's working memory and reduced how often their minds wandered mid-task.

You don't need long sessions, and even a few minutes a day of focusing your attention on your breath works the same mental muscle you use to stay on track while working.
