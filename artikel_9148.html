After Exile, Luis Manuel Otero Alcántara’s Prison Drawings Arrive in New York

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

In one of Luis Manuel Otero Alcántara’s prison drawings, swans skim a swimming pool inside a cell. In another, a clownish figure peers over the prison wall, perched with apparent ease on barbed wire. The Cuban artist and leader of the San Isidro Movement made thousands of such works in Guanajay, a maximum-security prison outside Havana, where he served time after his arrest on July 11, 2021, amid anti-government protests across the island. His release this year, after international outcry over creative freedom, was contingent on exile to the United States. A small selection of those works makes up “Tocar el cielo,” their first public showing, now at the New York gallery Foreign & Domestic (curated by Alexander Meurice, and on view through December 13). Art that emerges from incarceration tends to speak first in material, with scarcity making resourcefulness something like a protagonist. Otero Alcántara recounted drawing on drink cartons and cigarette packs, and smuggling in colored ink that ended up on other prisoners’ skin, when bodies were canvas enough. Who gets to make art inside, and who can get supplies to them, comes with its own politics. But he describes his five years in prison as one long performance, an “involuntary artistic gesture,” and the show reflects rather than transcribes the specifics of injustice. It’s an oblique testimony to how, bereft of sky or savior, the mind can slip its confines.

ARTnews spoke with Otero Alcántara via email about his experience making art in prison, and settling into his second life in Miami. The following conversation has been translated from Spanish and condensed for publication.

ARTnews: How is your spirit these days? What has surprised you most about settling into life here?

Luis Manuel Otero Alcántara: I think it’s been a shock. It’s unsettling, and it scares me a little.

People outside Cuba could connect with my work, but it grew out of life in the neighborhood, out of my connection with the people there. Above all, it came from knowing that neighborhood called Cuba, that neighborhood called Havana, where I was like a fish in water.

In New York, I’m getting to know other ways of thinking, other ways of doing things. I’m learning how art relates to people here and how people relate to art. There’s the language barrier, too. In my own practice, I want art to disappear into the social fabric, into people’s lives. I want it to have a real effect on the person walking down the street.

I feel like an embryo, like I’m learning from scratch how art and people relate to one another. So yes, it scares me. I’d call it a difficult process, but it’s a living process.

Can you tell me about making art in prison? How did you get supplies, and when did you work? What did materials typically cost you?

I started making art the moment I set foot in prison. They put me in a punishment cell, and I spent about two or three months there. Before prison, I was detained more than 50 times in three years. Even if they only held me for two or three days, I always made something in the cell. At first, I didn’t have the materials I had later. When I finished a drink my family had brought me, I’d keep the carton and draw on it. I used cigarette packs too. I’d stitch them together to make a surface I could draw on.

Once the materials from home ran out, my friends sent me more. When I called, all those friends of mine—Yanelys Núñez, Claudia Genlui, Coco Fusco, Camila Lobón, Anamely Ramos—picked up the phone. That was an act of resistance and an act of friendship. They took money they could have spent on food or on things they needed and used it to send me good art supplies, really good ones, all the way to Cuba, into the prison where I was being held.

That’s what I mean by cultural resistance. For my friends, it meant keeping the art alive and keeping the artist alive. They taught me how good it is to have a friend, and how good it is to be one.

Can you tell me about the recurring figure in the prison cell works? You’ve depicted him as a clown. Who does he represent? Who, or what, is the joke?

The clown keeps coming back in my work. When I look back at everything I made in prison, from beginning to end, I can see that the figures in those first drawings and paintings really were clowns. Over time, all that was left of the clown was the nose and traces of this strange makeup that gradually broke down. The colors started to bleed into one another. I feel that had something to do with how I was changing, how I was growing older in prison. At one point, even the paper tears. I tear it. I don’t know if it was hatred, if I was channeling hatred through my hands, or frustration. I don’t know why it was a clown.

As for the question about the joke, I remember being on hunger strike once, in a group of prisoners that included several men who’d protested on July 11. We’d become friends in there. They were getting sentences of ten years, eight years, five years. It literally seemed like a joke.

To me, the whole thing felt like a circus, like watching a show, only this show had real consequences for people’s lives. So if there’s a joke in it, if there’s an irony, that’s where I see it. They put up this whole circus tent. What happens under it affects so many people’s lives, and none of it makes any sense.What was it like to go years without seeing your whole reflection?

In prison, there was a tiny mirror. Mirrors are prized possessions in there. You hold yours out and someone in another cell, maybe three or six feet away, holds his out too. And there, in that little piece of mirror, you can see the other man’s face. You can see when a guard is coming.

If your mirror is only four inches across, all you ever see of yourself is a piece of your face. You never see your whole body. You go into prison looking one way and come out looking another. The first time I stood in front of a full-length mirror again, it was like coming back to see someone and finding myself face to face with a new person.

I think that found its way into my work. I have a series of self-portraits where my features start to disappear. They’re smudges, scratches, moods. Most of the work I made in prison was like that. I painted states of mind.

The only portraits that came close to being realistic were the ones I made of my friends. It was all about memory. I wanted to keep existing in my friends’ minds, and I wanted to remember them, too.Two of your works involve faith: a cross, and beside it a pile of idols sinking into an abyss. What is the relationship between your art and faith?

My art has a lot to do with faith and religion. For me, those are two different things. Both are tied to being Cuban. And for as long as I can remember, people have had faith that the system would fall, that the dictatorship would come to an end, that life would get better.

In Cuba, you have very little power to change the reality you live in. There’s no democracy, and the system makes you feel like you can’t fend for yourself. On the island, you’re always waiting for somebody on the outside to save you. And by “save you,” I mean a cousin or a brother sending you money. It’s like having faith in a god who lives somewhere out there, some kind of extraterrestrial being who’s going to keep you going on the island. You put your faith in finding a girlfriend or a boyfriend who has money, or in a foreigner coming along, marrying you and taking you away. The system is so rigid that you have very little room to change your own circumstances.

Before prison, I made performances that drew on images of the Virgin Mary and the saints. What interests me is how these objects change the way people behave, how they shape a culture, and how those in power use them to manipulate people.

Prison is maybe one of the last places where you lose whatever say you still had over your own life. Your future is in the hands of a prosecutor or whoever else gets to decide. It’s like living in a jungle where a lion decides which animal it wants to eat that day, and you pray it won’t be you.

Confinement also brings you face-to-face with yourself. You get in touch with something more visceral in yourself, something more animal. And all you’re left with is the faith that tomorrow might be better.

That reminds me of a story about a friend of mine in prison. He’d been waiting a year or so for his trial, and he was getting desperate. Nothing had been proven against him.

One day he used the five minutes a week they gave him on the phone to call his lawyer. He said, “Hey, tell me something. Any news?”

His lawyer said, “Don’t worry. Last night I dreamed they were going to let you go.”

In another work, a maze fills the cell. Is it a way out or another kind of trap?

I think the labyrinth has to do with how you feel in prison. When you’re in prison, you really have no way out. But there’s also the question of what you can do with that one small space. You have to turn it into your own amusement park. For me, the labyrinth is about the thousand ways you have to reinvent one space to survive in it.

There’s also something about getting lost so you can find yourself again, and finding yourself only to get lost again. As time goes by, you get tangled up in nostalgia, in sadness, in forgetting. You feel whole places in your mind starting to disappear, and you find yourself holding on to the memory of them.

Your practice is so community-driven. How did you keep that part of yourself alive in solitary?

The place where I lived was called Pasillo 20, Corridor 20. It was in the maximum-security section at Guanajay, cut off from most of the other prisoners. For five years, I lived among about 20 men.

Some of the men painted or drew. I started what I called the San Isidro Grants. For two or three months, as long as my money lasted, I gave some money to men who painted and bought them materials so they could keep painting. I brought in art history books and gave them away.

I also made work outside the prison while I was still inside. One piece that mattered a lot to me was Momento Cero, which I made with Coco Fusco. People counted down the days I had left in prison. There was also Fe de vida. During the Havana Biennial, we invited curators and artists to come to visit me in prison. Reaching out to people outside was connected to what I was doing with the men I lived with, finding ways to make room for community, kindness and love in a place that was so violent.

You arrived in the United States at a moment when artists here are facing censorship and funding cuts on a scale I haven’t seen in my lifetime. What has it been like to land in this context?

First of all, I feel like I haven’t quite landed here yet. I mean that physically, in my body, but also in my thinking and how I see things as an artist. What I can say is that the little I’ve seen and heard here doesn’t come close to the lack of freedom in Cuba.

Now I’ve come to a country I hope will be a place of passage for me. When you speak from New York, your words carry more legitimacy than if you’re speaking from, say, Havana or Haiti. I don’t know why the world gives that so much weight. Well, yes, I do know why.

Coming here, I can see that this country is going through a transition, a crisis. There’s conflict in the relationships between institutions, art, and artists. I think this is a moment when artists have to take responsibility. We can use art to resist, to change things, and to put forward a vision of a better world.

Do you see any parallels between what you experienced in Cuba and what’s happening here, or do you see them as fundamentally different?

The danger in drawing a parallel is that someone can always say, “Look what happens to people in Cuba. That doesn’t happen to you here. You’ve got me to thank for that, so you have to do what I say.” That’s where populism comes in. If you understand which freedoms an American artist enjoys and what artists elsewhere have to live without, you have a starting point. You can defend the ground you’ve gained and recognize when someone is using those freedoms to blackmail you.

We need to keep those differences in view. That’s how we recognize what we’ve already won as a nation and keep fighting for the freedoms we still need.

How have Cubans on the island and in the diaspora responded to the politics of your release and to your ability to keep making art in prison?

I think my art and the art of my generation, the work of the San Isidro Movement and others, have had an impact on Cuba, both on the island and in the diaspora. It’s only been five years since July 11, 2021, and people already recognize the part that my work, and the art we made together in San Isidro, played in the spirit of rebellion that emerged in 2018, 2019, 2020 and 2021.

For me, those five years in prison are one big work of art, one long performance. I call it “an involuntary artistic gesture.” The dictatorship acts on my body against my will, and what it does to me provokes an aesthetic response in other people.

The other thing that matters is social media and everything that played out there when I arrived in the United States. What struck me was how it exposed some bad habits that have taken hold in the way Cubans use social media. I’m talking about people acting irresponsibly. Some influencers have learned how to make money on YouTube, Facebook and Instagram. Controversy brings in viewers, and viewers bring in money.

What matters most to me is what I’ve experienced walking the streets of Miami. From 99 percent of the people I’ve met, if not 100 percent, what I’ve felt is affection. I’ve felt love.

When you think about what art is for and what an artist owes the world, has your view changed since prison?

I think art is one of the tools we have as human beings to transform society and culture. And when I say culture, I mean it in the broadest sense, right down to the way we behave. Those in power know what art can do. They use it when it serves their interests. When it doesn’t, they go after it. They try to put art itself in prison.

Art is one of the teeth on the gear that keeps society moving. And the artist is the one who thinks, who proposes something, who puts their own body on the line. So, I think the artist bears the greatest responsibility.

An artist’s responsibility extends to humanity as a whole. There’s a commitment to humanism there: understanding that you have power and, more than that, a responsibility. Because if you don’t use your art to help people, someone else will use it for purposes that don’t help them.

When you picture your studio, or your next show, a year from now, what do you see?

A year from now, I’d love the work I brought out of Guanajay to have found homes in archives, museums and private collections. I want someone standing there to get a sense of what one human being lived through in confinement. I was a Cuban in prison. I was also a human being locked up. Someone in a prison anywhere in the world could go through what I went through, you know? For my studio, what I want is room to work and room to try things. There are sculptures I want to propose, ideas I couldn’t develop in Cuba because of the repression.

I also hope I’ll have helped more Americans understand something about Cuba. Our two peoples are so close, yet there’s still a romantic view of Cuba here, and that has consequences for us Cubans. Fidel Castro was a dictator. We’ve lived under a dictatorship for more than 60 years.

More than anything, I’d like people in the arts here to understand our work and make room for it in the stories and images that are part of everyday American life. What happens on that island finds echoes in so many other places.
