Although rare, some routes, such as the Qantas 'Antarctica Flights' brand, conduct sightseeing flights of the continent. Several other flights operate near Antarctica, albeit not as a full transcontinental route.

Many operating limitations, such as the remote nature of the poles and harsh weather conditions, solicit special operating requirements and procedures for safe flight. While the South Pole is generally considered unsafe for full crossing, the North Pole is not subject to the same limitation. Canada, Greenland, Iceland, and Russia all provide acceptable diversion options and air traffic control services. The Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) is a global navigation aid comprising four major navigation constellations, as well as several regional systems. While the Global Positioning System (GPS) is the household name in the United States, there are equivalent systems worldwide that serve the same primary function:

GNSS consists of three components: the Space Segment, the User Segment, and the Control Segment. How that manifests in flight operations is that it is a closed system, though not immune to anomalies: the Space Segment consists of the physical satellites orbiting Earth at all times. Per the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), 31 satellites are currently operational in their orbit, and GPS requires a baseline core of 24 operational satellites to function globally. The User Segment consists of the physical GPS receivers installed on the aircraft, with functionality extending to many "user" modalities: Wide Area Augmentation System (WAAS) and Receiver Autonomous Integrity Monitoring (RAIM) are the two that apply to GNSS. Finally, the Control Segment is a series of ground-based monitoring stations that supervise the integrity of the satellite performance.

Some flights operate long distances from the nearest airport.

WAAS is not usable in polar regions because its geostationary satellites sit over the equator, leaving the high‑latitude areas with poor or no WAAS coverage, due to the curvature of the Earth. As a result, aircraft flying polar routes are not WAAS equipped, and rely on RAIM/ARAIM and true‑north navigation methods. RAIM remains fully usable in polar operations because it relies on GPS satellite geometry and onboard integrity algorithms rather than on equatorial geostationary augmentation such as WAAS. For polar flights, RAIM is the primary GPS integrity method.

However, while reliable, RAIM and the broader GNSS system are not foolproof in polar regions. While satellite-based navigation is highly reliable at lower latitudes, such as in the contiguous lower 48 United States, its reliability degrades toward the poles. Therefore, all flights operating polar routes are equipped with backup navigational aids (navaids) such as Inertial Reference System (IRS) navigation, ground-based navigation such as VOR/DME, or dead reckoning. Per FAA guidance (ENR 7.3), GNSS anomalies must be reported to ATC immediately, and the FAA offers an online report for a more detailed review once the flight is on the ground.

Contingency procedures from ENR 7.3 are critical when an aircraft cannot maintain its clearance. This is common in polar regions due to weather, system failures, or the aforementioned navigation degradation. Therefore, a standard maneuver is performed when an aircraft deviates from its course assigned by ATC: a pilot will turn at least 30° left or right, move onto a track that’s 5 miles parallel to the original one.

Maintain the assigned altitude if possible: otherwise, minimize the descent rate. Then, add a small vertical offset (500 ft, or 1000 ft above FL410). Lastly, broadcast intentions on 121.5 (colloquially known as 'guard, the emergency frequency) and 123.45, which is officially recognized by the ICAO as an air-to-air advisory frequency for aircraft operating over remote oceanic areas.

The standardized procedure primarily helps with collision avoidance and weather avoidance. ATC response can be delayed during flights over polar regions due to a lack of radar coverage. The procedure comes from ICAO, and the FAA adopted it into ENR 7.3. It was created over decades of international work on how to keep aircraft safely separated in places where ATC cannot see you, especially oceanic and polar airspace.

By having a standard operating procedure in the event of collision or weather avoidance, pilots can safely operate in polar regions with peace of mind. Finally, because GNSS failures and IRS drift are more likely at polar altitudes, the contingency maneuver helps mitigate the risk of two aircraft unknowingly converging when their navigation accuracy degrades.

By immediately turning 30°, shifting 5 NM off the track, and adding a vertical offset, the aircraft creates predictable, research-based, engineered separation even if its GPS position becomes unreliable or its IRS begins drifting. This standardized move ensures that even without radar, fast ATC response, or perfect navigation, every aircraft follows the same escape pattern, keeping them safely spaced until normal navigation can be restored.

To mitigate the risk of GNSS navigation leading to midair collisions and to avoid wake turbulence, crews use the Strategic Lateral Offset Procedure (SLOP). This allows pilots to offset up to 2.0 nautical miles right of their track, which is considered an SOP for all oceanic crossings, per FAA guidance (ENR 7.1).

"Along a route or track there will be 21 positions that an aircraft may fly: on centerline or at increments of ­0.1 NM (for example, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4...1.8, 1.9, 2.0) right of centerline out to a maximum offset of 2 NM. Offsets ­must not exceed 2 NM right of centerline. The intent of this procedure is to reduce risk (add safety margin) by ­distributing aircraft laterally across the 21 available positions."

Pilots who cannot program automatic offsets must stay on the centerline, or use the 1 NM or 2 NM right offsets when applying SLOP. Offsetting helps reduce wake turbulence, and pilots should choose the best available right‑of‑track position and coordinate on 123.45 if needed, especially when overtaking another aircraft. They must never offset left of the centerline or more than 2 NM to the right, ensuring all aircraft remain predictably separated.

High‑latitude flying squeezes aircraft closer together because the meridians converge, which reduces the natural sideways spacing between tracks. At the same time, navigation accuracy drops near the poles due to magnetic field unreliability and a higher likelihood of GNSS or IRS drift. SLOP spreads aircraft laterally to counter these risks, creating predictable right‑of‑track separation and lowering the chance of a head‑on conflict in non‑radar polar airspace. SLOPs are primarily used for aircraft traffic flow and spacing, rather than for weather; pilots should instead take a "see and avoid" approach in relation to weather, work with ATC, and the aforementioned contingency maneuver.

Polar Flights are placed on an ICAO flight plan. In Field 15, pilots or dispatchers are required to enter all intended waypoints for the planned route of flight. Of course, such flight plans are always subject to change due to weather, traffic, and other factors. Navigation reliability in polar regions depends on maintaining RNP capability and filing the correct ICAO codes. Because magnetic variation becomes unusable at extreme latitudes, aircraft must rely on true‑heading navigation and high‑integrity long‑range systems. Polar operations require PBN/A1 or PBN/L1, plus specific datalink codes like P2, D1, and J5/J7 for ATC surveillance.

These requirements ensure the aircraft can hold precise tracks where lateral separation is minimal and ground‑based navaids are nonexistent. Reliable datalink communication becomes essential because HF propagation degrades near the poles. ADS‑C and CPDLC provide continuous position monitoring, enabling controllers to maintain safe spacing in airspace with no radar coverage.

Ultimately, robust navigation capability protects against the unique risks of polar flying: magnetic unreliability, GNSS degradation, and increased IRS drift. By filing correct capabilities and ensuring all long‑range navigation systems are fully functional, pilots maintain compliance with FAA oceanic procedures and preserve the accuracy needed for safe polar route operations. An ICAO flight plan filed with precision and strong planning also helps mitigate flight disruptions over polar regions, whether by the pilot or a company dispatcher.
