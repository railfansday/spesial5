Physicists Discover a Strange New Form of Magnetism in a Simple Metal

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Electronics can use more than electrical charge, and evidence of unusual magnetism in europium silicide could help researchers explore how to put electron spin to work.

At very low temperatures, a simple metal made from europium and silicon develops a spiral pattern of magnetism that leaves it with no overall magnetization. Its electrons nevertheless appear to favor opposite spin orientations when they travel in opposite directions, an unusual relationship that could offer researchers new ways to explore and control electronic behavior.

An international team has found compelling evidence for this state, called p-wave magnetism, in europium silicide, or EuSi. The findings, published in Nature, provide an experimental foothold in a field where suitable materials have been difficult to find.

Spin is a quantum property that gives electrons a magnetic character, somewhat like tiny compass needles. In a conventional ferromagnet, spins tend to align in the same direction, producing net magnetization. In EuSi, between approximately 33 and 62 kelvin, or about −400 to −348 °F, the magnetic moments of europium atoms instead form an ordered spiral. The individual moments are substantial, but their contributions cancel overall.

The predicted relationship between opposite directions of electron motion and opposite spin polarizations is known as an odd-parity spin texture. Finding a material in which researchers can investigate it has been challenging. Previous candidates include electrical insulators and metals that develop magnetic order only at very low temperatures or have additional symmetry complications. EuSi combines a fixed chemical composition with a spiral magnetic phase that has the required symmetry, and researchers can grow high-quality single crystals for experiments.

Dr. Erjian Cheng grew those crystals approximately three years ago at the Max Planck Institute for Chemical Physics of Solids (MPI CPfS) in Dresden. Initial photoemission experiments, which use light to eject electrons and examine their properties, showed that magnetism had an unusually large influence on EuSi’s electronic band structure, the pattern of energies electrons can occupy inside the material. That raised the possibility that its magnetic order might link spin to the direction of electron motion.

The investigation brought together 38 researchers from 19 institutions, including ShanghaiTech University, the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, the Max Planck Institute for the Physics of Complex Systems, and Oak Ridge National Laboratory.

Combining theoretical calculations with several experimental techniques, the researchers examined both the atomic magnetic arrangement and its effects on electrons. Neutron diffraction revealed the spiral of europium magnetic moments and confirmed that its symmetry met the conditions for p-wave magnetism. Angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy, which maps electron energies and momenta, showed that magnetism substantially reshaped the electronic structure, producing energy separations exceeding 100 millielectronvolts.

Measurements that also resolved electron spin supplied a more specific test. The spin polarization changed sign across successive magnetic Brillouin zones, repeating regions in a map of electron momentum defined by the magnetic order. It also weakened sharply near regions where the predicted p-wave spin texture should vanish. Together, these observations provided spectroscopic evidence consistent with the theoretical state.

“When we first measured the electronic structure of EuSi, we were surprised by the exceptionally large effect of magnetism on the electrons,” explains Cheng. “That observation led us to investigate whether this simple compound could host a much more unusual magnetic state. The combination of crystal growth, neutron scattering and spin-resolved spectroscopy now provides compelling evidence for p-wave magnetism.”

The proposed spin pattern arises predominantly from magnetic exchange interactions, which influence how spins arrange themselves, and from the symmetry of the magnetic spiral. That distinguishes it from familiar spin-splitting effects driven mainly by spin-orbit coupling, an interaction between an electron’s spin and its motion.

The spiral also introduces handedness, or chirality. EuSi’s underlying crystal structure is centrosymmetric, meaning that it looks the same when inverted through its center. Its magnetic spiral, however, can twist in either of two opposite senses. Using magnetic force microscopy and spatially resolved photoemission spectroscopy, the researchers identified magnetic domains approximately ten micrometers across.

When examined with circularly polarized light, domains with opposite magnetic handedness showed distinct electronic responses. The results demonstrated that the spiral’s handedness is coupled to the electronic structure, connecting the organization of the atomic magnetic moments with the behavior of the electrons.

“What makes EuSi particularly exciting is that an unconventional electronic spin structure emerges from the magnetic order of a chemically simple metal,” says Prof. Claudia Felser, Director at MPI CPfS. “This demonstrates how the discovery of new materials can reveal entirely new electronic phenomena. EuSi now gives us an experimental platform to investigate how magnetic symmetry and chirality can be used to control electronic properties, with potential implications for future spintronics and unconventional superconductivity.”

Spintronics uses electron spin as well as electrical charge in electronic devices. The findings could inform that field, along with research on topological quantum materials and unconventional superconductivity. They also illustrate how a solid’s properties depend on its magnetic order and electronic quantum states, alongside its chemical composition and crystal structure.
