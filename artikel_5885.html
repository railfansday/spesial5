$275M Obama-era solar fraud case could net taxpayers $1B, but Trump's DOJ backs Biden dismissal

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Pat Hogan, CEO of CMB Regional Centers, alleges fraud in a $275 million Treasury grant obtained by Crescent Dunes’ former owner and companies linked to Spanish construction giant Cobra.

This article is Part 2 of a Fox News Digital exclusive series investigating the Crescent Dunes solar power plant in Nevada. You can read Part 1 here.

The Trump administration has made fighting fraud and rooting out government waste a top priority, even offering cash rewards to Americans who expose wrongdoing.

Yet investors in a failed Obama-era solar plant are questioning why President Donald Trump's own Justice Department is fighting to squash their fraud lawsuit over a $275 million federal grant — a case they say could recover up to $1 billion for taxpayers.

The investors allege that Spanish construction giant ACS and companies to tied its former subsidiary Cobra, misled federal officials into approving the grant by claiming the troubled solar plant was ready for use, even though it had suffered major construction problems and was shut down for months following a molten-salt-tank failure.

The lawsuit centers on the beleaguered Crescent Dunes solar plant, a billion-dollar, government-backed project that began operating in Nevada in 2015 but was later plagued by engineering failures, repeated shutdowns and two bankruptcies before being sold for just $7 million in July.

The plant was built around an emerging solar technology that used thousands of mirrors to concentrate sunlight onto a towering receiver, heating molten salt that could be stored and used to generate electricity even after sunset.

In 2020, a group of private investors who had put $90 million into the project and suffered substantial losses filed a whistleblower lawsuit, alleging the companies had misled the federal government to secure the enormous grant.

But in November 2024, just two days after Trump's re-election, Biden's Justice Department moved to shut down the lawsuit, arguing that pursuing the case would burden federal agencies.

The incoming Trump administration could have reconsidered that position in early 2025. Instead, its Justice Department defended the Biden-era dismissal in an April filing with a federal appeals court.

CMB, an investment group headed by Pat Hogan, is appealing the dismissal. Hogan is also urging Trump’s Justice Department to ask the court to reopen the case.

"It makes no sense," Hogan told Fox News Digital in a video interview.

Hogan said he had contacted Trump's Justice Department seeking help reviving the case but had received no commitment.

"The real problem here is, why isn't the government asking for this?" he said. "All in all, I would say this has just been a fleecing of the federal government by a foreign entity. And we have the opportunity to fix it, and they're doing nothing."

Hogan argues that allowing the lawsuit to proceed could potentially net hundreds of millions of dollars for taxpayers.

Under the False Claims Act, which CMB used to bring its lawsuit, companies found liable can face damages of up to three times the government's proven losses, plus additional penalties.

"This could be a billion dollars," Hogan said.

Tonopah Solar Energy, the plant's former owner and one of the defendants, has rejected CMB's allegations as baseless in court filings. Other companies named in the lawsuit have also backed the Justice Department's effort to dismiss the case.

In an April 2026 court filing, Trump's Justice Department defended the Biden administration's decision, making the same argument that continuing the lawsuit would drain government resources and that federal law gave it broad authority to shut down the case. In court filings reviewed by Fox News Digital, neither administration put a dollar figure on the cost of continuing the lawsuit.

The decision has left Hogan and his investors questioning why the government shut down the case — and what Treasury officials knew about the plant's serious problems when they approved the $275 million grant.

At the heart of the lawsuit is a dispute over whether Crescent Dunes was properly completed — and whether its owner, Tonopah Solar Energy, was entitled to collect the $275 million Treasury grant.

The grant was part of a $26 billion program within the Obama administration's broader $90 billion green-energy push, launched under the 2009 economic stimulus package to create jobs and turbocharge its green energy agenda.

Known as Section 1603, the program paid developers about 30% of their qualifying construction costs once their facilities were "placed in service" — meaning ready and available for their intended use. Treasury considered several factors in determining whether a plant met that standard, including whether it had passed critical tests, begun generating electricity and come under its owner's control.

Crescent Dunes began generating electricity in 2015, but CMB alleges it had produced no more than 32% of its expected output through June 2016.

Then, in October 2016, a failure involving the plant's hot molten-salt storage tank shut it down for months.

In December, while Crescent Dunes was still shut down, its builder, Cobra Thermosolar Plants, formally handed the plant over to its owner, Tonopah Solar Energy, under an arrangement known as "provisional acceptance."

But first, they changed the construction contract to allow the handover despite major defects and unfinished repairs.

That agreement came just days before the year-end deadline for solar plants to be ready for use to qualify for the Treasury grants.

In a later court filing, CMB alleged that more than 9,000 defects had been identified and grouped into roughly 500 outstanding construction items, including seven major deficiencies. The investors argue the problems showed the plant was not ready to be handed over.

The December agreement itself acknowledged that repairs to the failed hot molten-salt storage tank were still needed. The tank was central to the plant's operation, storing heat from the sun so electricity could be generated even after sunset.

CMB alleges the companies used the revised agreement to make Crescent Dunes appear ready for use and secure the Treasury grant, even though major problems remained unresolved.

Records reviewed by Fox News Digital show Treasury had questioned whether Crescent Dunes was ready for use months before approving the grant payment.

In September 2016, Treasury questioned a claim by Tonopah Solar Energy that Crescent Dunes had been ready for use since February of that year. The agency pointed to a July commissioning report suggesting the builder, Cobra Thermosolar Plants, had not yet formally handed control of the facility over to Tonopah.

In a Sept. 21 response to Treasury's questions, Tonopah Solar Energy defended its claim, arguing that the plant had passed key tests and was capable of generating electricity. It also acknowledged an earlier extended shutdown caused by problems with the plant's salt-piping system.

The following month, the plant's hot molten-salt storage tank failed, forcing the plant offline for months.

Despite that breakdown, Tonopah and Cobra went ahead with the revised handover agreement in December.

On March 7, 2017, just weeks into the first Trump administration, Treasury approved a $243.7 million grant payment — even though Crescent Dunes remained shut down nearly five months after the tank failure.

The plant would not resume generating electricity until July 2017.

The approval letter said Treasury was still reviewing some of the project's claimed costs before deciding whether to release additional money.

In February 2018, Treasury approved an additional $31.9 million, bringing the total grant awards to approximately $275.6 million, according to newly obtained federal records. The additional payment was scheduled for disbursement within seven business days.

But Treasury was not the only federal agency involved.

The Department of Energy, which had backed Crescent Dunes with a $737 million federal loan guarantee, also had a say in major construction decisions. DOE approved the revised December handover despite unfinished repairs. It also waived certain missed deadlines, allowing the plant to be formally recognized as operational under its loan agreement.

That approval did not automatically qualify Crescent Dunes for the separate Treasury grant.

A separate December 2016 financing agreement involving Tonopah Solar Energy and DOE required the Treasury grant money to go first toward repaying the project’s short-term federal borrowing, including principal and accrued interest.

Documents obtained by Fox News Digital show that Tonopah Solar Energy made a $218.9 million payment toward a federal loan in March 2017, shortly after Treasury approved its initial grant award.

"So in a sense, the federal government just paid themselves, right? So they're still out the money, right," Hogan told Fox News Digital.

On April 12, 2017, while Crescent Dunes was still offline, DOE published an article celebrating the plant as a breakthrough in solar technology without mentioning that it had been shut down for months.

What remains unclear is whether Treasury officials knew about the October breakdown, unfinished repairs and December contract changes when they authorized the initial grant payment — information that could have affected whether the plant qualified for the grant.

Fox News Digital has asked DOE and Treasury about the grant's approval process, the plant's condition and taxpayer losses, but neither agency has provided a full accounting.

In a statement, the Trump administration's Department of Energy criticized previous administrations' handling of the project, saying it had resolved its financial interests in Crescent Dunes in 2020.

"It is unfortunate that previous administrations irresponsibly managed taxpayer dollars in their support of the Green New Scam," a DOE spokesperson said. "Since 2020, DOE has had no ownership, operational, or financial interest in the Obama-era project."

Fox News Digital has also filed Freedom of Information Act requests seeking grant payment records, loan accounting and communications between the agencies.

In January 2020, Hogan's investment group, CMB, which had lent $90 million to a company tied to Crescent Dunes developer SolarReserve, sued Tonopah Solar Energy and companies tied to Spanish construction giant ACS, accusing them of defrauding the federal government.

The lawsuit was filed under the False Claims Act, which allows private whistleblowers to sue on the government's behalf and receive a share of any money recovered. Known as "qui tam" cases, the lawsuits can be taken over or dismissed by the Justice Department.

After investigating CMB's allegations for more than three years, Biden's Justice Department declined to take over the lawsuit in June 2023, leaving CMB to pursue it alone.

On Nov. 7, 2024, just two days after Trump's election victory, Biden's Justice Department moved to dismiss the lawsuit, arguing that continuing the case would burden the Treasury, Energy and Justice departments.

CMB challenged the move, arguing that Biden's DOJ had failed to explain what had changed since it allowed the investors to proceed.

Then, in July 2025, six months into Trump's second term, a federal judge granted DOJ's request and dismissed the lawsuit without deciding whether the fraud allegations were true.

CMB appealed to the Ninth Circuit, where Trump's Justice Department defended the dismissal in an April 2026 court filing.

Matt Dunn, an attorney at Covington & Burling who specializes in False Claims Act litigation, described DOJ's decision to intervene after initially allowing the case to proceed as "unusual, but not extraordinary."

Dunn said the government may have its own assessment of a lawsuit's potential value that differs from the whistleblower's.

"The government might privately have a different view of the merits of the case or the potential value of the case that it doesn't necessarily share publicly," Dunn said.

Dunn said the Supreme Court has given the government substantial discretion to dismiss such cases, describing the legal hurdle for whistleblowers seeking to overturn a dismissal as "an extraordinarily high bar for a relator to overcome."

Hogan, meanwhile, has a financial stake in the outcome. He says his investors lost the $90 million they lent to the SolarReserve-linked company.

If the lawsuit is revived and CMB ultimately succeeds, the government theoretically could recover up to three times its proven damages under the False Claims Act, and CMB could receive a share, potentially offsetting its investors' losses.

"That's my job for my investors to leave no stone unturned to try to make sure that, in fact, I get their money back," Hogan said.

The Ninth Circuit must now decide whether the lawsuit can move forward — potentially opening the door for the government to recover hundreds of millions of dollars in disputed funds — or whether DOJ's decision to shut it down will stand.

The Justice Department's handling of the case has also drawn scrutiny on Capitol Hill.

In February 2025, Reps. Lance Gooden, R-Texas, and Carol Miller, R-W.Va., urged Attorney General Pam Bondi to investigate why the Biden administration moved to shut down the lawsuit.

Gooden later urged Secretary of War Pete Hegseth to consider suspending federal contracts with companies tied to Spanish construction giant ACS, whose subsidiary Cobra built the plant and held a stake in Tonopah Solar Energy. He also called for an investigation into ACS and Banco Santander's roles in the project's financing and bankruptcy amid the allegations of fraud involving federal funds.

"When taxpayers are defrauded to a scale this massive, our government has a responsibility to get the facts and hold those responsible to account," Gooden told Fox News Digital. "That is why I have asked Secretary Hegseth to pause contracts with ACS-affiliated entities until these allegations are fully reviewed and every avenue to recover taxpayer money has been pursued."

One such contract involves a $2.84 billion Navy dry dock project at Pearl Harbor awarded to a joint venture involving ACS subsidiary Dragados. The project completion date has been delayed from September 2027 to March 2029, according to the Government Accountability Office. Dragados is not accused of fraud in the Crescent Dunes lawsuit.

Miller, meanwhile, also questioned how a project backed by nearly $1 billion in federal financing could end up selling for $7 million.

"That's not good math," Miller told Fox News Digital. She suggested another congressional letter could be warranted, saying that "if we can recover any of that taxpayer money, that would be wonderful."

In April 2026, Senate Judiciary Committee Chairman Chuck Grassley, R-Iowa, a longtime defender of whistleblowers who helped strengthen the False Claims Act in 1986, demanded answers from DOJ over its handling of fraud lawsuits, particularly cases it shut down despite whistleblowers wanting to press ahead.

In a July 10 response obtained by Fox News Digital, DOJ revealed that Crescent Dunes was one of just 12 cases in which it moved to shut down a lawsuit after initially allowing it to proceed — fewer than 0.3% of nearly 5,000 whistleblower cases received between 2020 and 2025.

DOJ also confirmed that other federal agencies can push to have such lawsuits dismissed, including to avoid the burden of producing evidence. But it did not say whether Treasury or DOE pushed to kill the Crescent Dunes case — or what investigators found when they examined the fraud allegations.

The companies accused of fraud have backed DOJ's effort to dismiss the lawsuit, arguing that federal law gives the government broad authority to shut down whistleblower cases.

Tonopah Solar Energy, which filed for bankruptcy in January 2026, has also fiercely rejected CMB's allegations, accusing the investors of pursuing a "scorched earth mission" through repeated legal challenges against the company.

"The reality is there is no merit to CMB's claims whatsoever," the company argued in an April 2026 bankruptcy filing.

Tonopah Solar Energy made those arguments while winding down its affairs in bankruptcy, before selling the plant's assets in July. The new owners had no involvement in the alleged 2016 fraud, and the bankruptcy sale generally shielded them from the former owner's legal liabilities.

In a separate 2020 lawsuit, Hogan's investors sought to recover an allegedly unpaid $90 million loan, suing companies tied to Crescent Dunes after SolarReserve failed to repay much of the money. They suffered a setback in 2024 when an arbitration panel rejected five of their claims and ordered them to pay $2.44 million in legal fees and costs.

The companies did not respond to Fox News Digital's repeated requests for comment.

The dispute was separate from the whistleblower lawsuit alleging fraud involving the $275 million Treasury grant.

Despite years of investigation, the Justice Department has never publicly disclosed what it uncovered.

Nearly seven years after Hogan's investors filed their lawsuit, the fraud allegations have never been tested at trial. And despite President Trump's pledge to crack down on fraud and wasteful green-energy spending, his Justice Department is still defending the Biden administration's decision to shut down the case.

"If we're wrong, the evidence will show it," Hogan said. "But don't let a Biden-era dismissal prevent the merits from ever being heard."
