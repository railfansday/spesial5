How Al Horford's ‘strange blessing' made him a 3-point pioneer, 20-year NBA vet

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

How Al Horford's 'strange blessing' made him a 3-point pioneer, 20-year NBA vet originally appeared on NBC Sports Bay Area

Programming note: Al Horford's interview with Bonta Hill and Dalton Johnson on the "Dubs Talk" podcast debuts Friday morning, and can be found HERE.

Running diagonally across the left collarbone of Warriors jerseys throughout the 2026-27 NBA season is a yellow "Nellie" band honoring the late coaching great Don Nelson, who died at 86 years old on Aug. 9.

Invisible further down the left side over the chest of Warriors jerseys is a badge of honor only Al Horford can see. He carries the rarity of making it to 20 years in the NBA deep inside his heart. This season, Horford became the 13th player in NBA history to reach the milestone, and at 40 years old, he is the oldest ever to play in a Warriors jersey.

Mike Budenholzer coached Horford his final three seasons with the Atlanta Hawks from 2013 to 2016. Those were Horford's seventh, eighth and ninth NBA seasons. The thought of Horford ever reaching 20 seasons is instantly met with a "no" from Budenholzer.

But then Budenholzer begins immediately with "Well …" and the same praise that everybody around the league has for Horford follows.

"In some ways I can because he takes such great care of himself," Budenholzer says. "He is so meticulous with his body and his nutrition and diet and such a pro. But, I mean … 20 years. Somebody just a couple weeks ago told me he's getting to 20. I was like, holy s–t."

"But I didn't know there's only 13 guys that have done it. That is super cool, and I'm just happy for him. He's just one of the all-time great guys."

Former Florida Gators teammate Corey Brewer isn't surprised by Horford's longevity. Brewer and Horford won back-to-back national championships together in college, and were both top picks in the 2007 draft. Horford is one of three players remaining from their draft class, along with Kevin Durant and Mike Conley, and Brewer last played in 2020 when he suited up in five games for the Sacramento Kings.

"I can believe it," Brewer said. "That man takes care of himself, and like I said, Al's always doing the right thing. He would be one the guy [to play this long]."

Their college coach isn't surprised, either.

Billy Donovan watched Horford apply his lessons into his own talent and grow offensively over three years at Florida. Up close as Horford's opposing head coach in Oklahoma City and Chicago, Donovan has witnessed his evolution as a pioneer in basketball's big man revolution behind the 3-point line.

He'd be the first to jokingly give Horford trouble for making threes against him, and Donovan, now in his first season as the lead assistant coach for the San Antonio Spurs, is first in line to still praise him to this day.

"I am not one bit surprised that Al individually has had a Hall of Fame career, and I'm not surprised that collectively on a team he's impacted winning at such a high level, because he did it in college," Donovan said in an exclusive interview an hour before the Warriors' blowout loss to the Spurs on Nov. 13.

Lasting this long in the NBA is most special to Horford because of how much change the game has undergone.

Horford is an undersized center at 6-foot-8, and has always been a presence as a rim protector. He moved faster than most big men early in his career, outrebounded them and hit mid-range jumpers over them. Horford found early success making the playoffs as a rookie, and was named to his first two All-Star teams in his third and fourth seasons with the Hawks.

Between his All-Star seasons in 2010 and 2011, Horford averaged 14.7 points, 9.6 rebounds, 2.9 assists and 1.1 blocks per game, and shot 55.4 percent from the field. Horford also shot a total of five 3-pointers in those two seasons, and made two.

Offense was played within 15 feet for Horford when he first became a pro. It was all paint, and mid-range at most. Shooting threes wasn't even a thought, until it had to be.

Bringing Budenholzer to Atlanta put an even bigger emphasis on the Hawks shooting threes. Much more so for big men like Horford. Through his first six seasons, Horford attempted 18 recorded threes. He took 11 in his first 29 games trying to adjust under Budenholzer in the 2013-14 season, before his first long-term injury sidelined him the rest of the year.

The injury derailed the Hawks' hopes. They were the No. 3 seed in the Eastern Conference when Horford sustained a torn pectoral in a double-overtime win where he scored 25 points to go with eight rebounds, four assists, two blocked shots and one made three on his only attempt.

Without Horford, the Hawks went 22-31 and finished as the No. 8 seed in the East. Without the injury, Horford might never make it to Year 20 as someone who changed his game for the betterment of the team, and extended his own career.

"As I started to be challenged as a player, like, hey, the league is changing, I had a GM with the Hawks, Rick Sund, and after my first major injury, I tore my pectoral muscle, he was like, 'Listen, you're a physical player. You want to play down low, but you're not gonna make it playing like that. These guys are bigger than you; they're stronger. You have to start expanding your game. You have to start shooting the ball.'

"It was hard for me to understand," Horford remembers on the latest episode of the Dubs Talk podcast. "But I was like, I'm gonna start trying it, practicing it, and it felt really unnatural shooting the three ball."

Horford began shooting from the corners and progressed from there. Sund didn't draft Horford. He became the Hawks' general manager before Horford's second season, and their conversations about expanding his game really began when Sund transitioned into a senior advisor role in Atlanta.

Sund remembers talking Horford into taking threes from the corner after a practice as a point to prove he had the ability to make the shots. Horford already was such a great all-around player to Sund, but he wanted him to find any advantage to not be left behind and prolong his career with a combination of skills and high IQ.

"You can quote me on this: He's certainly Hall of Fame worthy," Sund says. "There's no question in my mind."

But Sund makes sure to credit Budenholzer as a "progressive" and "innovative" coach who was willing to collaborate, and came into Horford's life at just the right time.

"I think he saw where Al could really kind of blossom or be really good in that type of system," Budenholzer says of his conversations with Sund. "All of us together, putting our heads together, that was what was cool about Atlanta – a lot of great teamwork and at all different levels from front office to coaches to players, a lot of good synergy and a lot of good things happened."

As Horford rested and rehabbed his torn pec the rest of the 2013-14 season, he watched the game grow from a new perspective and connected on a higher level alongside Budenholzer, who then was a first-time head coach at 44 years old. Despite the loss of Horford, the Hawks kept growing as a team and buying into a unique offensive style.

All of a sudden, Horford was watching power forward Paul Millsap shoot 212 threes. Pero Antic, a 6-foot-10 center from Macedonia, played two NBA seasons. They coincided with Budenholzer's first two years in Atlanta, and Antic took 171 threes his first season and 173 his second.

The Hawks spread out 7-foot-2 center Roy Hibbert, who finished second in Defensive Player of the Year voting, and pushed the No. 1-seeded Indiana Pacers to seven games. Horford saw the game from a different lens, beginning the next phase of his career being influenced by Budenholzer.

"I've always wondered if there was a strange blessing, particularly in just buy-in and really kind of appreciating what we were trying to do and what we could accomplish, that there was maybe some strange blessing for him to be injured that year, and for him to watch and see it," Budenholzer now says.

"He's so good, he's such a good player. We weren't going to do anything without him, but I think it was fun for him to watch. That team was just fun from Jump Street. First year, second year, third. It was, but him watching it, I think there was some strange blessing. I think it helped his buy-in, I guess, is a long way of saying it."

Horford recognizes Sund as the first person to get through to him on shooting threes, Budenholzer as the person who instilled confidence and encouraged him to let it fly, and Kenny Atkinson as the person who kept him mentally strong in transforming his game.

Atkinson was an assistant coach all three years Budenholzer coached Horford with the Hawks. They worked closely together, and as the Hawks pushed Horford to shoot more threes, Atkinson sent Horford articles about how Chris Bosh overcame mental obstacles going from an elite mid-range shooter to a dangerous threat from downtown.

The care that Atkinson showed Horford still resonates. So does looking at Budenholzer on the sidelines after taking a three and getting the nod of approval.

"One minute to go, tight game, it wasn't probably the best shot. I was taking it," Horford says. "He really instilled that trust in me, and that's how my confidence started to grow. From that point, I was like, I can actually do this.

"A lot of guys I saw that were coming up with me, they wanted to stay with the way that they were successful at the mid-range, and then you could see that three, four years later, then they started trying to do it, and by that point it was a little late. So, for me, I was very fortunate that I was able to make that transition early."

From a player perspective, nobody was better for Horford than Kyle Korver. The two became best friends during their four years as teammates in Atlanta. Korver is a master of his craft shooting the 3-pointer, and he set the tone for the Hawks by showing up early in the morning, caring in the weight room and paying attention to detail in his shot.

Korver noticed Horford's willingness to step out and shoot threes. Horford can still recall a day Korver, in passing, told him he liked how his 3-point shot was looking, which was gospel to his ears. Budenholzer says it also clicked for Korver, who made 2,450 threes in his 17-year career at a 42.9 percent clip, that others around him shooting threes can make life easier for himself.

"There's the Steph Currys of the world, and then I think there's probably four or five of them, and Kyle's in that group," Budenholzer says. "Al, just being around Kyle and watching it, and them talking – it was fun.

"They'd come up with little ideas and little wrinkles, different angles of screens, just like Draymond and Steph do. I think that it's always great when the players start taking ownership and they start figuring things out. And I think Kyle and Al were very much participants in that."

Horford was named to his third All-Star team in Budenholzer's second season as the Hawks' head coach. The Hawks won a franchise-record 60 games and Budenholzer won Coach of the Year. That season planted the seeds of a new Horford, nearly a decade into his career.

He went 11-of-36 on threes in the 2014-15 season after a combined 10-of-29 in the first seven years of his career, which Horford says were mostly just heaves. But the Hawks were swept by LeBron James and the Cleveland Cavaliers in the conference finals. Year 9 marked Horford's real transformation. He could have never guessed it would be his last with Budenholzer as his coach.

In the Hawks' 10th game of the 2015-16 season, Horford made four of his six threes in a 26-point performance to beat the New Orleans Pelicans by eight points. That night gave him 36 3-point attempts on the season, the same amount he finished the previous year with.

The 3-point shot was no longer an afterthought for Horford. It was now a main part of his game, and has changed his play in the second decade of an illustrious career. Horford again was an All-Star, played all 82 games and took 256 threes in the 2015-16 season, which now is the sixth-most of his career.

"It was pretty remarkable making that jump," Horford says.

Bosh in the same season shot 222 threes in 53 games. It was the last season of his Hall of Fame career because of problems with blood clots.

Dirk Nowitzki was the league's MVP the season before Horford was drafted third overall, and he shot 2.2 threes a game as a 7-foot marvel in 2006-07. Less than a full decade later, Horford took 3.1 threes per game and made more than Bosh ever did. Everything stemmed from a slogan Budenholzer lives by.

"There was a mantra that we were gonna let the motherf–ker fly," Budenholzer said.

Serving as an assistant to Gregg Popovich for 17 seasons highly influenced Budenholzer as a head coach. The two-time Coach of the Year calls himself "a big believer in confidence." He tries to give his players a roadmap that starts with a lot of instruction and structure, but then gives them lots of freedom and a ton of confidence to play.

"I think a good shot in the first couple minutes is the same as a good shot in the last couple minutes," Budenholzer says. "I'm not saying that's right. I'm probably in the minority, but I've kind of lived my life that way. You're open, you shoot it.

"And I think basketball and offenses really stall out and get hard when you pass up open shots, and whether it's the beginning of the game or the end of the game, you're open, you got to shoot."

All these years later, Budenholzer understands why Horford struggled with the change at first, yet was so coachable. It speaks to who he is.

"He's so unselfish, so that was probably one of the hard things," Budenholzer says. "And as a team, we played very unselfish too."

"So there were times where he's like, 'Is that OK?' And I'm like, 'No, you're open, shoot it.' And everybody's got to have kind of a similar balance of just you shoot it, but we still play unselfish. We still move the ball. We still move without the ball. You know, the kind of fundamental stuff. But yeah, it was fun watching him look over at me to see if it was OK."

Horford's final season playing for Budenholzer was his first of attempting at least 200 threes in 10 of his next 11 seasons. The one season he missed the mark, Horford shot 152 threes as a member of the Oklahoma City Thunder in just 28 games of the 2020-21 season. Horford attempted a career-high 5.4 threes per game in that span and made 36.8 percent of them.

He even made it to 200 3-point attempts in only 45 games with the Warriors last season at 39 years old.

Horford's four consecutive threes in the fourth quarter of the Warriors' play-in tournament win over the LA Clippers were a testament to how the big man has evolved. A decade after Budenholzer became his head coach in Atlanta, Horford made 35 threes in the playoffs for the Boston Celtics in their 2024 championship run, including eight in the NBA Finals over five games.

This last January, Horford joined Nowitzki and James as the three players in history to have made 1,000 threes, blocked 1,000 shots and grabbed 9,000 rebounds for a career.

"Once I started to understand that it wasn't only shooting, what it was doing to the rest of the floor and how it was opening our offense, then it even gave me more confidence," Horford says.

But the Hawks were swept again by James' Cavs one round earlier in the conference semifinals. Not everyone saw eye-to-eye internally after that, and Horford signed a four-year contract to sign with the Celtics in the offseason for his first stint in Boston. Budenholzer only lasted two more seasons in Atlanta.

The Hawks ranked second, seventh and seventh again in 3-point attempts per game during Budenholzer's three years as Horford's head coach. They dropped to 16th the year after when the Hawks tried to replace Horford with Dwight Howard.

"Atlanta made a big mistake in letting him go," Sund says. "I can't believe we just let him go to Boston."

Now, Horford has taken on a mentorship role for big men across the NBA. Players began coming to him asking how he made the transition as basketball went through its 3-point boom. His advice has been to "read the room." Horford feels he could have shot more sometimes, but his awareness of his teammates around him gives him the ability to know when it's worth it or not.

He has seen and felt how basketball ebbs and flows. Horford played for a championship team that shot more threes than anyone, and he points out that the Thunder (10th in attempts) and New York Knicks (12th in attempts) weren't recent long-distance juggernauts. The 40-year-old isn't blind to the amount of threes teams are taking, including the Warriors, but believes balance always solves itself.

As Horford explains, "The way that we look at things is quality shots, good shots. If it's a three, if it's a good shot, take it. We don't want to just shoot a three just to shoot them." They're here to stay, and with that comes new challenges.

"The new thing, it's really going to be those long rebounds, those offensive rebounds, and that changes the complexity of the game," Horford explains. "That changes transition. There's a lot of things that kind of go hand in hand with that."

Horford played Nowitzki in his NBA debut, and Rasheed Wallace his second game, two giants among floor-spacing big men. In his fifth career game, Kevin Garnett went to work against him in the paint and from everywhere inside the 3-point line for 27 points on 12-of-16 shooting.

The most taxing part of Horford's career is now, not then, and it's because of how the game is played rather than solely his age.

Defenses could get set much easier at the start of Horford's career. Now a player starts at the 3-point line, runs in for the missed shot, doesn't get it and has to run back to try and catch a guy that has already taken off. Imagine doing so at 6-8, 260 pounds and with 20 NBA seasons of wear and tear.

"It's more demanding physically. It's a whole 'nother component," Horford says. "And that's why it's gonna be interesting to see. I think the teams that have more depth are gonna probably thrive a little more because everybody, they're sending them. These teams, that's how we're playing. We're playing fast, and it's really just playing fast, no concepts, and that's kind of how it is."

Learning defense is Horford's first non-negotiable for the younger generation. Next, without a doubt, is developing an outside jump shot.

Choosing between the two can be difficult for a five-time All-Star who has made an All-Defensive team and made over 100 3-pointers three times, in Years 16, 17 and 18 of his career.

So, is swatting a shot or swishing a three more satisfying to Horford?

"I mean, I'm a shooter now," he says. "I like shooting the three. I like shooting the three, but there is a really good feeling when you get to block a shot. I'm still a defender, I think, at the core.

"When I get to block a shot, it feels good."

Horford has enjoyed the satisfaction of blocking a shot 300 more times than he has making a three. It's obvious which of the two had a bigger part in extending his playing days to two decades, and it all started with the strange blessing of an injury that let him sit by Bud for a season.

"Yeah, I love that guy," Budenholzer says. "He and I text occasionally. I'm not the best texter. The great thing I will say, he's always been one of the best guys after a game coming down to my locker room. Two minutes, five minutes, 15 minutes – just catching up, talking.

"He's got a very, very special place in my heart, my life, and hopefully, I do the same for him. He's the best."
