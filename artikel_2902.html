California sues OpenAI over Hugging Face, other AI model loss of control

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

California Attorney General Rob Bonta on Monday sued artificial intelligence giant OpenAI for allegedly endangering the public by developing advanced AI models without adequate safeguards and monitoring.

"OpenAI disregarded public safety, knowingly tested potentially dangerous AI models without proper oversight and safety measures, and misled consumers and the public," Bonta said at a press conference.

Bonta cited the "infamous" incident in July, where two OpenAI models broke out of a testing environment, gained unauthorized access to the open internet, and breached the internal systems of open-source developer platform Hugging Face.

OpenAI said the models were trying to find information that they could use to cheat on an evaluation, and succeeded. The break-in at Hugging Face, which Nvidia acquired for roughly $13 billion in September. rattled industry researchers and prompted government officials to call for more stringent oversight of the AI industry.

On Monday, Bonta said that OpenAI later disclosed to his investigators that it was aware of 80 incidents "involving loss of control and access to third-party networks and websites."

"But the company failed to provide the detailed information we requested to evaluate these incidents; this is concerning, and it puts the public at risk," he said.

Bonta's suit, filed in state Superior Court in San Francisco, alleges OpenAI violated California's Unfair Competition Law and accuses the company of being a public nuisance as defined by state law.

The attorney general told reporters he believed in AI's technological power, and the potential of AI models to "achieve scientific breakthroughs, boost economic growth, guard against cyber attacks, and benefit all Californians."

"But AI systems must be developed and designed safely," he said. "The companies that develop these models have a moral and yes, a legal responsibility to keep public safety at the forefront."

Bonta's suit comes as Congress has been presented with, but not passed, a comprehensive AI safety bill. AI companies, including OpenAI and Anthropic, along with critics of AI, have called for such legislation on the heels of the Hugging Face break-in and other incidents that have raised concerns about the safety of AI models.

Other AI companies, including Anthropic, Meta and Google, have since disclosed incidents where their own models went rogue. But OpenAI is the first to be sued in connection with such incidents by California.

OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri, in a statement on the lawsuit, said, "Since the Hugging Face incident, we've undertaken an extensive and transparent effort to understand and address misaligned model activity."

"We've published a detailed technical report, commissioned and published a separate independent report, notified affected organizations, strengthened safeguards across our research systems, and created a framework for additional identification and disclosure of misalignment," Pusateri said.

"This lawsuit appears to single out OpenAI over a challenge that's been identified by every major AI lab. We take misalignment seriously, and we have engaged — and will continue to engage — constructively with the Attorney General's office to share information and advance strong, practical safety standards for the entire industry," Pusateri said.

Bonta's lawsuit adds to OpenAI's growing list of legal woes.

In June, Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier sued OpenAI and CEO Sam Altman, alleging that the company knowingly released an unsafe product that could harm users.

OpenAI is also facing lawsuits alleging that the company's ChatGPT platform led users to experience harmful delusions and, in some cases, to commit suicide.

California's lawsuit comes almost three months after a suit by Bonta and a coalition of other state attorneys general ended with Meta agreeing to pay $16.7 billion after being accused of misrepresenting the extent of child mental health harms caused by its apps, including Facebook and Instagram. California could receive $1.5 billion to $2.1 billion from that agreement.

OpenAI confidentially filed its prospectus with the Securities and Exchange Commission in June, setting the stage for a potentially massive initial public offering in 2027.

It is not clear how, if at all, Bonta's lawsuit will affect the IPO.

Lise Buyer, partner at IPO advisory firm Class V Group, said she had not closely followed news about the suit.

But Buyer, in an email, said, "Potential IPO buyers weigh a laundry list of risk factors, some more heavily than others."

"Investors generally do their best to assess timing and a continuum of potential outcomes," Buyer said. "Often, they will speak with uninvolved law firms in calculating the potential outcomes. Then, the risks, the timing and each investors' preferred holding period factor into the price they are willing to pay - assuming they have the option to buy or pass."

"Overall the aggregate belief about the risks — all risks — factor in to the IPO price," she said.

Earlier Monday, OpenAI announced agreements with four independent evaluators to help support its safety work.

The organizations, Apollo Research, METR, Transluce and Goodfire, will be granted "deep access" to OpenAI's internal systems and conduct distinct pilot assessments around model behavior and safeguards, OpenAI said. The company could decide to expand the agreements in the future.Altman pledged to grant independent evaluators "employee-like access" to the company in September, shortly after Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei said his company would do so as part of his call to slow down the pace of advanced model development.Independent evaluators have taken on outsized importance following high-profile safety breaches, including the Hugging Face incident, this year.

President Donald Trump encouraged AI companies to work with an "independent external auditor" as part of a "morally binding" accord he unveiled in September.
