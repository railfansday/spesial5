Now hiring: Staff to power Democrats’ probes

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Ranking member Jamie Raskin, D-Md., arrives at the Rayburn Building for a House Judiciary Committee hearing in February. He will be a key player in leading oversight efforts next year — and so will Democratic staff. (Tom Williams/CQ Roll Call)

Democrats in Congress have promised to hit the ground running with sprawling oversight investigations in the new year, making the most of their coming majority.

That means staffing up major committees. And for the dozens of attorneys and other aides preparing to take on a belligerent Trump administration that has cast Democratic oversight as potentially criminal, the job will come with extra scrutiny.

Arya Hariharan, former chief oversight counsel to Sen. Richard J. Durbin on the Senate Judiciary Committee and now a partner at Holland & Knight, said the pressure will be on from day one.

“Are you prepared to not sleep very much and know that every single thing you do is going to be scrutinized, every footnote, every comma?” Hariharan said.

Still, the way Hariharan sees it, serving on the Hill is worth it. “I hope they all fall in love with it because it’s a lovely place to work,” she says of staffers headed there for the first time.

The work done by attorneys and other staff in oversight probes can quickly become part of constitutional law. When Trump adviser Peter Navarro defied a subpoena from Democrats investigating the Jan. 6, 2021, attack on the Capitol, it touched off debates about executive privilege and contempt of Congress. Probes of Hunter Biden led to similar court fights.

Some questions have gone all the way up to the Supreme Court, like in Trump v. Mazars, when the House fought to access Trump’s business records during his first administration in furtherance of toughening up government ethics laws.

There’s every indication that pressure will continue in the new Congress. James Melendres, a partner at Snell & Wilmer, said Democrats are poised for aggressive oversight efforts, even if they know it could be tough to make headway against litigation resistant administration officials.

“Democrats are going to have less than two years to show results. I think they are going to move really fast,” Melendres said.

Democrats will likely soon start to send out preservation letters to private companies, institutions and others they intend to probe in the new year, if they haven’t already, Melendres said.

“There may be significant pushback from the administration, from agencies and individual government officials who receive requests. I think third-party, nongovernment agencies and individuals are going to be high on the list for these committees,” he said.

In addition to the hiring that happens over the next few weeks, House Democrats will have major decisions to make about chamber and committee rules for the new Congress, including who will hold subpoena authority. And off-Hill observers will be watching closely.

“For companies, for individuals and their counsel, mapping all of that is going to be a big part of the job between now and early January,” Melendres said.

Lawmakers need to nail down funding levels for congressional committees and staff — the amount of money involved can make a big difference in how big investigations get. Major committees, and major investigations, take a large amount of staff and expertise to get going.

For instance, the special House panel that investigated the Jan. 6 attack on the Capitol spent about $14 million in less than 18 months, including for dozens of staffers.

Standing committees, such as Oversight and Judiciary, take a good chunk of change, too. In 2025 the House spent $12.9 million on the House Oversight and Government Reform panel, and $13.1 million on the House Judiciary Committee.

Those expenditures mean dozens of staff working long hours to review documents, send letters, send subpoenas and conduct interviews. Judiciary ranking member Jamie Raskin, D-Md., who is expected to serve as chair next year, said he wouldn’t get into specifics yet about how many resources the new Congress may devote to oversight efforts.

“But we are talking about the most corrupt administration in the history of the United States, and this is an overwhelming and daunting task that faces us,” Raskin said.

The Trump administration itself has already signaled it intends to push back forcefully on congressional oversight efforts.

Earlier this month, President Donald Trump described Democrats’ vows to investigate his administration as “extortion at the highest level” and called for the Justice Department to “look into this criminal act, immediately!”

Days later, the White House counsel sent a letter saying that subpoena threats from House Oversight ranking member Robert Garcia, D-Calif., and others “may cross the line into blatant criminality.”

U.S. Attorney Aaron Reitz for the Southern District of Texas followed up with letters of his own to Sens. Richard Blumenthal, D-Conn.; Elizabeth Warren, D-Mass.; and Elissa Slotkin, D-Mich., urging them to preserve documents tied to their promised oversight.

“The American people are entitled to freely associate without their elected officials terrorizing them with aggressive, harassing government action,” Reitz’s letter to Warren said.

Members of the incoming majority have signaled they intend to defend themselves and their probes, and Blumenthal said he won’t be deterred by the letter.

“There’s no way that I’m going to be intimidated or bullied. It would be laughable and ludicrous, but it is a clear abuse and misuse of prosecutorial powers and weaponization by the Department of Justice,” said Blumenthal, who serves as the top Democrat on the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations.

As for Raskin, he called the White House counsel letter and other missives “absurd on its face.”

“I’m sure they would love to get into a whole series of letters and counter-letters and litigation. They’re trying to distract everybody from the fact that they just lost the worst landslide electoral repudiation in at least a half century in America, and so they’re going to pick a fight,” Raskin told reporters.

Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, D-N.Y., the likely incoming speaker, told reporters last week that he intended oversight efforts in his chamber to be led by Raskin and Garcia.

“We haven’t ruled anything in. We haven’t ruled anything out. We’re going to follow the facts. We’re going to apply the law. We’re going to be guided by the Constitution, and we’re going to let the chips fall where they may,” Jeffries said.

Democrats have already publicly listed a handful of job postings for oversight staff and counsel, ranging from the Science to Armed Services to Judiciary panels, set to start early next year.

Beyond those hires, House Democrats will have to make one major pick they haven’t yet announced — the House counsel. The position, first created in the 1970s, is hired by the majority and advocates for the chamber in court. That attorney, along with a group of bipartisan House leaders controlled by the majority, will steer the chamber’s legal strategy.

In prior Democratic Congresses, that office has been active in court; Doug Letter frequently argued on behalf of the Democratic House majority seeking to enforce subpoenas on behalf of congressional committees. The position is held by Matthew Berry in the current Republican-controlled House.

“I mean, I think a lot of people are interested in that job,” Raskin said.
