Welcome back to World Brief, where we’re looking at the return of Nvidia’s Jensen Huang, collapsed negotiations at this year’s U.N. climate summit, and Ukraine’s naval drone attack.

Nvidia, the semiconductor giant at the center of the global artificial intelligence race, announced on Monday that it had reinstated its CEO, Jensen Huang, just 11 days after he was forced to resign following multiple alleged extramarital affairs.

Monday’s announcement marks a dramatic development in a saga that has roiled markets, seen the powerful company’s value plunge, and fueled uncertainty about the future of the AI boom. Plummeting stock prices had wiped some $2.6 trillion from Nvidia’s market value, and amid mounting scrutiny, the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission reportedly launched a probe into the company’s accounting practices.

Huang’s alleged affairs were made particularly scandalous—and geopolitically noteworthy—by the fact that one of those affairs was with a woman whom U.S. intelligence agencies suspect of being a Chinese spy. That revelation raised major national security concerns for a company whose most advanced AI chips remained banned from China.

Yet all those concerns now appear to have been cast aside at the behest of Huang’s most powerful backer: U.S. President Donald Trump. Trump has never wavered from Huang’s corner, calling him a “very high-quality person” and an “amazing guy” even after the Nvidia CEO resigned. Now, it appears that the U.S. president decided to put his thumb on the scale to bring Huang back.

On Monday, Nvidia’s stock price rose by 33 percent in the wake of the company’s announcement.

The Nvidia board said it had “received input from senior levels of the United States government, and based on that input, the Board is confident that Mr. Huang’s reinstatement poses no national security risk.”

Huang said in a statement shared by the board that he was “honored” to return to Nvidia and would do so with “renewed commitment.” The board, meanwhile, said its previously announced independent investigation into Huang’s possible Chinese espionage exposure would proceed as planned and that Huang had pledged to cooperate with it.

Trump, who posted on Truth Social Sunday night that he hoped the Nvidia board would reinstate Huang, also hinted on Monday that such a decision was coming, even before it was officially confirmed.

“He’s back where he should be,” Trump said in a phone interview with PBS NewsHour minutes before the Nvidia announcement. “Well, he will be soon, hopefully,” he added. Trump also told CNBC after the announcement that he had spoken directly to Nvidia’s board and provided them with assurances on the national security threat. “I convinced them,” he said. “I made the deal.” Afterward, Trump called Huang and asked him to “work hard,” he told CNBC.

Some U.S. lawmakers from Trump’s own party appear to be less convinced. “Jensen Huang was compromised and exploited by a Chinese spy. Nvidia should have moved on without Huang and ignored the Administration’s inexplicable desire to save Huang,” Republican Rep. Don Bacon wrote in a post on X.

Texas Sen. John Cornyn was more pointed in his own X post. “No national security risk? Based on what?” Cornyn wrote. (Both Cornyn and Bacon are leaving Congress at the end of this year.)

Utah Sen. John Curtis also slammed the decision, declaring in a post on X that Huang “was right to step down when the affair came to light.” Curtis also wrote, “I have some questions. Who in our government weighed in? What did they tell the board? And on what basis did the board conclude there was no risk?”

Trump dismissed those concerns in his CNBC interview, agreeing when CNBC asked whether Republicans would “come around” on Huang’s reinstatement. As for the national security concerns, Trump dismissed new reporting that the Chinese woman whom Huang had a romantic relationship with, Alice Huang, was in contact with China’s Ministry of State Security—the country’s main intelligence agency—during their affair. “Who isn’t in contact?” Trump asked. “China speaks to everybody,” he said, adding, “They speak to me.”

Trump said he had spoken to Chinese President Xi Jinping “at length” about Jensen Huang during the two leaders’ meeting in Shenzhen, China, last week. The U.S. president also claimed that keeping Huang out of Nvidia would risk the CEO defecting to China. “I could see China coming in and saying to Jensen: ‘Hey, how would you like to come to China?’” Trump said. “He’s got a little blood in him, you know,” the U.S. president added, alluding to Huang’s Taiwanese heritage.

After the interview, Trump made his backing of Huang far more explicit with another post on Truth Social. The post featured an AI-generated recasting of a photo of Trump raising his fist after an assassination attempt in 2024; instead of Trump, the AI-generated version depicted Huang with an Nvidia flag in the background, raising his fist with a red bra in hand. “Sex sells,” Trump told CNBC.

Deep divisions. Negotiations at this year’s U.N. climate summit, also known as COP31, collapsed on Sunday as countries clashed over a proposed deal and ultimately failed to reach consensus. It marks the first time that the climate talks have failed over a final deal in about 17 years, and it comes in a year that has seen record-shattering heat and other climate extremes.

The proposed deal had omitted any mention of fossil fuels, the primary driver of climate change. That exclusion sparked an uproar and prompted Pacific island nations and the European Union, among others, to reject it.

The proposed agreement “failed to meet the moment,” said Irish Environment Minister Darragh O’Brien, who was speaking on behalf of the European Union. “We will not endorse a decision that does not respond to the science and that actually risks deepening the chasm between global commitments and the action required.”

Maritime strikes. Ukrainian forces deployed seven naval drones in a weekend attack against two Russian vessels located off the coast of Libya, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said in a post on X on Monday. The strikes sank the Russian corvette Merkury, which is one of Moscow’s newest ships, Zelensky said.

Drones also struck a Russian-owned cargo ship in the Baltic Sea on Saturday; the vessel later floated into Finnish waters. Finnish authorities believe that Ukraine perpetrated the attack, but Kyiv has not taken responsibility for it. “War is spilling over into our immediate vicinity,” Finnish Prime Minister Petteri Orpo said in a post on X, though he added that “Ukraine has the right to fight back against the aggressor.”

Under pressure. South Korea’s education minister resigned on Sunday amid controversy over newly announced anti-cheating measures. After news emerged of a cheating scandal in a separate exam, South Korean officials unveiled a raft of rigid anti-AI cheating measures just days before the country’s annual college entrance exam, known as the Suneung.

The Suneung is a famously grueling eight-hour test that can be vital in shaping students’ career outcomes. Yet the last-minute announcement of the new rules, which included blaring emergency cellphone alerts, sparked confusion among test-takers. Ultimately, some 111 people were caught cheating, according to officials.

“I believe I am not free from criticism or blame from the public for failing to prepare adequately in advance,” the education minister said in a statement reported by local media.

On a stage in Ottawa over the weekend, Trump joked around with two Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carneys—or did he? The Canadian leader actually recruited two comedians to assist him with a roast at an annual dinner with journalists, enlisting impersonators both of himself and his U.S. counterpart. “I am pressing the emergency comedy button,” Carney joked.
