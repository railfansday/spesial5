Bed bugs aren’t just a poverty problem, new study reveals

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Genetic research across three states challenges the idea that persistent bed bug populations are confined to disadvantaged neighborhoods.

There’s a stubborn myth that bed bugs are a poverty problem.

In this view, infestations are confined to neglected housing, overcrowded apartments, and neighborhoods where residents can’t afford an exterminator.

New genetic research suggests the reality is more democratic – and more unsettling.

Bed bug reservoir populations, the persistent colonies that seed new infestations across a city, turn up in neighborhoods with both high and low social vulnerability.

The study was led by Cari Lewis from the Los Alamos National Laboratory and Warren Booth from Virginia Tech.

It is the first to combine population genomics with census-derived measures of social vulnerability to map how bed bug populations are structured across American cities.

Bed bugs have been living alongside humans for a very long time. The human-adapted lineage of Cimex lectularius split from its bat-associated ancestor roughly 245,000 years ago.

Bed bugs can’t fly or jump. Human-associated populations shelter indoors and spread by hitchhiking on our belongings.

“Bed bugs are expert hitchhikers,” Booth said. “They readily move into luggage, backpacks, purses, clothing, and other personal belongings.”

Adults are about the size of an apple seed. Immature stages are much smaller.

“Their ability to hide in tiny crevices makes accidental transport remarkably common,” Booth added.

The researchers collected specimens from 195 infestations between 2018 and 2022 across three states: Oklahoma, Illinois, and Ohio.

They extracted DNA and sequenced it using a technique called ddRADseq. This method surveys thousands of single nucleotide polymorphisms scattered across the genome.

Then they used network analysis and principal components analysis to identify genetically distinct populations within each state.

The genetic signatures were striking: Oklahoma’s bed bugs fell into seven distinct clusters, Ohio into ten and Illinois five.

Within each state, genetic differentiation between clusters was significant despite some clusters spanning huge geographic distances. Oklahoma’s most distant infestations were nearly 200 miles apart.

Geographic proximity alone did not explain the genetic relationships between infestations.

“Rather than spreading simply between nearby locations, these populations are connected through networks of human movement and the transport of personal belongings, similar to the models used to examine disease transmission within populations,” Booth explained.

The researchers layered their genetic data onto the CDC’s Social Vulnerability Index.

This standardized measure scores census tracts across four themes: socioeconomic status, household characteristics, racial and ethnic minority status, and housing type and transportation.

In Oklahoma and Illinois, certain genetic clusters were associated with higher or lower social vulnerability.

But the reservoir populations – the genetically cohesive groups that persist over time and keep seeding new infestations – showed up across the entire spectrum.

They were found in rich neighborhoods and poor ones, in multi-unit housing and single-family homes.

In Ohio, no significant association between genetic clusters and any vulnerability measure was detected at all.

“A key finding of the research was the identification of reservoir populations: bed bug populations that persist over time and contribute to the spread of infestations beyond communities in which they are established,” Booth said.

“Importantly, these reservoirs of bed bugs occur across the socioeconomic spectrum, challenging the misconception that bed bugs are primarily a problem of disadvantaged communities.”

The genetic data revealed something else about bed bug biology. These populations are extremely inbred.

Average inbreeding coefficients were high across all three states – up to 0.353 in Illinois.

Estimated effective population sizes averaged just 3.5 to 5.5 across the three states. This genetic measure is not a count of the breeding bugs present.

Each infestation essentially starts from a handful of founders, reproduces in near-isolation, and builds its own genetically distinct colony.

The bugs don’t need large populations or high genetic diversity to establish and spread.

A few hitchhikers in a suitcase are plenty enough.

The insects are also increasingly resistant to the chemicals designed to kill them.

“Many bed bug populations are resistant to products that were once highly effective, especially pyrethroid-based insecticides,” Booth said.

“When infestations survive treatment, they continue to reproduce, spread, and increase the likelihood of additional introductions elsewhere.”

Detection is another problem. “Not all people who get bitten by bed bugs react to them,” Booth said.

Some people only discover they have an infestation when black spots start appearing on white bed sheets.

“That’s because they feed and then defecate and a little blood comes out,” he said. By that point, the colony may already be well established and spreading through wall voids to neighboring units.

If reservoir populations span the socioeconomic spectrum, then targeting pest management at disadvantaged neighborhoods alone won’t work.

The reservoirs that keep reintroducing bed bugs into treated areas are scattered across the entire urban landscape.

Booth advocates coordinated communitywide control, using early detection and infestation mapping to tackle bed bugs as a shared problem.

Management would focus on “simultaneous inspections and treatments of connected units and buildings, reducing re-infestation from neighboring sources.”

Regional policy differences already hint at what this might look like. Chicago has implemented landlord reporting requirements, education programs, and mandatory treatment protocols.

The study didn’t directly test whether Chicago’s regulations affect infestation patterns.

Yet, the finer-scale genetic structure detected in Illinois suggests that denser management and policy infrastructure may shape how bed bug populations organize and spread.

The research is published in the journal Parasites & Vectors.
