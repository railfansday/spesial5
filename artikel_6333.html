Finnair Celebrates Men With An All-Male Crew While Some Airlines Still Won’t Hire Male Flight Attendants

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Finnair marked International Men’s Day with an all-male crew on a flight from Helsinki to London, drawing attention to how few men work as flight attendants at the airline. Encouraging women to become pilots and encouraging men to become flight attendants strike me as entirely consistent goals.

On November 19, 2026, Finnair operated flight AY1331 from Helsinki (HEL) to London Heathrow (LHR) with an all-male crew aboard an Airbus A350-900. The crew included two pilots and eight flight attendants. The gate agents were also men.

As noted by Ben from One Mile At A Time, Finnair says only about 10% of its cabin crew are male and would like that proportion to grow.

I have flown Finnair many times and cannot recall ever having a male flight attendant. That is very different from my experience on virtually any other European carrier I regularly fly. My sample size is hardly enormous, but the statistic helps explain why this initiative caught my attention.

Ben also addresses an interesting paradox at certain airlines, singling out IndiGo.

The Indian carrier has good reason to celebrate its female pilots. In March, IndiGo announced that it had more than 1,000 women pilots, representing 17.5% of its pilot workforce. Yet its published entry-level cabin attendant eligibility criteria specify:

“Female Indian nationals, with Indian passport, between 18 to 27 years of age.”

That is an explicit restriction in the recruitment criteria, rather than simply a workforce in which women happen to predominate.

The nationality requirement is a separate question from the gender restriction. But the latter creates an odd contrast: women are encouraged to pursue a career historically associated with men in the cockpit, while men are excluded from this advertised path into the cabin.

I agree with Ben that a boy who grows up wanting to become a flight attendant should be able to pursue that ambition, just as a girl who wants to become a pilot should. No, that does not mean every airline needs a precisely equal number of male and female employees in every role. Different people will make different career choices. But there is a considerable difference between who chooses to apply and who is allowed to apply.

Flight attendants are primarily present for passenger safety, yet that does not make their hospitality role unimportant…I do not object to airlines making a polished cabin crew part of their brand.

For example, I have no inherent problem with the glamour associated with the “Singapore Girl.” An airline can take pride in elegant uniforms and gracious service while ensuring its crew are trained and respected as safety professionals.

But none of that establishes that hospitality is exclusively a female vocation. Singapore Airlines employs male cabin crew too.

I have been an usher at my church for 20 years, and I love that volunteer work. There is something deeply gratifying about welcoming people and helping them feel comfortable, including those who may be visiting for the first time.

I am not equating passing out service leaflets or collecting offerings with managing an aircraft evacuation! But there is an element of hospitality common to both roles, and I enjoy it. Men can—and should—be hospitable too. There is nothing inherently feminine about taking good care of another person.

If you have seen On the Basis of Sex, you may remember the case involving Charles Moritz, a never-married man who was denied a tax deduction for expenses incurred caring for his elderly mother. Ruth Bader Ginsburg and her husband, Martin, represented him. The law allowed the deduction for women and certain categories of men, but excluded a man in his circumstances.

In Moritz v. Commissioner, the Tenth Circuit ruled in his favor in 1972.

I cannot oversimplify by suggesting American sex-discrimination protections began with male plaintiffs. Ginsburg also helped secure the 1971 victory in Reed v. Reed, involving discrimination against a woman. But representing men disadvantaged by sex-based rules was an important part of her strategy. It helped demonstrate that assumptions about the proper roles of men and women could harm both.

Ginsburg later discussed that litigation strategy, including the challenge of persuading judges and legislators who often viewed different treatment as protective of women. I find the principle relevant here. A rule premised on women being caregivers can disadvantage a man caring for his mother. A rule treating hospitality as women’s work can exclude a man perfectly capable of providing excellent service and can reasonably run afoul of public policy.

Removing those restrictions benefits people who do not fit the stereotype, whichever sex they happen to be.

An all-male Finnair flight is a small gesture, but one with a sensible purpose when men account for only about 10% of its cabin crew.

I welcome efforts to show people that an aviation career is open to them, whether that means a woman becoming a captain or a man becoming a flight attendant. I enjoy the elegance and hospitality that airlines can bring to flying. I simply see no reason qualified men should be excluded from providing it.
