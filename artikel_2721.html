What If Wolves: Ice, Ice, Jimmy!

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

"What If" Wolves Volume 8: A winning coach? What a novel concept.

Jet Helmbrecht: Dumpster fire, car wreck, cluster f&@$, hot mess, or an unholy cacophony of chaos. These are just a few phrases that, up until recently, perfectly describe the Minnesota Timberwolves Head Coaches through the years. Let's dive into that flaming dumpster, and I'll guide you through a bit of Wolves coaching history.

Since the franchise's inception in 1989, the Minnesota Timberwolves have had 16 different head coaching tenures. This includes two separate stints by Flip Saunders and Kevin McHale (whom I still hate). Let's do some quick math. 38 seasons divided by 16 different coaching stints comes out to a new coach every 2.375 seasons. Holy crap! I knew it was bad, but now that I see the actual number. Yikes!

Looking over the past coaches, two things stood out right away. First, barely any of these guys had been a head coach before the Wolves hired them. Second, every coach has an overall losing record, apart from two: Flip Saunders and Chris Finch.

The first point can probably be traced back to ownership being cheap. Hiring inexperienced coaches is one way to save a few bucks. Out of all the Wolves coaches, only six had been a head coach before the Timberwolves. That list is Bill Musselman (Cleveland), Jimmy Rodgers (Boston Celtics), Randy Wittman (Cleveland), Rick Adelman (four different stops), Sam Mitchell (Toronto), and Tom Thibodeau (Chicago). The rest of the coaches were lifelong assistants, college coaches, "one of us" hires, nepo babies, or the dumbest coach to ever set foot on a basketball court, Kurt Rambis, who was so bad that his GM sold draft picks until he could buy him out. David Kahn rewrote the rules of basketball!!

Due to most of the coaches being inexperienced, the Wolves' poor draft record (some of which I had previously covered here, here, and here), and free agents basically ignoring the team, the losing records start to come into context. I can tell you that not all of the coaches were that bad. Some have won Coach of the Year at some point during their careers (Dwane Casey, Sam Mitchell, Thibs x2), and Adelman is in the Hall of Fame. But even these coaches had losing records with the Timberwolves.

I mentioned earlier that only Flip (427 wins – 392 losses) and Finch (269-198) have winning records with the Timberwolves. They are also the only coaches to lead the team to at least one series win in the playoffs. I loved watching Flip's teams back in the day, and now I never miss a game that Finchy is coaching. But the "What if" Wolves need to win some championships. Time to use the power of hindsight to make some adjustments to Wolves history and see if we can win a chip or two.

Remember, this is all for fun and to exercise the demons that are lurking around in my basketball-obsessed head. Also, sorry, Lynx fans. Also also, again, bless you, Chris Finch, for this moment.

In the spring of 2016, the Wolves had just wrapped up their season, missing the playoffs for the twelfth season in a row. 12! Sam Mitchell was unable to find the magic that he had in Toronto that earned him the COTY back in 2007. The roster had exciting young players in Karl-Anthony Towns, Andrew Wiggins, and Zach LaVine. Things were starting to look up, but a coaching change was desperately needed.

Timberwolves owner Glen Taylor had hired consulting firm Korn Ferry to conduct a search for a new coach and someone to run the front office. At the time, there were some big names being bandied about. Scott Brooks had found success with the Oklahoma City Thunder, winning Coach of the Year in 2010, and leading the Thunder to the 2010-11 Western Conference Finals. Mark Jackson got a nibble after turning around the Golden State Warriors (despite some pretty disgusting opinions on gay people). Jeff Van Gundy was willing to leave the TV booth and start coaching again. But ultimately, Tom Thibodeau won the job of Timberwolves Head Coach.

As a cherry on top, Thibs was also given the role of President of Basketball Operations. This dual-hatted role was all the rage at the time. Coaches wanted control, and being the POBO or GM gave them control of the roster that they all coveted.

Around the NBA, Greg Popovich was in charge in San Antonio, Doc Rivers was running the Los Angeles Clippers, and Stan Van Gundy (Jeff's brother) was leading a rebuild of the Detroit Pistons. Thibs wanted that power, and he was in a good position to make that demand after guiding the Chicago Bulls back to the playoffs. Some people can thrive in both of those roles; Thibs was not one of them.

Tom Thobodeau as a Wolves Assistant Coach in 1989. That mullet is glorious!

When Thibodeau was hired, he set out to remake the Wolves in the image of his old teams, the Boston Celtics where he was the assistant coach that reshaped their defense around Kevin Garnett's unworldly ability, and the Chicago Bulls that relied on Jimmy Butler and Joakim Noah to beat up on opposing teams. The Wolves were not structured to be a defense-first team. The best players were not labeled as defensive stalwarts by any means.

To make the Wolves a defensive juggernaut, Thibs started making moves by drafting Kris Dunn, a defensive-first guard from Providence, with his first pick as the Wolves boss. After Dunn's rookie season, he would later trade Dunn, Zach LaVine, and the number seven overall pick that would become Lauri Markkanen to the Chicago Bulls for a Jimmy Butler reunion. Coach Thibodeau would then become my mortal enemy by trading Ricky Rubio to the Utah Jazz for a late first-round pick. The roster makeover would be finalized after the Wolves signed Jeff Teague and Taj Gibson.

I was crushed that Rubio was gone, but the Wolves started winning. They even made the playoffs, breaking the 12-season streak. The winning would be short-lived, however. A late collapse led to Minnesota falling from the third seed to fighting for a playoff spot on the last night of the season. Butler would come back from an injury to try and take on the Houston Rockets, but nobody could stop James Harden, and the series was over in five quick games. Butler's true colors would eventually show, and he would demand out. I will leave it up to Jeff Teague to explain what happened.

After all that drama, Thibs was fired just over two seasons into his 5-year, $40 million deal. He came in, yelled at everyone, drafted the wrong guard (Jamal Murray was taken two picks after Dunn), traded away a promising young talent along with a high draft pick for General Soreness, transformed the Wolves into the Minnesota Bulls, and gave away Rubio. I can still hear him in my nightmares yelling at KAT and Taj to "ice" the pick-and-roll coverage. Tom Thibodeau was a good coach. He was just in over his head as POBO and was too determined to win with "his guys" from his heyday with the Bulls.

I already mentioned a bunch of high-profile names that were up for the coaching gig before Thibs was hired. But one name was left off that list. The "What If" Wolves are not interested in hiring a coach who once came close to competing for a championship. We want proven winners.

In 2016, instead of hiring Korn Ferry, Glen Taylor should have just walked down the hallway for his next head coach. So, as the "What If" Wolves Overlord, I make the short stroll and hire Cheryl Reeve of the Minnesota Lynx as the next head coach and POBO of the Minnesota Timberwolves. Luckily, her opinions on queer people are far more commendable than that loser, Mark Jackson.

Again, sorry, Lynx fans. But have no fear. I have asked Canis Hoopus' resident Lynx expert, Mitchell Hansen, to put your fears to rest and provide some insight into how Coach Reeve would lead the Timberwolves to the championship and how the Lynx would be able to keep dominating the W.

Mitchell Hansen: Reeve is one of the best coaches the WNBA — and the game of basketball as a whole — has seen. She is ingrained in and invested in the Twin Cities community. She is Minnesota basketball. She is obsessed with the game and is incredibly knowledgeable and strategic with every decision that is made, and she has a unique ability to bring the best out of her players and coaching staff. Those characteristics alone lead me to believe that she could make the transition to the NBA and succeed.

Let's go back to the time Thibs was hired and led the Timberwolves. At that time, Reeve had already established herself as one of the best coaches in basketball, having led the Lynx to three WNBA titles (which would later turn into a WNBA-record-tying fourth championship) and was in the midst of one of the greatest dynasties the league has ever seen.

Reeve is honestly similar to Thibs in multiple ways. They both have an old-school approach and have had to change their philosophy a bit to address the "new wave" of playing style and athletes. However, their core characteristics are similar. They are both defensive-minded coaches who demand accountability, challenge their players, and have high expectations for excellence within their organizations and teams. Where Reeve could have made the biggest difference for the Wolves that we've seen with the Lynx is her ability to bring players together, establish a collective identity and build a culture that has a history of resulting in translating to on-court success.

Reeve has shown throughout her coaching career an ability to identify the strengths of her players and put them in positions to succeed while also challenging them to expand their games. Her experience working with some of the best post players in women's basketball could have been particularly beneficial for Karl-Anthony Towns, Andrew Wiggins, and Zach LaVine in their early days with the Wolves. At the very least, she would put them in situations to give them chances to succeed — which, you know, is something good coaches typically do.

Perhaps the biggest ripple effect of hiring Reeve would have been the Jimmy Butler trade. Without the connection between Thibs and Butler dating back to their time together with the Chicago Bulls, it's fair to wonder whether the Timberwolves would have ever pursued that trade in the first place. That's not to say Reeve would have been opposed to acquiring an established star to complement Minnesota's young talent — she helped do so in 2015 when the Lynx traded for Hall of Fame center Sylvia Fowles — but the Timberwolves might have been more inclined to commit to their young core rather than break it up.

The hiring of Reeve wouldn't have guaranteed the Timberwolves a championship like what was basically an every-other-year occurrence with the Lynx during their dynasty, but it could have provided the organization with something it had been desperately searching for throughout much of its existence: stability.

Now, with all that said, what would have happened to Reeve's role, both with the Timberwolves and the Lynx?

Perhaps the biggest impact of her moving over to the Timberwolves side would be on the team she has led successfully since arriving in Minnesota in 2010. In 2016, at the time that the Timberwolves were looking to hire for the job, Reeve and the Lynx were in the heart of their championship window. Maya Moore, Lindsay Whalen, Seimone Augustus, Rebbekah Brunson, and the aforementioned Fowles were dominating the WNBA and were looking for their fourth title to add to an impressive dynasty run.

Would Reeve have been willing to leave that? Or would she have attempted to coach both franchises at the same time? If you know Reeve, you know that the second option would have likely been her decision — or at least she would have tried her damnedest to make it work.

From a scheduling standpoint, at the time, with the WNBA schedule at just 34 games, it would have been doable. She would have had to rely on her assistant coaches at various points, notably during training camps and preseason action, but it could have been done. But from a realistic standpoint, that's not sustainable. If Reeve did that, it would have likely run up until the conclusion of the dynasty era, and she would have made the choice to go to one squad or the other and focus on just one.

But there's another avenue she could have gone — what if she decided to leave the Lynx in that championship window and officially moved over to the NBA to lead the Timberwolves? Who would have led the Lynx, and what would the Lynx have looked like upon her exit?

The first, most likely option would have been that Glen Taylor and the organization would have tried to get Jim Petersen out of the Timberwolves broadcast booth to take over as head coach. There is obvious familiarity there, with Petersen serving as an assistant under Reeve during numerous title-contending seasons. The other candidate could have been long-time Lynx assistant coach Shelley Patterson, potentially offering a more long-term solution than Petersen would have provided.

Regardless of who would have taken over, the leadership and chemistry already built inside that Lynx locker room likely would have kept the championship hopes alive for at least the next few seasons — I mean, the team was filled with five Hall of Fame players in the starting lineup. Not to diminish what Reeve built in any way or what the team did to work as a collective unit so flawlessly, but the talent on those teams was unreal.

The biggest impact of Reeve's departure to the Timberwolves would likely have come years later, when the Lynx were going through their post-dynasty transition period. Reeve did a miraculous job maneuvering that era while keeping the winning culture and chemistry intact, something that isn't easy to do when you're on the downswing from numerous title runs.

If Reeve moved over to the NBA, it's likely that we wouldn't have seen much of an impact on the Lynx dynasty run that essentially concluded in 2018 with four titles. But it could have drastically changed the way the Lynx look today, because Reeve has done an incredible job continuing to build and readjust the roster to be right there in the conversation seemingly every season.

Overall, that's what makes this hypothetical scenario so fascinating from the Timberwolves and Lynx organizations' perspective. By having Reeve take over the Timberwolves, the team would have acquired a capable coach to transform their organization. But simultaneously, it would be taking away the person responsible for transforming the Lynx on the other side of the organization.

The Timberwolves' gain might have been the Lynx's greatest loss.

Either way, I would no longer be traumatized by Thibs screaming "ice" for 48 minutes per game. Thanks, Mitchell!
