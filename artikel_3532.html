Beto O’Rourke Says He’d “Do Anything” for James Talarico and Gina Hinojosa—Including Work for Them

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

After the shocking Democratic victories in Texas last week, it was hard not to look over the shoulders of James Talarico and Gina Hinojosa and see the shadow of Beto O’Rourke, whose losses for Senate in 2018 and governor in 2022 had become cautionary tales of Democratic hopelessness in Texas. And perhaps proof that nice guys couldn’t win. But in the years after those failed runs, O’Rourke never really went away. He launched a voter-registration group called Powered by People, punched out missives on Substack, livestreamed his thoughts, and quietly advised his party from the background.

“Should I have talked to Joe Hagan when he was on my front porch in 2019?” O’Rourke says now, looking back at the cover story I wrote, in which he proclaimed he was “just born to be in it,” a premature declaration that almost immediately sank his prospects. “I’m glad I did. I’m grateful for everything that’s happened, because look where we are. It’s a beautiful new day in America.”

O'Rourke taks the stage during the Fight the Trump Takeover rally at the Texas Capitol in August 2025.

Like Stacey Abrams in Georgia, who lost her own race for governor but used her visibility to build a stronger coalition, O’Rourke can claim some credit for setting the table for Democratic success in Texas. Talarico won his first race, for a Republican-held state House seat, on O’Rourke’s 2018 wave. And as it turns out, O’Rourke himself weighed another Senate run this year. When Talarico asked whether he’d run, O’Rourke said only if Talarico ran for governor instead, eventually stepping aside after concluding that the moment called for “something and someone new.” When he launched his Senate campaign, Talarico said he wanted to replicate O’Rourke’s “energy.”

O’Rourke, meanwhile, became a behind-the-scenes player: Powered by People was one of four partners in the Texas Democrats’ $30 million coordinated campaign, and it says it reached more than 110,000 new and young voters in Texas this cycle. Ken Paxton noticed and tried to put O’Rourke “behind bars” for funding the Democrats who fled the state to block its Donald Trump–backed redistricting.

O’Rourke says the wave cascaded so far down the ballot in Texas that a candidate who tried backing out of her campaign before Election Day ended up winning. “At that point it was too late to take her name off the ballot,” he says. “She didn’t campaign, just won her election. That speaks to the magnitude of the wave, but also to the value of having a fighter on every front. Somebody said to me, ‘A wave’s great, even better if you have a surfboard.’”

Over an hour-long conversation, O’Rourke parses the political revolution in his home state, suggests how Democrats can hold on to their land grab, and, perhaps unsurprisingly, looks ahead to another run for office.

This interview has been edited for length and clarity.

Vanity Fair: The conventional wisdom is that this was a revolt against Trump, and that the candidates and the Democratic strategy were secondary. What’s your read?

Beto O’Rourke: So much of this election was a rejection of Trump and Trumpism: the corruption, the wars, the cruelty. But after 2024, people were anxious that we didn’t have a unifying national leader. [Joe] Biden was not going to be it. Kamala Harris turned out not to be it. President Obama, I think wisely, held back…. Chuck Schumer was persona non grata in any gathering of Democrats. The DNC was in the red.

What it did was allow for candidates in different parts of the country to really establish themselves in their own right and by their own merits, never having to fear being under the shadow of a larger figure. It’s really a blessing. That’s how you get Abdul El-Sayed, James Talarico, Mary Peltola, all these extraordinary candidates who are extraordinarily different from each other. In Texas, for the first time in half a century, Democrats fielded a fighter on every front: 150 state House candidates, 38 congressional candidates, 16 state Senate candidates. You had to have a historically terrible, corrupt president. But you also had to have this historically beaten-down Democratic Party; that the Democrats don’t have a message, they don’t have a leader. That all turned out to be a great thing.

A year ago you convened Talarico, Colin Allred, and Joaquin Castro to think about a statewide slate. Talarico went his own way. You warned him he’d “want friends on that ticket.” What did you mean?

I just wanted to win, and I put that before anything else. James and I had many conversations before he officially announced. He asked me, “Are you interested? Would you run?” And I told him, “The only condition on which I would run is if you were to run for governor, or somebody else who could draw that kind of attention and resources, and if we had the ticket rounded out by strong contenders for every position on the ballot.”

My advice to him was: “Look, I’ve done this before. No matter how talented you are, no matter how much you can raise, this state is so big, the Republicans are so entrenched, you need a fighter on every front.” And ultimately, that’s what happened. Gina really hit her stride at the right time. Her strength as a candidate a year ago compared to how she finished is worlds apart, and that is a testament to what an exceptional leader she is.

Clearly, it was the right call for James to be there. He is just an extraordinarily talented communicator. He was a phenomenally focused candidate. He was absolutely the right guy for the moment. I also think it was the right call for Jasmine Crockett to run too. I know from being on college campuses how electrifying she is, how many young people she brought into the electorate this time. They stayed in even after Jasmine was defeated in the primary.

The Atlantic reported that you told Hinojosa, “Not one person can win Texas. It has to be two.” Is that what you said?

I definitely spoke to Gina before she made the decision to run, and then after. To be honest with you, I can’t remember if I said “two.” What I said consistently to everybody who was thinking about it—Colin, Joaquin, Gina, Jasmine [Crockett], James—was that you need a fighter on every front and the strongest possible ticket. That was based on the last run I had, in 2022, and even the ’18 run. If Joaquin or Julián Castro had run for governor when I was running for Senate, who knows? Of course, at that time, we didn’t know the size of the wave that was about to crash.

I definitely did. I could have run…. You’d have to ask James, but I think he probably would have supported that if I had. We have an amazing organization in waiting, volunteers who’ve been with me, the ability to raise money. But was that the right thing to do? No. I had been before Texas twice before, and the moment demanded something different and, I would argue, something better.

You can’t discount a politician’s ambition and ego. I had to find a way to get past that. It helped having conversations with my wife. It helped having conversations with our kids. It helped having conversations with people who’d been on these runs with me before. Also, I spent a lot of time traveling Texas. My love language is a town hall meeting, where it’s just a free-for-all and anybody can step to the mic and ask any questions. At the end of that, I really felt this moment called for something and someone new. I don’t know that Texas could have produced a stronger—I was going to say candidate, but really a one-two punch. It was James and Gina. They’re both exceptional individually. Together, they transcended what had happened in politics for as long as I’ve been alive.

Honestly, my own doubt about whether Texas could be won was based on you. We’d seen this story before.

I wouldn’t have done this work as a volunteer for the last four years if I didn’t believe in Texas. I know in my heart and in my bones that we are so much better than the people in power. It’s the obscene abortion ban that has cost the lives of girls and women, the $7.25-an-hour minimum wage, the fact that we’re the least insured state in the country. Four years after Uvalde, and no one in power has done a single thing. It’s insane having Texas Republicans say, if you’re Muslim, regardless of whether you’re an immigrant, you should be deported from this country. That’s just not who we are.

O'Rourke speaks during the Fight the Trump Takeover rally, where 5,000 people attended. Attendees protested proposed redistricting, immigration enforcement, and other Trump-backed legislation and proposed changes.

When I ran for governor, 9 million registered voters didn’t cast a ballot. It’s not because they don’t care; it’s not that they’re lazy. This state makes it harder than any other to participate, so we’ve got to try that much harder. I knew it was a matter of time and effort. The greater the effort, the shorter the time. But did I know it would be 2026? Did I know it would be every statewide office and the state House? Fuck no, man. Nobody knew that.

Ken Paxton called you a “repeat loser” and tried to prosecute your PAC. He lost by 12 points.

We’ve spent more than $600,000; we have Powered by People defending our organization, defending me. He accused me of bribery. He tried to remove the license for Powered by People to do voter-registration work in the state of Texas. He promised to lock me up, to put me behind bars. The 15th Court of Appeals [judges], all of whom were appointed by Greg Abbott, so none of them friends of mine, have ruled against Paxton for attacking somebody for exercising their First Amendment rights. Do I think that I’m going to get that money back? I don’t know.

Couldn’t have happened to a better person.

Did Talarico and Hinojosa acknowledge your role, or did they keep their distance?

You’d have to ask them. I got a really nice message from James on election night, and I had a chance to campaign with Gina in the closing days of the election. That’s not an easy choice for Gina, right? Both of them did an exceptional job of being themselves and not seeming to be beholden to anybody. They invited nobody in from outside of Texas, and we all understood: This is their show. So getting to campaign with Gina here at the University of Texas at El Paso, at the University of Houston, it was really nice to hear her acknowledge the work that so many of us have been doing, especially all these volunteers who’ve been out there, whose names most people will never know.

I’ve known them both for a decade. James and I were on the campaign trail together in 2017 and 2018, when he was running his first race for state House in Williamson County. Somebody just sent me a picture of the two of us at an event in Williamson in 2017. These are really fucking good human beings, and they’re also really good at understanding power and how to win it. It is so rare that those two things come together.

O'Rourke speaks during a "Fighting Oligarchy" event in Houston, Texas on Oct. 20, 2026.

Are Chuck Schumer and Hakeem Jeffries the right leaders for this moment?

Chuck Schumer absolutely is not. Early 2025, [Elon] Musk is running wild with DOGE, Trump is steamrolling every center of power. They’re all bending the knee—the Ivy Leagues, the big law firms, the media companies. Hakeem marshaled every Democratic vote in the House but one to stop Trump, DOGE, and Musk, and the deal with Schumer was going to do the same. Then, inexplicably, Schumer bent the knee as well. In that moment, it was absolutely over for me.

Schumer deserves so much credit and praise for his work over the decades. But he doesn’t deserve to be majority leader at this major turning point for the country, not when you have James Talarico, Abdul El-Sayed, or Jon Ossoff. We have an embarrassment of talent in the Senate right now, and we should use it. The age when any American cares what the majority leader reads on the Senate floor is over. I don’t even know what his message is, and that says something.

Hakeem Jeffries hasn’t had the chance to lead as Speaker. We were elected in the same class in 2012, and I’m grateful that he stood strong early in the Trump administration. I wish he could communicate more effectively than he has so far. Maybe there’s a scenario where he’s Speaker, but he taps the far better communicators in his caucus, like Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, to do more of that work, and he’s focused on getting the legislative results.

Eleven House Democrats missed the vote to impeach [Pete] Hegseth, enough to have changed the outcome. What happened there?

Eleven is a big number. That certainly invites some conspiracy thinking, or, maybe just as bad, the idea that these folks aren’t up to the job. It’s really important that the Democratic leadership in the House address this directly, explain what happened, and take ownership and responsibility for it.

You’ve said Democrats wasted their power in 2009 and 2021, and that next time they should “drive that car like we stole it.” What happens in the first 100 days?

Right off the bat, you have to make our lives better in a way that we can feel. In Texas, that’s not hard, because we are in such a fucking hole. Raise the minimum wage from $7.25. Expand Medicaid when the federal government’s paying 90 cents on the dollar. Stop this voucher thing that is draining tax dollars from local schools, and pay teachers so they don’t have to work a second or third job.

Nationally, you have to do the same. The response to this from many is that Trump has the power of the pen and will veto that. Make him veto that. I don’t like anything about him, but I don’t know that there’s a more successful political operator than Trump. I don’t think there’s someone who reads power better than he does. Maybe there are deals to be had. Maybe you force the veto, and you set up a clear contrast going into 2028. At a minimum, you have to get caught trying.

The huge issues that Democrats have absolutely failed to lead on, we have to lead on now, so that would include immigration and that would include democracy. Immigration was arguably the issue that voters made their decisions on in 2016 and 2024, and if we continue to hope that it’s just going to solve itself, instead of making tough choices and using political capital on this, then it’s going to be our demise in future elections as well. [On democracy], there’s no democracy to restore or save. There is a democracy you can build anew. Getting corporations out of politics; what just passed in Montana is potentially a wonderful precedent from which we could build. Getting a voting rights act passed again. [Pushing] the popular-vote compact; I think we have enough trifectas now. I’m probably in the minority in the Democratic Party, but I’m a big believer in term limits. People give a speech and are like, “We’ve got to overturn Citizens United,” or, “We’ve got to do away with the Electoral College.” That’s a great applause line, but when is that ever going to happen?

O'Rourke spoke in October 2026 as the fight for the Senate came down to the wire.

The big dig on Democrats is: You talked a great game on immigration, then you won the White House; you had the House and the Senate. You didn’t do anything. You talked a great game about voting rights. You had the trifecta, and you didn’t do anything. Now we have the power. We’d better deliver, or watch out.

I’m surprised I’m not hearing more from you about prosecutions and subpoenas. You spent the last two years warning about fascism and calling for accountability.

That’s got to be part of it too. The law is king in this country; no one’s above it, no one’s below it. We’ve allowed the precedent, through the Epstein crimes, that you can be rich enough and well-connected enough to rape and traffic girls and women with complete impunity. I spent a lot of time with young people doing this voter-registration work, and that is probably the number one thing I hear from them for why they don’t vote: This shit doesn’t work. Ghislaine Maxwell [went to jail], but no man who actually did this has gone to prison or even faced trial.

Then there’s the corruption that flows from the president through his family, through his Cabinet officials, through the oligarchs [with whom] he surrounds himself. If we let this stand, much the way that we let stand his crimes against this country on January 6, 2021, that sets the most dangerous precedent. Look at the Roman Republic. It wasn’t Caesar crossing the Rubicon. It was Sulla turning his armies against Rome, and there was never a consequence or accountability for that, so the precedent was set and Caesar picked that up. [Editor’s note: O’Rourke is an avid fan of the podcast The Rest Is History.] There will be a more capable, powerful demagogue than Trump—he or she may be a Democrat; he or she may be a Republican—who will use that precedent unless that door is closed. I know this is an unpopular thing to talk about within our party, because folks want to turn the page. But that keeps biting us in the ass. Everyone who went to prison for [January 6] was pardoned by this president.

But if you spend the next two years investigating him and prices stay high, Democrats are cooked.

That’s exactly right. That’s why I said you’ve got to deliver for people, and they have to feel it immediately. Democrats are responsible for so many good things that are both slow-working and expressed through the tax code, like the child tax credit, and for which they don’t claim credit. Those things have to change.

I was in the Rio Grande Valley after 2020, doing voter registration, knocking on doors, and talking to this guy who voted for Trump. I said, “You had a front-row seat to kids in cages, to family separation, to this cruelty here. Tell me what’s going on.” And he said, “I hate all that stuff, but voting on those issues when I can’t feed my family and I may lose my home is a luxury. I don’t have that luxury.” We have to learn that lesson. You’ve got to make people’s lives better, and if you do that, you build trust and legitimacy: “Okay, these guys are really helping me. They really reflect my values and my interests. And now that they are pursuing these investigations and this accountability, I trust them, and I know that, regardless of the outcome of these investigations, my life is better because they are in power.”

If Trump felt that he or his family were under real legal threat, is there any world in which investigations become a chip to trade, say, for immigration reform? I realize this is very Trumpy thinking, but you are dealing with Trump.

No. Totally understandable, and it’s a recognition of how politics and power typically work. But part of how we got Trump in the first place was that there weren’t consequences for massive failures or crimes committed: these banks that were bailed out after they had driven so many Americans into poverty and homelessness in 2008 and 2009, these wars the Bush administration launched on false pretenses. They lied to us, they lied to the world, and it took the lives of so many Americans, Iraqis, and Afghans. Never any consequence or accountability.

This corruption has been so flagrant, it’s as though Trump and those closest to him were daring America to hold them accountable. Then there’s the open defiance of the law and the Constitution: killing people on fishing boats in the Caribbean, kidnapping a head of state, launching a war and continuing it in violation of the War Powers Act. When you allow these things to stand, they become the precedent. But if there’s accountability, then that becomes the precedent upon which future leaders will be able to act. Trump may feel he’s fighting for his life, that he’s backed into a corner. He may make it harder for Democrats to do the things that they need to do. But I don’t think we can operate in fear of him.

Some Democrats, like Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, argue that the corruption and the high prices are the same story. Do you buy that?

Talarico is masterful at saying that: Their corruption is costing you and your family. So many examples in Texas. Billionaires bought off Governor Greg Abbott and persuaded him to pursue a voucher scheme that took public dollars out of local schools and sent them to private schools, decreased the quality of your school, and raised your local property taxes to make up the difference. Abbott, beholden to corporate interests, opened the door [to data centers], and our electricity prices went up and the quality of our water and air was compromised.

O'Rourke says he doesn't know if there’s an office he can imagine running for in 2028 or 2030.

The biggest one was in 2021, when we had the winter storm. In the energy capital of the world, they couldn’t keep the lights on or the power running, and pipeline operators [and others in the energy industry], among the biggest donors to Greg Abbott, began to price-gouge, bankrupting utilities and causing ratepayers to pay sky-high prices. One of the pipeline operator CEOs wrote Abbott a $1 million check at the end of that winter storm, almost as if to say thank you for not prosecuting them. It’s not corruption in an abstract way; it’s corruption that’s costing you. And yes, that corruption should be prosecuted. It’s not enough to just win the election and throw the bums out. If the bums broke the law, then they have to face the consequences.

If Democrats hold this ground, it would seem to open the door for you again. Is there an office you can imagine running for in 2028 or 2030?

I don’t know, and people would have to answer that question for me. That’s what I’ve been trying to do these last four years, just listen to people and figure out where I can be most useful. I’ve tried to remain agnostic.

I had a conversation earlier in the year with somebody who just won in Texas, and they had been one of the people to encourage me to run for governor in 2022—what the fuck was I thinking, running in Joe Biden’s midterm?—and I asked them, “Did you think I had any chance to win?” And they said, “Oh God, no.” But they knew that we needed somebody to galvanize the state, protect our gains in Harris County, and shore up the wins that we’d [earned] in 2018. Somebody had to carry us through that next stage of the relay.

Just so I’m clear, I would not have run if I didn’t think there was a chance we could win in 2022. It was an incredibly tough environment that got tougher and tougher as the race proceeded. But it did a lot of good, and in some small way it was probably helpful toward what happened in 2026.

When [my wife] Amy and I made the decision in 2016 that we had to do something, I went to everyone: Joaquin and Julián Castro, Clay Jenkins, Michael Sorrell, Wendy Davis. I asked each of them, “Will you run? If you do, I will get behind you.” Ultimately, I was like, “Okay, if you guys aren’t going to do it, I am going to do it. I know this is crazy, and I know all of you are better positioned, I’m going to do it.” So if I feel the way that I felt that time, that I’m the right person for this, then, whatever the odds, I’m going to get out there. But if running could hurt us, if there’s somebody better—and that was a conclusion I drew coming into this cycle—then please step up, and I’ll get out of the way and support you as a volunteer.

The next statewide race in Texas is 2030. One thing I’m thinking about right now is 2028, and the fact that the 40 Electoral College votes in Texas are ours to lose. I want to be part of the team getting Texas ready for that nominee to win our state. I’m happy to follow James and Gina’s lead. They’re our leaders now.

Can you imagine working in a Hinojosa administration?

I will do anything for her, and the same for James. It’s just crazy, hearing that.

What about taking Powered by People to Florida? They could use some help, evidently.

These states of the former Confederacy have worked so hard to make it hard for people, especially young people, to vote. We don’t have Election Day data yet, but through early voting, 60% of our voters, overwhelmingly 18 to 29 years old, voted through early voting. That’s about 33 points ahead of the average for that demographic in Texas. I sure wish our party had put resources behind [Angie] Nixon and [David] Jolly in Florida. We could have won those races. What was [the margin] in the governor’s race? 1.3%. Heartbreaking. If I can be useful there, I’m happy to go.

About a year after my 2019 story came out, I texted you that I hoped there were no hard feelings, and you took responsibility for your own errors. It seems like you’ve been through a process since then.

That’s just life, right? I truly feel so incredibly lucky: getting to serve in Congress, getting to run for the US Senate, being considered for the nomination in 2020, being back out on the trail for governor, and then doing this work with Powered by People. When I get depressed is when I don’t have purpose. Then I’m prone to feel sorry for myself or to wonder what if. I’m a big believer that action is the antidote to despair. So I try not to dwell too much on decisions that I’ve made. Should I have talked to Joe Hagan when he was on my front porch in 2019? I’m glad I did. I’m grateful for everything that’s happened, because look at where we are. It’s a beautiful new day in America.

Joe Rogan suggested on his podcast that Talarico should run for president. Any advice for him?

I am the last guy to give Talarico advice, because he’s just been so freaking smart and amazing. Whether he runs for president, whether he serves out his full term in the Senate, he is one of very few leaders in this country who have met the moment. I trust him to make the best decisions for, first and foremost, the country, for Texas, and then for himself.

Who excites you in the 2028 field? You’ve got Gavin Newsom on the center-left, and AOC and Ro Khanna presenting themselves as heirs to Bernie Sanders.

We could have added another 15 people to that list. There was a lot of gnashing of teeth two years ago: We don’t have a national leader; what is Democrats’ message? That was liberating for all of these candidates, and you’re going to have so many diverse perspectives in 2028. What you did these last 10 years is going to be an important consideration. Gavin Newsom was one of the first national Democrats to recognize what Trump was trying to do by redrawing these congressional maps, starting in Texas, and I was really impressed with how quickly he moved and how strong he was on offense. Pete Buttigieg has been nonstop traveling the country supporting candidates. In the wake of 2024, with this sense of total defeat, a few people just got out there, and AOC was one of them. I like the folks who never stop fighting, who are true to themselves and not susceptible to the prevailing winds. Bernie Sanders is probably the best example of that.

Ro Khanna and I, when we were in Congress together, authored the No PAC Act. We asked our legislative staffs, “Can you find out who last authored a bill like this so we can amend it and improve it and make it our own?” There was no bill. Nobody had ever done that. He’s been so consistently focused on democracy. Greg Casar, he’s from Texas. He’s the chair of the [Congressional] Progressive Caucus and also has been crisscrossing the country. There are a lot of good people out there.

O'Rourke speaks at a campaign rally at the University of Houston on October 20, 2026.

After 2016 and 2024, a lot of women I know concluded that this country won’t elect a woman president. Can a woman be elected?

Absolutely. Gina Hinojosa—here in Texas, the conventional wisdom was that if a Democrat ever wins, it’s got to be a straight white man who fits the following characteristics. And here you have the first Latina from the border. It’s fucking awesome. It’s going to sound corny, but I really believe in this country, and that faith was vindicated last Tuesday. It wasn’t the country being like, “Go Democrats!” It was the country saying, “We can do better, and we’re going to take a chance on folks who may not fit the profile.” I think that holds true for the country in 2028.

You just turned 54. If you never ran again, would you be okay with that?

I think about it sometimes. Is the prize the office—junior senator from the state of Texas, president of the United States of America? Or is the prize a much better world for my kids and this country? I always want that to be the answer, and it is. I just fucking won the lottery in life. Our kids are absolutely thriving; my oldest is about to be 20, middle kid is 18, my youngest just turned 16. I’ve been lucky with Amy and living in El Paso, getting to do this fulfilling work.

But just to be clear: Would I love to serve in office again at some point? Absolutely. The El Paso City Council may have been my most fulfilling time in politics. Loved being in Congress—what a high honor. But if it doesn’t happen, I’m more than okay. I’m the luckiest guy out there. I’m good, no matter what happens.
