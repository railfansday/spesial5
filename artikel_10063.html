The will vs trust decision is primarily a financial calculation: does the upfront cost of a trust save more than it costs by avoiding probate?

For most FI-focused households who are building wealth toward financial independence, the trust pays for itself many times over. If your net worth exceeds $100,000-$200,000 and you own real estate, a trust is almost certainly the better financial decision.

Even with a trust, you still need a pour-over will — a short document that catches any assets not transferred into the trust during your lifetime and directs them into the trust at death. You also still need a healthcare directive and durable power of attorney. Most estate plan packages include all four documents together.

The FI-optimized approach to estate planning minimizes cost without sacrificing protection:

Start with an online will now. A $159 Trust & Will plan gives you a will, healthcare directive, and power of attorney today. Do not let perfect be the enemy of done — having a basic will is infinitely better than having no will while you save up for an attorney.

Upgrade to a trust when your net worth exceeds $200,000. At this point, the probate avoidance savings justify the $1,500-$3,500 cost of adding a trust.

Shop attorney fees aggressively. Estate planning is a competitive market. Get quotes from at least three attorneys. Many offer flat-fee packages, and prices can vary by 50-100% for the same work in the same city. Ask specifically whether the quote includes trust funding assistance and deed transfers.

Use online services for updates. Rather than paying an attorney $200-$500 for a codicil, many online platforms let you update your will for free or for a small annual subscription ($19-$39/year). Trust & Will, for example, includes one year of free updates.

Bundle documents. An estate plan package (will + trust + POA + healthcare directive) typically costs 20-40% less than purchasing each document separately. If you are going to see an attorney, get everything done in one engagement.

Here is the counterintuitive truth: the most expensive estate plan is the one you never create. Dying without a will (intestate) triggers state default rules that may not match your wishes, longer and more expensive probate proceedings, and potential family conflict. For parents of minor children, it means a judge — not you — decides who raises your kids. The $0-$259 cost of an online will is trivially small compared to these consequences.

Major online will makers like Trust & Will, Nolo WillMaker, and LegalZoom produce documents that are legally valid in all 50 states and cover the same core elements an attorney would include. The difference is that an attorney can provide customized advice, handle complex situations, and anticipate issues specific to your state law. An attorney charges $100-$500 for a codicil (formal amendment) or $300-$1,000 to draft a new will. For minor changes, a codicil is sufficient. For major changes (new spouse, additional children, significant asset changes), creating an entirely new will is cleaner and reduces the risk of conflicting instructions.

The most expensive part of estate planning is NOT the documents — it is probate. Probate costs 3-7% of your estate value ($15,000-$70,000 on a $500,000-$1,000,000 estate) and takes 6-18 months. By comparison, even the most expensive estate plan package ($5,000-$10,000) is a fraction of what your family would spend navigating probate without a trust. The second most expensive mistake is outdated beneficiary designations on retirement accounts, which can send six or seven figures to the wrong person with no legal recourse.

The cost of a will ranges from $0 to $10,000+ depending on your method and complexity, but the most expensive option is always having no will at all. For most FI-focused households, start with an online will for $0-$259 to get basic protection in place immediately. As your net worth grows beyond $200,000, invest $1,500-$5,000 in a comprehensive estate plan with a revocable living trust — the probate savings alone deliver a 300-1,000% return. Update your beneficiary designations today (free), review your plan every 3-5 years, and remember: the goal of estate planning is not to create perfect documents. It is to protect the people you love from financial chaos during the worst moment of their lives.
