Drinking Tea Too Hot Could Be Bad For Your Health—Here's What To Know

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Your morning cup might benefit from a little patience.

There’s more than one reason to brew your tea at the right temperature—and one of those reasons could affect your health. A recently published study followed nearly half a million adults in China for almost a decade, examining how tea-drinking habits related to their health. Researchers found that regular tea drinkers had a lower risk of vascular disease than non-tea drinkers. But one major caveat emerged: Those who reported drinking their tea “burning hot” had a higher risk of vascular disease.

According to the study, drinking extremely hot tea was associated with a 16 percent higher relative risk of vascular disease. But don't give up your daily tea habit quite yet. “This study is interesting, but we don't have a proven biological explanation yet,” Dr. Niraj Patel, medical director at Clara, says.

Dr. Chelsea Azarcon is similarly cautious about drawing conclusions from the findings.

“It doesn’t look like this study has any proposed mechanism for why burning hot tea would increase the risk of cardiovascular disease,” she explains. “However, we do know that drinks that are too hot can increase the risk of esophageal cancer by creating local injury and damage.”

That concern is supported by the World Health Organization's cancer research agency, which has classified drinking beverages hotter than 149°F as probably carcinogenic to humans. The classification specifically concerns esophageal cancer, though, so it doesn't explain the connection to vascular disease identified in the new study.

The study didn’t identify a specific temperature at which drinking tea becomes detrimental to vascular health. And although researchers found an association between drinking extremely hot tea and vascular disease, that doesn't necessarily mean one causes the other.

ScienceDaily also notes that drinking tea at extremely high temperatures may diminish some of the health benefits associated with regular tea consumption.

Azarcon believes further research into tea's chemical makeup and how it may change at different temperatures could help answer some of the remaining questions.

"I'd say enjoy your tea, but don't feel like you need to drink it while it's scalding hot,” Patel suggests. “There's a difference between brewing tea with hot water and actually drinking it at that temperature. Letting it cool for a few minutes is an easy precaution and doesn't require giving up something you enjoy."

So while I don’t plan on giving up my tea habit any time soon, I am definitely making extra sure that I let my cup sit out a little longer before taking that first sip.
