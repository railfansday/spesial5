Despite ubiquitous satellite imagery, incredible smartphone cameras, and a video doorbell on almost every house, there’s still incredible power in witnessing something with your own eyes. Unfortunately, despite eyesight being our dominant sense, our optics have some real limitations. Binoculars are considered relatively old technology, but they’re still recognized as the best tool to reach for when identifying a far-off animal or examining details on a backyard bird.

Hunters use binoculars to spot and pick out details on their quarry thousands of yards away, birders confirm species they’re identifying, and stargazers zoom in on constellations. They’re also just handy to keep in your house or even your vehicle for those moments when you just want to “see for yourself.”

You can extend your vision with telescopes, spotting scopes, and even zoom camera lenses, but the comfort and ease of use are why binoculars have such enduring appeal. Monocular devices—spotting scopes, for instance—can put strain on your eyes after only a few minutes, while binoculars offer a much more natural extension of normal vision, making them more comfortable for long sessions and easier for beginners to use. Binoculars are also lighter than those similar devices, which makes them packable and portable for trips into nature where they offer a more intimate look at shy creatures.

The challenge in shopping for a pair of binoculars is the sheer number and diversity of options from big, high-powered zooms that require a tripod to pocket-sized budget versions and everything in between. To help you narrow your search, we dive into how to select a pair that fits within your price range and how you intend to use them. We also share our favorites from years of testing in the field.

I’m a lifelong hunter and wildlife enthusiast who uses binoculars year-round for hunting as well as volunteer wildlife surveys for bighorn sheep and goats. Long hours behind glass, as well as more than a decade of testing dozens of pairs of binoculars in the field for editorial reviews, have informed my picks, along with conversations with brand representatives and optics dealers. For this update of my long-running guide, I tested six new pairs of binoculars that are new releases from established optics brands. Not all of them made the cut here, but several did, including the Nikon Trailblazer II 10x25, the first time I enjoyed a microcompact binocular enough to include it.

The new Leupold BX-3 Alpine HD binoculars deliver premium-enough outdoor optics while maintaining a price point almost everyone can stomach. The BX-2 Alpine HD, which occupied that exact niche for years, did it well enough that it became my favorite binocular in the midrange category and my everyday hunting optic.

This is a compact, lightweight binocular with good clarity and low-light performance, which makes it a solid choice for basically everyone from birders to stargazers, even though Leupold tends to market mostly to hunters and target shooters. While not the cheapest bino on the market, it’s worth the upgrade over the lowest-tier optics and, like any Leupold optic, comes with a lifetime warranty, meaning it could conceivably be the last pair you’ll ever buy.

The BX-3 doesn’t reinvent anything, but Leupold subtly refined the binos, updating coatings, tweaking ergonomics, and adding a few quality-of-life improvements while keeping the same general form factor, magnification options, and modest price point that made the BX-2 so successful in the first place.

The price jumped up slightly with the release of the BX-3, so while I’d buy the BX-3 for the improvements if you’re paying full price, retailers appear to be deeply discounting the existing stock of BX-2s, so it’s worth considering the older model if there’s a great price on it, at least until retailers run out of the now-discontinued model.

Zeiss is synonymous with good glass in consumer camera lenses and sport optics, so the mid-tier-priced Terra ED 10x42 binoculars are somewhat of a surprise in the manufacturer’s lineup. I’ve tested several higher-end Zeiss binoculars, which were unsurprisingly great given their price tags, but finding nearly as good clarity and color in this $500 pair was unexpected.

While $500 isn’t cheap for most casual binocular buyers, I’d argue getting Zeiss quality at this price presents a serious value if you’re looking for good glass but aren’t ready to put down a mortgage payment to get it.

The 10x42 Terra ED uses ED glass and Zeiss's hydrophobic multicoating to produce a bright, high-contrast image that will feel like a serious upgrade to budget options. It's not going to match the edge-to-edge perfection or low-light performance of Zeiss's flagship binoculars, but it delivers enough optical quality to be an obvious jump in clarity for the investment.

The 10x42 configuration is a versatile middle ground for hunting, hiking, birding, and general wildlife observation. The Terra ED has a 330-foot field of view at 1,000 yards and a 5.3-foot close focus, so it is adequate for birding, casual stargazing, and wildlife watching. At 25.6 ounces, it's also light enough to carry comfortably in a chest harness (not included) for long stretches without pulling on your neck.

The fiberglass-reinforced housing is waterproof and nitrogen-filled to minimize fog and condensation outdoors. The hydrophobic coating helps keep moisture from becoming a major distraction when you're using the binoculars in wet weather or going between temperature extremes. The large focus wheel is also easy to find and operate quickly.

The Terra ED isn't the cheapest 10x42 on the market, but that's not really the point. It's a relatively affordable way into a serious optics brand, and the difference is visible when you use them side by side with cheaper binos.

Most hunters don't need the biggest or most expensive binoculars they can buy, but many with the budget and obsession with the sport will spend thousands hoping expensive optics can buy success. While $3,000 binoculars are overkill for most, hunters do ask a lot of their optics. Early morning and late evening light and long distances expose the weaknesses of lower-quality glass, so many hunters get the advice to buy the best glass they can afford.

While the top-tier binoculars are undoubtedly better, there’s a mid-tier sweet spot that gives you most of the performance of those four-figure binos at a fraction of the price. The Vortex Viper HD 10x42 binoculars are squarely there. Vortex’s Triumph binoculars are our Best Budget pick above for the value they provide at $100, but dedicated hunters who get up early and stay out late will eventually want to upgrade.

The Viper HD 10x42s have a list price around $729, but can be found at most retailers for closer to $500, a good value for the upgrade. Still, that’s $400 more than the Triumph, so you’ll want to be sure you value the improvements. The benefits over cheaper options are better light transmission and reduced distortions such as chromatic aberrations. If you’ve used cheaper binos, you’ve seen off colors such as purple fringes on trees. In broad daylight, this isn’t a problem you can’t adjust your eyes to, but in low light and longer distances it can result in straining and struggling to tell a stump from a bear or deer.

A relatively low 24.9-ounce weight and compact form factor enabled by the roof prism design make these comfortable to carry in a chest harness over long days in the field or a tree stand. The rubber housing offers satisfactory grip and I notably appreciated the underside thumb grooves, though they don’t look as high-end as magnesium housings. The included chest harness is capable enough, but will be a throwaway for hunters who already own a specialized hunting chest rig.

Turned off by cheap compact binoculars in the past? These are worth it. They offer quality optics with powerful-enough magnification for bird-watching, hunting, and a range of everyday uses. In addition, they’re sealed, so they're waterproof and fogproof, and there is a lifetime warranty on the optical parts so you won’t need to replace them if you drop these binos even once.

Nikon’s Trailblazer II 10x25 compact binoculars make some compromises on field of view and low-light performance, but on the plus side, they’re small enough to fit in your pocket. These are binoculars you can toss in a daypack for when you want them, rather than committing to positioning them on you, front and center, and dealing with the weight.

The trade-off, however, is the 25mm objective lens. There's simply less glass available to gather light than you'll get with a 42mm binocular, and the field of view isn’t bad but feels cramped if you’re used to bigger binos. The 10mm eye relief also isn't particularly generous for eyeglass wearers. But for quick looks during daylight, the compact size makes these much easier to have on you when you need them.

Bigger is typically better when it comes to optical performance in binoculars, but there is always a give-and-take. With standard 10x42 or larger binoculars, you get better performance, but usually at least a pound of weight and a bulky item. Bird-watchers and hunters use chest harnesses to distribute the weight and keep them accessible, but that’s a commitment you don’t always want for a casual hike.

The Trailblazer II has a more palatable price at $130 than you’ll find with larger binos with similar optical quality, making these a no-brainer if you’re shopping for casual use.

Like Leupold, Vortex has a great reputation, in large part due to its accommodating lifetime warranty program—a comfort to those of us who are hard on our gear. One challenge with Vortex is the sheer number of lines and models, but the newer Triumph line is easy to understand: it’s the brand’s cheapest pair of binoculars in the 10x42 build and arguably its best value at $100. While the optical clarity and quality aren’t on par with that of the more expensive lines, the Triumph does surprisingly well, especially in daylight.

Like most cheaper binos, the clarity falls off the closer you get to the edges of the field of view, but is sharp and color-true in the center. Yes, you can spend less, but I wouldn’t recommend it, having tried a lot of $25 to $50 binoculars over the years. Despite the attractive price, the Triumph ships with a case, harness, and neck strap as well.

Nikon is best known for its cameras and lenses, but this Japanese optics brand makes a wide range of excellent binoculars as well. I’ve tested several models over the years, but the Monarch M7 stands out for the optical clarity it delivers at a mid-tier price point.

Nearly any pair of binoculars will work for birding, but for most species, you’ll be in trees and fairly close quarters where higher magnifications can make it harder to spot and track birds with the more zoomed-in view. Even when you spot a bird, even the slightly tighter 10x magnification can make it harder to follow flight.

The 8x42 version of the Monarch M7 binos gives you enough magnification to confirm details without spooking wildlife, but the wide field makes it easier to track them. The M5 series is also good and about $200 cheaper, but it has a narrow field of view even in the 8x42 configuration.

Like most binoculars, they’re nitrogen-purged and O-ring sealed, which just means they’re waterproof and fogproof. (Note: Fogproof only means there won’t be interior fog. You can still steam up the glass if, for example, your face is warm and the air or binos are cold.)

More money can, of course, get you even better glass from Nikon and other companies, but this is one of the best mid-tier options that delivers a clear and comfortable birding experience. Less money will downgrade your clarity, but cheaper and smaller options that weigh and cost much less abound if those are your priorities. (The entry-level Nocs Provisions Standard Issue 8x25 binos, for example, weigh about half as much.)

There are times when 10x binoculars simply aren't enough—and that’s especially true in Western hunting country where the animal you're looking for might be thousands of yards across a canyon. A 15x binocular like Maven's C.4 gives you considerably more reach while retaining the depth perception and comfort of looking through both eyes.

Extra reach is the reason to buy these. At 15x, details that can be frustratingly ambiguous through a conventional 10x42 become much easier to pick apart. I've found high-power binoculars particularly useful for searching large expanses of terrain where the ability to use both eyes makes extended glassing considerably more comfortable than squinting through a spotting scope. At distances of a mile or more, you’ll still want a spotter that’s 20x and up, but the 15x is a meaningful middle ground that’s wide enough to use in a forest, but reaches far enough for big mountain hunting.

The C.4 combines 15x magnification with large 56mm objective lenses, giving it a 215-foot field of view at 1,000 yards. This is noticeably tighter than, for example, the 342-foot field of view of the Leupold BX-3 10x42s, but that’s by design and the trade-off for 50 percent more zoom.

The other big trade-off is the weight and size. At 45 ounces, they’re still lighter than a spotting scope, but you’ll likely also be carrying a tripod, which adds several pounds to your pack. Compare this to 10x42s, which are usually 1 to 2 pounds total, and it’s a big difference. Still, if you’re using binoculars to cover big mountain distances and need the clarity and zoom to identify animals precisely, it’s likely a weight penalty you’ll be willing to pay.

The ED glass and phase-corrected, dielectric-coated prisms produce a surprisingly sharp image, and the 92 percent light transmission is key around dawn and dusk—impressive for the price. Most high-quality, high-power binoculars are at least four figures, but Maven delivers the C.4 for $550. Unlike the rest of its line that uses Japanese and Chinese components that are assembled in Wyoming, the C.4 is produced entirely in China. Despite this difference, I didn’t notice a sharp drop-off in build or optical quality from other Maven binos I’ve tested.

There's a catch: These aren't really handheld binoculars. At 45.1 ounces, the C.4 is almost twice the weight of many conventional 10x42s, and 15x magnification makes even small hand movements obvious. I used the C.4 handheld fine for short periods, but the size and weight wear on your stability much faster than lightweight hunting binos. Once mounted, however, the extra weight only affects your pack and the C.4 becomes a remarkably capable long-distance optic.

Any decent pair of binoculars can enhance casual stargazing, but if you’re closer to “astrophile” than “occasional noticer of the night sky,” you’ll want a somewhat specialized pair and the best low-light performance you can afford. As binoculars go up in price, into the thousands, the returns are diminishing, giving you small increments of improved clarity and edge-case benefits, but they are better.

Celestron is a budget binocular brand, but its SkyMaster series is astro-focused and worth a look if you’re focused on stars. The massive objective lenses, high magnification, and Porro prism combine for a heavy build, but one that’s designed for night use at the distance of light-years.

The 25x magnification won’t be much good for backyard bird-watching or any handheld use, but they’re designed for tripod mounting, and for use as a stationary sky-watching setup that’s more comfortable for longer sessions than telescopes.

Going with binoculars for sky watching, especially ones as zoomed-in as these, adds a layer of dimensionality that’s lost looking with the naked eye (or with one eye as with telescopes). Unlike cheap Amazon optics brands, Celestron has friendly price points but still gives you a lifetime warranty. If your stargazing habit sticks, you may eventually want to upgrade to a higher-end model, but this pair will show you what you’ve been missing for a few hundred bucks.

Yes, this is an article about binoculars, but I humbly present the little-known monocular as a better tool for certain jobs. Like spotting scopes, monoculars aren’t as natural to use since you have to close one eye and miss out on the depth of a stereo picture. However, there are times when the weight and bulk of binos mean you leave them at home even though you might want them in the field.

I personally carry this Nocs Field Tube monocular every time I go backcountry skiing and find it quite literally worth its weight for scouting potential approach and descent lines, using it to pick out details about snow quality, avalanches, and terrain that my naked eye regularly misses. It’s also easy to throw in a pack or pocket for casual hikes or pre-hunting season scouting walks where I never know when I might want a closer look at something but am not motivated enough to harness up with my binoculars.

There aren’t a lot of companies making monoculars, but Nocs has several options. I love the 8x32 size of the Field Tube, but you can get a 10x32 version that offers a bit more zoom but a slightly narrower field of view in the same size package. There’s also the newer 8x32 Zoom Tube that’s significantly cheaper and slightly lighter and smaller but isn’t fully waterproof.

The magnifications of most consumer binoculars are fixed (i.e., not zoomable), making it one of the most important decisions for buyers. More isn't always better and can feel claustrophobic in tight quarters such as trying to track a small bird in dense forest, while wider-angle, lower-power lenses can feel underpowered if you're trying to see across longer distances.

Most binos range between 7x and 18x. Much less than 7x isn’t enough of an effect to bother with for most applications, while 15x and up becomes difficult to use without a tripod. In general, lower-power binoculars are a better fit if you’re mostly glassing shorter distances, such as in forests, and don’t need precise detail. Higher-power binoculars make sense if you’re looking to avoid also carrying a spotting scope and glassing longer distances up to a mile or need precise detail for identifying wildlife.

Objective lens diameter is the size of the lens farthest from your eyes and impacts the field of vision you get, as well as how bright the image is since, generally, bigger lenses capture more light. Jeremy Bentham, senior manager of SRO Sales & Operations for Nikon, explains the trade-offs associated with objective lens size.

“One of the key things that needs to be decided is brightness versus weight,” he says. “Larger objective lenses allow more light into the optic but are also heavier to pack and carry with you.”

Objective lens sizes in binoculars range anywhere from 20 mm to 100 mm, but the common sizes are mostly in the 30 mm to 60 mm range. I find ultracompact binoculars of less than about 30 mm annoying to use for more than a quick look at something nearby since the field of view is small and it can be harder to keep in position on your eyes. On the other end of the spectrum, most binoculars larger than about 55 mm become heavy and unwieldy if used without a tripod.

Binoculars are listed by size spec, where the first number is magnification and the second is objective lens diameter. Probably the most common size is 10x42, which permits a big enough field of view and magnification for viewing things as near as 15 feet but still allows you to spot larger objects such as wildlife as far as a mile away.

For backcountry hunters and hikers, weight is one of the most important considerations in a binocular. You need to balance the desire for a larger, brighter optic with your ability and interest to carry it around your neck over long distances. For me, anything heavier than about 35 ounces is too much to carry in a chest harness when actively hunting and covering several miles a day. If you’re a hunter or hiker who doesn’t need ready access to your binoculars and can keep them in a pack until you need them at specific spots, you may be able to tolerate heavier options.

Even folks who mostly use binoculars at home or from a vehicle need to consider weight. Heavier binoculars are harder to use for longer periods of time since your arms and shoulders do eventually fatigue and become shaky. If you’re mostly using binoculars on a tripod (a must for higher-magnification binos), you may want to prize optical quality over weight savings.

Aside from range-finding binoculars, most have surprisingly little tech as we usually think of it in terms of electronics and connectivity. What makes great binoculars hasn’t changed much over the years and still boils down to quality glass and craftsmanship.

Where tech does show up is in the glass quality and the coatings used. High-tech coatings are used to make colors true, enhance brightness, and protect your expensive lenses. Coatings and glass often use nonstandardized terms such as ED, HD, and UHD glass, or proprietary branding such as LotuTec or Swarobright, making it hard to compare apples to apples when talking about something as subjective as optical quality. What you can look for is whether lenses are “fully multi-coated,” meaning that all glass surfaces have multiple coatings for durability and performance, whatever the branding.
