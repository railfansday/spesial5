What is code rot? How a software term became TikTok slang.

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

An old video game can look just the way it did years ago, right up until a door that used to open won't budge, a character stops responding when you try to talk to them, or the game freezes every time you load a saved level. That's one way to run into what programmers call "code rot." The game itself may have stayed the same while the platform it runs on keeps getting updates. Over time, parts of the game stop working altogether.

On Gen Z TikTok, anything can be "code rotted," from a report card full of failing grades to a slushie machine that won’t dispense the goods. The joke is that real life is starting to break down like an old Roblox game, especially when something that's supposed to go right just doesn't.

Creators write text overlays in the voice of someone who's just confirmed a long-held suspicion: A baffling homework assignment? "Yup yup yup i f*cking knew it this assignment is definitely code rotted." Can't log into your Google account? "google is coderotted. it's coderot." Puppy acting up? It's code-rotted, too.

The phrase first gained traction on Roblox, specifically when some players revisited games they grew up with. In May, TikToker @planetaryequinox posted a clip of the apartments in Royale High, a school role-playing game, glitching in a loop. The game's developers said in an X post they'd received more bug reports from older areas and had already released several patches.

Later that month, another TikToker @forgottenfuture1 started a series revisiting older Roblox games, beginning with Greenwood Town, one of the first games they remembered playing. Users also found "code rot" in other games, like Fortnite and Genshin Impact.

By October, a Despicable Me 4 TikTok filter had brought the "code rot" joke beyond Roblox. The filter is a mini-game where players steer Gru toward gates marked with different characters' faces. But in an Oct. 13 TikTok from @diaryofawimpykidfan01, Gru barrels straight through every gate as if the choices don’t matter. The creator called the filter "code-rotted," and the clip's eerie, slowed-down soundtrack made a glitch in a Gru mini-game feel like the opening of a horror movie.

The joke borrows from something that really does happen online. The internet can feel permanent right up until an old bookmark leads to a 404, or an old forum thread still exists but the photos that made it useful have disappeared. That’s link rot, and it adds up. In a 2024 study, Pew Research Center found that 38 percent of the webpages it sampled from 2013 were inaccessible a decade later.

There's a visual version of that decay, too, with a similar name. "Pixel rot" is used to describe images breaking down into visible blocks and smears. A familiar version is the meme that's been saved and reposted so many times that the text is barely legible. Each round of compression can strip away a little more detail, so a picture can stay in circulation long after it stops looking like the original. For more of a visual, see PetaPixel's 2020 experiment with repeated Instagram uploads.

"Code rot" joins a growing vocabulary among Gen Z and Gen Alpha slang for describing real life as though it were a video game. An "NPC" is a "non-playable character," or someone who’s acting like a background character in someone else’s story. And "XP" measures experience in a game, which is why streamer Sykkuno once joked that his XP bar was low when talking about his dating life.

For generations that grew up learning, playing, and making friends online, it makes sense that the internet would shape the words they use to understand the world around them. We'll see which gaming term makes its way into the group chat next.
