Teen messaging and well-being: Who is on the other end matters

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

A study of Swedish teens links messaging with offline friends to higher well-being and contact with online-only friends to lower scores.

The usual question about a teenager’s phone is how many hours. A survey of 3,034 Swedish 15-year-olds asked a different one: who is on the other end?

Teens who messaged close friends or their wider circle several times a day or more scored higher on a well-being test than teens who rarely did.

Teens who kept up with friends they had only ever met online, even just weekly, scored lower.

Viviane Straatmann of Stockholm University is the study’s senior author.

She conducted the study with first author Madeleine Björnsdotter Orfanidou, also at Stockholm, and Kenisha Russell Jonsson of the University of Gothenburg.

The survey was taken at one moment in each teen’s life, so it can show a pattern and not a cause.

In an interview with Earth.com, Straatmann said parents should take an interest not only in how much time teens spend messaging but in whom they message.

“Online communication can be a valuable way to maintain and strengthen friendships with people adolescents know offline,” she said.

The teens came from two rounds of the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children survey, a long-running study of young people’s health across many countries.

One round ran in the 2017 to 2018 school year, the other in 2021 to 2022, and the team pooled them.

Each teen was asked how often they sent and received texts, emojis, photos, videos or voice messages, or made video calls, with three kinds of people.

One was a close friend. One was a larger group of friends. The third was friends they had met through the internet and never in person.

The answers were sorted into three levels: rarely, weekly to daily, and several times a day up to almost all the time. Two-thirds of the teens were in that top band with their close friends.

Well-being was measured using seven questions about the past two weeks, including feeling optimistic, useful, relaxed and close to other people. Higher scores mean better well-being.

Teens who messaged a close friend several times a day scored about 1.6 points higher than teens who rarely did.

Once the team accounted for gender, family finances, family situation and survey year, that gap shrank to about one point and was no longer statistically significant.

Frequent messaging with a larger friend group remained associated with higher well-being after the same adjustments, with a gap of nearly one point.

Straatmann called that an important finding, because it means online communication was not uniformly negative.

Digital contact, she said, can be a positive tool when it complements a teen’s everyday offline relationships.

The pattern reversed for friends a teen knew only online. Teens who messaged such friends weekly to daily scored about 0.9 points lower than teens who rarely did.

Teens who did so several times a day scored about 0.7 points lower. Both gaps survived the adjustments.

The split is not new. The authors wrote that studies in the United Kingdom and Finland found the same thing.

Teens in touch online with friends they knew offline reported higher well-being, and teens communicating mostly with online-only friends reported lower.

Those are small differences on the scale. For comparison, girls scored almost three points lower than boys.

Teens from families they described as well off scored almost three points higher than teens who said their family was not.

The association with online-only contact remained after adjustment, but the differences by gender and family finances were larger.

“This does not mean that all online-only friendships are harmful, but it underlines the importance of open conversations, age-appropriate guidance, and a safe digital environment,” Straatmann told Earth.com.

Girls were more likely than boys to message a close friend several times a day or more: 77% compared with 65%.

Teens from the most affluent families were far more likely to do so than teens from the least, 82% to 62%.

Boys were slightly more likely than girls to be in frequent contact with online-only friends, though the gap was not clearly beyond chance.

The team adjusted for those differences. But the authors wrote that their models did not test whether the link between online-only friends and well-being differs for girls or for lower-income teens.

The study could not establish which way the relationship runs.

“As this was a cross-sectional study, we cannot say whether these online relationships contribute to poorer well-being, whether adolescents with lower well-being are more likely to seek such relationships, or whether both processes are involved,” Straatmann said.

The study also measured only Swedish 15-year-olds, couldn’t apply the survey’s population weights, and didn’t ask about cyberbullying or how good the friendships were.

Teens surveyed in the 2021–2022 round scored almost two points lower than those in the earlier round, and the design can’t separate the pandemic from everything else that changed between the rounds.

Sweden’s public health agency recently issued its first screen guidance for teenagers, built around a daily limit of two to three hours for ages 13 to 18.

The authors wrote that the nature of online contact, not only its length, might belong in future updates of that advice.

Straatmann said the next step is following the same teens over time, to watch how messaging and well-being shape each other.

She also wants to know whether the pattern holds for neurodivergent teens, including those with ADHD or autism, for whom online contact may offer an unusually important route to connection.

The full study was published in the journal Child and Adolescent Mental Health.
