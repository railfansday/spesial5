Her Father Figure Raped Her As a Child. Then She Was Charged With His Murder

An old Dodge pickup sat parked on the street in Zachary, Louisiana, a small city about 16 miles north of Baton Rouge. Two figures clad in black slipped out into the warm July night: a young man and a girl. Jace Crehan was 21, muscular and stocky with a youthful face and cropped dark hair. Brittany Monk was 17, just over five feet tall — pale, bespectacled, and heavily pregnant, with her long brown hair in a high bun. It was July 3, 2015, around 2 a.m. The two crept across the lawn to a small pale-gray trailer. Jace urged Brittany to stay outside, but she refused. He removed an air conditioner from the window, hoisted himself up, and then pulled her inside. She looked around: This had once been her room.

Together, they tiptoed toward the kitchen, past the pleather couch, the box TV, the grimy bathroom with its cracked tub, the piles of children’s clothing and toys, an old “Happy Birthday!” sign. Brittany and Jace headed to a door. Jace opened it and turned on the light. There, Robert “Robbie” Noce — balding, heavyset — was jolted from sleep. He sat up, disoriented. He gazed at Jace with confusion; they’d never met. As Jace shifted, Brittany came into view. A look of terror crossed Robbie’s face.

“Oh no, no, no,” he yelled. Jace moved toward Robbie, and the two began to tussle. Brittany approached as Jace pulled Robbie from the bed. Jace handed her a can of Axe cologne, and she sprayed Robbie in the face. “You ruined my life,” she yelled. “God doesn’t love you.”

According to court documents and Brittany’s recollections, Jace held Robbie by the neck, trying to subdue him. Robbie reached for a large rechargeable battery. Brittany was fairly certain that Robbie hit Jace in the head with it. The two men fell to the ground.

Jace told Brittany to get him a knife and something to tie Robbie up with. She ran to the kitchen and grabbed the biggest knife. She saw a handful of ties and belts hanging on a door, handed them to Jace, and stepped into the bathroom. She could hear the sound of the knife tearing into Robbie’s skin and muscle, and she began to dry heave. After about 90 seconds — she could never remember exactly how long she was in the bathroom — Brittany emerged to find Robbie slumped on the floor, blood soaking the carpet. She and Jace looked at each other. It wasn’t supposed to go this way.

They sneaked out and climbed back into Jace’s truck. They drove home, showered, and then threw away their clothes in the trash at a local car wash and tossed the knife into a nearby lake. Then they got into bed. They hadn’t spoken much to each other. Jace turned to Brittany.

But years later, when Brittany looked back on that night, she didn’t remember feeling much at all. All her life, she had “floated,” she tells me, disconnected from her emotions and experiences. Immediately after Robbie’s killing, it was no different.

BRITTANY MONK WAS BORN IN BATON ROUGE, Louisiana, in 1997. Her fate, she suspected, was sealed from the start: Her mother was Maggie Dixon, age 15. Maggie had been working as a babysitter when the father of the kids she cared for had sex with her. After she told him that she was pregnant, he moved away, and her own mother kicked her out. Homeless, with infant Brittany, Maggie bounced between abusive boyfriends, from Louisiana to Arkansas. Eventually Robbie Noce, a family friend 14 years Maggie’s senior, invited her and Brittany, then age three, to live with him in Baker, in East Baton Rouge Parish. Within a year, Maggie gave birth to Robbie’s daughter Kamron. Robbie and Maggie fought relentlessly. One day, Maggie left for good. Robbie obtained sole custody of both girls. Life in Robbie’s trailer was muted and isolated, punctuated with whippings for Brittany if she stepped out of line. She believed Robbie was her father and called him dad. There were few visitors. Robbie worked as a shipping clerk at a nickel-alloy distributor. The girls, with their long, dark hair and quiet demeanors, attended daycare, and then grade school. Robbie cooked pasta Alfredo or took them to Wendy’s for chicken nuggets or fed them SpaghettiOs and Uncrustables. They watched Wheel of Fortune nearly every day, cartoons, movies. Kamron idolized Brittany. Though Brittany was only four years older, she served as Kamron’s mother figure. Maggie was scarce, rarely stopping by. The sisters played in the mud and slept in bunk beds. They talked about almost everything — but there was one topic they didn’t touch on for many years.

At night, Robbie bathed the girls. According to court documents and interviews with Brittany, Robbie rubbed lotion on them and gave her “sweet medicine” or “sleep medicine,” as he called it: a variety of over-the-counter syrups and pills that caused Brittany to nod off. She would then wake in her own bed with her sister sleeping nearby in a crib, and Robbie performing oral sex on her. She believes the abuse began when she was around four years old, soon after Maggie left. Over time, Robbie taught her to perform oral sex on him and often moved her into his own bed. Sometimes, he would instruct her to get dressed and go outside to wait for her mom. She would sit on the front steps for hours. Then Robbie would tell Brittany that Maggie was never coming back. He was all she had.

When Brittany was around seven, Robbie informed her that he was not her biological father. He told her he loved her differently than he loved Kamron. That’s why he did what he did to her. He told Brittany that if anyone found out, their small family would be broken, and she would be separated from Kamron. Increasingly, he treated 10-year-old Brittany like a girlfriend. When she resisted his assaults, he began to pay her, up to $100 for sex acts. Or he’d give her Play-Doh and magic markers as a reward. Brittany began to think of herself, she later told authorities, as “the woman of the house.” She feared and resented Robbie but also admired and adored him. A part of her was even grateful: Neither of her parents had wanted her, but Robbie did.

When Brittany was around 12, Maggie, pregnant and engaged, finally returned for her. Kamron stayed with Robbie, and Brittany moved in with Maggie. At Maggie’s house, Brittany witnessed a lot of drinking and partying. Strange people, many of them drunk or high, were in and out. She was on her own a lot, doing her homework, attending school, making A’s and B’s. She missed Kamron, worried about her, and felt deep guilt that she had abandoned her. Was Kamron safe? Was Robbie hurting her?

In 2011, Maggie gave birth to a son. Brittany, then 14, was often charged with his care. She gave him formula, changed his diapers, bathed him. But as time went on, she grew frustrated that she was a permanent babysitter. She texted and met up with boys, despite Maggie forbidding it.

Late one night at the end of July 2012, Maggie and Brittany had an argument about Brittany’s behavior. Maggie demanded to know why Brittany seemed so angry at the world. Brittany told her: She couldn’t forgive herself for leaving Kamron behind with Robbie. What about Robbie, Maggie wanted to know.

Maggie did not tell Brittany then, but one reason she left their home, she later confessed, was because Robbie had also forcibly sodomized her. The day after Brittany’s disclosure, Maggie brought her to the ER. Patients mother states her boyfriend used to give the kids Xanax and Sominex, the records read. Mother states her boyfriend is the legal guardian of the patient and her younger sibling… Patient states the boyfriend tried to make her have oral sex, rectal sex, vaginal sexual intercourse.

A detective from the East Baton Rouge Sheriff’s Department was assigned to the case. He arrested Robbie on Aug. 1, 2012. When law enforcement searched Robbie’s trailer that day, they seized three handguns, marijuana paraphernalia, and several medications that cause drowsiness, including more than 20 bottles of cold and flu medications, sleep aids, prescription painkillers, prescription allergy medication, and prescription opioids. Brittany did not know what she had been given as “sweet medicine,” but she tells me there were a wide variety of medications used nightly throughout her childhood. On Aug. 5, Robbie was charged with aggravated rape. After about two weeks, his brother bonded him out.

Detectives spoke to an ex-girlfriend of Robbie’s. According to law enforcement files, she “told the detectives that Noce had shown her child pornography on his computer with photos of children who appeared to be around 11 years old, that the pictures were not on a website but photos saved in Noce’s computer.” Kamron was pulled from Robbie’s home. She moved in with Maggie and Brittany. The East Baton Rouge Parish District Attorney’s Office began building a case against Robbie. (There were no allegations of Noce sexually abusing Kamron.)

In September, Maggie, shocked to learn that Robbie had been released from jail so quickly, emailed the DA’s office to complain. I believe that he is guilty and i also believe that he will not be punished for what he has done, she wrote. In January 2013, a grand jury swiftly indicted Robbie. That November, more than a year after her first message, Maggie wrote to the DA’s office again. An employee responded with an update and also offered Maggie a Thanksgiving meal. The employee wrote that Hillar Moore, the district attorney, was donating food to needy families. Maggie responded that this was “the best news we got all year!” Aww that’s awesome, the employee wrote back. And Hillar may have gotten you guys some paper plates and forks/knives.

Hillar C. Moore III has been the elected DA of East Baton Rouge Parish since 2009. A former criminal defense lawyer with deep Louisiana roots, he campaigned as a champion of victims, especially women and children and survivors of domestic violence and sexual assault. Moore’s office was tasked with prosecuting Robbie. In interviews, Moore has stated that he “can’t think of anything worse than raping a child” and that he would consider seeking the death penalty for people who sexually abuse children. Louisiana is also the first state to allow surgical castration as a punishment for aggravated sex crimes against children under 13, a practice Moore has said he is open to using.

But when it came to Brittany’s case, other than the delivery of a fried turkey and plastic cutlery, she recalls that she had little contact with Moore’s office after she reported being raped. She attended sophomore year, then junior year. She turned 16. She tried not to reflect much on her past. Maggie was mostly sleeping, gone, or in a rage. Brittany was increasingly left to care for her younger siblings. After she’d fed them dinner and put them to bed, she’d scroll Instagram and post cute selfies.

Jace Crehan, then 21 and working at a pizza place in nearby Denham Springs, had been commenting on Brittany’s photos online for about six months before she responded. They met in person a week later and were soon in a relationship. Brittany told Jace that she had been abused as a child and that she hated living at her mom’s. He fashioned himself as her savior, though he later concluded that he had not been equipped to help anyone. “I was self-absorbed, egotistical, self righteous, proud, with machismo,” he tells me. Brittany was immediately devoted to Jace, but she later recognized this as a sort of trauma response. “I was so young and naïve and inexperienced in life, having been through what I been through, anybody that said nice things to me, I would have felt like I loved them,” she says.

Immediately after Brittany turned 17, the two moved into a rental apartment. Brittany started to feel hesitant about going forward with the DA’s case against Robbie; she was increasingly frightened by the possibility of facing her abuser in court, and unnerved by how long the process had dragged on. In September 2014, more than two years after she first reported the abuse, she met with a victim’s assistance coordinator and Leslie Ricard, an ADA from Hillar Moore’s office. Brittany appeared “visibly nervous and anxious,” according to Ricard’s notes.

“Growing up, he was stronger than me, and a man. I thought men were strong and women were weak … I had this belief that no matter what, I could never win.”

That winter, Brittany became pregnant with Jace’s son. The baby was due in September 2015. As the pregnancy progressed, Brittany again told the DA’s office she didn’t want the case to go forward. Her pregnancy was high risk due to the shape of her uterus. She worried the stress of a trial would harm her unborn baby. She didn’t want to look at Robbie’s face or be cross-examined by his attorneys. “I didn’t feel strong enough or brave enough to walk into a courtroom and face him,” she tells me. “Growing up, he was stronger than me, and a man. I thought men were strong and women were weak … I had this belief that no matter what, I could never win.”

Concerned that the case was falling apart, the women from the DA’s office met with Brittany and Jace again at a coffee shop. They explained that dismissing the case was out of the question. Robbie needed to be held responsible, the coordinator wrote in her notes. Jace remembered the DA’s apparent commitment to nailing Robbie. He recalled Ricard saying that Robbie deserved “nothing less than a life sentence.” According to emails from Ricard to another prosecutor, Brittany decided that she didn’t want to testify. Ricard asked Brittany: If the DA could convince Robbie to take a plea, and avoid a trial for Brittany’s sake, what kind of sentence would she be comfortable with? “She informed me that a 10-year sentence would be the lowest number she would be comfortable with,” Ricard wrote.

On June 22, 2015, Robbie was brought before the judge. The charge against him, once aggravated rape, was amended to the lesser charge of felony carnal knowledge of a juvenile. Robbie pled no contest and was sentenced to 10 years of hard labor. The judge then suspended his sentence.

Jace explained to Brittany what a suspended sentence meant: Robbie would not spend any time in prison. He would simply be on probation. Jace was surprised by the outcome. “I was fully under the impression that no probatable deal would be offered to him,” he tells me. Brittany and Jace, sitting in their small rental apartment, processed the news that Robbie would walk free. The TV was playing The Office, their favorite show; Brittany looked at the gray tile floors, at her expanding stomach, at Jace sitting nearby. She thought of Kamron, back at Maggie’s house. Is Robbie going to be released today? she texted Ricard. Into the public? Ricard replied that Robbie had been free for years already. Brittany tells me that she reasoned that he’d been claiming he was innocent and, now that he had a record, he’d have to register as a sex offender. What if that enraged him? Brittany started to panic. “I didn’t think that he was brave enough to do anything, but then at the back of my mind, there’s that chance that he’s angry with me,” she tells me. “If he gets away with raping me for 10 years, surely he would get away with anything else.” She would later be diagnosed with PTSD, but at the time, she just felt “crazy,” she says.

She began to have dreams of Robbie hurting her or Kamron, of Maggie’s house combusting in flames, and Robbie walking away. She woke up every night in a sweat, sobbing. Jace would try to calm her and then head to work at the car dealership the next day. Neither was sleeping more than four hours. Brittany began to suggest that they move to Baton Rouge so Robbie couldn’t find their baby and their new family. Jace’s mother, Shannen Bursee, tells me that her son was “scared to death that Robert could come hurt her. He said, ‘Mom I never realized that a man could rape a child …’ He said, ‘They get caught for molesting and they go for two or three years and then they get out and they kill their victims.’” Much later, in a letter from jail, Jace wrote that every night after Robbie’s plea bargain, Brittany “would have night terrors specifically about him breaking in our home, killing me, beating and raping her.”

About a week after Robbie took his plea and walked out of court a free man, Jace had an idea: Since the state of Louisiana hadn’t held Robbie accountable, perhaps he could do it himself. Jace would go to Robbie’s home to rough him up to send the message: Stay away from us. He’d do it alone. No, Brittany said. This was her story, her abuser, her pain. She insisted: She would accompany him. And so the decision was made.

They have always maintained that they never intended for Robbie to die. “If I wanted to kill him, I would have went there with a gun and blown his head off,” Jace tells me. They didn’t think Robbie would even fight back. He had a weak stomach, gastrointestinal issues, and he was in poor health. From their vantage point, Robbie, age 47, seemed so old. They figured he would take the hits. In retrospect, Brittany understands this was ridiculous.

“If I had raped someone for 10 years and got away with it and they came back in the middle of the night, they were coming to kill me,” she tells me.

So after they sneaked across the dark street in Zachary and broke into the trailer on July 3, Brittany and Jace were not prepared for Robbie’s reaction. “He fought for his life,” Brittany remembers. “That was the factor that escalated the situation … There was a lot of rage in me, but more so, the fear: We’ve gone too far now.”

Once Robbie started to tussle with Jace, everything spiraled. Brittany handed the knife and the belt to Jace and she dry heaved in the bathroom. When she stepped back out, Jace, covered in blood, was looking at her, and Robbie was lying on the floor, unresponsive.

Back at their apartment, they showered. They discarded their clothes at a car wash and tossed the knife into a murky pond. The next day, there was a July 4th celebration at Jace’s grandparents’ house; not going would seem suspicious. “He was very pale, not himself,” Jace’s grandmother remembered of him that day. His mother would later notice that his legs were covered in marks. Brittany barely spoke. They didn’t eat and left quickly. That same day, Robbie’s brother stopped by his trailer. When Robbie did not answer the door, his brother called police, who entered the property and found Robbie dead.

Authorities contacted Jace and Brittany and requested that they come to the police station that night. At the station, Jace refused to talk. Brittany sat down with detectives and lied about her involvement in the crime, feigning ignorance. The police seemed to believe her, at least in part; they assumed that Jace had acted alone. “I know you wasn’t there,” one detective said to Brittany, in an exchange recorded on an interrogation video. “Whatever happened over there doesn’t have anything to do with you,” another added.

Brittany walked. Back at home, she and Jace scanned Facebook, where news of Robbie’s death was met with happiness from internet commenters. Jace reassured Brittany: They didn’t do anything wrong. But if they did get in trouble, he was willing to take the fall.

On July 8, detectives brought Brittany in for questioning again. This time, they said they believed she had taken part in the crime. That same day, they circled Jace’s workplace, then followed him as he left. He saw them trailing him, pulled over, hopped out, and popped the hood of his truck. The police drew their guns. Did he want to help Brittany, they asked. If so, now would be the time.

Jace told them everything. “My goal during confession was to take the fall, to completely exonerate her,” he says. When he was done, he told detectives: “I feel a lot better. She’ll suffer for a couple weeks in jail, and then she’ll get bond.”

Instead, both Brittany and Jace were arrested and charged with second-degree murder, which carries an automatic life without parole sentence in Louisiana. Brittany’s bail was set at $400,000. She was declared indigent and provided with two public defenders. She sat in jail, seven months pregnant. Nobody could meet that bail; she had few visitors. Instead, her mother Maggie spoke with detectives, calling Brittany “gullible” and “naïve.” Maggie focused on her own reputation. “I’ve had people tell me, ‘This is not your fault, you did all you could for her,’” Maggie told the detectives. Brittany tells me that she felt that Maggie “blamed herself so hard that she started to blame me.” They never did speak meaningfully after that. “Even when she went against me, I said, ‘She just had a hard life, it is her fault, but not her fault, give her grace.’ I unconditionally love her. I can’t help it.” In 2021, Maggie died of a heroin overdose at age 40.

On Sept. 8, 2015, two months into her jail stay, Brittany went into early labor. She was transported to the hospital. Medical students, she tells me, delivered her baby. She believed this was because she was a prisoner; she did not think free women were treated thusly. Half a dozen students crowded around her. She recalled a doctor and two officers watching, too. She felt like a “science project.”

Her son, Vaan, was just over five pounds: He has brown hair, Brittany wrote to Jace. I’m not sure what color eyes yet because he is so young but they look like they will be brown. He has your nose. And little lips like me. She knew she would only be with her newborn for 48 hours, so she forced herself to stay awake the whole time, to stare at him, hold him, feed him. On Sept. 10, the state took Vaan from Brittany. Back at the jail, she sat in booking for hours, feeling like her pelvis was “ripping in half,” she wrote to Jace. She was returned to her bunk in a wheelchair, her breasts engorged. Jace’s father and stepmother agreed to take Vaan, but because Brittany’s section was on lockdown, she was unable to make a phone call to confirm that her baby made it to his new home. Telling me not to cry is telling a fish not to swim, she wrote to Jace. Not having Vaan in my stomach is hard. I miss him so much. I don’t really know how to deal with any of this.

Many of the older women in jail had taken to Brittany, whom they treated like a daughter. Her case was high-profile locally, and most of the incarcerated women were sympathetic. They told her, and the guards agreed: Your story is so relatable, you’ll go home soon. Jace was similarly optimistic. The world is supporting us and our case is very unique, Jace wrote to Brittany. We can do this together baby.

Soon after Brittany gave birth, she was sitting in a common area, watching SpongeBob SquarePants, when another prisoner approached her.

“People are telling you that you’re gonna go home, right?”

“Yeah,” Brittany said. “I am gonna go home.”

“You’re not,” the woman said softly. She had been in and out of jail for years. “You’re very young, and I have been in your shoes,” she said. “This system is really fucked up.” She looked Brittany in the eyes: “You are gonna do time. I hate to see you get the slap in the face going in there expecting that you’ll be released, because that’s not what happens here.”

In that moment, Brittany knew the woman was right: “I was watching women who had done less getting more time,” she tells me. “And women who had done more doing life sentences.”

As years went on, Brittany would learn that her case was not uncommon. Louisiana faces what experts deem “a health and economic crisis.” A 2025 study showed that an estimated 1.9 million adults in Louisiana have experienced physical violence, 1.5 million have experienced sexual violence, and 1.8 million have experienced intimate partner violence in their lifetime.

Women’s prisons across the U.S., including those in Louisiana, are full of survivors like Brittany, with studies showing that up to 94 percent of those in women’s detention facilities experienced physical and sexual violence prior to incarceration. For my book, Unreasonable Women, I spent seven years investigating a related phenomenon: criminalized survival. In criminalized survival, people — usually women and girls — are not just abused before their incarceration, but are incarcerated because they reacted to the abuse, usually by trying to protect themselves or a loved one from violence, as Brittany had. The genesis of my work was a 2015 report called “The Sexual Abuse to Prison Pipeline,” which was updated in 2023. “The legal system punishes girls and women without meaningfully considering the context of their actions,” says Rebecca Epstein, the executive director of the Center on Gender Justice & Opportunity at Georgetown University, a co-author of the report. “They are victims of crime, they have experienced violence, and their abusers wield immense power. And when the survivor is a girl, there are even more vulnerabilities to consider. Many survivors shouldn’t be in prison at all.”

In the years after I first began reporting on the issue, I conducted a survey of 10,000 women in prison on murder or manslaughter charges across the U.S. and received more than 1,000 responses. Brittany’s was among them. I found that approximately 30 percent of my respondents were criminalized survivors. In Brittany’s survey response, she called Noce “my victim, though … I was his victim.” He had gotten, she wrote, a “slap on the wrist.” I felt treated unjustly and took it into my own hands yet my case shines light through the cracks of a failing system, she added.

Brittany’s case demonstrates a stark pattern I saw repeatedly: Criminalized survivors are not adequately protected by the state when they are victims, but as soon as they do wrong, the state reemerges as resource-rich and competent in order to punish them severely.

As a criminalized survivor, Brittany confronted a system that demands binary categorizations: Those who do harm are offenders; those who are harmed are victims. There is no space in the criminal legal system for a girl like Brittany, who had been raped many times over, who was let down by the people and institutions that were supposed to protect her, and who, left to her own devices, had reacted to those failures. Like many women in prisons and jails, Brittany also was not the primary perpetrator: A man she was involved with committed lethal violence, but she was nonetheless considered liable, too.

According to Brittany, the East Baton Rouge Parish DA’s office tried to dissemble her story of abuse — the very story they had used to convict Robbie years earlier. An ADA named Darwin Miller was assigned the case. Brittany remembers having 11 meetings with Miller. At the second meeting, she began to feel that he was trying to downplay or to negate the years of rape she endured. “I was like, ‘Oh I see what is happening; y’all want me, too.’” In one memo from the DA’s office, Miller and other ADAs observed that “Brittany is at a crossroad [sic], where all the lies and the BS from the past years need to end.”

I feel like they’re punishing me for being raped, Brittany wrote Jace from jail. Or punishing me for speaking up about it. I know one thing; if anything like that were to happen to me again, I wouldn’t say a word.

Brittany and Jace sat in jail, awaiting trial. Eventually, Brittany decided to cooperate with the state in their case against Jace. Maybe one of them could go home to their son. As part of her deal, she would plead guilty to manslaughter, sit down with a detective and tell him everything, work with the DA’s office, and testify against Jace. In return, she would get a sentence of between zero and 40 years. “She had very few options,” John Meixner, an assistant professor of law at the University of Georgia and sentencing expert, tells me. “This is the incredible bind defendants are in.” Faced with the threat of life without the possibility of parole, Brittany felt she had no choice but to agree to the open-ended plea offered by the DA’s office.

Brittany pled out on June 26, 2017. She believed that her cooperation, plus her mitigating circumstances, would lessen her sentence significantly. After all, a similar case was one of the most famous in Baton Rouge history. Brittany had never heard of Gary Plauche before Robbie’s killing, but after, it was referenced relentlessly by the press and the public. She learned that in the early 1980s, Plauche’s young son was sexually abused by his karate instructor, Jeffrey Paul Doucet, who then kidnapped him and took him to California. When Doucet was returned to Baton Rouge to face trial, Plauche ambushed and shot him in the head. Plauche was initially charged with second-degree murder, but then he was offered a plea deal: a much-reduced manslaughter charge, probation, and 300 hours of community service. Brittany, by contrast, had sprayed some cologne in Robbie’s face and punched him. The judge who would decide her sentence was Anthony Marabella, one of Plauche’s former defense attorneys.

In June 2017, Brittany met with Detective Scott Henning of the East Baton Rouge Sheriff’s Office. Henning, a tall, rangy man with a brown buzz cut, sat across a table from Brittany, diminutive in her prison greens. All she had to do, he said, was “give us the truth, no matter what that truth is.” These were the terms of her plea. Henning started at the beginning: her childhood, the rapes by Robbie. Brittany explained the ways in which Robbie bribed her: the toys and money in exchange for being more amenable to assault. In the interrogation video, Henning, who did not reply to repeated requests for comment, seems to interpret this as exploitation — on Brittany’s part. Though Robbie was nearly 40 and Brittany was nine, though he was her sole caretaker and father figure who had been molesting her for years, Henning recast Brittany’s case as a story of a young female manipulator: “So you’re able, at this point, you’re able to manipulate Robert to give you money in exchange for sex, other things, toys, food … and you use that to your advantage to get whatever it is you want.” “Yes,” Brittany answered. When she was a child, she had felt, she tells me now, “almost honored” to be allowed to act as a girlfriend figure to Robbie. At 19, talking to the detective, she still identified with that old self.

Brittany detailed her adolescence, telling Henning about the boys she dated. “You figure out you can use sex to get affection or anything else you would want,” he said, again characterizing her as calculating, as is captured on old police videotape of the encounter. Later, during the same interview, Henning implied she was self-serving: “So it was always seeking out something with Brittany in mind. It was always the number one goal to let Brittany benefit as most as you could, it was never about the other person, whoever they may be.” Brittany didn’t argue. She tried to be measured and calm when authorities were around. Near the conclusion of the interview, Brittany began to cry softly. When Henning left the room, she can be seen on camera breaking down.

Brittany still thought highly of Henning; he was relatively nice to her, which distinguished him from most men. After the interview, she recalled that he took her aside and told her that he believed her. “It was one of the few moments I really hung onto,” Brittany tells me. “He gave me that little bit of something.” But whatever one detective may or may not have felt about Brittany, the state of Louisiana was coming for her.

Last year, I acquired the DA’s file from Brittany’s case. Brittany’s medical records were included — from her first visit to the hospital after she reported the abuse to Maggie, back when she was 15. The medical professional had indicated that Brittany had no problems with her “gastrointestinal or genitourinary.” Nearby was a hand-scrawled note, presumably made by somebody in the DA’s office: No damage? No damage? When she went to the hospital, it had been years since Brittany had been abused, and in any case, such abuse does not always leave a physical mark. In the hospital records, there was also an area seemingly highlighted by someone at the DA’s office: No Known Injuries.

On Dec. 17, 2017, Brittany was taken to Marabella’s courtroom to testify against Jace. Among the first topics that ADA Miller addressed was the reliability of Brittany’s rape claims from five years earlier. When Jace was attacking Robbie, Miller said, Robbie never “admitted to the rape of Brittany Monk … did that ever happen?” “No, sir,” Brittany said. “He is staring death in the eye, and he still does not say, ‘I raped Brittany Monk,’” Miller continued, though there was never any suggestion, by Brittany or Jace, that they demanded Robbie admit to anything. Miller also referred to the years of abuse Brittany endured from preschool, repeatedly, as “y’all continued to engage in sexual acts,” even though Robbie had pleaded no contest to abusing Brittany, for which he had been charged and prosecuted by Miller’s colleagues. The DA’s office that had once sought a life sentence for Robbie was now willing to suggest that he may have been innocent all along. This issue was raised by Brittany’s attorneys in an unsuccessful motion to recuse the DA’s office from the case. They argued that Brittany once had victim status with the very same office that then turned around and prosecuted her.

It was important to Brittany to maintain her dignity on the stand. DA Hillar Moore himself, she remembered, sat in front of her and watched. “I could tell they were trying to intimidate me, make me mess up,” she says. “I tried not to cry. I tried to speak clearly.” After testifying, Brittany returned to jail, feeling broken and humiliated. She’d been used as a pawn, she believed. Jace was found guilty by a jury and given a life sentence without the possibility of parole.

In January 2018, Brittany was brought before Marabella for sentencing. She and her attorneys thought she might get 10 years. After all, she had so many mitigating circumstances: She was a pregnant juvenile abuse victim when the crime occurred; she’d been stripped of family support, and traumatized by her abuser’s lack of accountability. Plus, she had never intended for Robbie to die and was not the main perpetrator.

When offered the opportunity to make a statement, Brittany read from a letter she had written. She focused on her regret for hurting her half-sister Kamron, whom she hadn’t seen for years, but also expressed sympathy for Robbie’s family — and regret for the act that took him from their lives.

“Kamron did love her father,” she said. “She also loved me, and now she’s lost us both. I love her more than I can put in words … I do hope that the victim’s family is able to find peace as well as Jace’s family and my own family, and I hope that one day we can all move forward.”

When Brittany was done, Marabella began to speak. “This court has spent many, many hours agonizing over what is a just sentence in this case,” he said from the bench. He explained that Brittany — who had been trying to keep from crying in public and who felt intimidated by Moore — appeared “cold and emotionless on the stand.” He pointed out that Brittany “exhibited anger” about the fact that Robbie had served about two weeks in jail for molesting her multiple times a week for starting at the age of four. He repeatedly questioned if the rapes ever happened. “If true, what happened to Ms. Monk, it is horrible … if those things are true,” he said.

Marabella then sentenced Brittany to 35 years of hard labor.

“I think 35 years is an extremely long sentence that almost is never appropriate, especially for a manslaughter case like this one,” Meixner, the sentencing expert, says.

On March 1, 2018, Brittany was transferred to state prison. There, her instinct was to retreat from the world. She told Jace’s stepmother not to bring Vaan. She would cut herself off. But Jace’s stepmother refused. She brought the child to prison every other weekend, an act that Brittany later recognized as the greatest gift. Vaan would run his hands over her face; he loved her, the guards said. It wasn’t his fault. He still needed her. Brittany did not have the luxury of waking every day in the same house as her son, so she would have to learn to be a present mother from behind bars.

Over the years, Brittany began to take correspondence courses at Tulane, earning her bachelor’s degree in social sciences and her license in cosmetology. She learned to crochet. She read voraciously: romantasy series mostly, often about fairies. She had two romantic relationships with other prisoners. She listened to a lot of Wayne Dyer motivational speeches, to Mel Robbins and Oprah. She studied Buddhism and Eastern Medicine and learned how to do breath work.

Her means of coping in prison was to keep silent, as she had been accustomed to doing since childhood. She tried to stay under the radar, tried to never misbehave, to keep quiet. But at the 10-year mark, she crumbled: What had all her self-imposed rehabilitation done for her? She was 27, staring down another quarter century inside.

A year earlier, a Baton Rouge defense attorney named Jarrett Ambeau had heard about Brittany’s case. “This woman was unjustly punished by the court, so here I am,” he tells me. Ambeau offered to represent Brittany pro bono. His first step was to schedule a forensic psychologist to evaluate Brittany in prison. The psychologist’s evaluation would allow Ambeau to bring the case back to the DA for reconsideration.

“Two weeks after the psychologist was booked, the prison summarily denied the evaluation, citing some amorphous security concern,” Ambeau tells me. “We then filed a motion with the court to have the court order the jail to allow us to visit her. The court has an ex parte conversation with the warden and denies my motion.”

Brittany, who had begun to feel hopeful, deteriorated. She was put on a mixture of medications, including two that had an interaction. She had her first fight in prison. Brittany says that this year, after a decade of near-perfect behavior, she was thrown into solitary confinement, stripped of her job as cosmetology clerk, and locked in an enclosed 8-by-6 room with a bed and toilet. After keeping her emotions in check for so long, she felt a fury rise within her.

“With the people running this place, with the people that put me here, with myself even, for having been broken that way,” she tells me. “They want me to be quiet. No.”

I have known Brittany since 2022, soon after she sent me her survey response. I had always found her polite, formal, subdued, and hesitant to speak freely. But when she called me after her stint in solitary, she was different, suddenly far more direct and confident. She says the punishment was “really pivotal.” She had spent her life trying to hide her story, but now she thought her only shot at freedom lay in telling it — at length, whenever possible. “There is nothing worse than to have me sit here away from my child for 35 years,” she tells me. What more could they do to her?

“I’ve been a victim of rape, but I’ve also been a victim of a system.”

She used to be, Brittany adds, “a damsel in distress who needs saving … I don’t have that mindset now. More like: Save my own damn self.” She believes she has served enough time. “I’ve been a victim of rape, but I’ve also been a victim of a system. People minimize how much the system victimizes you and how many people are here because they were victims and they acted out because of that.”

The system continues to work against Brittany, now via the courts and the Department of Corrections. When I asked for an in-person visit with Brittany in prison, the DOC denied my request, without explanation, as well as requests by other media outlets. Meanwhile, after two denials by a judge, Ambeau was finally able to get permission for his evaluator to interview Brittany, but only virtually. On October 13, Brittany spoke to the forensic psychologist on a video call. She and Ambeau await the report. Ambeau is also considering employing a new Louisiana law, which went into effect on Aug. 1, requiring courts and the state’s Board of Pardons to account for a defendant’s history as a victim of domestic abuse, human trafficking, or sexual assault when sentencing or reviewing clemency applications.

Jace, for his part, successfully appealed his conviction in 2020, on account of his jury being split; nonunanimous juries were banned in the state in 2018. To avoid a second trial, he took a plea. Instead of serving life without the possibility of parole, he is now serving 35 years, like Brittany.

“Do I acknowledge that I was wrong?” Jace asks me. “Yes. I never shoulda been there, never shoulda went there.” He felt he deserved time, but believed that he and Brittany were harshly prosecuted and massively over-sentenced because the state had something extra to prove. “It was more or less: We didn’t do our job with Robbie, so we’re gonna make sure we do our job with them,” Jace says. The DA’s job, as Jace understood it, was not to do justice, but to secure a conviction. “Everybody got the wrong idea of what happened that night,” Jace tells me. He maintains that he was not trying to avenge Brittany, but to help her get “some sense of justice, some sense of closure, maybe peace of mind.” It didn’t work out that way. “But I had her best interests at heart,” he says.

Today, Brittany and Jace are no longer in contact. Each harbors frustrations and resentments. But they share a son. “I never speak ill of Jace,” Brittany tells me. “I do think we’re bonded for the rest of our lives.”

Over many months of reporting this article, I made repeated attempts to contact multiple state actors who had been involved in Brittany’s various cases: former ADA Leslie Ricard, who prosecuted Robbie; former ADA Darwin Miller, who prosecuted Brittany and Jace and now works for the Louisiana Attorney General’s office; Hillar Moore, the elected DA; Detective Scott Henning, who questioned Brittany and investigated the case; and Lindsay Blouin, who acted as Brittany’s public defender. None responded to my requests for comment. I could not locate retired judge Anthony Marabella, who handed Brittany her 35-year sentence.

“The judge believed it was vigilante justice, and that’s something we can’t have with 104 murders last year,” DA Moore told CBS affiliate WAFB in 2018. “How would you like this to happen 104 other times to your family member or someone’s family member who loses someone as important as this one is to them?”

But Kamron, Robbie’s closest family member, wants Brittany to be free. “I grieve my dad every day still and will for the rest of my life,” she tells me. “I just believe that people can change. I believe that people deserve the chance to grow and become something different from who they were at their worst. At the end of the day, I want my sister to come home. I want her to have the opportunity to experience life outside of prison and to have a future … she has a child to get back home to.”

“They are trying to cover this case up,” Brittany says. “Whether or not they grant me getting out of here or I have to stay and do the remainder of the 19 years I have … I am gonna tell my story…. The truth is gonna get out.”

Brittany would never get her innocence back, or the years she spent at Robbie’s trailer and inside state prison. But for the first time in her life, she felt worthy of something better, and wanted to fight for it.
