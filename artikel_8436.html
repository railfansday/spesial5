A Thru-Hiker Turned Her Trail Recordings Into an Interactive PCT Sound Map

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

When Anna “Rooster” Wozniewicz hiked the PCT in 2025, she wanted to create something that would outlast the hike itself: a record of the trail’s sounds, a snapshot of its changing ecosystems, and a way for people who might never get to hike the PCT to experience the trail for themselves.

Together with her friend and former business partner Haiden McGill, Wozniewicz turned hours of audio recordings taken along the PCT into Offbeat, an interactive sound map of the trail. The project features 74 recordings spanning nearly 1,800 miles from the Mexican border to Sky Lakes Wilderness in Oregon, where an overuse injury ended Wozniewicz’s hike early.

Anna “Rooster” Wozniewicz on the PCT. Photo: Anna Wozniewicz

McGill and Wozniewicz both have backgrounds in audio engineering. The pair met in college and founded a sound design company together at age 20 and ran it successfully for four years.

“When I got back from the PCT, all I had was an SD card full of audio files and an ambitious idea, but I knew there was exactly one person who could help me turn that idea into reality,” Wozniewicz told The Trek. That person was McGill.

“When Anna first mentioned she wanted to build an experience to showcase her recordings from the trail, I immediately saw the entire thing in my head,” McGill said of the Offbeat project. “The challenge was that I wanted the user to actually ‘hike’ the trail, not fly from place to place.” They set out to design a web experience that would feel both immersive and meditative, centering the sounds themselves.

Wozniewicz came off the trail with 137 separate audio recordings, and the pair have spent the last year turning those raw files into something more polished. Each of the 74 sounds that made it onto the Offbeat website is carefully edited into a seamless loop with no beginning or end, and a user scrolling through the sound map finds themselves meandering northward — indeed, much like a hiker would.

“Sound is subliminal, and we live in a very visual world. Throughout the build there was a constant temptation to add more visuals and UI elements to ‘enhance’ the story. This project was an exercise in restraint: keeping the listening experience calm and uncluttered. Instead of adding things, I used subtle camera movements, which I picked up from our filmmaking days, to draw listeners in and give the experience a meditative feel,” McGill said.

“I didn’t go into my hike with the intention to create Offbeat, but whenever I stumbled upon a cool sound, I got my recorder out and started recording,” Wozniewicz said. All she had with her was a cheap, lightweight Zoom H1essential recorder that she’d bought last-minute before her hike, but it was enough. “If you ask the UL bros, (it weighed) far too much, but in my opinion, it was surprisingly little.”

Her guiding principle, she said, was to capture sounds that would be hard to find in any other context — wind inside a hollow tree trunk, footsteps crunching up the icy ascent to Forester Pass, and an apocalyptic thunderstorm on Hat Creek Rim being some of her favorites. “It became kind of a game, to try and find the coolest, most unique sound I could each day.”

She aimed to capture natural sounds and, most of the time, exclude mechanical ones like traffic and airplanes. The hardest thing to capture? The silence. “Sometimes, the silence was overwhelming — no wind, no bugs, no rustling anywhere — this was the one sound (or lack thereof) that was impossible to record, that I feel isn’t represented in Offbeat, yet occurred pretty frequently on trail.”

Wozniewicz with her recording device. Photo: Anna Wozniewicz

Wozniewicz started to envision Offbeat about three months into the hike. “Hearing a specific sound can bring you back to a specific time, place, or memory. I’ve seen the PCT documented ad nauseam through images and video, but rarely have its sounds been captured, so it felt like a gap that I felt compelled to fill. My primary goal with Offbeat was to recreate the meditative experience of being in the wilderness.”

She hoped the project would evoke that feeling for others. “Getting to spend half a year just walking is an immense privilege, and many people will never get the chance to do something like that, so my hope is that Offbeat can bring the trail a little closer to a lot more people for whom a thru-hike isn’t possible.”

Navigating Sierra snowpack. Photo: Anna Wozniewicz

Offbeat is also intended to preserve an auditory record of the PCT’s diverse ecosystems, many of which are threatened by climate change. “Maps are a gift from the people who went before us, and I love that as a metaphor for continuing stewardship: you inherit the way, and you leave it better for the next person. In that sense, Offbeat is an archive we may soon look back on,” said McGill.

“Also — recognizing that I’m not even close to being the first (or last) person to hike the PCT corridor was important to me, which is why in the Audio Library view I’ve included the names of the Native peoples whose land each recording comes from,” Wozniewicz added.

Climbing Mt. Whitney. Photo: Anna Wozniewicz

Interestingly, Offbeat isn’t the only PCT sound library to come out this year. Field recordist Tom Kelly hiked the PCT in 2023 with over two pounds of recording equipment and just launched his own PCT sound map earlier this fall, featuring nearly 200 recordings up and down the trail. Like Wozniewicz and McGill, Kelly aimed to create a resource that would help people far from the PCT feel connected to the trail.

Wozniewicz and McGill could imagine doing a similar project on another long-distance trail someday. First, though, Wozniewicz wants to finish off the remaining miles of the PCT — and bring Offbeat’s audio pins as far north as the Canadian border. McGill said they hope to join for at least part of the remaining section.

“I’ve never felt more present in my life than on the PCT, and Offbeat is my way of packaging all those feelings up and sharing them with others,” Wozniewicz said.
