The Endowment Effect Makes You Overvalue What You Own – Think Like a Buyer

The endowment effect makes you value what you own above what a buyer would pay, so you overprice what you sell and keep things you don’t need. Base your price on what the same item actually sells for, and keep something only if you’d buy it today at today’s price.

In a 1990 study by Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch, and Richard Thaler, students given a coffee mug asked for a median $7.12 to sell it. Students without one would pay a median $2.87 for the same mug.

The sellers hadn’t paid anything for their mugs, and chance alone decided who got one. Owning a mug was the only thing that set them apart from the buyers.

That gap between owner and buyer is the endowment effect, and it costs you money when you decide whether to sell or keep what you own. The bread maker you priced at what it’s worth to you is still on the garage sale table at closing time, or still listed online a month later. A losing stock stays in your account and an unused subscription stays on your card, because selling or canceling feels like giving something up. Free trials and at-home try-ons put the product in your hands before you’ve decided to buy, so giving it up feels like a loss too.

To beat the effect, decide the way a buyer would. When you sell, set your price by what the same item has actually sold for. On eBay, search for the same model and look only at sold items in the same condition as yours. Ignore the asking prices on listings that haven’t sold, which other owners set the same way you would.

For anything you’re holding on to, ask whether you’d buy it today at today’s price. Sell the losing stock you wouldn’t buy at its current price, and cancel the subscription you wouldn’t sign up for today.

The day you start a free trial or an at-home try-on, put its end date on your calendar. Ask the same question a few days before that date, while you can still cancel or send the item back.
