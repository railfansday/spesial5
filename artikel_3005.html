Here's What the Average Man's Social Security Benefit Looks Like by Age

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Men's average retirement benefit runs $409.85 higher monthly than women's, a gap rooted in lifetime earnings with survivor benefit implications.

Men's average Social Security benefits run higher than women's at every age. This breakdown lays out the specific figures across the early retirement years based on the most recent data snapshot.

As of December 2025, the average retirement benefit among men is $2,282.28 per month, according to the Social Security Administration (SSA). The average for women is $1,872.43, which leaves a monthly gap of $409.85.

The SSA bases your benefit on an average of your wages across your 35 highest-earning years. These wages are adjusted for inflation and run through a formula designed to replace about 40% of your pre-retirement income. Most retirees need a much higher share of their prior pay to cover their costs.

These averages represent a snapshot of current beneficiaries, including those who claimed early and those who waited for higher payments. As of December 2025, the monthly averages show consistent differences between genders across the board.

The higher average for men reflects historical differences in lifetime earnings and years spent in the paid workforce. While individual work histories vary, these broader patterns shape the current benefit landscape across every age group.

A man who is 65 today is expected to live about 18 more years, while a woman is expected to live about 21 more years, according to the SSA. This means that men's Social Security benefits typically cover a larger proportion of their retirement costs than women's, who have to pay for more years of living expenses with smaller checks.

A 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) will take effect in 2027. The above figures don't yet reflect that increase, which will add about $91 a month to the $2,529.62 average for 70-year-old men. Full retirement age (FRA) ranges from 66 to 67, depending on your birth year, and it's 67 for everyone born in 1960 or later.

Married people have the option to claim a spousal benefit on their partner's work record while their partner is still alive and a survivor benefit after their partner's death. You only get these checks if they're worth more than your own retirement benefit.

Since women have historically had fewer years in the workforce and lower average earnings, they're more likely to claim spousal and survivor benefits, which means married men also need to think about how their claiming decision could affect their spouse.

Your partner cannot claim a spousal benefit until you are receiving your retirement checks, so your claiming age may affect theirs. Talk your options over with your spouse and make sure you're both on the same page about when each of you plans to sign up. If one of you wants to change things up down the road, keep the other in the loop and adjust your retirement plan accordingly.
