Same. And I think when stressed, it often leads to a racheting down of equity exposure in pursuit of a peace of mind that proves illusory when the next stress test comes along. Or even just when chatter about a possible upcoming stress test is common, which turns out to be a large majority of the time.

I personally think the better way to address what is sometimes called myopic loss aversion, based in actual behavioral research, is explained well in Page 20 of this document:

https://advisors.ubs.com/mediahandler/m ... y_2024.pdf

I view it as one of the fascinating quirks of this forum that many are hostile to any sort of mental "buckets", in contrast to a rigid "whole portfolio" approach, because buckets don't necessarily have a strong justification in pure financial mathematics . . . and at the same time many of the same individuals are happy to endorse determining your overall asset allocation within that whole portfolio based on speculation about how you might feel in various hypothetical market scenarios.

So assuming we are going to attempt to deal with behavioral issues through modifying our financial plans from the theoretical optimum, we might as well do it in a well-informed way. And as UBS (and others) have explained, you can actually use the mental accounting bias behind buckets to help counteract myopic loss aversion. Basically, let your different inner demons fight each other to a draw.

And I might note in most cases, this will lead to an overall asset allocation "glidepath" for retirement savings that most Bogleheads would recognize as within a standard reasonable range. So it isn't exactly revolutionary, but it could perhaps make that plan actually feel better in a serious negative market event, and therefore actually help prevent adverse de-risking.
