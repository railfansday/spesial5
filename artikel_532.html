Earth's core may be leaking a 4.5-billion-year-old secret

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Carbon is essential to every living thing on Earth, from microscopic organisms to humans. Yet scientists are still working to answer a fundamental question about our planet's history: Where did all this carbon originally come from?

A new study of volcanic rocks from Hawaii offers a surprising possible answer. Researchers have uncovered chemical evidence suggesting that much of Earth's carbon may have arrived aboard ancient meteorites during a particular stage of the planet's formation. Even more remarkably, the findings suggest that some of this carbon became trapped deep inside Earth's core before eventually making its way back to the surface.

The discovery centers on unusual carbon dioxide signatures preserved in volcanic rocks. Researchers believe these chemical fingerprints may reveal carbon that originated in Earth's core and traveled upward through the planet's interior.

"We think that we found evidence of carbon from Earth's core leaking into the core-mantle boundaries and traveling all the way to the surface," says lead author Yves Moussallam, an associate professor in Columbia University's Department of Earth and Environmental Sciences at Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory.

The findings were published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

How Carbon Could Escape From Earth's Core

To understand how carbon might travel from Earth's deepest interior to its surface, it helps to look at the planet's internal structure.

Beneath Earth's thin outer crust lies the mantle, a vast region of mostly solid rock extending approximately 1,800 miles downward. Below that is the outer core, a roughly 1,400-mile-thick layer of molten metal surrounding the extremely hot, dense, solid metallic inner core.

Scientists believe that most of Earth's carbon is stored in its core. However, directly examining this material is far beyond the reach of current technology, and researchers may never be able to obtain samples from such extreme depths.

Fortunately, Earth's volcanic activity offers another way to investigate what lies below.

Recent studies suggest that certain elements can escape from the outer core and enter the overlying mantle. From there, they may be transported toward the surface by enormous rising columns of hot rock known as mantle plumes.

These plumes begin near the boundary between the core and mantle. As they ascend, some of the rock partially melts, producing magma that can eventually erupt through volcanoes.

When that magma cools, crystals begin to grow. Occasionally, these crystals trap tiny droplets of molten material before dissolved gases have a chance to escape. The trapped droplets, known as undegassed melt inclusions, preserve valuable chemical information about the original magma.

"These melts are fossils of the magma," Moussallam says.

Because magma contains dissolved gases, including carbon dioxide, these microscopic pockets provide scientists with a rare opportunity to study carbon that may have traveled upward from Earth's core.

Hawaiian Volcanoes Reveal an Unusual Carbon Signature

Hawaii is particularly well suited for this kind of investigation. Previous research has established that its volcanoes receive magma associated with a deep mantle plume, potentially providing access to material originating near the boundary with Earth's core.

Moussallam and his colleagues examined volcanic rocks from eruptions at Kīlauea and the Tantalus Rift. Using a recently developed analytical technique, they measured carbon isotopes preserved inside undegassed melt inclusions.

Carbon isotopes are different forms of the same element that have slightly different masses. By comparing their relative abundance, scientists can identify chemical signatures that offer clues about where carbon came from and what processes it experienced.

The Tantalus Rift samples are thought to represent magma originating near the outer margins of the mantle plume. Earlier research established that typical mantle carbon has an isotopic signature between approximately -5 and -7 per mil, a measurement expressing small differences in isotope ratios. The researchers found that the Tantalus Rift samples closely matched this expected range.

The Kīlauea samples, however, told a very different story.

Magma beneath Kīlauea is believed to originate closer to the center of the mantle plume, where material may rise from near the core-mantle boundary. In these samples, the scientists detected carbon with a much heavier isotopic signature of approximately -1 per mil.

This unusual value has rarely been observed before and differs substantially from the signature normally associated with Earth's mantle.

"This signature is very, very distinct from the normal mantle," says Moussallam. "This means it must have been contaminated by another carbon source and that source is probably carbon from the core."

Although the team considers Earth's core the most likely source, Moussallam emphasizes that the evidence is not yet definitive. Additional measurements will be necessary to establish whether the carbon truly originated there.

Ancient Meteorites May Explain Where Earth's Carbon Came From

The unusual carbon signature also provided researchers with a possible clue about events that occurred more than 4.5 billion years ago, when Earth was still forming.

According to Moussallam, the isotopic composition of carbon in Earth's core should preserve "the signature of the building blocks of Earth when it was created."

To investigate that possibility, the team compared the distinctive Kīlauea measurements with carbon signatures found in ancient meteorites. They discovered similarities to a group of meteorites called carbonaceous chondrites.

Chondrites are among the oldest known materials in our solar system, dating back more than 4.5 billion years. They preserve clues about the primitive material from which planets formed.

"All of Earth is made of chondrites," Moussallam explains. "They're the building blocks of all the terrestrial planets."

Scientists generally distinguish between two broad categories of chondrites. Noncarbonaceous chondrites formed in the inner solar system, while carbonaceous chondrites, which contain more carbon, originated in the outer regions of the solar system.

Exactly how much each type contributed to Earth's formation has remained a subject of scientific debate.

The new findings support the possibility that carbonaceous chondrites made an especially important contribution during the later stages of Earth's development.

According to the researchers' proposed explanation, these carbon-rich meteorites struck the growing planet at a time when it had become better able to retain carbon delivered by incoming material. Some of that carbon may have accumulated deep within Earth, eventually becoming part of the planet's enormous internal carbon reservoir.

Over billions of years, carbon from that reservoir could have migrated upward through mantle plumes and emerged in volcanic eruptions.

If the interpretation is correct, some of the carbon essential to life today may have arrived from the outer solar system aboard meteorites during Earth's earliest history.

A Remarkable Journey From Space to Life on Earth

Despite the intriguing evidence, important questions remain unanswered. The researchers have not conclusively demonstrated that the unusual carbon came from Earth's core or established precisely how much of the planet's carbon was delivered by carbonaceous meteorites.

"We don't really know. We need more measurements," cautions Moussallam.

Nevertheless, the findings offer a fascinating possible connection between the formation of the solar system, the inaccessible depths of our planet, and the carbon that makes life possible.

They also raise the possibility that some of the carbon found in living organisms today has followed an extraordinary path. After arriving on Earth billions of years ago, it may have become incorporated into the planet's metallic core, moved slowly upward through the mantle, and eventually returned to the surface through volcanic eruptions.

"It's fun to think about. We're made of carbon, right? To think that some of this carbon might at some point have been linked to iron in the planet's core, made it all the way to the surface from out of a volcano, and is now part of the biosphere, is very interesting."

Rose-Koga of Institut des Sciences de la Terre d'Orléans; Allan Lerner of the US Geological Survey; Paul J. Wallace of the University of Oregon; and Natália Gauer.
