Here’s how high achievers self-sabotage their goals

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

We’re getting close to the end of the year, and if you’re an ambitious individual, you might have your new year’s goals on your mind. You might be thinking about what goals you want to commit to for the year ahead, and assessing your progress toward the goals that you set for yourself at the beginning of the year.

And if you find yourself not making as much progress as you would have liked, it might be tempting to give up on the goal altogether. After all, there isn’t that long to go until January 1. What’s the point of continuing to work on it if you’re not sure whether you can complete it 100% by the end of the year? 

If you find yourself with these kinds of thoughts in your head, you might just be suffering from a mindset that is preventing you from making major progress on your goals. 

Where “all-or-nothing” thinking comes from 

That mindset is an “all-or-nothing” approach—where you view situations and circumstances in absolute terms, with no nuance or middle ground. An all-or-nothing mindset can manifest in different ways. In goal-setting, it can look like reviewing your week, month, or year, and looking back at the times you faltered or struggled and telling yourself some versions of “I did terrible this week” or “I blew it,” says Allison Niebes-Davis, a licensed psychologist who runs a practice in Downers Grove, Illinois.  

Niebes-Davis explains that our brains are constantly making meaning of the things around us. It can do so quickly and efficiently as a survival tool. “The problem is that when the brain is doing that, it doesn’t always do that accurately. And so sometimes when it is trying to make meaning and draw conclusions, it doesn’t always draw the most accurate conclusions or the most nuanced conclusions,” she says. 

Many high-achievers might struggle with this mindset, Niebes-Davis explains, because they can have a dichotomous way of seeing themselves. “Sometimes, high-achievers can be very self-critical, and so they use a lot of words like failure, success, win, and lose.” Because of this, they “think that they will motivate themselves by seeing things in that all-or-nothing mindset,” she continues. 

How the mindset sabotages your goals

For high-achievers, this mindset might have worked in the past, or even in other areas of their lives. This is true for Erica Dhawan, author, keynote speaker, and advisor to CEOs and leaders. Dhawan has ticked off many goals she set for herself this year—from family to keynote speeches. But she found herself struggling with her weight goals. She noticed an all-or-nothing approach creeping in when she had growth and setbacks. “I do get into a wave of good health, but then when I lose it, I don’t fall back on progress. I’m known as an ambitious, high-achieving person who gets a lot done. I also wonder if all-or-nothing is perfectionism, where I do achieve the perfect goal in certain areas, and I don’t in others,” Dhawan reflects. 

Niebes-Davis stresses that while the all-or-nothing mindset can push some people toward success, it’s unlikely to be an approach that is healthy and sustainable in the long run. “Our thoughts and emotions are so directly connected,” she says. The path to achieving a goal is “rarely linear,” says Niebes-Davis, and “nobody’s getting 100%, 100% of the time.” If the only way you can describe something is success and failure, she says, you’ll end up seeing struggles and setbacks as failures. 

“That doesn’t motivate anyone to keep working or to keep trying,” she continues.  

The importance of language 

Both Niebes-Davis and Dhawan say that culture can also encourage this type of thinking. Social media, says Dhawan, gives us “picture perfect” images that can condition us into thinking anything less isn’t good enough. This has also created a culture where “we struggle so much in our current day with nuance. Things are either/or. They’re in extremes. We don’t teach kids from a young age about how to have more nuanced language.”

Niebes-Davis says that rather than the overly rigid language we often use when we fall short, it’s more accurate to say things like “I struggled around these three things. This month was hard. I made some choices that were not in line with my goals.”

A better approach  

Adopting this kind of language in your goal-setting approach, says Niebes-Davis, shifts your mindset toward prioritizing progress rather than an arbitrary outcome. She gives the example of someone who, like Dhawan, has a goal to be at a specific weight. Say that person’s goal is to lose ten pounds. “If you’re only going to give yourself credit or positive reinforcement when you lose ten pounds,” she says, “what happens at one pound? Two pounds? Three pounds?”

Being able to celebrate smaller progress, says Niebes-Davis, gives you the option to “look at things from a data-driven mindset.” That means tracking smaller things and paying attention to what moves the needle. That way, if you find yourself having a week where you ended up gaining a pound, you’re more likely to assess the situation accurately and, in turn, make the necessary changes. “Maybe you made some choices that weren’t super in line with your goals,” she says. You can then course-correct and get back on track rather than giving up on your goals altogether. 

Focusing on process, habits and progress 

Niebes-Davis also recommends that individuals focus on process rather than outcomes. Rather than just tracking outcomes, she recommends people ask themselves, “What are the processes that would contribute to movement around that goal?” She acknowledges that this is harder to do, and is something that we don’t see often. “When we look at people and their progress toward their goals, we tend to really see and celebrate that outcome-based approach.” 

This is a practice that Dhawan is committing to. Rather than giving up on her all-or-nothing weight goal, she’s committed to practicing healthy habits until the end of the year. That might mean eliminating alcohol and sugar, writing for 30 minutes, or meditating every morning. 

Moving away from the all-or-nothing mindset, says Niebes-Davis, is ultimately about training ourselves as humans. “We tend to give greater attention to the things that we struggle with, or the times we didn’t align with our goals. So a lot of being able to move the needle is to be very intentional about recognizing the successes.” 

She continues, “That takes work, and that takes intention, but that is going to put us in a much healthier place for goals, relationships, health and all of that.”
