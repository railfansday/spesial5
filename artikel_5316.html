OpenAI asked me to assess GPT-2 before its release. The stakes are even higher now

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

In 2019, I was at an artificial intelligence workshop at a D.C. think tank when OpenAI gave a presentation about a new language model they were developing called GPT-2. The presentation was intended as a show-and-tell, but I I started thinking beyond the technology to its potential implications for political influence and national security.

I followed up with OpenAI and raised the potential, following on the heels of the 2016 foreign election interference, that the technology could be used by nefarious actors for large-scale misinformation campaigns. OpenAI brought me on as an early academic collaborator to study whether a machine could write political content enough to fool the people it was aimed at, such as leaders or the public.

In retrospect, I was primed to see those risks because I had encountered a similar dynamic in my military career. As an Air Force officer, I had seen how emerging technologies could take on lives of their own. The Predator drone had gone from a surveillance platform to an armed aircraft that became a hammer for the U.S. military that made everything look like a nail. Obama said in reference to armed drones that it was “so easy to use them without thinking through all the ramifications.”

I wondered whether AI-generated content might follow a similar trajectory, developed as a research experiment in advancing artificial intelligence’s ability to understand and generate human language, but ultimately being captured for misuses, perhaps political manipulation or misinformation at scale.

Working with a colleague at OpenAI and another who would later join Anthropic, we researched that possibility and found, well before anyone knew about ChatGPT, that the technology could produce “news” indistinguishable from human-written content.

OpenAI’s staged release is the closest thing I have seen to what I argue for here: a lab asking people who study political consequences before it ships. The goal was not to stop development but to bring a different type of expertise into decisions about technology evaluation and anticipate its consequences. Some of the threats turned out differently than we anticipated. For example, the bigger risk now is not that people are necessarily fooled by AI content but that the proliferation of AI content means that people are more skeptical of everything they encounter. Nonetheless, the initial studies were not misguided and augured goalposts into place by which future evidence could be assessed, its own form of progress and preparedness.

The developers are certainly doing robust recruitment of economists, philosophers, and political scientists to study technological consequences in-house. But the companies still seem to look outward to “pace the frontier” when they are simultaneously at the vanguard of acceleration, one driven by enormous financial investments and stakes.

Indeed, AI development has raised the stakes of an old paradox: the people who build a technology are not necessarily the people best equipped to judge its consequences. AI companies are developing tools that are technically impressive, but are shaping everything from warfare to the global balance of power.

That some of the people building these systems assign a greater than 10 percent probability to existential annihilation—a number of uncertain provenance and questionable methodological foundation—raises the question of whether the capacity to develop sophisticated algorithms confers any particular expertise in assessing political or societal risks, let alone determining how much or what type of risk society should tolerate. The latter is a question of political judgment, rather than technological ingenuity.

The tension between scientific brilliance and political judgment is not a new problem.

At the 1954 security hearings on Robert Oppenheimer, Norris Bradbury, the physicist who succeeded him as director of Los Alamos, described the scientist’s “curiosity about facts, about systems, about life” and a “willingness to examine, to be convinced and without a priori convictions as to the rightness or wrongness.” Bradbury reveals a good deal about what motivates scientists but also intimates the blindspots, a paradox about some of the most accomplished innovators. They are capable of extraordinary technological breakthroughs but, in part perhaps because they do not have those a priori convictions, are not prepared for the political consequences, while also manifesting considerable confidence about those same consequences.

For example, Albert Einstein, whose scientific work helped lay the foundations for atomic weapons and who had urged President Roosevelt to support research that ultimately led to the Manhattan Project, believed that the nuclear age demanded a fundamental rethinking of national sovereignty. In a 1947 meeting with a U.S. government representative, Einstein argued that preventing atomic war required establishing a supranational world government to which nations would transfer their military power. The official who met with him was struck by the contrast between Einstein’s scientific genius and his political judgment, observing that international politics was “clearly not his métier.”

The episode foreshadowed Mark Zuckerberg decades later. He had envisioned Facebook as a vehicle for global connective tissue, but the same technology that connected people also enabled political polarization, mental health problems for teens, and the coordination of political violence. In 2017, he wrote a manifesto that promulgated a 21st century version of world government whose goal entailed “establishing a new process for citizens worldwide to participate in collective decision-making,” using the company’s technology of course.

For Elon Musk, the innovation was a Starlink satellite network that completely revolutionized communications and became indispensable for Ukraine’s war against Russia. But in 2022, Musk declined a Ukrainian request to use Starlink for attacks on Russia’s Black Sea Fleet, concerned about escalation. That the genius of satellites might carry over to geopolitics was an act of supreme confidence.

The common thread is not that these innovators necessarily made the wrong political decisions, but that their technological accomplishments gave them an outsized role in making decisions for which technical expertise offered no particular preparation. Qualities that would allow someone to make discoveries on the photoelectric effect that earn someone a Nobel Prize in physics are not necessarily the same as those dealing with social institutions and political interest.

Now, more than 100 years after Einstein’s Nobel-winning discoveries, the question of whether aptitude in one domain should provide legitimacy in another has become more urgent in an era of artificial intelligence. In the modern instance, a small group of technology leaders and companies are both building new technologies and making political judgments about risks. What do the earlier innovators say about the limits of technological expertise?

My recent book, Harnessing Disruption, reveals two patterns of technological innovation. One is that technological expertise does not translate into political judgment. The second is that even being able to anticipate political consequences does not fully equip them to control the technology.

Consider Anthropic, which split off from OpenAI in order to make AI safety the cornerstone of its platform, but then partnered with Palantir to provide decision support systems to the Pentagon. The military then used Claude for operations in both Venezuela and Iran, triggering a confrontation between the company and the government about control over military applications of AI. Anthropic anticipated catastrophic risks but not the more mundane risk of having limited influence over powerful government customers that minimized its capacity to dictate the terms of use.

Congress is now trying to force the issue: House Democratic committee leaders have asked five leading AI companies to report model misalignment incidents to Congress within 48 hours of detection, with a full accounting due Nov. 11. So far, the companies have demurred, and not surprisingly. At this point, the letters are functioning more as performative politics than a mobilized attempt to require disclosure. Lacking the votes and a developed legislative alternative, these members can try to nudge action but falls far short of Senator Dianne Feinstein’s admonition to Meta in 2017, “you bear this responsibility. You created these platforms, and now they’re being misused. And you have to be the ones who do something about it — or we will” that I argue in my book forced Meta’s hands on social media reform measures.

Another lesson comes from the first. Instead of reacting to political capture, innovators need to design these inevitabilities into the initial concept. In the Air Force, acquisitions officers such as myself were told to design-in important features early, which meant working with operators, policymakers, and lawyers to ensure that we were designing something that could not just fly but be politically and operationally usable.  In 1999, E-3 AWACS operators I worked with were telling stories about buying GPS receivers at Radio Shack because the military-issued equipment was bulky and cumbersome. Those receivers did not meet every military specification but were far easier to use, an example that stays with me as an illustration of what happens when engineers design systems that look good on paper but without talking to the people who actually use them downstream.

The same idea applies to new technologies. OpenAI’s staged release was a version of it. An engineer at a blockchain technology company whom I interviewed for my recent book explained why they didn’t invest in policy expertise early on. The competition to develop the technology was intense and if they didn’t build it first, someone else would. They could worry about the policy implications later. But a modest investment in understanding the political environment, drawing on historians, political scientists, and game theorists, might avoid far greater costs down the road.

The goal is not to restrain genius as a matter of principle, but to recognize its limits and broaden the aperture. AI companies have increasingly embraced red teaming that subjects models to adversarial testing and commendably publishing results, which is certainly more self-aware than the largely reactive experience of social media platforms, but they need to do more to look further down the game tree not just to see what the models can do but what the deployment might set in motion as second or third order effects. But those questions are difficult to answer when those asking the questions are also under pressure to ship the next model, and some of the third-party audits can face similar pressure if their access or funding hinge on the success of the company they’re examining.

As Peter Drucker, who spent decades studying the intersection of politics, economics, and organizational behavior, wrote, “the most serious mistakes are not being made as a result of wrong answers. The truly dangerous thing is asking the wrong questions.”
