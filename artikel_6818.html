Who Will Be the New Bond Villain? We Nominate These 5 Actors.

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Robert Pattinson has been confirmed as the new 007, but every Bond needs a worthy adversary.

As Alfred Hitchcock put it: “The better the villain, the better the picture.” And nowhere is this truer than for James Bond—a character whose movie is only as good as its baddie.

Jaws, Blofeld, Le Chiffre, Goldfinger—they are the daddies of all baddies; they put the “fun” in funeral, the “ooh-er” in evildoer. They all have a thing, too, because a good baddie always needs a thing. Jaws has the teeth, Le Chiffre the bleeding eye, Blofeld the needy cat, and Goldfinger, well, he’s a kinky old bastard who paints women head to toe in gold to feel like he’s bonking his own bullion.

Which brings me to the question tugging at every Bond fan’s cufflink. Now that Robert Pattinson has been confirmed as the new 007, he’ll need a gold-plated villain to go about “expecting” him.

So which actors would you like to see as the next Bond villain? We have some ideas.

Not famous enough, you say? Not got the chops for such a big-ticket role? Hasn’t earned it, has he? Pah, I’m here to say the actor-slash-musician has already played the best villain on television this year in MobLand’s Frankie Cooper. I’ve gushed about that performance on this website before, and I will again. He plays Cooper with the creeping, glassy-eyed energy of Jimmy Savile—all tracksuits and mirthful threats. But into that he stirs a dollop of Ben Kingsley’s Don “Yes you fucking will” Logan in Sexy Beast. There’s a light nod to Heath Ledger’s Joker there too. In all, it’s a monumental performance—laconically camp, cockney, impishly insane, and properly cackle-out-loud funny. For copyright reasons, I suppose we can’t expect a carbon copy of Frankie transplanted into Bond. But he offers the same vibe: a villain at once unsettling and amusing, which is the hardest trick in the Bond playbook.

There was something in the way he played Pennywise in the adaptation of Stephen King’s It that crawled under my skin like a hookworm. I think it’s the eyes. Bill Skarsgård has a mild lazy eye, which he uses in certain roles to create a disorienting swivel-eyed gaze. He used it for Pennywise. He’s a uniquely physical actor, too, well versed in roles that make children cry. He uses his body like a prop, twisting, deforming, looming, leering as the part demands. He played Count Orlok in Nosferatu, Eric Draven in The Crow and Marquis Vincent Bisset de Gramont in John Wick: Chapter 4. A small snag: he recently said he was “spent” playing bad guys. But he added that, if the right Bond villain landed on his desk, he’d be tempted out of baddie retirement for one last job. “[I] just want to go in and do one Mads Mikkelsen villain,” said the actor, referencing Mikkelsen’s character, Le Chiffre, in Casino Royale.

It’s time for a female lead villain. Sure, Bond has known plenty of miscreant women and seductresses, from Xenia Onatopp (GoldenEye) to Pussy Galore (Goldfinger). But few outright lead villains—hat tip to Lotte Lenya (Rosa Klebb in From Russia with Love) and Sophie Marceau (Elektra King in The World Is Not Enough). It’s a part for a proper actor, a doyenne of screen and stage. A dame, no less. Judi Dench played M across seven films as the one person Bond couldn’t charm, scold, or seduce his way past. Imagine that same withering, headmistress-like authority pointed the other way. Helen Mirren has the gravitas and the twinkle. She’s an Oscar winner who has played a queen, a gangster’s mum in the Fast & Furious films and, of course, the foul-mouthed matriarch Maeve Harrigan in MobLand. She would hardly break a sweat as a Bond villain.

Look at that face: the heavy brow, the gaunt cheeks, the dark, brooding eyes. It’s a face that looks like it was carved by candlelight—a mug made for lurking in the shadows. He’s got a bunch of baddies in his locker, from M. Bison in Street Fighter (2026) to Gareth, the Gemini Killer, in Dexter: Resurrection (2025). But perhaps his biggest ace in the hole is his turn as Piter De Vries in Denis Villeneuve’s Dune (2021). Villeneuve likes to work with actors he knows.

Oh, and the other thing: he really wants it. Dastmalchian explained why in a 2023 interview with /Film: “Trust me when I say that I can bring something to an enemy of 007, whether he or she or they be played in a way that no one has ever seen Bond brought to life before, which is, of course, the way that you guys always do it. I think there’s no one to push Bond to the limits of their capabilities the way that I could, and I would love to do it. So there’s my pitch to them. I can’t wait for them to read this.”

She’s already won an Oscar for playing a corporate villain in Michael Clayton, and she’s never met a prosthetic nose, fur collar or killer blow-dry she couldn’t weaponize... see Snowpiercer’s Minister Mason, Okja’s Lucy Mirando or the White Witch of Narnia—tick, tick, tick. She’s a chameleon with a stare that could freeze a martini.

More than that, Swinton is a master (mistress?) of the in-between, able to embrace and blur lines of gender, age, and species at the click of a slender finger. She possesses that rarest of powers: to change her physical presence, voice, and demeanor so completely as to render herself unrecognizable. Give her a lair, great hair and a long cigarette holder (maybe a Strawberry Ice vape—it’s 2026), and you have a thing of beauty and terror. Because if every great Bond villain needs a thing, Swinton’s is that she can be anything.
