DOJ lawyers ordered to swarm 'frivolous' 97,000-lawsuit wave from ICE detainees

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Campbell filed a federal lawsuit Monday seeking to block ICE from making civil immigration arrests inside state courthouses without a judicial warrant.

Attorney General Todd Blanche is mobilizing civil litigators across the Justice Department in an all-hands-on-deck push to fight tens of thousands of legal filings by detained migrants asking federal courts to review whether their imprisonment is lawful.

Blanche issued a three-page memo Tuesday calling on attorneys working outside of immigration issues to assist in the lawsuits described by Blanche as "frivolous" and an attempt to "thwart President Trump's promise to the American people to prioritize their safety and security."

The pre-Thanksgiving memo comes as the Justice Department has been hit with 97, 000 habeas corpus petition filings since January 2025, including close to 10,000 new petitions filed since October. Typically, the Justice Department faces only a few hundred habeas corpus cases a year.

As President Donald Trump has made mass deportations a centerpiece of his second administration, with Department of Homeland Security figures showing nearly 1.2 million illegal immigrants removed to date, the Justice Department has seen a wave of petitions.

The surge in lawsuits comes after the Trump administration changed its detention policy to deny bond hearings to migrants arrested inside the U.S. — even those who have lived in the country for years. Without access to bond hearings before immigration judges, detainees have turned directly to federal district courts to challenge their confinement.

In a post on X sharing the memo, Blanche accused judges who have granted detained immigrants' habeas corpus petitions of being "corrupt" and seeking to "release criminal aliens back into communities and avoid deportation."

"Many of the Department's U.S. Attorneys' Offices have been overwhelmed by the influx of petitions and impracticable procedures set by the courts," Blanche wrote. "That is why it is imperative that all Department attorneys who litigate civil matters support prosecutors who have shouldered the burden of habeas petitions for nearly two years."

Blanche's memo calls on attorneys from four civil litigation divisions — Antitrust, Civil Rights, Civil, and Environment and Natural Resources — as well as the U.S. Trustees Program and senior leadership offices, including those of the attorney general, deputy attorney general and associate attorney general, to assist. Within the next 30 days, leadership from the respective divisions must submit an "aggressive appellate strategy" for tackling the habeas corpus claims and deploy attorneys to high-volume districts. The memo does not explicitly state how many of the department's roughly 2,000 civil litigators will be utilized.

Blanche acknowledged that divisions are already carrying "a significant workload in light of personnel reduction."

"I also know that many attorneys have already volunteered or offered support in litigating habeas matters," Blanche wrote. "By ensuring that attorneys are assigned no more habeas matters than each attorney can adequately and zealously defend, combined with providing jurisdiction-specific resources, I hope to mitigate any significant disruption to attorneys' current dockets."

Blanche's memo drew criticism from immigration attorneys, including Reza Mazaheri, who called the attorney general's characterization of judges as "corrupt" "slander."

"If I walk into a courtroom and accuse a judge I disagree with of corruption, I would undoubtedly face disciplinary action," Mazaheri wrote on X. "In the public service sphere, the word 'corruption' has a distinct meaning."

Meanwhile, Bridget Pranzatelli, a staff attorney at the National Immigration Project, told Newsweek that the influx of habeas corpus petitions is the result of a change in how the Trump administration interprets bond hearing statutes.

"Many of them would have gotten a bond hearing before this administration suddenly changed its interpretation of a decades-old law to fill beds that private prison companies get paid for," Pranzatelli said in a statement.

The memo comes as the U.S. Supreme Court prepares to hear arguments in early 2027 in Rhoney v. Barbosa da Cunha, a high-stakes challenge to the administration's mandatory detention policy. In October, the justices agreed to review a second U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals ruling that struck down ICE's policy of denying bond hearings to illegal immigrants who have lived in the U.S. for years. The court's decision — expected by late June or early July 2027 — will resolve a split among federal appeals courts and determine the fate of tens of thousands of pending habeas petitions.
