Why Most Enterprise AI Spending Automates The Wrong Part

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Co-Founder of Superlinked, building enterprise-grade open-source inference for production-scale Agentic AI search and document processing.

​After Superlinked narrowed its focus to a single open-source product, I had to rebuild our go-to-market engine with far fewer people. My first move was an obvious one these days: I pointed Claude at our existing outbound process and asked it to write more emails to more people.

It worked, in a way. We did send many more messages, but the total number of replies barely moved. We made the easy part of the process faster. The hard part—knowing who to contact and when—hadn’t changed at all.

Organizational inertia is real, and it’s hard to let go of a well-established and familiar process, but we had to. What follows is how we redesigned the process after the volume experiment failed.​​​

​Companies expect to roughly double AI spending this year to about 1.7% of revenue, according to BCG’s January 2026 survey of 2,360 executives. Yet, in PwC’s “2026 Global CEO” survey, only 12% of CEOs said that “AI has delivered both cost and revenue benefits,” and 56% said they’d “seen no significant financial benefit” so far. And McKinsey analysts reported in April 2026 (registration required) that 60% of respondents to its global survey “still have not seen enterprise-wide EBIT impact from their AI programs.​”​

From both sides of the table (as an operator today and a management consultant previously), I think most of this money buys speed on individual tasks. However, speed on one step rarely changes the outputs of the whole process. The bottleneck just moves somewhere else, and the faster step just piles work up in front of it.​

​When redesigning our outbound process, I focused on the desired outcome rather than which tasks AI could automate. The goal wasn’t to increase email volume but to reach engineers experiencing the specific problems we address, at the right moment.

That reframing exposed a constraint that a small team couldn’t remove. Many of the best buying signals were public: engineers describing their problems in GitHub issues on open-source projects adjacent to ours. Reading thousands of those threads every week wasn’t realistic for a human team, but for an AI agent, it’s routine.

The new process looks nothing like the old one. Agents watch for specific pain signals, match each person to a professional profile and reach out to them with a message tailored to their pain point. An ML engineer fighting memory crashes receives something different from a CTO worried about infrastructure risk. The old process started from a purchased list. The new one starts from evidence of a problem.

The redesign was more successful than I expected. Each response and even each ignore helps us identify which signals indicate genuine interest, improving targeting over time. For example, we initially targeted everyone who starred a relevant repository, but learned that stars indicated curiosity rather than usage. Those who filed issues were more responsive, so we shifted our focus accordingly.

​Process redesign doesn’t mean removing people altogether. It means being deliberate about where they bring most value. My first test is simple: How costly is a mistake, and can it be undone?

Scanning and scoring signals is cheap to get wrong and easy to reverse, so agents run it unsupervised. A first message to a senior leader at a target account is expensive to get wrong, so a person signs off on each one.

My second test often gets less attention than it should. How much effort does it take to check the AI’s work? If verifying an output costs more than producing it by hand, automating that step is a net loss.

Before a workflow goes live, I’m looking to answer four questions. How can the AI agent know it’s unsure? Who does it hand off to? How can we stop or reverse an action? How does a correction feed back into the process so the same mistake doesn’t repeat?

Early on, our pipeline reached out to the people most active in those issue threads. Several turned out to be the project’s own maintainers (often, direct competitors), answering questions rather than asking them. They were the last people we wanted to pitch to.

That incident taught us more about the design than weeks of smooth runs: The system couldn’t tell someone asking for help from the person offering it.

​If your AI investment isn’t (yet) showing in your P&L, avoid purchasing additional tools. Instead, select a critical process and clearly define its intended outcome. Then, redesign the process based on current AI capabilities, rather than sticking to the workflows that you inherited.

Expect the first redesign to be wrong, and keep human supervision where the consequences are significant. Importantly, treat their feedback as a design input rather than a bug to solve. Track how long it takes a person to check the agent’s work. If this time is trending down, the redesign is working.​

The biggest gains I’ve seen came from teams willing to throw the old process away. A faster version of last year’s workflow often produces last year’s results.​
