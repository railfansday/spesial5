Choosing a Gravel Bike? These 12 Editor-Tested Models Deliver the Tire Clearance, Geometry, and Value That Matter

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

From bikepacking to gravel racing, these tested bikes cover every kind of dirt-road ride.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

Instead of riding busy, congested city and suburban roads, many cyclists now head to lightly traveled gravel and unimproved roads. Gravel riding is a great way to see beautiful fall foliage, enjoy the fresh air, and avoid traffic. Most brands sell at least one gravel model; some offer multiple gravel platforms or bikes designed for particular gravel-riding niches. Whether you need a new bicycle for a big gravel fondo or race, a versatile bike for multi-surface rides, or a rig for bikepacking adventures, there’s a gravel bike for you.

The term “gravel riding” is very broad and doesn’t necessarily mean riding on gravel surfaces the entire ride. Gravel rides can include anything from hard-packed dirt roads (which you can often ride on a traditional road bike) to singletrack trails (typically associated with mountain bikes). Gravel riding also includes almost any other type of unimproved roadway; forest access roads, farm roads, jeep roads, rail trails, and canal paths are all commonly lumped under the gravel umbrella. And many of the best gravel routes and events incorporate a few of these surfaces.

Because they get ridden in such varied terrain, gravel bikes come in many shapes and sizes. They span the gamut of aero-optimized bikes that look similar to WorldTour road bikes to full suspension models that resemble mountain bikes with dropbars—But most gravel bikes fall somewhere in between these polar extremes. Most gravel bikes share a few key traits: Generous tire clearance, a more upright riding position, and geometry that prioritizes stability and confidence on rough surfaces.

Buying Tip: Don’t pass on a great gravel bike because you don’t like its tires. Focus on a bike’s tire clearance rather than the stock rubber’s tread or width. Brands usually equip gravel bikes with tires that work well in average conditions. If those tires don’t work for you, it’s a simple fix. Upgrading to tires that better suit your needs is often the best—and easiest—first change you can make, and it usually shaves weight.

The Canyon Grizl is a great all-arounder. It’s a gravel bike built for all sorts of adventures, whether it’s a casual after-work gravel spin, commuting around town, or a multi-day bikepacking trip. The SRAM Apex XPLR 1x12 drivetrain shifts quickly and has a wide gear range. The Grizl’s carbon frame also helps dampen the bumps on rougher roads and trails.

Since we tested the bike a few seasons ago, platform updates have improved its capability and appeal. The Grizl now features clearance for wider tires (up to 56mm), internal downtube storage, and an easily sourced UDH hanger. If this model doesn't speak to you, Canyon offers several Grizl options priced from $1,899 to $4,699.

If you don’t have thousands of dollars to spend but need a bike for riding actual gravel roads, your choices might seem limited. However, if you dig into brands’ websites and look for budget picks, you will find a few gems. The $1,300 Salsa Journeyer Essa is a good example; it stands out for its robust parts, wide tires, and size range.

Unlike some cheaper gravel bikes you may find on Amazon, the Journeyer’s aluminum frame comes in six sizes to fit riders from 4’8” to 6’5”. Additionally, it’s available through REI. This way, you’ll have warranty support if anything goes awry, and the bike is professionally assembled (if you choose in-store pickup).

For components, Salsa equips this Journeyer model with Shimano’s 1x8-speed Essa group (including Shimano mechanical disc brakes)—the parts aren’t fancy, but they’re robust. The only negative point of the Journeyer Essa is its standard QR dropouts, which make upgrading to lighter-weight or tubeless wheels slightly more difficult.

The Topstone 1 is Cannondale’s top-tier aluminum gravel bike. Designed for versatility and adventure, it’s equally at home on pavement or dirt, with a utilitarian setup that includes mounts for racks, bags, fenders, and extra bottles—perfect for long-haul rides. But don’t mistake utilitarian for boring; the Topstone still feels lively and efficient on the road, making it a great choice for days when your rides lean more toward pavement.

This model comes with 37mm tires, but the frame clears up to 45mm rubber. This allows you to choose tires for the terrain and rides you take. The Shimano GRX 2x11 drivetrain is another standout, offering a wide gear range for steep, rough climbs while keeping gear jumps tight—something you’ll appreciate when riding extended stretches of pavement.

Salsa practically invented the modern gravel bike with its Warbird, and the Minnesota-based brand remains as relevant as ever. Its recently launched Flyway C is a lightweight and responsive gravel bike for gravel racing or all-day rides. It features generous tire clearance (up to 57mm), a UDH dropout (for easy replacement or upgrades), and revised geometry (long reach and short stem) for improved control on rough terrain.

Several Flyway configurations are available (I tested the Force AXS XPLR model), and I think the Apex Eagle AXS model sits right in the sweet spot for many riders. As the name suggests, Salsa outfits this bike with a combination of SRAM’s Apex AXS and Eagle X1 AXS 1x electronic drivetrains to provide a superwide gear range. In addition to the great components, Salsa also equips this model with a flared carbon bar and a carbon seatpost. It’s an all-around great package for anyone looking to up their gravel game this season.

Trek’s Checkpoint ALR 5 is versatile, well-rounded, and a great pick for riders new to gravel cycling. In his review, Chabanov says, “The value sweet spot: modern geometry and feature set borrowed from the [Checkpoint] SL, delivered in a durable alloy package with smart mounts and standards—at a starting price that leaves room in the budget for meaningful upgrades. Because there are no proprietary parts, upgrading and swapping things like the handlebar, stem, and seatpost is super easy.”

Overall, the Checkpoint ALR gets far more right than it gets wrong. Sure, you can nitpick the stock wheels and tires—or wish for the carbon model’s downtube storage and IsoSpeed—but those trade-offs help keep the price in check while preserving what matters. This bike has confident handling, generous tire room, real-world durability, and an upgrade-friendly platform. The result is a thoroughly well-thought-out aluminum gravel bike that feels ready for anything from weeknight dirt spins to loaded weekend overnighters, or even that gravel race that’s on your bucket list.

If you’re a gravel racer on a budget, or simply like going fast and don’t have the money for bikes like the Specialized S-Works Crux or Colnago G5-X, Canyon’s new Grail should be on your short list. As my colleague Matt Phillips described in his review of the revised platform, “And “fast” was the word that stuck with me as I rode the Grail across singletrack, dirt roads, and paved roads. It feels fast. It made me feel like I was fast.”

Wide tire clearance is one of the latest Grail’s upgrade highlights. At 57mm, the frame and fork can fit the widest gravel tires and even 29 x 2.25-inch mountain bike rubber for rocky terrain or singletrack (or if you want a super comfortable feel). The entry-tier Grail CF SL models use a heavier carbon frame than the Grail CFR and CF SLX versions, but only 140 grams more than the high-end CFR bike (about the weight of a standard butyl inner tube). All models feature a carbon cockpit, seatpost, and wheels.

The Grail CF 7 Speed is a great bang-for-the-buck gravel racer. Canyon equips it with SRAM’s excellent Rival XPLR AXS 1x13 electronic shifting gravel groupset. Although Canyon doesn’t ship this model with a power meter, you can easily upgrade to a Rival power crank for under $200.

You’re not buying this bike for its tire clearance, gear-hauling options, or aerodynamics; plenty of other gravel bikes do those things better than the G5-X. You buy this bike because it’s a Colnago.

On fast gravel roads, double track, and even technical singletrack, Colnago’s superb geometry, fit, and ride comfort won me over. The G5-X feels super composed and planted when descending and over choppy gravel road sections. The bike is precise and refined—pick your line and take it. On climbs and rolling terrain, the G5-X is comfortable and efficient, and responds immediately to bursts of power.

Overall, Colnago’s latest bike is a beautifully fast gravel racer with sublime handling. However, while the G5-X has more tire clearance (53mm) than the brand’s previous gravel offerings, it falls a few millimeters shy of the clearance found on some new gravel models. This will matter to some, but many riders will seek out the G5-X for the name on the downtube and Colnago’s cycling legacy.

Inspired by the Tour Divide—America’s longest mountain bike race—this bike carries more and handles a wider range of terrain than almost any other gravel bike. It’s loaded with mounts for racks, camping gear, bike accessories, and water bottles.

Although it’s intended for burly rides, the Cutthroat features a carbon frame and fork, which helps to reduce weight. And Salsa’s Class 5 Vibration Reduction System absorbs uneven terrain for a more comfortable ride. The Cutthroat C GRX model also includes a Shimano GRX 1x12 mechanical, wide-range drivetrain and a dropper seatpost.

With its steel frame, bar-end shifters, and triple-chainring crankset, the Masi Giramondo is a throwback to a bygone era of bicycle design and construction. While the bike’s aesthetic and features might not appeal to athletes chasing fast finishing times in 200-mile gravel races, it’s perfect for riders who want to hit the gravel, dirt, or paved roads loaded down with gear for touring adventures.

Unlike most bikes sold for touring or bikepacking, the Giramondo comes stock with a front and rear rack. This means you won’t have to add them to your bike-buying budget. To make it slightly easier to pedal your fully laden bike up hills, Masi equips this model with a triple crankset. While triples aren’t as trendy as 1x drivetrains, they offer an extremely wide gear range: Its lowest gear is close to many mountain bikes, and the highest gear rivals many gravel race bikes.

While State labels this model “All-Road,” it’s very much a modern gravel bike. Starting at $2,400 for a complete bike, or $1,400 for the frameset, the All-Road Carbon checks all of the boxes without totally draining your bank account. It accommodates up to 2.2-inch tires (complete builds ship with 50mm Vittoria T50s), is UDH-compatible, and takes a T47 bottom bracket. The All-Road Carbon also features internal storage in the frame’s downtube with a Fidlock magnetic latch.

The Carbon All-Road’s frame isn’t super aero-shaped, and it doesn’t use funky proprietary parts—and that’s a good thing. This bike is made for riders who prioritize durability and functionality over flashy graphics, pro teams, and marketing hype. The All-Road’s geometry falls on the “race-inspired” end of the spectrum, compared with the more upright feel of adventure-style gravel bikes. The bike carries speed on hardpack dirt and gravel and feels adequately stiff when sprinting and out of corners. The ride quality is a little harsher than premium gravel offerings, but a complete Carbon All-Road costs less than most big-name brands’ frames.

The all-new Specialized Crux has shed its cyclocross roots and turned into a modern gravel racer. In his review of the fifth-generation platform, Test Editor Dan Chabanov says, “It is built around the demands of professional gravel racers, and around the idea that going fast off-road now means balancing low weight, aero efficiency, tire clearance, and stability rather than just chasing the lightest possible frame.” The result is an excellent gravel bike that, while optimized for racers, anyone who loves going fast can enjoy.

Senior Test Editor Matt Phillips rode the Crux 5 for several days on the gravel roads in Durango, Colorado, and walked away very impressed by the updated bike. “The Crux 5 leans more toward stability and predictability,” he said. “It’s fast, yes. Very fast. But it’s also composed. It lets you settle in. It doesn’t feel like it’s trying to prove something every second.”

That balance is a big part of what makes the Crux great for non-racers. “A lot of us don’t need a gravel bike that only makes sense at 300 watts,” Phillips said. “We need a bike that feels sharp when you want to go hard, but isn’t a pain in the ass the rest of the time.”

Of the five models in the Crux 5 lineup, the Crux 5 Comp stands out for its performance-to-price ratio. It features a slightly heavier carbon frame than the high-end S-Works variants, but it has the same great geometry, tire clearance, and aerodynamics. Specialized equips the Comp model with SRAM’s excellent Rival AXS XPLR 1x brakes and drivetrain, and the bike rolls on 26mm-wide inner diameter alloy wheels wrapped in 45mm-wide Pathfinder tubeless tires.

The bike used by two-time gravel World Champion Marianne Vos (🐐), the Áspero-5 is what happens when Cervélo combines aerodynamic principles of the Paris-Roubaix-winning S5 with a gravel race bike. This bike is eye-wateringly fast on smoother gravel and dirt roads. The Áspero-5 is fast enough that many riders will want to use it on paved roads too.

Much of this speed advantage comes from Cervélo’s aerodynamic tuning of the frame, fork, and bar; the 42mm-wide Vittoria Corsa Pro Control TLR rubber enhances it. An excellent road tire for broken pavement in narrow sizes, the Pro Control really shines in the 700x42c size on dirt and gravel surfaces.

With so many types and sub-niches of gravel bikes, it can seem overwhelming to pick the right one. My first advice: start by honestly evaluating the terrain you ride, then consider how you like to ride it. From there, narrow your choices to the bikes that meet your budget, personal tastes, and other factors.

Racing-oriented bikes typically feature an aggressive fit and handling with fewer mounts for bags or extra water bottles. At the other end of the spectrum are bikes built for long-distance, multi-day adventures, equipped with all the mounts you could want for gear and accessories. In between, there’s a wide range of subtle variations that can make a big difference.
