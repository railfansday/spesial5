When Setting Boundaries Backfires

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Lisa was at the end of her tether. The demands of her life had started to weigh on her so heavily that she felt crushed under the burden of it all. Lisa came to me because she had difficulty getting out of bed in the morning; she felt numb, flat, and low most days, and nothing in her life gave her joy anymore.

She had three kids and had, through sheer grit and self-abandonment, built a successful business that allowed her to work from home, while her husband worked a highly demanding city job. She was responsible for all her kids’ activities, school runs, the house, and everything that came with it. When her mother became ill, and Lisa had to add regular hospital visits to her to-do list, something in her broke. She was done.

Lisa was suffering from burnout. She had been raised to be the “good daughter,” “good mother,” and “good wife,” and any departure from those self-assigned roles felt like a betrayal to the life she had built. In therapy, we started to work on Lisa just noticing her own feelings and needs, but it was difficult for her to stay with herself. Any stretch she made to step out of one of her roles, an all-mighty guilt force would pull her back in. If she looked after herself, her kids might suffer, her mum might be alone, her husband might feel more overwhelmed. Lisa felt stuck, and she was.

She got there in the end, but only because the alternative now felt untenable. She had been barely sleeping for months, and her body was under constant tension that she couldn’t switch off from anymore. She let go of bits of her to-do list; she asked her sister to check in on her mum and her husband to drop the kids off a few days a week. She started to create boundaries around her own capacity and communicated them to the family system around her. She felt elated. But neither of us was fully prepared for what happened next.

When Lisa’s husband realized that she was asking for permanent changes to their daily routine, he started picking fights, accusing her of being selfish and putting too much pressure on him. He invalidated her feelings and reminded her how much time off she had compared to him. Lisa’s children rebelled, and her sister became distant and stopped taking Lisa’s calls.

Lisa was devastated and felt completely unseen. She was stuck between her new and old self, unable to move forward in either direction. What was happening to her family? Why could they not understand what she was going through?

Families, according to Bowen’s family system theory, are deeply interconnected. If one person changes, the whole system has to change. Lisa setting boundaries for the first time triggered her family to push back because the easiest way for the whole system to regain equilibrium would be for Lisa to return to her overfunctioning self.

Lisa’s systems had grown around the role she had been playing in them. She had taken on most of the childcare responsibilities, and her husband’s and her children’s expectations were built around that. With Lisa playing her part, the system was in balance, seemingly. When Lisa changed the rules, the system tumbled out of kilter.

Bowen suggested that differentiation of the self in an emotionally interconnected system goes through several stages—the act of setting the boundary, the system pushing back, and the boundary having to be maintained in the face of that pushback so that the system can adapt. Bowen essentially postulated that, in a system under high pressure or anxiety, you cannot expect boundary setting to go smoothly. Pushback is a predictable step in achieving differentiation and also what makes “saying no” so unbelievably hard.

Most of Lisa’s therapy sessions from that point forward revolved around understanding whether Lisa wanted to continue in her marriage. Lisa’s guilt had kept her going for years. It had protected her from the very real possibility of a huge relational rupture with her partner and her family. But now, if her husband couldn’t see how much she was suffering, how could she be with him?

Bowen’s theory clues us in as to why change in Lisa’s family was so difficult, and it also reminds us that things are rarely as black and white as they seem at first glance. Lisa was self-employed, and setting boundaries for her had less impact on her job but saved her from burnout. Her husband’s boss, however, noticed him being late a few mornings and had a chat with him about his commitment to his role. Lisa’s husband suddenly felt helpless, and his anxiety became overwhelming. He blamed Lisa without thinking and pushed her further away.

When Lisa changed the rules, she and her husband had to re-evaluate their lives in fundamental ways. Lisa’s husband had been working in a job he hated for years because of the financial obligations of having three children. When Lisa and her husband were honest with each other, they realized that Lisa’s burnout had brought to light fissures in their family system that had been there for a long time. It took a lot of effort and honesty for them to rebuild a life that took both of their needs into account.

Other times, relationships will fall apart when one person steps out of their assigned role, and we have to be honest about how interconnected and dependent on each other we are as human beings. Boundary setting is not always as easy as our quick-fix culture would like us to believe. Saying no when your guilt has been keeping you overfunctioning for decades is empowering and freeing, but it will likely mean altering the relationships in your life, sometimes drastically.

If you are finding it hard to set boundaries, remind yourself that this is not meant to be easy. Seek professional help and always take small steps to give the systems around you and yourself a chance to adapt. True change is hard; don’t try to do it on your own.
