AI coding agents used firms’ own code under 60% of the time, study finds

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

DesignVerse’s AI coding drift report tested agents on 15 legacy codebases in sectors including banking, aviation and defence. One client freed 5,600 engineering hours a year, worth $882,000, by giving them company context.

Andrei Manolache, CEO and co-founder of DesignVerse.

AI coding agents without company context used an organisation’s own approved code in fewer than six tasks in ten. That is the main finding of new research from DesignVerse, a Bucharest-based enterprise AI startup. The rest of the time, the agents invented components or swapped in public ones. Engineers then had to find and fix them by hand.

The AI Coding Drift Report, published today, tested agents on 15 private enterprise codebases in aviation, banking, defence, software services and consultancy. Each system is 15 to 20 years old and has more than a million lines of code. A team of about 20 engineers looks after each one. Most are written in Ada, C++ or COBOL.

The report compared two of the most widely used coding agents, anonymised as Agent A and Agent B, with DesignVerse’s own context-engineered system. The two agents ran in their standard setup with full repository access, but no custom rules or extra company context, Andrei Manolache, DesignVerse’s CEO and co-founder, told TNW. That is how most teams use them, he said.

DesignVerse’s system ran on its own model. Before each call, it pulled the relevant parts of the client’s code and engineering and business documents.

“So this isn’t a comparison of the same model with and without context. It’s a comparison between the typical coding-agent setup available to engineering teams today and a context-engineered alternative,” Manolache said.

The unmanaged agents used the organisation’s own internal software components 55% to 59% of the time. DesignVerse’s system did so 92% of the time. A task failed if the agent invented an interface or library, or picked a public one when an internal one existed.

Given the same task repeatedly, the unmanaged agents gave the same answer 61% to 69% of the time. DesignVerse’s system did so 97% of the time. There were about ten tasks per codebase, and up to 40, from small refactors and unit tests to changes across several systems. Each was the kind of change that could take an engineer around 100 hours, Manolache said.

Without a context layer, agents read a median of ten tokens for every token of accepted code. On one client’s legacy system, measured outside the main benchmark, the ratio reached 150 to one.

DesignVerse’s team and the clients’ own engineers scored the results together, against the rubric published in the report. Every setup got the same tasks, prompts, tool access, time limits and grading. The one difference was the context layer.

“It’s a fair challenge. We sell the system, so we have an obvious interest in the outcome. That’s precisely why we made the methodology public and reproducible. We don’t think anyone should have to take our word for it,” Manolache said.

The 15 organisations were already working with or evaluating DesignVerse, which the report discloses. That gave it access to private, million-line systems missing from public benchmarks, he said. The company is not naming them because of confidentiality agreements. The report includes a two-week test any team can run on its own code, with any model, without DesignVerse.

The cost figure comes from one client’s product over 12 months, not from the benchmark. Without a context layer, the work took nine developers and 9,600 hours, Manolache said. With one, it took five developers and 4,000 hours, a 58% cut.

DesignVerse values the 5,600 hours freed at about $882,000. It used a blended rate of $157.50 an hour, taken from the client’s $1.5m labour bill. At $120 an hour the figure would be about $672,000, and at $200 about $1.12m. It leaves out platform, licensing and token costs, and the cost of building the context layer. Nobody lost their job, he said: the hours went to the backlog.

“If companies are paying for AI to write code, only to pay their engineers to fix it afterwards, they need to ask some serious questions about the return on that investment,” Manolache said in the release.

In Qodo’s 2026 State of AI Code Quality report, 90% of US engineering leaders said they were confident reporting AI’s impact to their board, but only 45% could trace AI activity to the code it produced. Gartner has warned about rising AI coding costs.

To work in a regulated industry, a general US model needs three things, Manolache said. It needs the company’s context, has to run under the company’s control and must fit how it builds software. Most of that knowledge is not in any model’s training data, he said. He expects smaller, specialised models with a context layer the company owns. Otherwise, he said, a company could spend $100m on infrastructure for 50 engineers.

“My message to European companies is simple: don’t wait for a US model to solve your sovereignty problem. Build the layer of intelligence and knowledge that you should own. The model underneath should be interchangeable. And that remains true whether you work with us or not,” he said.

The fight between coding tools has moved beyond the models. DeepSeek’s Super-Agent reads team chats to get its context.

The report ends with five recommendations. DesignVerse raised a $5.5 million seed round in May, led by Begin Capital and Gapminder VC. It said then that it had rebuilt a 15-year-old EUROCONTROL application in just over a month, against an estimated six months.
