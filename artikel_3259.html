Federal Appeals Court Judges Press Nexstar About Its Quest To Narrow Tegna Merger Order

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

A three-judge panel at the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit peppered a Nexstar Media Group lawyer with questions about the company’s efforts to salvage its contested acquisition of rival Tegna.

Pay-TV operator DirecTV and attorneys general of 13 states sued last March to block the $6.2 billion transaction, which would create a local TV station behemoth with reach to some 80% of U.S. households. The day after the suit was filed, the FCC approved the merger and Nexstar swiftly declared it closed.

Just as the companies were starting to integrate, however, a federal judge granted the lawsuit plaintiffs’ request for a temporary restraining order, freezing the deal pending a trial. While Tegna shareholders were paid out and the company’s stock ceased trading, the company’s Virginia-based operations have been kept separate from Nexstar’s Texas-based footprint pending the outcome of the suit. Nexstar lawyer Deanne Maynard sought to lay out the company’s view that the TRO should only apply to stations in 31 markets where the two companies both own stations. She asserted that the plaintiffs accepted that reduced scope in legal briefs filed during the appeal. Numerous times as she was starting to make her point, though, she was frequently interrupted by federal judges Marsha Berzon, Johnnie Rawlinson and Xavier Rodriguez during the proceeding in a Pasadena, CA courtroom. “In this broadcast merger context,” Maynard argued, “it’s very common to hold separate stations. Across Democratic and Republican administrations, the federal DOJ has challenged mergers making very similar claims to the ones that plaintiffs make here.”

The judges all voiced skepticism that there was a clear playbook to reduce the number of stations to be held separate to just 31, rather than just blocking the entire deal, as the TRO did. “My major concern here is that we’re an appellate court,” Berzon said. “The district court is there, he’s considered what’s feasible and what isn’t, and for us to start redoing that, we’d have to have a very high confidence that there was a feasible alternative.” She later interjected, “This is a discrete kind of issue which requires an understanding of a large, integrated organization and how it can function.”

Rodriguez raised the possibility that the appellate court could remand the matter to the district court. “Why do we have to decide anything now?”

That question hung in the air as attorneys for DirecTV and the states stated their case. Emily Curran of the California Department of Justice, said focusing on 31 stations rather than the full Tegna portfolio “is not a coherent proposal.” In the lower-court proceedings, she said, “We presented evidence that a full unwinding of the merger, such that the previous version of Tegna would be fully restored, would restore that lost competition, for the simple reason that the day before the merger, it was competing. And it was competing as a integrated entity that was a successful company.”

Ben Horwich of DirecTV said the plaintiffs “challenged the transaction that they put together.” It is not the job of the plaintiffs, or the court, he added, “to figure out how to achieve their business objectives by fashioning some other transaction.”

While Maynard referred to the process of narrowing down the scope of the TRO as routine, Horwich countered that the question of antitrust harm raised in merger reviews has doomed some multi-billion-dollar deals. Sinclair’s attempts to merge with Tribune, and Tegna’s thwarted combination with private equity firm Standard General “couldn’t go forward, because there were these concerns.”

In the case of Nexstar-Tegna, he added, the notion of a subset of Tegna being kept separate as a quasi-subsidiary pending the outcome of the suit doesn’t fit with Nexstar’s M&A strategy. Already, eight agonizing months have passed without the usual post-merger focus on synergies and efficiencies, with Nexstar stock slipping 10% during the legal limbo period.

“It is the defendant’s own business thesis that you need to be at least a certain size to cut it in this industry,” Horwich said. “To be successful, to be competitive.” Carving out 31 stations, he said, would be tantamount to “creating a bunch of orphan stations, and then trying to find homes for them. That’s not what we’re aiming for here. We want the competition we had before the illegal hardship.”

After the oral arguments, the judges took the case under advisement and will now work on arriving at a decision, which is expected sometime in the early part of 2027. If the panel rules against Nexstar, the company could request an en banc hearing or appeal the matter to the U.S. Supreme Court.

Meanwhile, a jury trial of the lawsuit has been scheduled for next July in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of California.
