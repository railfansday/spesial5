Three Routes to VPP Revenue for Installers, EPCs, and Developers Utility programs, wholesale markets, and bilateral contracts each come with different eligibility rules, payment structures, and commercial responsibilities.

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

By Intersolar & Energy Storage North America

In June 2026, Google and Voltus announced a three-year agreement to unlock up to 100 megawatts (MW) of distributed capacity annually in PJM. Under Voltus’s Bring Your Own Capacity model, Google funds procurement while Voltus aggregates qualifying distributed energy resources (DERs) and compensates participating customers.1

The deal is one of three distinct paths to earn revenue from DERs. Across the U.S., utility programs, wholesale electricity markets, and bilateral contracts each have their own eligibility requirements, payment structures and commercial responsibilities.

Utility programs offer an established pathway for compensating customers and asset owners who reduce demand, shift consumption or discharge stored energy during periods of grid need. Payments may be tied to enrollment, committed capacity, measured performance or a combination of these factors. Massachusetts, for example, offers residential battery customers $275 per kW of average summer performance through ConnectedSolutions2.

Beyond customer incentive programs, utilities also procure distributed flexibility through contracts to address local grid constraints or broader capacity needs. Unlike standardized incentives, these arrangements can establish specific delivery requirements and compensation terms, creating opportunities for developers and service providers to assemble and manage DER portfolios. During a July 2026 heat event, Green Mountain Power dispatched 90 MW of stored energy capacity, helping customers avoid an estimated $6 million in peak-related costs during one peak hour3.

Aggregated DERs can earn revenue from energy, capacity, and ancillary services in wholesale electricity markets, subject to regional market rules and resource qualification. Unlike utility-specific programs, wholesale markets can provide access to broader regional revenue opportunities and multiple grid services. For developers and installers, participation typically occurs through an aggregator or other qualified market participant rather than through direct market registration.

While Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) Order 2222 established a broader framework for DER participation, uneven implementation across regional markets continues to constrain commercial opportunities for aggregated resources. Market access does not automatically translate into revenue, as individual resources must still meet qualification requirements and comply with market-specific rules. New York Independent System Operator (NYISO), for example, requires DER aggregations to meet minimum capacity and real-time telemetry requirements, which can increase participation costs and limit the economic viability of smaller resources.

Wholesale participation can also expose VPP portfolios to fluctuating market prices, performance obligations and restrictions on receiving compensation for overlapping services. In Texas, ERCOT’s Aggregate Distributed Energy Resource (ADER) pilot allows aggregated DERs to participate in wholesale energy and ancillary services markets, subject to qualification, telemetry, and dispatch requirements.

Bilateral contracts allow utilities and large electricity consumers to procure distributed flexibility directly from aggregators and service providers. Growing interest from data center operators and other large energy buyers is expanding the potential market for VPP capacity beyond traditional utility programs. Unlike standardized programs, bilateral agreements can be tailored to a buyer’s specific needs, with negotiated terms for capacity, delivery periods, compensation, and performance obligations.

The appropriate route depends on existing customer relationships, market access, and the responsibilities each partner assumes. Developers and EPCs working on commercial storage projects may find opportunities through utility procurement or capacity contracts, while residential installers with established battery customer bases may be well positioned to incorporate VPP enrollment into utility programs. Wholesale markets can expand revenue opportunities through aggregation partners, and bilateral contracts can create opportunities to develop resources around specific buyer needs.
