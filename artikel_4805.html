Supportive Design in the Waiting Room: Puzzles Anyone?

Posted November 10, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

On a recent trip to my dentist’s office for a dental cleaning appointment, I was pleasantly surprised to see a puzzle on the coffee table in the center of the waiting room. The puzzle was being worked on by a patient waiting for her appointment. There was already progress on this 500-piece Halloween-themed puzzle; the borders had been completed. When I came back to the office for a follow-up appointment a week later, there was even more progress. And this time, two waiting patients, sitting across from each other, were actively engaged with the puzzle.

Puzzles are a form of positive distraction in waiting rooms and elsewhere. Positive distraction is one of three pivotal variables in Ulrich’s theory of supportive design (Ulrich, 1991). The other two variables are social support and perceived control. Social support refers to the idea that elements in the surroundings, such as furnishings, can support visiting friends and relatives (the concept is usually applied to in-patient settings). Perceived control refers to the idea that the setting offers opportunities for the individual to make changes to the environment (i.e., for agency). An example would be turning lights on or off.

Positive distraction refers to the idea that there are pleasant elements in the surroundings that distract us from worrisome thoughts. Most often, positive distraction takes a visual form, such as a window view of nature or artwork, but positive distraction could also be provided by an auditory source, such as background music, which has been shown to lower anxiety in adults when played before a medical procedure (Biddiss et al., 2014). Closed-caption television is a somewhat trickier form of positive distraction to use, as the news on many channels may be upsetting rather than positively distracting. In the waiting room at my dentist’s office, there is a large wide-screen television hanging on the wall, but no news is played; rather, it plays old episodes of Antiques Roadshow, which seems fitting for New England, where I live.

Most often in the literature, games and puzzles (and coloring books) are associated with pediatric settings, but coloring has also been shown to decrease anxiety in parents waiting while their child is in surgery (Robinson et al., 2018). There is relatively little literature on the use of adult games and puzzles in healthcare settings. But not only do puzzles have the potential to offer positive distraction, but they also may engage perceived control. Of Ulrich’s three variables, it has been more difficult to show the impact of perceived control compared with the other elements. In research involving perceived control in outpatient waiting rooms (Devlin et al., 2023), the authors concluded that people likely have a schema that discourages manipulation of environmental elements, but that they are more likely to adjust controls when such controls are perceived to be individual (such as a table lamp next to your chair). In that study, a participant wrote, “I can’t rearrange items in the room without people wondering what I am doing,” which the authors argue suggests that “people have a script for the behaviors that are appropriate in a doctor’s waiting room.”

Puzzles offer the possibility of engaging all three of Ulrich’s variables. They offer the opportunity for positive distraction; they also accommodate social support because puzzles can be worked on by more than one person at a time. Further, there is perceived control because puzzles afford engagement (as demonstrated by the fact that the Halloween puzzle was being worked on in the dentist’s office—it invited participation). The notion of affordances comes from the work of American psychologist J. J. Gibson and refers to the idea that the environment offers opportunities that can readily be perceived. For example, a chair (as well as many other entities such as low tables and steps) affords sitting because we can see a horizontal platform to support us.

Given the schema mentioned earlier that people have a script guiding what behaviors are appropriate in the doctor’s waiting room, it isn’t clear what circumstances need to exist before people, of their own accord, would start working on a puzzle. For example, would people open a closed puzzle box and start working on it? Or would it take something like an open box, or an open box with a completed corner, for people to realize they were being “invited” to engage with the puzzle pieces?

Research investigating this puzzle process might help us better understand the role of the elements that invite (i.e., afford) perceived control in healthcare settings.

Given concerns about contamination, research should also consider concerns that may be raised by handling items that others have handled. There may be different levels of acceptability to engaging with puzzles depending on the nature of the healthcare office. For example, would people be less concerned about engaging with a puzzle on a visit to the dentist for teeth cleaning than to a primary care provider during flu season?

Ulrich, R. S. (1991). Effects of interior design on wellness: Theory and recent scientific research. Journal of Health Care Interior Design, 3, 97–109.
