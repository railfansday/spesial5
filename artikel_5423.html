From subway cars to Civil War ships, 10 sunken objects and the reefs they became

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Ships, subway cars, oil rigs and household junk all become reefs on the seabed. Ten examples show which ones help marine life.

Whatever people lose or leave on the ocean floor becomes a place for something to live, whether anyone meant it to or not. A ship, an oil rig and an old refrigerator all count as hard ground, and much of the seafloor has little of it. Marine life begins to settle on a sunken object from the start. "From the minute it sinks, it starts to form a new habitat underwater," said Avery Paxton, a researcher with NOAA's National Centers for Coastal Ocean Science. Shipwrecks come to hold microbes, algae, corals, sponges, fish and crustaceans, according to NOAA NCCOS. A single hull can end up supporting everything from microscopic life to the animals divers come to see. The same thing happens to a ship lost in a storm and to a ship sunk on purpose, and the difference between accident and plan matters far more to people than to the fish. With nearly any hard object able to turn into habitat, the real choice people face is which objects belong on the bottom.

People usually make the case for artificial reefs in terms of fish, and those who run reef programs say the benefit is real. The Post and Courier reports that Bob Martore, who oversees the South Carolina Department of Natural Resources' artificial reef program, credits the reefs with spreading anglers across more spots and sparing natural reefs from heavy fishing. Yet Paxton says it remains unclear whether the fish on artificial reefs are drawn away from nearby natural habitat or newly produced over time. A busy reef is not proof of a good one. If the fish are only moving from one spot to another, a reef can make them easier to catch without adding to their number. The fairer test is what each object does to the sea around it, and the answer depends on what the object is made of and where it sits.

Smithsonian Ocean set out to show how many different things become homes for marine life on the seabed, including retired New York City subway cars, fridges people toss into the sea, a Navy ship sunk with explosives off the Florida Keys and offshore oil platforms where anglers now find fish. Some went down on purpose and some by accident, and at least one wreck was later hauled back out. Some reefs now arrive with permits and careful cleanup, while others still form from dumping that nobody approves. Each object deserves the same measure: what it left behind on the seafloor.

Many of the ships that now support sea life were never meant to sink. Roughly three million wrecks lie on the seabed, according to Smithsonian Ocean, and marine life thrives on many of them. Nobody chose where those ships would land or planned for fish to move in, yet the habitat formed anyway.

That total counts ships lost, not reefs formed. It takes in every kind of wreck, from warships to small dinghies, and not all of them host reef life. "Many" is the most anyone can honestly say about how often a wreck becomes a thriving reef. The figure shows how often marine life has had a chance to settle on a sunken hull, not how often it actually did.

Whether a wreck helps the water around it is settled at the moment it goes down. Nobody can clean a ship before an accident sinks it, so its fuel, cargo and fittings go to the bottom with it. Fish can gather along a hull while the fuel inside it leaks into the same water. What a wreck leaves on the seafloor depends on what it was built from and what it carried, and in an accident nobody made those choices. That is the whole difference between a wreck and a planned reef. When people sink a ship on purpose, they decide which ship goes down, what gets stripped out first and where it comes to rest.

Building homes for fish began as a fishermen's answer to a poor seabed, long before anyone engineered a reef. Fishermen in South Carolina were sinking log huts in the 1830s to raise their catches, Smithsonian Ocean reports, and the seafloor they fished explains why the work seemed worth the effort. In 2021, Scott Whitaker, executive director of CCA South Carolina, put the share of sand on the seabed off the state at 90%, according to The Post and Courier. On a bottom that is almost all sand, hard structure is rare, and a pile of logs could look like a real way to change a day's haul. The huts were built on purpose, while most of what reached the seabed got there by accident. Their aim was narrow, and it never reached past more fish for the people who built them.

For generations, reef-building off South Carolina was something fishermen did on their own, and the state started its artificial reef program only in 1973, according to The Post and Courier. By 2021, South Carolina had approved 43 locations for reefs, so the state rather than a lone angler had signed off on each one. A permit means someone other than the builder decides where a reef goes and what it is made of. The log huts never faced that check.

The sandy coast that made logs worth sinking also sets a hard bar for anything meant to stay in place. "Couple that with hurricanes, big storms, heavy currents and big tides, and you need a substantial structure to make a big impact and have any kind of longevity," Whitaker told The Post and Courier in 2021. Reef builders have changed what they sink since the 1830s, even though their reason for sinking it has stayed the same. In 2021, Bob Martore, who runs the state's reef program, said both the kinds of material going into the water and how good that material is had shifted over time.

The fishermen had the right idea, which was to put hard ground where there was none. Later builders added the durability and oversight to make that idea last on a coast this rough.

Offshore platforms went up to pump oil and gas, yet anglers came to prize them for another reason. As oil and gas drilling spread offshore, the platforms became fishing hotspots, according to Smithsonian Ocean. Lawmakers from both parties now want to keep more of that fishing ground in place. E&E News reports that a House bill, H.R. 5745, the Marine Fisheries Habitat Protection Act, would keep retired platforms in the water as reefs. Democrats make up four of the bill's five co-sponsors, and its sponsor is a Republican. A House panel has taken up the measure, which remains a proposal rather than law.

The bill's sponsor pitches retired rigs as a gain for more than fish. "Instead of tearing down structures that have become vibrant marine ecosystems, we can repurpose them to benefit the environment, fishermen, and taxpayers alike," Rep. Mike Ezell, R-Miss., told E&E News. Ezell names taxpayers alongside the environment and fishermen, which makes fish habitat one benefit among several in his pitch rather than its sole aim. With motives that mixed, the outcome for the sea deserves a closer look than the pitch gives it.

The idea that rigs help fish rests on assertion rather than measurement. Ezell's view of fishing near the platforms comes from advocacy and personal experience, not from a study. Good fishing beside a platform shows that fish gather there, but it does not show what the steel does for the sea over the years it stays in place.

Until someone measures the steel's long-term effect on the sea, support for the bill rests on how the fishing looks beside the rigs.

Some reefs begin when someone throws away things they no longer want, with no permit and no plan. Smithsonian Ocean reports that individuals dump fridges, shopping carts and car frames into the sea, creating unsanctioned reefs full of fish and coral. Nobody approved those sites or answers for what happens on them, and marine life settles on them anyway. The same kind of household gear turns up on the other side of the world. In 2017, prawn trawlers off Queensland, Australia, hauled up stoves, tool boxes, outdoor furniture and buckets of cement, according to ABC News. None of these objects was made to serve as a reef, whoever put them there.

A sunken fridge is a reef or a menace depending on who works the water above it. Queensland's trawling grounds are mud and sand, according to ABC News, and crews drag their nets across that soft, open floor. Any heavy object there can catch a net. "I call them amateur bombs now," Peter Nebe-Kerridge, a prawn trawler fisherman in far north Queensland, said. Junk works as fish habitat because it is hard and stays in place, and those same qualities make it a hazard to anyone fishing that bottom.

Off Queensland, the question of who dumped the junk and why remains open. In 2017, trawler crews made allegations about who was responsible, according to ABC News, and John Robinson of Sunfish Queensland says those allegations were not substantiated. Robinson also finds it hard to picture anyone dumping on trawled mud and sand, where he says nobody would come out ahead. A pile of junk on the seabed proves only that someone dumped it.

In law, this is dumping, not reef-building. In 2017, individuals who dumped illegally at sea faced fines of up to $50,000, according to ABC News. A fine deters only people who expect to be caught. Elaine Lewthwaite, a prawn trawler operator, doubts that authorities can watch every private boat on the coast unless those boats carry the same vessel tracking system that trawlers do.

A reef that nobody permits is also a reef that nobody monitors, and it can shelter fish while it snags the next crew's nets.

The Duane became a reef because people chose it, not because of a storm or a dumping run. Someone picked the ship and picked the place to put it down. The vessel was deliberately sunk after fifty years at sea, according to Smithsonian Ocean, so its end was planned rather than left to chance. The National Park Service reports that the wreck lies inside the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary, so the reef rests in protected waters. The ship's working life went down with it. Crews aboard the Duane took part in four rescues at sea and picked up 346 survivors. The hull that marine life now covers once helped pull people out of the sea. The sinking added more than hard ground to the seafloor. It turned a service record into a place people can visit.

The Duane is a reef for trained divers rather than casual swimmers. Even the top of its mast sits 60 feet below the surface, according to the National Park Service, far beyond a snorkeler's reach. Thousands of people make the dive each year anyway, so divers and fish use the same structure. Avery Paxton, a researcher at NOAA's National Centers for Coastal Ocean Science, says artificial reefs can serve fish extremely well as habitat. The Duane adds a steady stream of visitors to that benefit. Judged by what it left on the seafloor, this planned sinking pays off in three ways: as habitat, as a destination and as a piece of history kept in view rather than scrapped.

A ship that once picked survivors out of the water now gives fish a home and thousands of divers a reason to visit.

The Hoyt S. Vandenberg was placed on the seafloor on purpose, inside waters set aside to protect the ocean. The retired Navy transport ship was sunk with explosives in 2009 within the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary, according to Smithsonian Ocean. A protected area usually works by keeping things out, but here protection meant putting a whole ship in. The goal was to spare the sanctuary's living coral by giving visitors somewhere else to go. Joe Weatherby, the project organizer at Reefmakers, told The Associated Press that the ship was "giving the reef a breather, which is what it needs." The Vandenberg is one of 61 artificial reefs in the sanctuary, which shows that building reefs on purpose is a regular part of managing these waters.

Most of the work of turning the Vandenberg into a reef took place above water, in making the ship safe to sink. The project cost about $8.6 million from the purchase of the ship through its cleanup, according to The Associated Press, and that cleanup meant hauling 193 tons of material that could potentially cause cancer off the ship before it went into the sanctuary in 2009. Without that work, all of that material would have gone to the bottom inside the hull. The price therefore reflects the preparation of the ship and not only the sinking. The Vandenberg is reef-building as an engineered public project, with millions spent to give the seafloor new habitat without the ship's toxins.

The people who backed the project measured its return in visitors as well as in fish. Officials in 2009 expected the reef to bring in up to $8 million a year from tourism, according to The Associated Press. That figure was a forecast, not a measured result. The case for marine life also rests on potential, since Avery Paxton, a researcher with NOAA's National Centers for Coastal Ocean Science, says artificial reefs are capable of giving fish an excellent home. The Vandenberg's value therefore rests on two hopes, one for divers' spending and one for the life that settles on the ship.

The Vandenberg should be judged as a dive destination that keeps visitors off fragile coral as much as a home for fish, and its success will show up in visitor counts as well as in the water.

When New York City retired a generation of subway cars, thousands of them went to the bottom of the ocean instead of to a scrapyard. More than 2,500 of the cleaned, retired cars were sunk along the Atlantic coast from 2001 to 2010, according to Smithsonian Ocean. Delaware took a large share, and CBC Radio reports that New York donated more than 600 of the cars to that state in 2001 alone. Delaware wanted the cars because its own waters offered fish so little.

The seabed off Delaware gives fish little natural shelter. CBC Radio reports that Jeffrey Tinsman, project manager for Delaware's artificial reefs, considers the mid-Atlantic poorly supplied with habitat. The cars went down onto a sandy bottom, and their value begins with that placement. Hard structure does the most for fish where the seafloor has almost none of its own.

The cars were cleaned before they were sunk, and marine life settled on their frames. On the reefs off Delaware, a blue mussel community supports a food web that sand alone cannot. "There are about 30 or 40 other species of invertebrates, shrimp, crabs and worms and so forth, that are all associated with that blue mussel community. And in total, there's about 400 times as much food for fish per square foot as there is in the natural sand bottom," Tinsman told CBC Radio. A food supply on that scale is stronger evidence of benefit than a crowd of fish. A reef where food grows does more for the sea than gather fish in one place, even if it does not prove the water holds more fish overall.

Subway cars are only one of the materials Delaware has sunk, and concrete is another. Tinsman says an automobile body that rusts away quickly costs as much to place as concrete that lasts for centuries. Reef managers judge a material by how long it keeps working, not by how interesting it is.

By that measure, the worth of a subway car on the seafloor depends on how long its frame holds up, not on the years it spent carrying New York riders.

The reef off Key Largo, Fla., began with a towing accident rather than a plan. The SS City of Washington spent years hauling passengers and cargo, went down off the island in 1917 and today gives fish a place to live, according to Smithsonian Ocean. By its last voyage the ship no longer carried passengers. NOAA Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary reports that it was a coal barge, towed by the tug Luckenbach #4, when it ran aground. Its cabins and steam machinery had been removed when it was converted to a barge in 1911, so the ship that reached the seafloor was already stripped of much of what it once held. No one put it there for marine life, and fish settled on it anyway.

More than a century after the grounding, the wreck's condition is on record rather than left to guesswork. A 3D model of the site was built from photographs taken on June 1, 2017, according to NOAA Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary. The iron hull rests in shallow water, at a depth of 25 feet. The sanctuary lists the wreck on the Florida Keys Shipwreck Trail. That listing gives the City of Washington value as a historic site in addition to its value as a home for fish.

The City of Washington became a reef through bad luck and through no one's effort. It now serves both as fish habitat and as a documented piece of maritime history. A wreck that nobody chose to sink can still leave something useful on the seafloor.

Some objects that land on a reef make the seafloor worse. Smithsonian Ocean blames a harmful "black reef" at Palmyra Atoll on the iron chains of a ship that ran aground there in 1991. The wreck was removed in 2013. The harm fell on a protected reef, because Palmyra Atoll, south of Hawaii, is a U.S. National Wildlife Refuge. Nobody chose to sink anything there. The wreck earns its place here for the damage that grew around it, not for any fish it attracted.

The black reef was a local animal that grew out of control. The corallimorphs that covered the coral around the wreck belong to a species native to the atoll, according to a 2020 report in the Honolulu Star-Advertiser. The same report described corallimorphs as animals that settle in disturbed areas of reef and then dominate them. A ship crashing onto living coral creates exactly that kind of disturbance. In 2020, iron leaching from the hull was only one theory for the spread and not a confirmed cause, even though Smithsonian Ocean pins the black reef on iron from the ship's chains. The firmer lesson concerns placement. Dropping anything heavy onto a healthy reef can start a takeover that nobody planned.

Removing the wreck helped the spot where it had lain. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service led a multimillion-dollar project to haul the ship away. Afterward, corallimorph growth near the ship site greatly slowed, according to a 2020 report in the Honolulu Star-Advertiser. Elsewhere on the atoll the growth increased, and the outbreak did not end when the ship left.

Sailors have sunk objects on purpose to stop ships, not only to give fish a home. Smithsonian Ocean reports that Roman and Persian sailors sank objects to trap or divert enemy ships. They acted for naval strategy rather than to attract fish. These sinkings should be judged by the result their builders wanted, and that result was a blocked passage for the enemy. The fair question is whether the tactic ever delivered it.

The Union still trusted the tactic enough during the Civil War to commit a whole fleet to it. It bought dozens of vessels, called the Stone Fleet, for the purpose of scuttling them off Charleston, S.C., according to the Post and Courier. The barrier did not hold. James Spirek, an underwater archaeologist at the University of South Carolina Institute of Archaeology and Anthropology, says blockade runners got past the obstructions with little change of course once the ships broke apart. The Union paid for a fleet to block a harbor and got scattered wreckage instead.

The failed barrier still matters off Charleston, because the wrecks now count as a piece of history. Isle of Palms, S.C., is spending $32 million to renourish its beach with sand taken from an offshore site, according to the Post and Courier. Patrick Barrineau, who works in coastal science with CSE, considers the wrecks a cultural resource. The project was once barred from that entire stretch of seabed, Barrineau says, and his team now coordinates with the state's historic preservation office so the sand work can go ahead while leaving the wreck sites alone.

Union sailors sank those ships to close a harbor, and coastal planners still have to work around them more than 150 years later.
