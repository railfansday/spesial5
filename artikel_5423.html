The Hidden Risks of Building Your Own Delivery Fleet as a Growing Business

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

You're viewing the US website. Looking for the Site edition?

Building an in-house delivery fleet may seem like a smart way to reduce costs and gain greater control, but these challenges can quickly impact profitability.

Opinions expressed by Entrepreneur contributors are their own.

The invoices from the regional carrier keep getting bigger. For the owner of a growing home furnishings retailer, delivery has become one of the largest expenses outside of inventory, and the complaints that come with it — missed windows, damaged pieces, vague updates — still land on her desk.

So she runs the numbers on three cargo vans and three full-time drivers. On paper, the savings look obvious. What the spreadsheet leaves out is that she is not just buying vans. She is starting a transportation operation inside a company built to sell furniture.

The case for in-house delivery is legitimate. You decide when orders go out and how customers are treated at the door. You depend less on a third party’s capacity during busy weeks, you can coordinate dispatch directly with inventory, and at sufficient volume, you may lower your cost per delivery.

The catch is that an outside carrier bundles its costs into a price per stop. Once you own the operation, those costs scatter. Maintenance appears in repair invoices, insurance in renewal paperwork, depreciation on the balance sheet and recruiting and supervision both in payroll and in the owner’s calendar. Vehicles also sit idle on slow days and break down on busy ones.

In-house delivery is not automatically cheaper. Build a fully loaded cost per delivery that includes idle time, downtime, fuel, insurance, depreciation and management hours, then compare it with outsourced pricing across slow weeks as well as peak ones. A model built on your best month will flatter the fleet.

The same principle applies to broader ecommerce operations. Growing order volumes require companies to reconsider operational processes, technology investments and cost structures.

With two vans, the owner knows who drove which route and when the oil was last changed. That informality works, which is why it tends to persist long after it should be replaced.

At 10, 20 or 50 vehicles, decisions that once lived in one person’s head need to live in a system. Assign ownership for scheduling, maintenance, driver qualification files and performance. In a smaller operation, that may be one capable operations manager, but it must be a named role. Then track a short list of numbers: on-time rate, cost per delivery, vehicle downtime and incidents. Without them, you are managing by anecdote.

This transition reflects a broader challenge of business expansion. Building systems and teams that scale successfully emphasizes the importance of repeatable processes, clear responsibilities and infrastructure that can support increasing operational demands.

A driver is often the only person from your company a customer meets, and also the person operating a heavy vehicle on public roads with your inventory inside. Informal hiring does not scale. A repeatable process does.

Define written qualification criteria, including license status, driving history review where permitted and experience suited to your vehicles, and apply them to every candidate. Onboarding should cover cargo height and clearance, securing loads, customer handoffs and what to do when something goes wrong.

Put your standards for company vehicle use in writing, including phone use, personal use and defect reporting. Some vehicle sizes and operations trigger additional federal or local requirements, so verify what applies to your fleet.

Imagine a company that advertises tight two-hour windows and stacks routes to hit them. It works until a customer isn’t home or a freeway slows. Now the driver is behind, and the easiest way to recover is to speed up, skip a break or glance at a phone.

Fleet safety is partly a scheduling problem. If your routes only work when nothing goes wrong, you have designed in pressure. A few practical fixes help:

Tight windows can remain a selling point where routes are dense and predictable.

Reliable delivery also depends on operational consistency. Efficient workflows and well-organized teams help businesses maintain service standards as demand increases.

Relying on drivers to notice mechanical problems works for a few vehicles. It fails once drivers rotate through different vans and each assumes someone else reported the strange noise.

Set preventive service intervals based on manufacturer guidance and actual use. Require a short pre-trip inspection and make defect reporting penalty-free. Keep records in one place so patterns become visible, such as a van that keeps returning to the shop. Decide in advance that a vehicle with a safety-related problem comes out of service, even on a heavy day.

Then plan for downtime. A spare vehicle, a rental arrangement or an outside carrier on standby keeps one broken van from becoming a week of broken promises. Customers experience your maintenance program as reliability.

Most owners picture an accident as a repair bill. The real cost is wider: employee injuries, injuries to other road users, vehicle replacement, insurance claims and possible premium changes, delayed orders, lost productivity, administrative work and reputational damage. Depending on the circumstances, it can also raise legal questions, which is one reason consistent records matter.

The human side deserves honest attention. Delivery vans and box trucks are often heavier and taller than the passenger cars around them, and the injuries associated with commercial vehicle collisions, including head trauma, spinal damage and fractures, can change someone’s life and disrupt a business for a long time.

A business that outsources delivery pushes much of its transportation risk onto the carrier. Once you operate your own vehicles, that risk is yours to evaluate.

Work with a qualified broker to review limits, exclusions, covered drivers and how cargo is treated. Pay particular attention to the difference between company-owned vehicles and employees’ personal vehicles used for business, since those situations are often treated differently. Requirements and policy terms vary by jurisdiction and insurer, so confirm the details for your own operation.

Insurance also cannot replace good systems. Coverage helps absorb a loss. It does nothing to prevent the missed inspection, the rushed route or the untrained hire.

Sustainable growth requires systems that can operate consistently without constant intervention from the business owner. This principle is explored in an Entrepreneur article about building repeatable systems for long-term business growth, highlighting the importance of documenting, evaluating and continuously improving business processes.

Owning vehicles buys control, but control only pays off through discipline: clear ownership, consistent hiring, realistic schedules, preventive maintenance, an accident plan and a sober view of cost. The businesses that succeed with in-house delivery do so because they built a system around their vans, not because they bought good ones.
