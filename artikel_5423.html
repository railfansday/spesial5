From documents to decisions: Keeping loan data in sync across mortgage capital markets

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Consolidated Analytics applies its loan due diligence expertise to mortgage hedging, pricing and investor delivery through a shared loan record in loanDNA

A borrower switches loan products after locking a rate. The lock desk updates the terms, but the hedge desk is still working from a file imported earlier that day. If the market moves before the next update, the hedge desk is managing exposure against a pipeline that has already changed.

Lenders have spent years buying specialized tools for pricing, rate lock, hedging and loan due diligence. Each can do its job well while keeping a separate copy of the loan record. Rudy Zabran, chief operating officer at Consolidated Analytics, sums up the result as “five systems, five versions of the truth.” When terms change or an appraisal comes in, someone has to figure out which value is current and carry it into the next system.

A rate lock is also a hedge event. The lender has promised a price on a loan that may take another month to fund, and the market can move during that window. When the hedge desk relies on scheduled imports, it can learn about a lock or a change hours after the pricing engine and lock desk have recorded it. Extensions, relocks and float-downs can leave the desk estimating which loans will close based on an older view of the pipeline. If the mortgage hedging loan data still reflects the original product, the desk may need to revisit its coverage when the revised terms arrive.

Information must also flow back to pricing, too. Investor pay-ups, execution results and levels in the to-be-announced mortgage-backed securities market can inform the next rate sheet. When those inputs sit apart from pricing, the secondary team has to bring them back into the process. By month-end, the team may still be tracing which version of the loan data drove a pricing or hedging decision.“The people who sit between the systems are rekeying data, re-importing tapes and deciding which number is the source of truth. The month-end P&L variance in secondary is usually a data variance in costume. Specialization is fine when volume pays for the overhead. As scale, you can afford a reconciliation team,” said Zabran.

The cost of mortgage hedging errors and the work it takes to reconcile loan data rarely show up on a software invoice.

Together, those measures give lenders a baseline for judging whether a technology change makes day-to-day operations more efficient.

A new platform can leave manual work in place if one department still depends on a feature in the old system. Employees may still have to key changes into both systems, import another loan tape or recheck a field someone already verified. The work a lender can retire matters to the return on that investment.

“What does this let me stop doing? Can I stop rekeying and stop reconciling and stop re-verifying data that we’ve already verified?” said Zabran.

After implementation, the same four measures can show which steps disappeared and where reconciliation still consumes staff time.

The discrepancies Consolidated Analytics sees in loan tapes offer a practical test for a vendor demo. Change the loan-to-value ratio on a sample loan, then compare the mortgage hedging loan data with the due diligence record to see when each reflects the revised figure. Ask the provider to follow the loan ID through pricing, lock, hedge, delivery and diligence and show whether the audit trail records the change. If the reports still disagree, trace how each module receives and stores the update.

The test should also cover access to the loan history. Can the lender retrieve it if a module goes offline? Which data and audit records can it take when it leaves a vendor? A specialist can still fit the workflow if it can read and update the shared loan record. That gives lenders room to keep a tool that serves a specific function without adding another copy of the data for staff to reconcile.

Consolidated Analytics built loanDNA around a question at the heart of its due diligence work. Can an investor trust the data on the loan? By the time an investor or rating agency asks, the lender may already have priced, locked and hedged the loan using figures that diligence will later flag. loanDNA maintains a shared loan record from rate lock through securitization, so teams can validate information earlier and preserve that work as the loan moves downstream.

The platform’s document intelligence tools extract data and classify documents, with human review supporting validation. The same engine handles pricing and rate lock, including for correspondent sellers. The hedge workflow reads the lock as an event, and standardized loan tapes carry data into investor delivery and diligence.

An appraisal illustrates what that can mean in practice. If the appraisal changes the loan-to-value ratio, the secondary team and diligence reviewers need to know which figure was used and when it changed. Carrying that history forward helps the lender explain why a figure in an earlier commitment differs from the one in the delivery tape. It also gives the lender a record to consult if an investor later asks how the loan was evaluated.

If volume doubled in 2027, how many people would a lender need to add in secondary and post-closing? The answer depends in part on how much work still falls between systems. When staff have to import tapes, reconcile lock changes and rebuild loan histories for investors, a surge in production can send more files into those same queues. Measuring that work now can show which handoffs would limit capacity first.

Consolidated Analytics plans to add live market feeds and execution capabilities to loanDNA. For lenders evaluating their systems today, the capacity test starts with the next change to a locked loan. How quickly does it reach the hedge desk, and how much work will it take to explain the change when the loan reaches an investor? The answers can show where a lender can absorb more volume and where growth will require more manual work.
