‘We’re having to overcome brand America’: Kraft Heinz CEO says made-in-U.S.A. designation is more ‘complicated’ in appealing to Canadian customers

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

As U.S.-Canadian relations go frosty, one American food brand is having to be more intentional about appealing to its northern neighbors.

Speaking at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum in Detroit on Monday, Kraft Heinz CEO Steve Cahillane said the made-in-the-U.S.A. moniker doesn’t carry the same positive connotation as it did in decades past. As a result, he said, the Chicago- and Pittsburgh-based food giant—behind Jell-O, Lunchables, and Heinz ketchup—is having to tweak its messaging.

“Brand America is not as strong as it once was, and that’s been the case for some time,” Cahillane told Fortune Executive Editorial Director Diane Brady. “We’re having to overcome brand America. Whereas, in the ‘50s, ‘60s, ‘70s, into the ‘80s, brand America was a badge of honor, it’s a little bit more complicated today.”

Ongoing trade tensions, including a 50% tariff on $20 billion of Canadian goods, have chilled Canadian attitudes towards American productions, as have repeated threats from U.S. President Donald Trump about making Canada the “51st state,” claiming it relies too heavily on the U.S. economically. While Canadians have continued to pour money into U.S. investments, many have also made a concerted effort to boycott American goods in favor of Canadian counterparts.

In some areas, these boycotts are taking a toll on the U.S. economy. A report from the Canadian government published last month found that despite Canadian visitations to the U.S. beginning to increase, visitations from Canadians are 29% lower as of September 2026 than they were two years ago. According to the U.S. Travel Association, those decreases in visits cost the U.S. economy $4.5 billion last year.

Kraft Heinz has worked to adapt to Canadians’ changing attitudes toward American goods by giving its consumers reassurance their products are being made in the Great White North, according to Cahillane. In March, subsidiary Kraft Heinz Canada announced a $250 million investment to bolster operations at its Montreal production facility.

“We had to remind the Canadian consumer that all of the Heinz ketchup that they’re consuming in Canada is made in Canada,” Cahillane said. “We have a couple of big plants up there, and so when we talk to people about the local nature of our products, it may be brand America, but it’s produced in your backyard by your friends and neighbors, grown locally,” he added.

As Kraft Heinz navigates a turnaround after a 10-year slump, the company is beginning to find more solid footing outside the U.S. Despite reporting falling revenue last quarter, it was particularly offset by growing sales in Canada. Its Kraft Mac & Cheese Cheesy Ramen debuted in Canada in July, and, according to Cahillane, it sold out three months of inventory in a week.

Kraft Heinz is also finding ways to navigate tariffs after initially being hit hard by the implementation of the import taxes. The food giant reported in May 2025 it would need to shift suppliers and potentially reformulate products as a result of the levies.

“We’re already local. We have to be hyperlocal,” Cahillane told Fortune. “We have to build in redundancy. We have to build in the ability to do things faster than we ever have before, and that becomes hyperlocal for us.”

Sasha Rogelberg is a reporter and former editorial fellow on the news desk at Fortune, covering retail and the intersection of business and popular culture.
