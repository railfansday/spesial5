Two Biologics for Psoriatic Arthritis Square Off in Big Trial

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

— Winner emerges (maybe) from first study to compare anti-IL-23 with anti-IL-17A/F

ORLANDO -- A large head-to-head trial reported here that pitted bimekizumab (Bimzelx) against risankizumab (Skyrizi) as treatment for psoriatic arthritis (PsA) yielded a winner, albeit with a couple caveats.

Arthritic symptoms were relieved more effectively with bimekizumab, an inhibitor of interleukin (IL)-17A/F, with 49.1% of patients achieving ACR50 responses (50% decrease in symptom severity by American College of Rheumatology criteria) after 16 weeks of treatment, compared with 38% of those treated with the IL-23 inhibitor risankizumab, according to Joseph Merola, MD, of the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center in Dallas.

Bimekizumab was already superior by this measure by week 4, and the advantage was maintained out to week 24 (55.6% vs 43.8%, P=0.0037), he told attendees at the ACR's annual meeting. Effects on skin manifestations were similar at study end, but Merola reported that resolution of psoriatic symptoms came on faster with the IL-17A/F inhibitor.

But about those caveats: Safety data favored risankizumab, because Candida and other fungal infections were more common with bimekizumab at 5.8% versus 1.5%. And cynics may find it relevant that the trial was sponsored by UCB, bimekizumab's manufacturer.

Both drugs are currently approved for PsA, which has become a very competitive market for treatment. Naturally, clinicians and patients want to know how the different options differ and whether any can be said to be superior. Merola observed that IL-23 acts at an upstream point in the pathological sequence underlying PsA, helping to drive production of adaptive lymphocytes; IL-17 species, meanwhile, mediate their proinflammatory activity. He said that by acting on this downstream segment to prevent inflammation, bimekizumab might yield greater clinical benefits.

The current trial, dubbed BE BOLD, randomized more than 550 PsA patients in equal numbers to the two drugs, administered according to their labels. The primary outcome measure was the ACR50 responder percentage at week 16, with treatment and outcome tracking continuing to week 24. Secondary outcomes included achievement of minimal disease activity (MDA), and the proportion of patients achieving both ACR50 and PASI100 (100% reduction in Psoriasis Area and Severity Index scores). He also reported PASI100 response numbers separately.

Mean patient age was 51 and just over half were men. Time since diagnosis averaged about 6 years; about 20% had previously used tumor necrosis factor inhibitors. Tender and swollen counts averaged 17 and 10, respectively. Psoriatic lesions were rated moderate to severe in some 11%, and the mean PASI score was about 8.5.

Something similar was seen for achieving both ACR50 and PASI100. At week 8, about 20% of the bimekizumab group got there, compared with less than 10% with risankizumab (P=0.0015). The significant advantage for bimekizumab continued to week 12, but the gap narrowed thereafter.

This was true as well for PASI100 by itself, with a big difference during weeks 4-12, then closing to almost nothing by week 24, at which point 59% of both groups had achieved it.

Another "skin-facing" metric, as Merola put it, was achievement of low disease activity or remission with Disease Activity Index for Psoriatic Arthritis scores. This, too, followed the same pattern: an earlier response with bimekizumab with risankizumab catching up as the trial continued. By week 24, over 60% in both groups reached these milestones.

In general, Merola said, safety findings were as expected from previous studies. Rates of treatment-emergent adverse events overall were similar with the two drugs. Just seven participants stopped therapy because of adverse events, split evenly. One patient with a history of cardiovascular disease receiving risankizumab died of a myocardial infarction, which was considered unrelated to the drug. Upper respiratory tract infections were a bit more common with bimekizumab.

That fungal infections were increased with bimekizumab was not surprising, Merola said, because IL-17 is important in resisting these organisms. In randomized trials leading to the product's approval, 9% of patients developed oral candidiasis, actually a much higher rate than in the new study, in which it occurred in just 2.5% of patients.
