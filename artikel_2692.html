Slander, the 1957 film directed by Roy Rowland, is supposed to be a ripping attack on the 1950s' scandal sheet Confidential. Unfortunately, Rowland was a bit of a hack director who turned in a fairly toothless attack on the gossip industry that it only redeemed in part by a terrific supporting performance by Steve Cochran.

Cochran plays H.R. Manley as the editor of The Real Truth. Manley is a stand-in for Confidential publisher Robert Harrison. To tell you the truth, the real story of Confidential is more interesting than the movie. Harrison was a longtime newspaper man who had been working in the "yellow journalism" field since the 1920s. By the early fifties, he had been publishing "cheesecake" magazines filled with suggestive photos that even included some light sadomasochism references. But he drew the line at actual nudity and thus, his business got wiped out when Hugh Hefner came out with Playboy in 1953.

Harrison then hit upon the idea of a scandal sheet that would publish the hidden dirt of celebrities and other figures in the public eye. He relied a lot on paid informants, who either needed money or had a grudge to settle, and Confidential, despite its protests to the contrary, didn't bother the double-check most of the claims. Harrison also wasn't above using blackmail by agreeing to kill a scandalous story about a star in exchange for money. Additionally, he also took a page from the Red Scare by getting lesser celebrities to "flip" and provide dirt on bigger celebrities in exchange for not publishing their own story. This is where the movie Slander steps in.

The actual history of Confidential is pretty lurid and worth checking out, even it it's just the Wikipedia summary. There are a bunch of celebrity lawsuits and even a murder-suicide in the back of a taxicab, although admittedly, that sensational crime took place after the movie Slander came out.

As far as the actual events of the movie go, Van Johnson plays Scott Martin, a down-on-his-luck puppeteer. Martin has a loving wife Connie (Ann Bylth) and a young son (Richard Eyer). He gets his big break when he fills in on a children's program at the last minute and becomes a big hit.

But Martin has a dark secret in that he served four years in prison for armed robbery when he was young. As the show's sponsor points out later in the film, that's something that could possibly be explained away as a youthful mistake and that he paid his debt to society, except that he performs for small children and parents get jumpy around children's performers.

Cochran as Manley isn't particularly interested in a children's host who has been famous for ten minutes. But Martin grew up in a rough New York neighborhood with movie star Mary Sawyer. Martin knows something about Sawyer from when they were kids and Manley wants him to spill it. Manley offers to kill the story on Martin doing time for armed robbery if Martin talks about Sawyer.

The actual scandal about Sawyer, who never appears on screen, is left unsaid, but from Martin's vague description it certainly sounds like she had an abortion twenty years ago. The Production Code of the 1950s wouldn't let them say that, of course.

So Martin is presented with a Red Scare-type scenario. He can either rat on a woman he hasn't spoken to in twenty years or he can keep his dignity and lose his job. This isn't a bad setup for a film, but the film pretty much wastes it. No one really struggles with the moral dilemma here. Scott Martin never for a second really considers selling out a movie star whom he used to know but doesn't know now. His wife Connie briefly goes through the motions of arguing with her husband, but she never really understands what's going on and she comes around to Scott's point of view pretty quickly, even if it sends her family into poverty. The film also adds in a pretty cliché tragedy that sets the climax in motion, but that doesn't exactly inject any moral ambiguity into the story.

Cochran's H. R. Manley isn't an ambiguous villain and his single-minded drive to increase circulation (and profits) at all costs doesn't leave much room for sympathy. But even as a proverbial mustache-twirling villain, Cochran is just so damn entertaining as Manley that we can overlook his cartoonishness. He's an extension of the amoral newspaperman stock character of the Golden Age of Hollywood, except his cynicism is replaced by a firm belief that his scandal-mongering is actually a righteous crusade. There's certainly some ties to the Red Scare there, as Manley certainly could have been a congressman on the House Un-American Activities Committee. Except that there is no way that any Hollywood film, let alone an MGM movie, would be blasting the blacklist in 1957, except by distant proxy.

Johnson and Blythe aren't terrible, but they are overwrought and uninteresting. Harold J. Stone is a bit better as Scott's manager Seth, but that's a pretty small part.

All-in-all, I was more appreciative of Slander for sending me down the rabbit hole of the story of Confidential magazine than I was for the film itself. Obviously Confidential was an easy target for Hollywood—the magazine was devoted to tearing down the studio's carefully-crafted illusion behind the stars and didn't much care if their information was accurate or not. (Sometimes it was, such as when Confidential carefully insinuated/outed Tab Hunter as gay. Warner Brothers had to set up fake romances between Hunter with Natalie Wood and Debbie Reynolds to tamp that down for another 50 years until Hunter officially came out.) Combine this with Rowland's pretty tame direction, and you've got a film that should be a scandal but turned out to be a tame Slander. Still, Cochran is great.

Ken Rosenthal and Will Sammon reported over the weekend that the Giants are open to offers for right-hander Logan Webb. (sub. req.) And Max Ralph has a good article as to why Webb would be a perfect fit for the Cubs. So is this something that the Cubs should go after?

To sum up, Webb is a three-time All-Star who got Cy Young Award votes in every year from 2022 to 2025, finishing second to Blake Snell in 2023. Like the rest of San Francisco, Webb had a down year in 2026, going just 8-9 with a 4.31 ERA over "just" 160.2 innings. But Webb's fielding independent pitching (FIP) number was 3.45, which is high for him but still a very good number. It's also a sign that the wretched Giants may have been bringing Webb down with them. (The Giants were either an average or a below-average defensive team, depending on which metric you look at.) Webb isn't a pitcher who relies on velocity, but if you're concerned about that, there doesn't seem to be any significant drop in velocity to explain Webb's less-than-excellent 2026 season.

In Ralph's article, he lists two reasons for why Webb would be a great fit for the Cubs. The first is that he's a ground ball pitcher. Last year his ground ball rate of 50.7 percent (per Fangraphs) was good, but a big dropoff from his career number of 56.3 percent. Webb also rarely gives up home runs, with just 13 in 160.2 innings last year. Even that was down from the 14 he allowed in over 200 innings in 2025. The Cubs want pitchers who keep the ball in the park and on the ground. Webb does both.

The second reason Ralph mentions is that Webb has been incredibly durable over the course of his career. No pitcher in baseball has thrown more innings than Webb over the past five years. Last year, he missed three weeks in June with knee bursitis, which is the first time he missed a start in five years. He was also shut down in September with a blister, but that may have been a case of the Giants not taking any chances with their best pitcher in a lost season.

I'll add that Webb is a strike-thrower. He led the league in strikeouts in 2025 which was partly because he threw a league-high 207 innings but also because he does miss bats at a terrific rate, at least before last season which was merely good. But Webb also keeps the walks down. He walked just 6.1 percent of the batters he faced last year, which puts him in the 89th percentile for walks allowed. Again, that was up from his numbers from 2023 to 2025. Webb's career walk percentage is 5.8 percent.

Webb just turned 30 and is under contract for the next two years for a total of $47 million. That's a bargain for a pitcher of Webb's quality.

The downside trading for Webb is that the Giants aren't just going to give him away. In fact, in both the Rosenthal and Sammon article as well this piece from Grant Brisbee (sub. req.), they note that the Giants are unlikely to trade Webb. He's on the market because the Giants are a crappy team and probably won't be good before his contract runs out in 2027. If they can get a haul for him now, why not consider it? But the Giants are a large-revenue team and they have no financial reason to dump Webb's reasonable contract. Indeed, Bob Nightengale reports that other teams are speculating that the Giants would only trade Webb along with other players with bloated contracts like Matt Chapman, Rafael Devers and Willy Adames. The Cubs would have no use for Chapman and Adames and while Devers might make a good DH, the Cubs don't like to have a full-time DH and his contract runs for seven more years at over $30 million a year. The Giants would have to eat a lot of that for it to be worthwhile, but then why wouldn't the Giants just keep the still-productive Devers in that case?

So basically, the Giants are saying "Hey, blow us away and we'll trade you Webb. Or take on one of our crappy contracts and still give us a lot of talent."

The Giants reportedly would like to get young pitching back and the Cubs don't have a ton of that. They could also use a second baseman, and the Cubs have extras of those. Certainly the Cubs could package Pedro Ramírez and Cade Townsend as the headliners in a deal. Then they'd probably have to throw in Jaxon Wiggins as well. That's the Cubs' top two pitching prospects and a solid young second baseman for two years of Logan Webb.

If that sounds like a lot, it's because it is. I'm not even sure that's enough to tempt the Giants, but I assume that top Cubs prospects Josiah Hartshorn and Owen Ayers are off-limits. Maybe they could subtract Townsend if they agree to take on Devers' bloated contract, but that sounds like more headache than it's worth for the Cubs.

But from the Cubs and the Giants point of view, with Skubal signing with the Mets, Webb is clearly the best pitcher available if San Francisco decided to trade him. If the Cubs won't meet their demands, maybe another team will. And if no team meets their demands, then they can just keep Webb. They can still trade him before the Trade Deadline this year or next winter and still get a lot for him. The Giants just don't need to trade Webb at all.

So would you "blow the Giants away" in a deal for Logan Webb? Would you offer what I put forth or would you just say "pass."
