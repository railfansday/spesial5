Researchers found a crushed Celtic helmet in Poland that proves wealthy southerners traveled along the Amber Route

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

A crushed Celtic helmet lay in a charred pit at Łysa Góra, Poland, with four iron adzes, a scythe and nearly 350 metal finds from the site.

Łysa Góra is a glacial sand dune between the Orzyc River and one of its oxbow lakes, near the village of Bagienice in northern Mazovia, Poland.

Inside its fortifications, archaeologists found a pit filled with charred remains that held a crushed bronze helmet, four iron adzes and an iron scythe.

The helmet is a Berru-type helmet of La Tène (Celtic) origin. Five authors, Kaczyński, Maciałowicz, Borkowski, Sobczak and Aniszewski, described the find in Antiquity on October 14, 2026.

They propose that people from the Celtic south may have been on the dune themselves. The idea has not been tested yet.

The name means Bald Hill. From the sixth to the fourth century BC, members of the West Balt Barrow Culture lived on the dune.

According to the paper, these communities built fortified sites, lived off rivers and lakes, and buried their dead together in urns.

Archaeologists logged the site in 1957 and dug it in short bursts between 1971 and 1992. Those digs recovered several La Tène imports. No full analysis was ever published, so the paper says the site’s importance went unnoticed.

In 2024 the State Archaeological Museum in Warsaw and the Faculty of Archaeology at the University of Warsaw resumed the work. Kaczyński, Borkowski, Sobczak and Aniszewski work at the museum. Maciałowicz is at the university.

The pit lay within the fortifications. The authors found the helmet crushed and fragmented, lying alongside several iron tools, including adzes and a scythe. They read the pit as one of the traces of ritual activity at the site.

The paper’s plan view of the pit, based on a photograph by Kaczyński, numbers the helmet, the scythe and four adzes marked 3a to 3d. The helmet closely parallels finds from Dürrnberg in Austria.

Two kinds of timber-and-earth defenses guarded the dune around the middle of the first millennium BC. In the south, a rampart with a palisade and an outer ditch was cut partly into the natural sediment. In the north, the team found a fortification built entirely of timber.

A second ritual zone lay in the marsh at the foot of the dune’s southwestern slope. Two deposits there held bronze ring ornaments and earrings with glass beads.

One deposit included a spiral armlet made of bronze ribbon and decorated with incised lines. The team found traces of repair on one end, where two rivets once sat and left circular holes. B. Karch drew a reconstruction of the armlet.

Nearly 350 metal objects have come out of the ground so far, among them ornaments, weapons and evidence of bronze working. Pottery, animal bones, cereal grains, seeds of cultivated plants and charred wood turned up as well.

Many of the bronze ornaments were broken into pieces and packed into tight bundles. The authors think they were prepared for remelting, and excavators had already recorded ceramic casting crucibles in the 1970s.

The site also yielded amber beads and an amber ring that was never finished. The authors say these finds suggest the people on the dune took part in long-distance amber exchange with regions south of the Carpathian Mountains.

Amber was prized in northern Europe long before: in Karelia, a 5,400-year-old grave held 140 amber ornaments.

The team also looked at the carbonized wood left in the socket of one iron adze. Under the microscope, a cross-section showed pores arranged in rings and both very wide and narrow wood rays, which are features of oak.

The dune has also given up 31 mostly broken Early La Tène brooches and ring ornaments, dated to around the middle of the fourth century BC.

According to the paper, helmets in the Early La Tène world symbolised “high social status and affiliation with the warrior elite.”

The authors consider that concept likely alien to the local societies, which were presumably without centralized leaders.

For that reason they argue the helmet may reflect “the physical presence of southern groups, including members of their elite.”

Trade from hand to hand is the alternative, since smaller La Tène ornaments did reach distant parts of Central and Eastern Europe.

The authors find that explanation less convincing, because the helmet was a prestige object and no comparable helmets are known outside the La Tène zone in the fourth century BC.

The dune lies close to the amber-rich Kurpie region, and the paper ties it to routes that led to the Sambian Peninsula on the Baltic coast.

Most Celtic sites of that age lie well to the south and west. Road surveyors in the Czech Republic found a lost Celtic town full of gold, silver and amber, and in central France, archaeologists dug up 2,300-year-old Celtic swords adorned with gems and symbols.

The authors say their hypothesis “will require verification through further research.” The ore source of the helmet’s metal is not yet known.

Lead isotope analyses are planned, and the results will be compared with similar objects from Bohemia, where a 2021 study of the Duchcov hoard examined Celtic movements in the fourth century BC.

The northern and southern defensive lines may or may not have stood at the same time. The team plans electrical resistivity tomography and magnetic surveys to check.

Money for the project came from the museum, the Chorzele commune authorities and the Faculty of Archaeology. The lead isotope results are still to come. Until then the helmet only points south, and nobody can say who put it in the pit.

The full study was published in the journal Antiquity.
