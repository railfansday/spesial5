A tiny injectable sensor could change how millions of people monitor blood sugar

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Continuous glucose monitors help millions of people with diabetes keep track of their glucose levels in real time, making it easier to manage medications, dietary choices, and everyday activities. However, the high cost of these devices prevents some patients from accessing the technology.

Scientists at Texas A&M University have identified a potential way to make future glucose monitors more affordable. In a study published in ACS Measurement Science AU, the researchers found that a key sensing material for an experimental injectable glucose sensor performed just as well as a commonly used alternative at approximately one-tenth of the cost.

Many existing glucose monitoring technologies depend on enzymes to detect glucose. The Texas A&M team is pursuing a different approach that uses fluorescent molecules and near-infrared light. By measuring variations in the fluorescent signal, researchers can determine changes in glucose concentration without relying on enzymes.

The technology relies on a process called competitive binding. Within the sensor, glucose and a fluorescent sugar compound compete to occupy binding sites on a protein called concanavalin A, or ConA. As glucose levels rise, this competition alters the fluorescent signal, providing a way to measure glucose concentration.

After evaluating several compounds that bind to the protein, the team identified β-mannopentaose as a promising alternative to α-mannopentaose, a more expensive compound used in previous versions of the sensor. Despite its substantially lower price, β-mannopentaose delivered comparable sensing performance. It also produced promising results in artificial interstitial fluid, a laboratory solution that recreates conditions beneath the skin.

"The surprising part was how closely the lower-cost material matched the performance of the more expensive alternative," said Dr. Sayantan Tripathy, assistant research scientist in Texas A&M's Optical and Bio-Sensing Laboratory and the study's lead author. "In this case, the cheaper option wasn't really a compromise, and that's an important finding when you're thinking about future glucose-monitoring technologies."

"Developing an affordable glucose sensor means considering the cost of the individual materials as well as how well they work together," said Dr. Gérard L. Coté, principal investigator of the study and a professor of biomedical engineering in the College of Engineering. "This study shows that we can substantially reduce the cost of one sensing component while maintaining comparable performance, giving us a promising foundation for further development."

A Tiny Injectable Sensor Beneath the Skin

To create an injectable sensor, the researchers embedded the glucose detection materials within a small hydrogel designed to remain in the tissue beneath the skin. The gel holds the sensing materials in place while allowing glucose molecules to move through it, where their concentration can be measured.

During laboratory experiments, more than 94% of the sensing assay remained within the hydrogel after seven days. The results demonstrate that the less expensive sensing chemistry is compatible with an injectable glucose monitoring system.

"When new medical technologies are expensive, access can be limited," said Dr. Melissa Grunlan, study co-author, professor of biomedical engineering in the College of Engineering and professor of chemistry in the College of Arts and Sciences. "Our goal is to develop sensors that are not only effective but affordable for the people who rely on them."

Although the experimental sensor has shown encouraging results, it remains at the proof-of-concept stage. Further development and testing will be necessary before the technology can be considered for clinical use. Nevertheless, the findings indicate that reducing material costs does not necessarily require sacrificing sensing performance.

Making Diabetes Monitoring More Accessible

The research highlights the importance of factoring affordability into the design of new medical technologies from the outset. Finding ways to reduce manufacturing expenses while maintaining performance could eventually expand access to continuous glucose monitoring, particularly among patients who cannot currently afford advanced monitoring devices.

The researchers intend to further refine their technology and investigate other components that could enhance its performance, extend its useful lifespan, and make it more practical.

"While the work remains in the research stage, the findings suggest that future glucose-monitoring technologies may not have to rely on expensive materials to deliver effective results," Tripathy said.

"Our long-term goal is to develop a reliable, affordable injectable glucose sensor that can expand access to continuous glucose monitoring, particularly for people in under-resourced communities and those without adequate insurance coverage," Coté said. "Reducing the cost of the sensing materials is one step toward making these technologies accessible to more people who need them."

The research team consisted entirely of Texas A&M researchers. Alongside Tripathy, Coté, and Grunlan, the team included Dr. Carolina Martinez, Anthony Dongchau, and Samyucktha Ganesapandian.
