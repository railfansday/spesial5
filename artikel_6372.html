A whale buried for 9.5 million years had unexpected company

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Scientists discovered ancient brittle star fossils beside whale bones, revealing how marine animals gathered around carcasses millions ago.

Brittle stars gather around dead whales on today’s seafloor, sometimes at much higher densities than on the surrounding seabed. Yet no fossil had ever placed them at a whale carcass.

Studies of fossil whale carcasses have mostly reported clams, snails, and animals that eat bone.

Now fossils from beside a beaked whale skeleton in Denmark suggest that brittle stars joined these gatherings 9.5 million years ago.

If that reading holds, it’s the first fossil record of a brittle star tied to a whale fall. That’s the term scientists use for a sunken whale carcass and the community of life it feeds.

The clues are tiny skeletal plates from a species new to science, now named Ophiura huebnerjenseni. The fossils are loose pieces rather than whole animals, so they can’t show what the brittle stars ate.

Ben Thuy, curator of paleontology at Luxembourg’s National Museum of Natural History, led the study.

Thuy worked with Mette Elstrup and Trine Sørensen of Museum Sønderjylland in Denmark. The fourth author, Lea Numberger-Thuy, works at the Luxembourg museum and at a dinosaur park in Germany.

The Gram Clay Pit in southern Denmark exposes about 23 to 25 feet (7 to 7.5 meters) of clay that built up on the floor of an ancient North Sea. Geologists call these layers the Gram Formation.

The pit is famous for its fossil clams, snails, and animals with backbones, including whale skeletons and giant megalodon vertebrae. Sea urchins, sea stars, and their relatives have been far scarcer there.

Scientists call that group of spiny-skinned animals the echinoderms. Before this study, only one echinoderm species had been reported from the Gram Formation.

Brittle stars are echinoderms too, with long, thin, flexible arms built from many small skeletal pieces.

The North Sea has plenty of them today, yet none had been found in these old clays. Thuy and Numberger-Thuy had already searched for them in clays of a similar age at Groß Pampau in northern Germany, without success.

The new fossils came from sediment collected in 2018, during the excavation of a beaked whale‘s lower jaw and four vertebrae from its backbone.

The bones were found about 21 feet (6.5 meters) below the top of the exposed clay. That level dates to roughly 9.5 million years ago.

Five sediment samples came from around the skeleton. The team dried each one, soaked it in water, and washed it through screens, then washed the leftovers a second time with a detergent-like chemical.

Under a microscope, the researchers picked out the smallest fossils one by one. The best brittle-star pieces were then photographed with a scanning electron microscope.

For comparison, they also processed a sample packed with tiny fossils. It came from 2 to 4 inches (5 to 10 centimeters) below a fossil log that was dug up in 2023.

“Only three samples taken directly beside the whale bones contained brittle-star remains. Samples from the same layer farther from the skeleton, and other comparison samples, yielded none,” Thuy said.

The three samples held 33 skeletal pieces in all. Most were plates from the sides of the arms, along with one piece of an arm’s inner backbone and one plate from the central disc of the body.

The arm plate chosen as the reference specimen for the species came from a sample taken 2 to 8 inches (5 to 20 centimeters) from the whale’s jawbones. Its shape placed the animal in the genus Ophiura, which still lives in the North Sea.

The closest living match there is Ophiura albida. The fossil plates differ from it in two ways: they have a slanted upper edge, and they lack the faint spurs that O. albida plates carry.

A cluster of fossils beside a skeleton doesn’t automatically mean the animals lived there, so the team considered other explanations.

“We considered whether currents had simply concentrated the plates around the bones, or whether local chemical conditions had helped preserve them,” Thuy added.

“However, sea-urchin fragments of comparable size and mineral composition occurred in every sample studied. This makes a preservation-only explanation less likely and supports the interpretation that the brittle stars lived on or close to the whale carcass.”

Scientists divide a whale fall’s life into stages. In the early stage, the carcass enriches the seabed around it and feeds animals that take advantage of any sudden meal.

In the deep sea, later stages can follow. One of them supports life that runs on sulfur compounds released as the bones decay.

Fossils around the Gram whale included clams that filter food from the water and meat-eating snails, some of them scavengers.

The team found no clams of the kind that live off chemical energy with help from bacteria. The bones had no animals cemented to their surfaces and showed no signs of burrowing.

“We found no evidence for the later sulfide-based community stages that can develop at deep-sea whale falls,” Elstrup said.

“The discovery therefore adds a rare fossil perspective on how animals responded to a large food source on an ancient shallow-water seabed.”

Ophiura huebnerjenseni honors Holger Hübner and Thomas Jensen, who founded the heavy metal festival Wacken Open Air. It joins a long line of species named after musicians, but this one comes with a geological link.

“The clay that accumulated on the bottom of the ancient North Sea not only preserved the remains of a 9.5-million-year-old brittle star, it also turns Wacken Open Air into the muddy battlefield renowned by metalheads worldwide,” Numberger-Thuy said.

Brittle stars have been found before beside the carcasses of ichthyosaurs, ancient sea reptiles, in rocks from the Early Jurassic. A fossil whale fall had never produced one.

The researchers can’t yet say whether this species lived only at whale carcasses.

They favor the idea that it lived on the surrounding seabed and gathered at the carcass in numbers large enough to leave a fossil trace.

Living brittle stars at shallow whale falls also come from the nearby seafloor. Finding plates of this species in ordinary Gram mud, far from any bones, would support that idea.

For now, the only place Ophiura huebnerjenseni has been found is the mud right beside one whale.

The full study was published in the journal Neues Jahrbuch für Geologie und Paläontologie – Abhandlungen.
