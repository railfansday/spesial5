Plant roots have a clever way to tell good and bad fungi apart, even though they both give off the exact same molecules

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Good and bad fungi release the exact same chemical signal but plant roots still know which one is dangerous

A fungus growing on a crop sounds like bad news, but not all fungi are harmful. Plants also rely on helpful fungi to take up nutrients through their roots.

Attackers and partners alike are built from the same material, a sugar chain called chitin. Fragments of it end up in the soil whenever a fungus grows or breaks down.

A new study shows how a receptor on legume roots sorts those fragments by length.

Receptors tightly grip long chitin chains, the kind that come with a fungal attack, and raise the alarm. Short ones, the kind that partner fungi shed, barely register.

Kira Gysel and Kasper Røjkjær Andersen of the Department of Molecular Biology and Genetics at Aarhus University in Denmark led the work.

Simon Boje Hansen and Henriette Rübsam are the first authors, with co-authors at the University of Copenhagen and the French research center CERMAV. The experiments were done in two small legumes used as laboratory models, not in crops.

In an interview with Earth.com, Gysel said the puzzle was that chitin looks the same along its whole length.

“It’s the same sugar repeated, like a string of identical beads, with nothing to tell one part of the chain from another,” she said.

Chitin is the main building block of a fungal cell wall. When a fungus grows or gets broken down, pieces of the chain of different lengths end up in the soil. Roots read these fragments of fungi to keep track of the microbes around them.

Long fragments usually come with an attack and, in legumes, they set off a much stronger defense than short ones. Short fragments come with the helpful fungi that plug into roots and exchange nutrients with the plant.

How a root told the two apart was the open question.

The team produced the outer part of a root receptor called CHIP13 and tested how hard it holds chitin chains of different lengths. It held an eight-sugar chain more than 1,000 times more tightly than a five-sugar chain.

Then they froze the receptor into crystals with the long chain attached and mapped it, atom by atom.

The receptor holds the chain at three points. Two are standard chitin-binding pockets found in related receptors. The third sits between them, and the team named it the bridge domain, because it spans the middle of the chain.

Gysel told Earth.com that the structure answered the question at a glance.

“CHIP13 holds the chain at three main contact points, and the central one, the bridge domain, is what coordinates binding of the long chitin chain,” Gysel said.

“Only chains long enough to reach all three are held tightly and can activate the plant’s immune response. It’s really a molecular measuring tape.”

Swapping out two amino acids near the bridge cut the receptor’s grip 100-fold. In a second legume, barrel medic, mutant plants given a version of the matching receptor with the same two swaps didn’t regain their response to chitin.

Legume roots also need a second receptor, CERK6, to respond to chitin, and CERK6 can barely hold chitin at all. Boje Hansen said that had been a puzzle.

“It turns out the two receptors split the work: one recognizes the signal, the other passes it on. Neither can do the job alone,” he said.

In leaf cells engineered to carry both receptors, CHIP13 pulled in CERK6 only once chitin was bound. The weakened version of CHIP13 didn’t recruit it at all.

The team’s model is a pair of CHIP13 receptors holding chitin and recruiting two CERK6 partners, which then pass the alarm into the cell.

To test the receptors in a living plant without altering its genes, the team raised nanobodies – tiny antibody-like proteins made by immunizing a llama – that plug each receptor’s chitin-binding site.

They soaked intact seedlings of the model legume Lotus japonicus in them. When asked by Earth.com what else about the research stood out, Gysel pointed to that experiment.

“We simply applied them to plants from the outside, and we weren’t sure it would work at all,” she said. “We thought the plant cell wall would block the nanobodies from reaching the receptors on the surface of the plant cells.”

The nanobodies reached the receptors. Seedlings treated with the CERK6 blocker responded to chitin about as weakly as plants missing that gene, and the CHIP13 blocker cut the response sharply too.

Both legumes are laboratory models, and the measuring tape hasn’t been checked in a crop. The bridge domain can be predicted in many related receptors across higher plants, but a predicted domain isn’t a tested one.

Gysel said the two receptors are only a glimpse of a larger immune system. Plants use many related receptors to monitor soil microbes. How those receptors work together, and whether others read signals by length, is what her group wants to find out next.

The stakes she described are agricultural. Strengthening the chitin alarm could make crops more resistant to fungal disease, but only if it doesn’t also fire at the fungi the roots depend on.

“Knowing how the long chitin chains are read helps us improve crops without tipping the balance, so we don’t weaken their defenses to make room for helpful fungi, or the other way around,” Gysel said.

Which crop receptors carry a bridge domain, and whether they measure chitin the same way, is the next thing to check.

The full study was published in the journal Science.
