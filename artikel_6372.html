Scientists Discover That Young Chimpanzees May Develop Accents Just Like Humans

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

The sounds animals make may carry clues to their social worlds, and young chimpanzees are helping scientists explore how communication takes shape beyond inherited biology.

Young chimpanzees may develop something resembling regional accents, with the sounds they make becoming increasingly different depending on the community in which they grow up.

Much like people who pick up subtle differences in speech from those around them, chimpanzees appear to develop population-specific vocal patterns as they mature. The finding challenges the long-held belief that their calls are largely fixed by genetics from birth.

A research team led by doctoral researcher Kassandra Giragosian from the School of Biological and Behavioural Sciences at Queen Mary University of London, in collaboration with the University of Portsmouth, compared the vocalizations of infant and juvenile chimpanzees from two geographically separated populations. By examining grunts, whimpers, and laughter, the researchers found that differences between the groups became more pronounced as the animals grew older.

Not all of these differences appeared at the same time. Some population-specific vocal characteristics were already detectable during infancy, while others emerged as the chimpanzees entered their juvenile years. This provides the first evidence that differences in chimpanzee vocalizations become increasingly distinct throughout early development.

“Understanding how communication develops during early life is fundamental to understanding animal behavior. Our findings show that chimpanzee vocalizations are not simply fixed from birth but continue to develop as young animals interact with their social and ecological environments,” says doctoral researcher Kassandra Giragosian.

Social interactions, environmental conditions, and other influences beyond genetics may therefore help shape how young chimpanzees communicate. Although the research does not establish precisely how these factors contribute to vocal development, the findings suggest that inherited traits alone cannot explain the differences between populations.

The discovery does not mean chimpanzees possess language or human-like speech, which involves an agreed vocabulary and grammar. Instead, it offers a new perspective on how communication develops in primates and how vocal patterns can differ between animal communities.

By examining communication at different stages of early life rather than simply comparing adults from different populations, the research provides a framework for investigating how communication systems emerge and diversify. It could also help scientists better understand the biological and environmental influences that shape vocal behavior across other species and the evolution of increasingly complex communication systems.

Reference: “Ontogeny of dialects across chimpanzee vocalisations” by Kassandra Giragosian, Marina Davila-Ross and Derry Taylor, 2 September 2026, PLOS ONE. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0355313
