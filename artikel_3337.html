John Patrick Shanley On His New Off Broadway Play ‘Mountain Girl’, Industry Upheaval And Why He “Deeply Opposed” WGA Strikes

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

John Patrick Shanley is a connoisseur of contradiction. When the New York Public Library’s main branch on Fifth Avenue comes up in conversation, the Bronx native takes note of the role of the robber barons in creating the library and other foundational parts of the city. Attending a recent wedding at the library, he says, “was like being in the ballroom of the Titanic. … It’s the craziest opulence I have ever experienced.” Citing the contribution of Andrew Carnegie to the library and other landmarks, he muses, “All those awful people did wonderful things.”

The latest play from the Oscar (Moonstruck) and Pulitzer (Doubt) winner is the Off Broadway world premiere Mountain Girl, which channels his knack for extracting drama and wry comedy from life’s paradoxes. The NylonFusion Theatre Company and Axial Theatre production recently opened at 154 Theatre and its run has just been extended to November 28. The show centers on Connie, an aspiring writer navigating a turbulent marriage, a highly complicated relationship with her mother, and a would-be artistic rivalry between the spouses. The play shares its title with the novel that Connie has never finished, and the show is a meditation of the craft and emotional torment of writing, or even the idea of writing. Speaking with Deadline New York Business Editor Dade Hayes, Shanley detailed the play’s development, which had work-in-progress presentations in L.A. in 2022 and then a five-day workshop in Paris in 2025. He also sized up the many changes in Hollywood of late, including what he considers to be self-inflicted wounds by the Writers Guild of America from its two most recent strikes. Watch on Deadline

This interview has been edited and condensed for clarity and length.

DEADLINE: Without giving too much away, this play has potency in part because it ends up being about the messiness of something incomplete, a goal unrealized. When did that idea come to you?

JOHN PATRICK SHANLEY: I was initially interested in two writers – one can’t start and one can’t finish. So, one of them keeps writing and the other one can’t get started. That is something that I’d experienced many years ago. And it creates its own peculiar madness. It struck me then that we do a lot of modeling in life, and that’s definitely something that I’m dealing with in the play. People do things to demonstrate to their partner, ‘This is how you do that. This is how you should be acting.’ The person modeling the behavior is trying to get the other person through manipulation, which is not a dirty word to me. To talk about change instead of just going ahead and changing, and that sets in motion a lot of crazy things.

DEADLINE: You put the novel manuscript on the table, almost like Chekhov’s loaded gun, right at the beginning. We expect someone to finish the book, steal credit for it, or do some conventional thing. But you’re more interested in the characters’ interior lives than a story of artistic inspiration.

SHANLEY: Most writers have a lot of bad writing to do. Sometimes when I talk to writers who are just starting, I’m like, ‘You have a lot of bad writing to do, you might as well get started.’ It’s like a road you have to go down in order to get to the destination you want. You have to go through a lot of dreary territory. But I see nobility in anyone’s struggle to create something, express something. It is almost immaterial to me whether the product is good. Is the struggle noble? Is the struggle worthy? In the play, the girl, she’s trying to write to please her mother. She had some talent, but then her mother got very excited because it would make the mother look good. And she immediately started to breathe down her neck, like ‘When are you going to write the next part?’ and all that, and the girl got completely frozen. A kind of paralysis descended on her. So, it’s sort of a Mexican standoff.

DEADLINE: I understand there’s going to be a French-language production of Mountain Girl and a West End revival of your play Four Dogs and a Bone in London. What’s it been like over your career to work in Europe, the UK and back home?

SHANLEY: At one point, many years ago, I did Moonstruck and I had been doing plays for a goodly while, and I ended up in Spain doing a week-long gig with [1983 play] Danny and the Deep Blue Sea working with local artists and stuff like that. They had me speak to the press. And the press’s attitude towards the arts there was utterly different. The American thing was, ‘What’s Cher like?!’ How much did the movie cost to make?’ Stuff like that. Over there, they were like, ‘You seem like you have a troubled soul. That reminds me of certain moments of Dante.’ I’m sort of trying to talk the language that I learned from the journalists in America and very quickly realizing, ‘Oh, this is another whole thing, and this is more interesting to me.’ I found myself doing a press junket for [1995 film] Congo, of all things. An interviewer said to me, with incredible solemnity, ‘What’s it like, adapting Michael Crichton?’ I looked at him and said, ‘Well, we’re not talking about Thomas Hardy.’ And the guy looked completely shocked.

DEADLINE: You have always managed to balance film and theater. Is that intentional?

SHANLEY: I’ve been fortunate. But in my head, I have visual skills as well as language skills. So I can picture things, which helps.

DEADLINE: It’s been quite a daunting period of years for Hollywood, with the Skydance merger, layoffs, labor unrest, AI, Covid, on and on. Anxiety has always been common in the industry but we seem to be in uncharted waters. Are you discouraged at all by the state of things?

SHANLEY: I think that the culture is now going at a speed that none of us can keep up with. There’s a constant feeling of being ungrounded. Whenever you start to get a point of view developed about what’s going on with AI or what’s going on with conglomerates, it changes. That’s one of the reasons that theater has been so important for me. Theater will weather all this shit. It may be, you know, back to the caveman situation, but you can always get some people and even if there’s no electricity go out in natural light and perform a play. The money, the technology of filmmaking can very quickly make it out of reach. There are so many very gifted writers and directors that one, and they start to do well and they move to Los Angeles and then, you know, they have like five or 10 or 15 great years. But then, if they physically survive, they usually have a really significant period where they can’t work. They’ve completely invested in things that require large amounts of money and technology. And they’ve left behind the bedrock that they can depend on, and to ground themselves as individual human beings and have a relationship with the sun coming up in the morning and the moon coming up in the evening, in favor of this artificial environment.

DEADLINE: We have written recently about New York overtaking L.A. in terms of production spending, which seemed unthinkable during the worst of Covid.

SHANLEY: I’ve been very concerned, especially for Los Angeles. They have been through every kind of calamity. And it’s a culture utterly different than New York. New York is not a one-industry town. L.A. really is a one-industry town, and the problem is that the industry, we’ve gotten to be in great trouble and also great upheaval and at the same time environmentally. You know, they don’t have any water. They have these enormous fires. It’s this wonderful giant talent pool that had to endure multiple strikes, including the Writer’s Guild strikes. I was deeply opposed to [the strikes], but I did not speak to the media about it because I felt that that would be undercutting. These guys, rightly or wrongly, they’re trying to do something. I thought they were fighting battles that had already been lost and putting people on ice for significant periods of time. [Combined with Covid], it ended up being like a 5-year hiatus from work, and it’s very hard to restart your career after a blow like that.

DEADLINE: Do you still go back and forth to L.A. for meetings?

SHANLEY: Very rarely. Everything that I’ve done pretty much has been through personal associations. When I did the [2008] film of Doubt, [producer Scott Rudin] just said, ‘Do you want to direct it?’ And I said, ‘Yeah.’ He said, ‘I think it’s a great idea.’ So I, you know, I’ve been lucky. [Executive producer] Steven Spielberg said that about [1990 film] Joe Versus the Volcano: ‘I hear you want to direct it.’ And the truth was, I didn’t remember ever saying that. But I was like, ‘Yeah, yeah.’

DEADLINE: Since you mention Joe, I wonder what it’s like to have a film be so misunderstood initially by critics and the public and then go on to develop a following. People were conditioned to expect something else from a major-studio movie starring Tom Hanks and Meg Ryan.

SHANLEY: It was epic. It was almost an 80-day shoot. I was living in a hotel in Los Angeles. My assistant had a car and I got around that way. Once we started shooting, I was in fairly constant struggles with Warner Brothers because I was shooting exactly the movie I told them I would shoot. And they seemed astonished by that. And I had sort of his storyboarded the whole thing, so I’m like, ‘Why would they be surprised? It’s exactly what I said.’ And then, uh, you know, we did it and uh, uh, New York press in particular was savage towards it.

DEADLINE: Warner Brothers has had its share over the years – just last month with Digger. And it wasn’t Warner, but look at the reversal on Ken Russell’s The Devils 55 years later!

SHANLEY: Absolutely. So anyway, I crashed and burned emotionally when I got such a rough reception to Joe. I had to refine my balance, refine the way I did what I did. And I got my confidence back and my certainty of voice back. I did the screenplay for Alive, and that was solid and good, but it took time.
