Why Chris Pratt Chose Way Of The Warrior Kid For His Next Movie Explained By The Star

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Based on Jocko Willink's children's book, Way of the Warrior Kid is a family-friendly throwback to the best of '80s cinema, and thus ironically makes perfect sense as Chris Pratt's next movie between seasons of The Terminal List. While both projects see him playing Navy SEALs, the tone of each could not be more different. Directed by McG from a screenplay by Will Staples, the new Apple TV movie is more akin to The Karate Kid than to Pratt's hit Prime Video series.

Way of the Warrior Kid follows Marc (Jude Hill), a young boy fresh out of eighth grade who yearns to leave the days of being bullied behind once he reaches high school. His mother, Sarah (Linda Cardellini), hopes that an extended visit from his uncle, Jake (Pratt), will provide Marc with the guidance he needs to flourish. Jake is dealing with his own demons after his most recent SEAL Team mission went awry, which means that the burgeoning bond between uncle and nephew could be just what the doctor ordered for each of them.

ScreenRant's Liam Crowley interviewed the stars and author of Way of the Warrior Kid, getting a perfect play-by-play on how Pratt and Willink went beyond collaborating on products to actually bring the heartwarming film to life. Pratt also explained why he wanted to take on such a project, while Hill shared the lessons he's taken from film set experiences as a child and teenager.

ScreenRant: It's a big year for Chris Pratt playing Navy SEALs. I feel like Uncle Jake is the antithesis of The Terminal List's James Reece. James had his family taken away from him. Uncle Jake is kind of forced into being a father figure in this film. Did you feel that way when filming?

Chris Pratt: It's a really funny point. Yeah, you're right. I've played with a lot of Navy SEALs, especially this past year. And you're right; I think it speaks to the difference in tone of the type of film that we made with Way of the Warrior Kid. This is a feel-good family movie geared towards younger kids.

In something like The Terminal List, obviously our north star is authenticity and grit. We're making a psychological thriller and an action thriller. But in this film, we're trying to root it in the reality and the heart of a relationship between a man and a boy. So, it's a little bit different. I don't know that the characters are actually different at all. I think just as I've always played every character, the entire set of circumstances around them and the tone and the story that they're told is different. And so I think it's the same person in a way.

ScreenRant: Jude, you've had quite the career at just 16 years old, and it's fair to say you've had a pretty unique childhood, effectively growing up on film sets. Did this filming experience teach you anything new about what it's like to be a kid in today's day and age?

Jude Hill: Totally. I mean, I moved from my home country when I was about 12 or 13 years old. I was playing this American kid, and thankfully I had spent some time in a school in Florida, so I could get around the head of American kids because they're different everywhere. But yeah, you're so right. It was different for me because I hadn't really experienced it that much. So, it was nice to do a little bit of research, and then I had friends and stuff I could go to just with certain questions I had.

ScreenRant: Chris, I understand you have a long history with Jocko specifically. It seems like you really seek out your creative partners with projects like this. How did this process with Jocko begin and make its way to launching this film?

Chris Pratt: Well, I'll tell you exactly how. I went to my agency, and I was discussing how it was important for me to try to find some work at home so that I could be present at home with my kids. We were talking about various promotional opportunities, and they gave me this long list of companies they're close with, and none of them really stuck. Meanwhile, I'm wearing a pair of Origin jeans and boots, and I'm drinking a Jocko Go, and I didn't see Origin or Jocko on this list. And I was like, "Listen, guys, I'm just going to actually call Jocko myself."

So, I literally cold-called him from my house. He was up in the mountains literally training snipers at the time. I was like, "Listen, dude, we know each other. We've spent some time together." We didn't know each other that well, but I told him I want to promote things and I want it to be authentic. "I love Origin, I love Jocko Fuel, and I really just think you're great. I want to be a business together." He was like, "Sounds great. Let me finish this up, and I'll ping you back."

Apparently, he then told that to his internal people and without his knowledge, they sent me the script. He had told me, "I'm not going to just send you the script," because it was embarrassing that he had a script written that he paid for to do a screenplay. He didn't want to be that guy that's like, "Oh, you want to go in business? Well, here's my script." But I'm glad his people did because I sat there and out of reverence for him, I opened page one.

I did not even move until I finished the entire script, and I thought it was great. I thought it was really meeting a cultural moment. I sent it to my team. I was like, "I love this movie. I think there's a way for us to do this movie." And that was the beginning of doing this movie.

ScreenRant: Jocko, I understand you have a pretty tenured relationship with Chris Pratt. Where did this all begin?

Jocko Willink: Thankfully, I had some mutual friends. Jared Shaw, who was on my SEAL team, trained Chris and eventually worked on Terminal List. Jack Carr, who wrote Terminal List, I was friends with both those guys. Through those guys, I met Chris Pratt, and then Chris got interested in Jocko Fuel. He's a partner in Jocko Fuel. He's a partner in Origin USA, the clothing company I have. So, we're business partners there. And eventually he got the script for Warrior Kid. And as soon as he read the script, he said, "We got to make this movie."

ScreenRant: I understand too that Uncle Jake is largely based on your own life experience. I can imagine that's a little easy when you're writing him on the page, but when he's brought to life as Chris Pratt standing in front of the camera, did you see yourself in Chris on set?

Jocko Willink: Yeah, and it was really neat to watch. Uncle Jake is a SEAL. In the book, he's a little bit more of a superhero. He's kind of perfect in the book. In the movie, he's more realistic. He's a human being, and Chris did a great job of capturing that.

And, look, we filmed some scenes that are very powerful. The scenes up at Rosecrans National Cemetery, which is where I have my friends buried, we shot up there, and the interactions that Chris and I were having and the discussions about the emotions and what that feels like were very important and very powerful. Chris just did an incredible job of bringing that to life in the movie.

ScreenRant: I find that a lot of times when people have a singular purpose, whether it be as an athlete, a performer, or a Navy SEAL, your mind can get jumbled when you're removed from that. You're like, "What do I do now, because I always had just one mission moving forward?" How did you reshape your life purpose?

Jocko Willink: This is what I tell veterans all the time: when you get done with your military career, you have to find a new mission. And if you don't find a new mission, you're going to be lost and discombobulated. That can become problematic.

For me, as soon as I retired from the SEAL teams, I started teaching leadership to companies across the country, and then that turned into writing books, and then that turned into podcasts, and that turned into a clothing company and a supplement company and eventually kids' books. To me, it's finding that next mission and pouring into that mission and keeping your head on a swivel and keeping your situational awareness high. So when other opportunities arrive or arise, you have to take advantage of them.

ScreenRant: It's very interesting to do this film because you're dealing with a younger demographic. When you're writing this story, and you're bringing it to life with a child effectively, how do you reach them?

Jocko Willink: I have my own kids. I got four kids myself. And not only that, I was a kid, and I do remember what it was like being a kid. When I was writing Way of the Warrior Kid, I was always kind of surprised at what it said because it is really written from me as a 10-year-old. It almost feels like I didn't write it because I wrote it in the character, in the mindset of, "Hey, I'm a 10-year-old kid. I remember what this was like." That's why I think it comes across as natural because I just jumped in.

Look, I'm not the most mature person in the world. I know I might look a little older, but I have no problem devolving into a 12-year-old sometimes and keeping that mindset. So, that's what I did when I wrote the book; I think that came through when Will wrote the screenplay, and I think that's why it worked.

Way of the Warrior Kid is now streaming on Apple TV.
