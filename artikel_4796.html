How Gossip Is Good for Us

As the saying goes, “If you don’t have anything good to say, don’t say anything.” But in the words of Alice Roosevelt Longworth, perhaps more of us prefer: “If you can’t say something good about someone, sit right here by me.” After all, deep down, we all love some good gossip, don’t we?

In fact, by some estimates of everyday conversations, upwards of 65 percent of what we talk about involves people and social interactions, both self-disclosures and information about others’ escapades. And “gossip,” defined as the telling of stories about the everyday adventures of the people in our lives that we tell to, with, and about others, began deep in our evolutionary history. As soon as we began to use language, we started to gossip!

As we gather around the holiday table this year with friends and family we may not have seen in a while, we will share stories of our lives, stories of their lives, and stories of our friends and family not able to be present at the table. The good news is that this is actually good for us. Whereas gossip has a bad reputation, in fact, it serves a critical function in weaving our lives together and keeping our social groups connected.

Dunbar, an evolutionary anthropologist, argues that gossip began very early in our ancestral history. Living in larger social groups benefited our survival, and gossip is a necessary part of keeping larger social groups cohesive. Through sharing stories about how Uncle Joe and Aunt Louise are settling into their new living arrangements in Timbuktu, we maintain a sense of belonging to the group, and this sense of belonging adds to our own sense of meaning and purpose in life. When we feel socially isolated, we actually experience physical pain; Williams and Nida show experimentally that social exclusion for as little as two minutes leads to physiological pain responses and emotional distress, and longer periods of social isolation can result in depression, helplessness, and alienation. Maintaining social connections by integrating our lives with others through stories and gossip brings us together and creates a sense of well-being.

Gossip also serves to help us with advice and discussion of possible problems—if you are thinking about changing jobs, a story about cousin Bill who just did that could provide invaluable information as you consider your options. With my colleagues David Pillemer and Dorthe Thomsen, we showed how these kinds of vicarious stories, stories about other people that resonate with who we are, can promote successful adaptation, help us make good decisions, and teach us morals and values for how to live a good life. We don’t have to experience things directly to learn from them. We can learn from the experiences of others through gossip.

Vicarious stories are particularly prominent in family storytelling. Alongside stories of shared family experiences, such as the Thanksgiving when Dad dropped the turkey on the kitchen floor, and we all had to eat hot dogs, are stories of the family past: stories about parents growing up or grandparents, aunts, and uncles. When the younger generation hears these stories, whether from the person who experienced them (“When I was your age….”) or third-hand as gossip (“Your grandfather loved Thanksgiving, and every year, he would invite friends from work who had nowhere else to go”), these stories become frameworks for how to live a life. Gossip provides us with maps for how the world works.

Of course, there is a dark side to gossip. As Dores-Cruz and colleagues point out, gossip can be used to purposely damage someone else’s reputation, perhaps in the service of self-aggrandizement, and thus can lead to mistrust within the group. They conducted a series of studies to assess people’s motivations and tendency to gossip, especially when the person being gossiped about was experimentally manipulated to be a victim of malicious rumors. They found that group members were more likely to gossip when a group member was being maliciously maligned in order to protect the victim’s reputation. At the same time, people are motivated to spread the word about members of the group who violate norms, suggesting that gossip serves to keep everyone in line. Gossip is a little bit like herding sheep, making sure the group maintains positive bonds and bringing those who deviate from this back into the fold.

This holiday season, there will be gossip! I will spend Thanksgiving with my “framily” (friends and family), and there will be some dear framily we will miss who are not able to be there. But we will gossip! “Did you hear about so-and-so?” and “Let me tell you what happened…” We will laugh, and we will shed tears. And we will cement our bonds. So, this Thanksgiving, if you have something juicy to tell, come sit by me!
