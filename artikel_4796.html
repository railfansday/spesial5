The Latest Enamel Pieces by Tiffany & Co. Channel Jean Schlumberger’s Dazzling 1960s Icons

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Jean Schlumberger didn’t have a background as a gemologist. The Tiffany & Co. designer got his start in the 1930s with costume jewelry, crafting clips, buttons, and other accessories for Elsa Schiaparelli’s couture collections. “The way he came to jewelry was unpretentious: simple coats of paint on metallic works that looked shiny and lacquered,” says Sylvain Cordier, the Paul Mellon curator and head of the department of European art at the Virginia Museum of Fine Arts. “I’mconvinced that starting with costume jewelry very much informed a taste for bold colors.”

A 1960s Jean Schlumberger drawing of a Paillonné enamel bangle for Tiffany & Co.

In the ’60s, at Tiffany, Schlumberger would return to this vivid style in more sophisticated form through the art of paillonné, a process that layers translucent colored enamel on delicate gold or silver leaf, requiring a series of kiln firings. His first paillonné piece, the Croisillon bracelet, debuted in 1962. Philanthropist and Schlumberger devotee Bunny Mellon soon introduced her friend Jackie Kennedy to the style, which the first lady wore so often it became known as the “Jackie bangle.” Schlumberger also applied the enameling to earrings and decorative objects prized by collectors like Mellon, whose Schlumberger collection is held at the Virginia MFA.

Rings from the Enamel by Tiffany collection

Tiffany, which recently announced a collaboration with the Musée des Arts Décoratifs in Paris to preserve Schlumberger’s archives, continues to honor the artistry of his paillonné pieces with its Enamel by Tiffany collection. The house, which employs some of the only remaining artisans skilled in the technique, is expanding these offerings this fall with new pieces that include rings and watches. Despite the decades that have passed since the Croisillon’s debut, Cordier still finds it to be fresh. “When you look at the Schlumberger pieces, there’s this sort of abstract effect,” he says. “I think it’s very modern.”
