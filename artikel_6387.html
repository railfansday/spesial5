California schools must drop ultra-processed food, and kitchen managers explain why that’s hard

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

California will phase ultra-processed foods out of school meals, but kitchen directors say money, staff and small kitchens stand in the way.

Taking junk food off school menus sounds like a matter of writing a rule. California has written one, and starting in 2029 its schools must begin phasing out many ultra-processed foods.

But when researchers asked the people who run school kitchens what makes cooking from scratch hard, the answers were money, staff and kitchens built mostly to reheat.

Many districts even buy basic ingredients, then pay to have them turned into the processed foods that the law targets.

That’s the gap between a law and a lunch line, and the directors said closing it will take more than a deadline.

The Real Food, Healthy Kids Act requires California schools to begin phasing out certain ultra-processed foods in July 2029 and finish by 2035. These are factory-made foods with ingredients that home cooks rarely use, such as flavorings, colorings and emulsifiers.

Such foods make up about two-thirds of the calories U.S. children eat, according to studies the authors cite. School meals can supply about half of a child’s daily calories, which makes the cafeteria a big lever on what kids eat.

Andrea Pedroza-Tobias, then an instructor at Stanford Medicine and now at UC Irvine, led the study with pediatrician Anisha Patel.

Community groups, including the Dolores Huerta Foundation and Cultiva La Salud, helped shape it, along with the Nutrition Policy Institute at the University of California.

From January to June 2024, the team interviewed food service directors from 20 school districts in California’s San Joaquin Valley, a farming region with many low-income families.

The districts were picked at random from urban and rural areas, and from districts with and without a state grant for freshly prepared meals.

Sixteen of the directors also filled out a short survey. On average, their districts served some scratch-cooked items about 2.5 days a week and heat-and-serve foods about 3 days a week.

That is a small group in one region. The authors note that the results may not match other parts of the country, and that directors who agreed to talk may care more about scratch cooking than those who didn’t.

Two-thirds of the surveyed directors identified staff shortages and a lack of money for labor as obstacles. Many rely on part-time workers who can reheat food but don’t have time to cook it. Storage and kitchen space came next.

State grants for kitchen equipment helped, but the money went different ways. Some districts bought skillets and ranges for cooking, while others bought more ovens to assist with reheating. Directors also said one-year grants made it hard to plan for the long run.

Most did credit California’s free meals for every student with bringing in more money, and in some districts more cooks.

In an interview with Earth.com, Patel and Pedroza-Tobias said the money has to come with guidelines for using it. “However, funding alone is not enough,” they said. They pointed to policies, practices and technical assistance paired with funding.

Schools get raw ingredients through a federal food program, but many districts ship them out to be processed. “I’m sending my chicken to, let’s say, Tyson and getting chicken patties and chicken nuggets,” one director told the researchers.

Directors also said most products labeled and sold for schools, the ones built to meet federal nutrition rules, are ultra-processed.

Amanda Harvey, who runs nutrition services for Fresno Unified and sat on the study’s advisory board, has seen supply problems firsthand. When her district dropped products with food dye, pickles made without green dye became hard to find.

“They’re pricey for pickles, and it can be quite challenging for our vendor to get them,” Harvey said. Another director recalled spending years removing cooking equipment and buying more processed food, and now using grant money to buy the same kinds of kettles and skillets back.

Some directors worried students would skip scratch-cooked meals and throw more food away. Others said students preferred fresh, familiar food, and they described ways to win kids over, including taste tests, family nutrition nights and student recipe contests.

Asked by Earth.com what surprised them most, the researchers pointed to districts that found creative ways around tight budgets. “One food service director even dressed in costumes to encourage children to try healthier foods,” they said.

They also noted that at least seven other states have passed laws on specific food additives or dyes, though most still allow some ultra-processed foods. Brazil’s national school meal program, they said, requires at least 85 percent of federal food funds to go to fresh or minimally processed foods.

The directors described what they expect and what they’ve tried. The study didn’t measure what children actually ate, how their health changed, or whether the new law’s timeline is realistic.

The authors call for money to hire and keep staff, upgrade kitchens and train cooks, along with rules that make ultra-processed products less attractive to buy.

Whether California’s districts can make that switch by 2035 will show up on real trays over the next decade, and no one has measured it yet.

Research on kids’ behavior and toddler foods has kept attention on processed food in childhood, and on its health risks. Schools may be where the change is easiest to track.

The full study was published in the journal Public Health Nutrition.
