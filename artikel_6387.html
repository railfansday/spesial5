Exercise may be one of the most overlooked treatments for depression

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Psychiatrists say doctors should prescribe exercise more clearly because physical activity may improve depression, memory, and mental health.

A five-minute walk can sound easy until depression has taken away your energy. Then a doctor’s advice to get more exercise may leave you wondering where to begin.

Trials show that exercise can ease symptoms of depression.

Three Canadian psychiatrists argue that doctors should prescribe exercise with the same care and attention they give antidepressant medications.

Carl Zhou and Nicholas Fabiano work in the psychiatry department at the University of Ottawa, and Stanley Wong works in the psychiatry department at the University of Toronto.

Their opinion piece draws on earlier research, with no new experiment of their own.

“We still tend to treat exercise as general lifestyle advice,” Zhou said.

“The evidence we reviewed supports something more deliberate: a prescription that names the type of activity, the frequency, the intensity, the duration, the safety considerations, and a plan for follow-up, just as we would for a medication.”

A muscle doesn’t just move a limb when it contracts. It also releases molecules called myokines, which may interact with chemical pathways involved in mood and thinking.

Researchers call that proposed connection the muscle-brain axis. The authors discuss it as one explanation for what exercise does, while making room for several others.

The brain needs a healthy blood supply and metabolism, and it needs inflammation kept under control. Learning, meanwhile, means changing the connections between its cells.

Exercise influences the heart, blood vessels, immune system, and growth signals that help with learning.

Better sleep, more confidence and social contact, and lower risks of blood vessel and metabolic disease may help explain its effects as well.

Kirk Erickson’s team spent a year studying exercise and the brains of 120 older adults without dementia.

In that 2011 trial, half the volunteers did moderate aerobic exercise, while the rest did stretching.

The team was interested in the hippocampus, which helps us remember things.

This region grew by about two percent in the aerobic group, mainly at the front, but shrank in the stretching group.

The researchers described the increase as reversing one to two years of age-related shrinkage.

Among those who exercised, greater growth went along with greater improvement on a memory test.

Being active has also been linked to better thinking skills and lower risks of dementia and Alzheimer’s disease.

Those links come largely from watching people’s habits, so those studies alone can’t tell us that exercise made the difference.

Researchers have put exercise to the test for depression and anxiety in randomized trials, letting chance decide who goes into each group.

The results show that activity programs can ease symptoms.

Walking and jogging helped, as did yoga and strength training.

A 2024 review in The BMJ reached that finding after bringing together 218 trials with 14,170 people who had depression.

More intense exercise generally brought greater relief, but there were reasons to be cautious about the research.

Only one trial passed the reviewers’ strictest check for avoiding bias, and their confidence in the findings was low or very low.

Someone struggling to exercise may find the next finding more encouraging.

A 2022 analysis of 15 long-term studies, covering 191,130 adults, found a link with lower depression risk even below the recommended activity level.

People who did about half the recommended amount had an 18% lower risk than those who reported no activity. Those doing the full amount had a 25% lower risk.

These studies observed people’s habits, so the figures don’t prove cause and effect. The largest difference appeared between doing nothing and doing a little.

For patients who can’t reach the full target, the authors suggest about 75 minutes of moderate activity a week as a place to start.

That’s roughly half the usual recommended minimum.

Medicines for schizophrenia offer limited help with thinking difficulties, even though those difficulties affect how a person manages everyday life. Exercise may have something to offer there.

Ten trials involving 385 people with schizophrenia went into a 2017 analysis, which found better overall thinking with aerobic exercise.

Exercise also showed potential benefits for working memory, which helps people hold and use information, and social cognition, which helps them understand others.

People with schizophrenia and other severe mental illnesses tend to spend long hours sitting and have high rates of heart disease, metabolic disease, and early death.

The authors point partly to the illness, social disadvantage, and the metabolic side effects of some medicines.

“In schizophrenia, exercise can work on psychiatric symptoms, cognition, and physical health at the same time, in a population with substantial unmet needs in all three,” Wong said.

The thinking results came from only a few small trials, though.

The World Health Organization recommends 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week for adults, plus muscle-strengthening work on at least two days.

Brisk walking counts as moderate; for vigorous activity, such as running or fast cycling, the range is 75 to 150 minutes.

A patient doesn’t have to begin at that level.

If depression has drained their energy, motivation, and concentration, the first step might be a five-minute walk, a walk with the dog, or exercises in a chair.

“The starting point does not have to be impressive,” Fabiano said. “It has to be possible.”

For most patients, the authors suggest a low-risk activity that they can gradually do more of.

Pain or dizziness may signal a need to adjust the exercise plan, while chest pain or severe breathlessness calls for immediate medical attention.

Others may need a medical check, physiotherapy, supervision, or a brief pause before increasing their activity.

That includes people who are frail or have unstable heart or breathing symptoms, recent falls or fainting, an infection, or uncontrolled pain.

Wong wants larger trials to find out whether the thinking gains seen in schizophrenia hold up. The existing trials leave that question open.

Researchers are also exploring exercise mimetics, drugs or other agents that might copy some of the chemical changes exercise causes.

These could, in theory, help with depression in people whose symptoms, disability, or frailty prevent them from moving enough.

The authors see these as a possible addition to treatment, with safe activity continuing wherever it is possible.

They also point out that exercise prescriptions could widen health gaps if patients can’t get the help they need to follow them.

They want funded community exercise programs, help with transportation, and partnerships with recreation and community centers.

The goal is not to turn every patient into an athlete, but to make physical activity a realistic, supported part of mental health treatment.

The opinion piece is published in the journal Brain Health.
