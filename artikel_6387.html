Memory tests may miss clues hidden in the time it takes to answer

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Memory tests may reveal more than correct answers, as slower responses in older adults could signal subtle changes.

Getting an answer right on a memory test is usually considered a good sign. But what if it takes someone much longer to reach that answer?

That delay might tell us something important.

New research suggests that the time older adults take to answer memory questions could reveal subtle changes in how their brains work, even when they manage to give the right answers.

Anglia Ruskin University researchers found that older adults with possible mild cognitive impairment (MCI) answered some memory questions more slowly than healthy older adults. Distractions made the gap clearer.

MCI affects memory and thinking beyond normal aging, but people can usually manage daily life. Some later develop dementia, though many do not.

Professor Shahina Pardhan, who worked on the study, thinks measuring answer times alongside memory scores could help doctors spot early changes. The idea needs more testing.

The study brought together 115 people. There were 34 young adults, aged 18 to 30, and 61 healthy older adults, aged 55 or above.

Another 20 older adults had scored below the cutoff on at least one of two standard screening tests. The team placed them in an at-risk group for mild cognitive impairment, or MCI.

Nobody in that group had a confirmed MCI diagnosis. Those screening results were a reason to investigate, rather than proof that someone had the condition.

People with MCI have more difficulty with memory and thinking than normal aging would explain, but can usually get on with everyday activities. Some later develop dementia, though many do not.

In the word task, people watched two or four color words appear briefly in different places. The computer then showed one again and asked whether it was in the place where they’d seen it before.

Participants had to remember each word’s location while ignoring its ink color.

A mismatch between a color word and its ink, such as “red” printed in blue, can create interference known as the Stroop effect.

The at-risk group got more answers wrong than both the young and healthy older groups.

With two words to remember, they took longer to answer too; the timing differences between groups weren’t clear enough to rule out chance with four words.

There was no instruction to hurry. Participants were asked to get the answers right, while the computer recorded how long each response took.

In the arrow task, an arrow could point in one direction while moving in another. For example, it might point left and move right, or point and move in the same direction.

At the time they saw an arrow, participants didn’t know what they’d be asked to recall about it.

Either its movement or the way it pointed could come up in the question, so they had to remember both.

It wasn’t possible to pick one detail and ignore the other throughout the task, as they could with ink color.

Remembering both might have changed how they dealt with a mismatch, the researchers suggested.

With two arrows to remember or four, the at-risk group answered more slowly than healthy older adults.

But unlike the two other groups, they showed no clear extra delay when an arrow’s movement and pointing direction clashed.

Healthy older adults did about as well as young adults with the words, but made more mistakes with the arrows. The two groups answered at similar speeds on the arrow task despite that difference in mistakes.

For young adults and healthy older adults, wrong answers on the word test generally took longer than correct ones.

Perhaps they hesitated because they weren’t sure, the researchers suggested.

The 20 at-risk participants took roughly the same time whether they were right or wrong. One possibility is that they were less aware of making mistakes, though the study didn’t establish this.

And that pattern didn’t carry over to the arrows. All three groups took longer when they gave a wrong answer on that task.

For both older groups, words in matching ink were quicker to deal with than words in clashing ink. Young adults showed little measurable change between those conditions.

The at-risk participants were slowest of all on the conflicting word trials.

They were also slower than healthy older adults on the arrows overall, so the lack of an extra delay from mismatched arrows wasn’t evidence of better performance.

Answers to the word task weren’t clearly less accurate when the ink clashed.

That’s something an accuracy score alone could miss: people could still choose correctly, while needing more time to do it.

The researchers suggested that ignoring the ink took extra effort.

Age can make it harder to keep unwanted information out, and memory and thinking problems may add to that difficulty.

Researchers tested participants once, so they couldn’t link slower answers to later memory loss or MCI. The at-risk group included only 20 people, none with a confirmed MCI diagnosis.

The reasons for the delays remain unclear, and the lab tasks may not reflect everyday memory.

Larger studies following people over time, including adults with diagnosed MCI, could help test the approach.

Professor Shahina Pardhan, who worked on the study, sees promise in measuring response times.

“These findings suggest that measuring reaction times alongside memory performance could help clinicians detect subtle cognitive changes at an earlier stage,” Pardhan said.

“As populations continue to age and dementia becomes an increasing public health challenge, there is a growing need for sensitive, accessible tools that can identify people at greater risk before symptoms become more severe.”

The full study was published in the journal Brain Sciences.
