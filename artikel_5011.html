There's a lot coming at you on a daily basis to stress you out. But even though you probably lean into the big de-stressors like exercising regularly, doing breathing exercises on demand, and talk therapy as needed, sometimes you just need a way to dial down the tension building up inside you without visiting a provider's office or hitting the gym. That's where acupressure pens come in.

These handheld tools, which are sometimes called acupuncture pens, are designed to target pressure points in your body to help relieve tension and pain. "Acupressure stimulates acupuncture points without using needles," says Tom Ingegno, DACM, acupuncturist and founder of Charm City Integrative Health. "It lets people take a more active role in their own health without necessarily seeing a licensed [acupuncture] practitioner."

An acupressure pen has a shape that's optimized to stimulate acupuncture points, or areas on the body that are linked to certain health benefits, explains Margie Navarro, DAOM, doctor of acupuncture at Vivacity Healthcare in New York City. "The tool has a rather small head that is smooth and rounded, so that it is a relatively comfortable way to stimulate pressure on a small area or point," she says.

People use these tools to ease anxiety, headaches, and general pain, and there is some data to suggest acupressure in general may be helpful in treating all of these. (Because each acupressure pen is slightly different, it's a little tricky to prove that they'll work for these issues.)

Still, acupuncture experts say these pens are unlikely to hurt if you want to give them a try. "It gives people a way to play an active role in their health," Navarro says. "Even just a sense of agency can improve health outcomes." Sounds like something you can get behind? These acupressure pens can get you started.

You have a few options for use with an acupressure pen. In general, these are designed to stimulate pressure points in your body. Most people use acupressure pens for some type of pain relief, whether it's headaches or TMJ discomfort. But these pens can also be used to ease anxiety and stress.

Many acupressure pens are made of medical-grade stainless steel. However, some are made from brass or other metals. You can also find acupressure pens made of wood, although these are less common.

Every pen is slightly different, making it important to read the manufacturer's directions first. But you can typically clean an acupressure pen by wiping it with a soft cloth before you use it.

Acupressure pens shouldn't break your budget, and most cost $25 or less. "The key is the stimulation of the point, not necessarily the tool you're using," Ingegno says. "You don't need an expensive device to practice acupressure, but a pen can make it easier."

There is data to suggest that acupressure can help with easing a range of pain and discomfort. "Manual pressure can have therapeutic benefits in a variety of conditions, including muscular pain," says Navarro. But you need to know which areas to target. And, of course, not everyone will feel pain or stress relief from acupressure, regardless of whether you get it from a pen or a provider.

Ingegno suggests learning about pressure points in the body before using an acupressure pen. "I used to recommend books, but these days there's so much information online that it's pretty easy to find a starting point," he says. Once you've figured out the area you want to target, he suggests applying gentle to moderate pressure for about 30 to 60 seconds. "Some points may feel a little sore or tender when pressed, and that's not necessarily a bad thing, but you shouldn't be causing yourself pain," Ingegno says. "More pressure isn't always better."

You can apply steady pressure on a point or gently rub or massage the area with the tool, according to Ingegno. "Neither approach is wrong, and both can be useful," he says.

There are electronic acupressure pens, which deliver small electrical impulses to pressure points. "Follow the manufacturer's instructions, since there are several different types available and they don't all work the same way," Ingegno says.

Most people can safely use acupressure pens, according to Ingegno. But there are a few caveats. If you're pregnant, he recommends taking a pass. If you're using blood thinners, Ingegno also suggests being gentle, since you may bruise more easily. "Electronic acupressure pens also require a few additional precautions," Ingegno says. "People with pacemakers or other implanted electronic medical devices should avoid electrical stimulation unless their healthcare provider has specifically cleared them."

If you have broken skin or areas where the tissue is already damaged, Navarro recommends avoiding using the acupressure pen on that area.
