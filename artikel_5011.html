Fed Chair Warsh Wants Answers on AI Inflation—and Bitcoin May Feel the Impact

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh will convene a public forum Monday to examine whether artificial intelligence will bring down inflation, drive prices higher, or replace American workers.

The November 16 conference in San Francisco will bring together technology executives, economists, and employers to examine how AI is reshaping the economy.

The findings could influence how the Fed approaches interest rates, a major factor in Bitcoin's price movements as investors weigh whether to put money into risky assets or safer alternatives.

Warsh has argued that AI could help companies produce more goods and services at lower costs, easing inflation without requiring the Fed to keep borrowing expensive.

But some of his colleagues see a different problem: The AI boom is already making things more expensive, while the promised productivity benefits remain largely theoretical.

Building data centers requires enormous quantities of electricity, computer chips, construction materials, and skilled workers. When technology companies compete for those limited resources, prices rise.

Fed Governor Lisa Cook warned in September that AI investment was adding inflationary pressure and delaying the central bank's return to its 2% inflation target.

If AI eventually allows businesses to produce more without raising costs, inflation could cool, giving policymakers room to lower interest rates.

Lower rates generally make risky assets like Bitcoin or tech stocks more attractive because government bonds and savings accounts offer smaller returns, encouraging investors to seek higher gains elsewhere.

But if AI spending keeps inflation elevated, the Fed could maintain high interest rates or raise them further. That would make borrowing more expensive, strengthen the appeal of interest-bearing assets, and potentially drain money from speculative investments, including cryptocurrencies.

Bitcoin is already feeling that pressure. The cryptocurrency dropped below $69,000 Friday as stronger-than-expected inflation figures fueled concerns about another Fed rate hike.

AI also has a more direct connection to the Bitcoin industry than most may think of.

Both AI data centers and Bitcoin mining operations need access to large amounts of electricity. As AI companies compete for power and infrastructure, miners face pressure to secure affordable energy or consider more profitable alternatives.

Some are already abandoning Bitcoin mining altogether. Hyperscale Data shut down mining operations at its Michigan facility in September to accommodate an AI customer under a deal potentially worth more than $1.2 billion. The same has happened with other big names like Coreweave and other former Ethereum miners from its proof-of-work era.

Meanwhile, AI's potential impact on employment adds another complication.

Fed Governor Michael Barr warned Thursday of possible “serious short-term disruptions” as businesses increasingly replace human tasks with AI systems.

Widespread job losses could weaken consumer spending, slow economic growth, and eventually increase pressure on the Fed to cut rates. But a deteriorating economy could also push investors away from Bitcoin as they look to reduce risk.

Warsh himself acknowledged in August that AI represents “a new variable” for monetary policy.

The question now is whether AI's economic benefits will arrive quickly enough to outweigh the costs of building it.

Monday's conference will not determine the Fed's next interest-rate decision, scheduled for December 9. But it could offer clues about how policymakers view a technology boom that is increasingly influencing both Wall Street and the crypto market.
