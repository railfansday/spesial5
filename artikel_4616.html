If philosophers can contribute anything to the debates in biology, it is at least conceptual clarity. Few debates needed it more than the one over group selection, i.e. the idea that natural selection can favour what is good for the group and not only what is good for the individual. It is a debate that in fact continues to this day. This is why I've recently published an academic article on the topic.

One of the most important additions made to biology was to bring clarity into the group-selection debate, by clarifying what group selection actually means. Thanks to Damuth and Heisler, much conceptual confusion was able to be resolved. They distinguished two kinds of multi-level selection.

In the first kind, “group selection” refers to the effects of group membership on individual fitness. Fitnesses are properties of individuals, and populations consist of individuals, organised into groups. Group fitness is here simply defined as the average fitness of the group’s members. Groups reaping the benefits of cooperation will do better than groups who fail to do so.

In the second kind, “group selection” refers to change in the frequencies of different kinds of groups. Fitnesses are properties of groups, and populations consist of groups, composed of individuals. Groups are here Darwinian individuals themselves, taking part in Darwinian processes without having their fitness reducible to the fitness of their members. Nevertheless, akin to matryoshka dolls, lower-level entities constitute the higher-level entity, with selection acting on both levels.

This ambiguity lies at the heart of much of the dispute in the group selection debate.

While Elliott Sober and David Sloan Wilson defended group selection in the first sense, John Maynard Smith attacked their model for not presenting a genuine case of group selection, i.e. for failing to satisfy the criteria of the second. Both sides of the debate were partly right.

Samir Okasha’s Evolution and the Levels of Selection elegantly showed that much of the debate was ill-informed and could have been resolved if only there had been more clarity about what group selection is actually supposed to mean. As group selection has become an almost derogatory term, some of its proponents were led to use the term multi-level selection instead.

It arguably took the combined efforts of biologists and philosophers of science for multi-level selection to become an accepted explanatory strategy again, serving as an exemplar of the importance and impact of philosophy of biology.

There is just one major conceptual question left, and that is how we move from mere cooperation to having a genuine individual. A group of cooperating individuals is still distinct from a group of cells constituting a new higher-level individual, such as a multicellular organism. The biologist Richard Michod termed the move from one to the other fitness decoupling: the fitness of the ‘group’ becomes independent of the average fitness of its cells. It becomes a Darwinian individual, with multiple groups forming Darwinian populations themselves.

The experimental biologist Paul Rainey points out that researchers working on the evolution of individuality have focused too much on group selection in the first sense, rather than the necessary transition to the second. Mere cooperation between group members is insufficient to get from one to the other. This led him to engage in theorising on the evolution of individuality, seeking contact with philosophers such as Kim Sterelny and Peter Godfrey-Smith. The result is a series of interdisciplinary papers, ranging from philosophy of biology to experimental biology, proposing a re-evaluation of the role cheats play in evolution.

Okasha’s philosophical work on multi-level selection proved to be quite successful in generating a progressive research programme. In a mere decade, the study of evolutionary transitions underwent significant progress. Philosophers should be able to ease conceptual confusions and make further theoretical work much more fruitful. Here they did.
