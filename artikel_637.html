‘Focker-in-Law’ Review: Ariana Grande Sporadically Livens Up a Tired Comedy Retread

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

The fourth entry in the wearily aging 'Fockers' series calls back to lots of old jokes, and makes precious few new ones.

Midway through “Focker-in-Law,” perma-neurotic nurse Gaylord ‘Greg’ Focker (Ben Stiller) has a sudden, unhappy realization: He’s a few years older now than his hardass father-in-law Jack Byrnes (Robert De Niro) was when they first met. It’s sobering for him, sure, but for us too. 26 years after “Meet the Parents,” 60-year-old Stiller does actually have a few years on 2000-era De Niro. More startling still is that they’ve both spent over a quarter of a century milking easy snickers from the name “Focker,” now over the course of four feature films. Time is long, but also short, and we feel both those truths in “Focker-in-Law,” a pretty haggard sequel in which, joke-wise, time also stands perfectly still: Now Greg is the cranky patriarch wary of his adult child’s future spouse, and a whole pile of rusty plot points have cycled back into view. It goes almost without saying that nobody needs “Focker-in-Law,” a film that, 16 years after we last checked in on these characters in “Little Fockers,” arrives on screen with a dispiriting air of exhausted obligation — like a stilted family reunion arranged in the wake of some ruinous, unspoken rift. Stiller and De Niro have agreed to go through the motions and not a muscle strain more; Owen Wilson, Teri Polo and Blythe Danner have all committed to rather less than that; an absent Dustin Hoffman and Barbra Streisand send their apologies via a one-line alibi in the script. It’s practically by default, then, that new marquee name Ariana Grande is the film’s brightest presence by several hundred watts: Joining the series keen to further prove her screen comedy chops after “Wicked,” she’s the only star who looks remotely glad to be here.

Assuming the director’s chair after writing credits on all three previous entries, John Hamburg offers scant reward for her enthusiasm: Not given a lot of outright comic material to work with, she settles for propping up proceedings with generally peppy charisma. In this reworking of the “Meet the Parents” narrative, Grande assumes Stiller’s former place: As Olivia, the go-getter girlfriend of Greg’s son Henry (Skyler Gisondo), she’s the eager-to-please newcomer in the family. Where Greg was a hapless social disaster, however, she’s annoyingly collected and capable at every turn. A former FBI hostage negotiator turned executive leadership coach, she immediately impresses everyone in all the ways that Greg did not all those years ago — with ex-CIA man Jack, her potential future grandfather-in-law, particularly enraptured.

A gracious, attractive overachiever winning hearts with smarts and kindness isn’t exactly a prime setup for comedy of embarrassment, however, and so it’s Greg who must once again assume bumbling stooge duty — something Stiller can do in his sleep, and more or less does here. Animosity between Greg and Olivia is contrived early on, as various Fockers head east for Jack’s 80th birthday celebrations; an awkward misunderstanding on the flight over, before either character realizes the other’s identity, has Olivia thinking her boyfriend’s dad is at best an idiot, and at worst a meddling pervert. Even after they’re formally introduced, he proceeds to prove that best-case scenario several times over, across a strained parade of competitive scenarios: a vegan-versus-carnivore cookoff, a power-play in a party supplies store, a calamitous bicycle race for no reason at all.

These face-offs are suitably cringey, but they’re not terribly funny. Where “Meet the Parents” thrived on the farcical tension between De Niro and Stiller as, respectively, alpha and beta male, there’s considerably less mirth in Grande’s formidable superwoman repeatedly besting Stiller’s faintly tragic doofus — and not much chemistry between the two stars either. Hamburg’s script attempts to juice up the emotional stakes with a who-cares subplot about whether Henry will study medicine alongside his father in Chicago or in Olivia’s hometown of San Diego, but the character is such a formless dullard that he hardly seems worth tussling over.

As Henry, the reliably sympathetic Gisondo gets little to do; Beanie Feldstein, an afterthought as Henry’s ditzy twin sister Samantha, would be glad of that much. For a film so thin on ideas, “Focker-in-Law” feels over-populated with supporting characters — as Samantha’s zany boyfriend, Eduardo Franco occasionally makes laughs out of nothing at all, but more often just adds to the clutter — with the result that De Niro, who’s meant to be the driving star force of the series, too often fades grayly into the background. Despite Grande’s sparkiest efforts, it hardly feels coincidental that the one hilarious setpiece here plays out between the two male leads: a toe-curlingly misplaced serenade to John Legend’s treacly ballad “All of Me” that the film cuts oddly short.

Elsewhere, Hamburg merely settles for coasting on past jokes — not even replaying them much of the time, but merely pointing at their props and punchlines. There’s an urn, and a polygraph machine, and a taxidermied cat, all hoping to remind us of how we laughed then. Consider us reminded. But is it churlish to ask for more? Or, perhaps, less? Like a favorite dad joke told too many times, the “Fockers” franchise gets staler with each spin, while Olivia’s sequel-teasing mention of her own parents at the close prompts a kind of existential dread. Never mind how old Greg is, or how old Jack is, or indeed how old we are: The Fockers, it seems, will outlive us all.
