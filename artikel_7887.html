What Your Cat Can Teach You About Aging Well

How to recognize the subtle difference between normal aging and cognitive decline, and help your senior cat stay engaged, comfortable, and independent

Watch a healthy older cat move through her day.

She may take a little longer to rise from her favorite chair. She may think twice before jumping onto a countertop she once reached without hesitation. She sleeps more, chooses her routines carefully, and has become remarkably skilled at persuading the people around her to bring things closer.

But she is still interested in breakfast. She still appears when a favorite person enters the room. She watches birds through the window, investigates a new cardboard box, and may still insist that 4:57 a.m. is a perfectly reasonable time to discuss breakfast.

The difference matters, and I learned that the hard way with my own cat.

Houdini was an eighteen-year-old black long-haired male who had never given me reason to worry. Then he started calling out at night, insistent enough that I could not ignore it. It would have been easy to write off as just aging. Old cats do that.

I ran a full workup instead. It showed kidney values just slightly above normal and nothing else. Apart from the calling, he still seemed to be enjoying his life. Between the pattern of the vocalizing and a workup that ruled out anything else driving it, the diagnosis was feline cognitive dysfunction.

I lost Houdini to kidney failure about six months later.

His story is a small, real version of the point this whole article is making. The calling was not eccentricity, and it was not only age. It was cognitive change, arriving quietly, in a cat who by almost every other measure still seemed like himself.

Scientists increasingly distinguish between two ideas: lifespan, how long we live, and healthspan, the years we remain healthy, active, independent, and engaged with life.

It is a concept that applies beautifully to cats.

For a cat, aging well means more than reaching 15, 17, or even 20. It means continuing to move comfortably, use the litter box, groom, sleep peacefully, interact with family, explore familiar surroundings, and remain interested in the world.

And sometimes, the first sign that something is changing is not found in a laboratory.

Cats are remarkably good at making gradual change difficult to notice.

A dog that suddenly struggles to climb the stairs tends to make the problem obvious. A cat simply stops going upstairs. The favorite sleeping place moves from the top of the bookcase to the sofa. The cat who once greeted you at the door is always asleep when you come home.

The litter-box accident is blamed on stubbornness. The midnight crying is dismissed as eccentricity. The cat who no longer jumps onto the bed is just getting old.

Some slowing is expected with age. But new or progressive changes should not automatically be dismissed as a normal part of aging.

Jump less often or choose lower resting places.

Need easier access to food, water, litter boxes, or favorite furniture.

Changes to discuss with your veterinarian

Stare at walls or hesitate at familiar doorways.

Sleep much of the day but become restless after dark.

Become unusually withdrawn, clingy, anxious, or irritable.

Begin eliminating outside the litter box.

Show a marked decline in activity or interest.

Changes in memory, awareness, sleep, and behavior can occur with feline cognitive dysfunction, the feline counterpart of age-related dementia, but they may also be caused by pain or illness.[1]

Arthritis can make a cat stop jumping. Kidney disease, diabetes, or thyroid disease can change drinking, urination, activity, and sleep. High blood pressure can affect vision and behavior. Pain, hearing loss, and declining eyesight can all make an older cat behave differently.[2]

That is why "sheâs just getting old" can be one of the most misleading explanations in senior-cat care. Old age itself is not a disease. Sometimes a subtle behavioral change is the first clue that something treatable is happening.

How she asks for food and whether she greets you at the door.

Which chair she prefers and how often she plays.

Whether she has always talked at night, or only started recently.

Those ordinary observations are valuable because many age-related changes happen gradually. Once a month, stop and consciously look at your cat as though you had not seen her for several weeks.

Do her back or hindquarters look thinner?

Has nighttime activity or vocalization changed?

Does she still interact with people and other pets in the same way?

Does she ever seem confused in familiar places?

Is she still interested in food, play, windows, smells, and everyday family activity?

Look for change from your catâs own normal. A 17-year-old cat who has always slept most of the day is very different from a 12-year-old whose sleep pattern suddenly changes.

Owners cannot observe their cats 24 hours a day. A few simple tools can help fill in the gaps.

Some owners use a cat-friendly wearable activity monitor to track movement and rest over time. The value is never one isolated number. It is the pattern, and whether that pattern is shifting from the catâs own normal.

The simplest tool is your phone. Every few months, record your cat walking, jumping, climbing stairs, playing, or interacting with you. A video from six months ago often reveals gradual change that memory alone smooths over. Senior-cat care guidelines specifically encourage tracking mobility and behavior at home, because subtle changes are otherwise easy to miss.[2]

One of the most useful discoveries about feline aging concerns something owners can actually see and feel: muscle.

Researchers who repeatedly examined 209 pet cats, some for as long as seven years, found that muscle condition often began declining between about 7 and 10 years of age and deteriorated more rapidly as cats became older.[3]

Body weight did not always fall at the same time.

That means a cat can appear to maintain roughly the same number on the scale while quietly losing muscle. Muscle that helps an older cat stand, balance, jump, climb into the litter box, groom, and remain independent.

Look along the spine, shoulders, hips, and hind legs.

Does she pull herself onto furniture with her front legs instead of springing upward?

Has she stopped visiting favorite places because they are harder to reach?

These changes deserve attention even when body weight seems stable.

Aging well is not simply about surviving another birthday. It is about preserving the ability to do things.

Cats teach another useful lesson about aging: Independence sometimes depends on accepting help.

A cat who no longer jumps onto the bed may still want to sleep beside you. A small step or ramp can restore that choice.

A cat with painful joints may stop using a litter box not because she has forgotten her training, but because climbing over the side hurts.

A cat who becomes confused at night may do better when furniture stays in familiar places, and the path between sleeping areas, water, food, and litter boxes is simple and predictable.

Consistent placement of important resources.

Easy access to windows, scents, toys, and other forms of stimulation.

Regular social contact, on the catâs terms.

The goal is not to make an old cat behave like a young cat. It is to help an old cat do the things that make her life her own.

The science behind feline aging has become especially interesting in the past few years. A 2026 study comparing thousands of observations across cats, humans, and other mammals found that cat and human brains age in strikingly similar ways, estimating that a 15-year-old cat is at roughly the same stage of brain aging as an 80-year-old person.[5] That is not a simple cat-years-to-human-years formula, but it does suggest cats experience brain aging in ways surprisingly relevant to us.

Scientists have also found deposits of amyloid-beta, the protein associated with Alzheimerâs disease in people, in the brains of cats that had shown signs of cognitive dysfunction.[6] Cats are not tiny humans, but the overlap is strong enough that naturally aging cats may help researchers understand how brains age in both species.

None of this points to a single cause or a single fix. Muscle, movement, sleep, pain, metabolism, kidney function, and brain health all seem to influence each other in aging cats,[7] which is why healthy aging rarely comes from one supplement or treatment. It is a whole-animal process.

A major 2024 study examining nearly 8,000 cat deaths in the United Kingdom estimated average life expectancy at birth at about 11.7 years, although females lived somewhat longer than males and mixed-breed cats lived longer than purebred cats as groups.[8]

But averages do not predict the future of one individual cat.

One encouraging finding was that a cat who had already reached 15 still had, on average, about 2.7 additional years of expected life in that population.[8]

A 15th birthday does not necessarily mean the end is near.

How can we make the years ahead good ones?

There is something wonderfully symmetrical about all of this.

We are beginning to study cats because they may help us understand how brains and bodies age, and at the same time, they may already be teaching us the larger lesson.

Aging well may not mean remaining unchanged. It may mean adapting while preserving what matters:

And the desire to remain engaged with the world.

There may even be a benefit flowing in the other direction.

A 2025 study found that cat and dog owners showed a slower decline in some measures of memory and thinking than people who did not own pets. Among cat owners, the association included verbal ability and delayed memory.[9]

The study cannot prove that cats caused the difference, but it offers a delightful possibility:

While we study our cats to understand healthy aging, sharing our lives with them may also be connected with aspects of healthier aging in us.

That is part of why my colleagues and I at Veterinary Health Research Centers developed CATMA, Cats Advancing Toward Mindful Aging, to help owners and veterinarians recognize meaningful change earlier.

The next time you see your older cat asleep in a patch of sunlight, consider what she may be telling you.

She knows whether she can reach her favorite chair.

Whether her legs carry her where she wants to go.

Whether there are still interesting things to investigate.

Perhaps that is as good a definition of aging well as any of us has found.
