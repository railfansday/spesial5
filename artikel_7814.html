A right-angle drill solves problems when a standard drill can’t reach—navigating between studs, working inside cabinets, or tackling tight corners. While this makes these specialized power tools handy in these situations, who truly needs a right-angle drill?

Plumbers, electricians, carpenters, and pretty much anyone involved in construction and renovation projects will appreciate having one handy. Even hobbyists and homeowners may need a right-angle drill when tackling certain jobs, such as installing cabinetry or assembling furniture.

We test the top right-angle drills to find the best models for pros and homeowners. Our favorite was the Ridgid thanks to its comprehensive kit, durable build, and impressive power. Below, find the results of our hands-on tests and learn how these models earned their spot in this lineup of the best right-angle drills.

Any of the following right-angle drills will make it easier to drill holes and drive screws in tight areas, but each one comes with some pros and cons. We’ve detailed both the benefits and the downsides noted during our tests. Some models are best suited to light-duty use, while others are powerhouses designed with construction pros in mind. There’s sure to be one that will suit your project needs.

Ridgid’s cordless right-angle drill manages to be compact and lightweight while still offering  tremendous drilling and driving power.

Its head measures just 3.5 inches long, making it one of the shortest 18V or 20V angle drills we tested. Couple that with its slim profile and short length, and it fits easily into tight spaces inside cabinets and under-sink vanities.

Right-angle drills tend to be top-heavy, which can make them awkward to hold. With its compact size and light weight, the Ridgid was an exception. It felt comfortable and well balanced in the hand, which is significant given that these tools are often used in tight spaces where it’s not possible to get a two-handed grip. Adding to its maneuverability is a long trigger that’s easy to access from various hand positions. The trigger has ample rubberized grip, making it easy to keep a firm hold on the tool.

Given its smaller size, we expected it to come up lacking in power. It didn’t. With a max speed of 1,900 RPM, the Ridgid had no trouble handling the larger bits we tested, easily boring holes with a 1/2-inch twist bit and 1-inch spade bit. It followed up that impressive performance by effortlessly driving ten 3-inch carpenter screws into a couple of stacked 2x4s. And if you need additional torque, you can get its max 375 in-lbs (inch-pounds) by dropping down to its lower speed setting.

The PSBRA02 isn’t the most compact right-angle drill out there, nor is it the largest. Nonetheless, it fits into compact spaces thanks to a head that measures just 3.5 inches long. The width of the head is perhaps the most crucial feature of a right-angle drill because it determines the space into which it can fit. However, there are several other key factors to take into account, including motor speed, power source, and weight. Ahead, learn more about these and other important attributes to think about when choosing the best right-angle drill.

Right-angle drills come in corded and cordless varieties, each with its own pros and cons. Cordless drills run on a rechargeable lithium-ion battery and offer the convenience and flexibility of not needing an electrical outlet or a cord. This lets you use them virtually anywhere.

Corded models, in comparison, require a standard electrical outlet. While these drills aren’t as convenient, a 120V power supply makes them more powerful while providing a reliable and constant power supply. Corded drills are also generally less expensive than cordless models. As with many power tools, spending more on a cordless model makes sense if it will see regular use. A less expensive corded model should suffice for DIYers who may use a right-angle drill only occasionally.

Size and weight determine a right-angle drill’s functionality and ease of use. Because a right-angle drill is meant to fit into tight spaces, size is crucial. Most right-angle drills have a head between 3 and 4 inches wide, so they can reach places standard corded and cordless power drills can’t.

Right-angle drills can also be short and lightweight enough to manage with one hand—although heavy-duty models typically come with a secondary handle.

Chuck size is also important. Most right-angle drills have a ⅜-inch chuck, which helps keep the head compact. Some larger models feature a ½-inch chuck for drilling larger holes or driving in bigger screws. These models typically have a wider head.

Right-angle drills have ergonomically shaped handles with rubberized grips that let you hold them securely with one hand while drilling. Many also have detachable side handles that let you apply more leverage with the other hand. Some manufacturers equip right-angle drills with paddle-style triggers that run the length of the handle. This feature lets you better regulate trigger pressure to control the drill’s speed.

A drill’s speed determines how quickly the tool can drill holes or drive screws. It also gives an indication of how much torque the drill produces if the manufacturer does not provide that information. Speed is measured in RPM and, in most cases, is directly related to the power source. Most cordless 18V right-angle drills top out at around 1,500 RPM, while some 20V drills can reach about 2,000 RPM.

Corded right-angle drills have access to more power, which means they can produce more RPM. A corded 4-amp right-angle drill boasts around 2,400 RPM. While speed isn’t the same as torque, motors that can produce higher RPM are more powerful and thus capable of producing more torque.

When assessing a cordless tool’s battery life, consider not only how long each charge lasts but also how long the battery itself will hold a charge before it needs replacing. Most batteries for right-angle cordless drills will last about 3 hours on a single charge with intermittent use and will take about 3 hours to recharge.

Lithium-ion batteries typically last about 3 years, or about 300 to 500 charging cycles, before they begin to lose their ability to hold a charge. This doesn’t mean the battery will stop working altogether. As a battery nears the end of its life, it will simply last for a shorter period of time and require more frequent charging.

As with standard drills, right-angle drills are capable of using various types of bits and attachments. These drills are adept at drilling holes with standard drill bits or driving in screws with Phillips-head, flat, star, and other styles of screw-driving bits. These drills can also use socket attachments to drive bolts. Like other power tools, right-angle drills use a variety of accessories, including drill bits, screwdriver bits, chuck keys, and other accessories that can be misplaced. For this reason, many right-angle drills include a storage bag that holds the tool, accessories, batteries, and chargers all in one place for easy storage and transport.

Right-angle drills also often offer convenient features like LED lights that illuminate the work area and switches that make it easier to control the drill speed, permitting the user to increase or decrease the speed range to maximize control and improve torque.

A right-angle drill has a geared head that rotates the chuck 90 degrees from the body’s axis, letting it reach tight spaces and drill or drive at angles a conventional drill can’t.

This versatile tool is helpful for a wide range of projects. It can be invaluable for fitting into tight spaces when working on a vehicle. A right-angle drill is also ideal for drilling between floor joists or wall studs, which are spaces that are typically too narrow for a standard drill.

A ⅜-inch drill has a chuck that can accommodate drill bits up to ⅜ inch. Similarly, a ½-inch drill has a larger chuck that can handle drill bits with a maximum diameter of ½ inch. A ½-inch drill generally offers more power and torque, making it suitable for drilling larger holes and tackling tougher materials. It’s commonly used in construction and heavy-duty applications.

A right-angle drill attachment is an accessory that can be added to a standard drill or driver to enable drilling and driving in tight or confined spaces that are difficult to access with a regular tool.

Most cordless drills don’t require you to do a lot to maintain them, but there are few things you can do to keep them in tip-top shape. Wipe off any sawdust or debris after each use, paying close attention to the chuck and air vents. Remove the battery when the drill isn’t in use and store it in a cool place. Lubricate the chuck periodically by applying a small amount of machine oil to the chuck jaws to prevent stiffness.

The biggest danger with a right-angle drill is sudden kickback that can cause wrist injuries. If possible use two hands when operating a right-angle drill, one to control the trigger and the other on the back of the head to stabilize its motion. If that isn’t possible, brace the drill body or your arm against a solid object, like a wall stud, to prevent the tool from spinning out if it jams.
