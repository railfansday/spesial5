Tokenized Stocks Open 24/7 on OKXICE, but Only Crypto Firms Signed Up: Will Wall Street Follow?

OKXICE, the joint venture of crypto exchange OKX and NYSE owner Intercontinental Exchange (ICE), opens round-the-clock trading in tokenized stocks for eligible US customers on Sunday, November 23. The only companies whose shares it will offer at launch are crypto firms: Coinbase, Robinhood, Circle, Strategy, BitGo and Securitize.

Of the 62 companies the venue first picked, 56 objected. Its November 5 notice lists 67 objectors in all, including every member of the Magnificent Seven.

That leaves buyers with a narrow menu. We checked what they will actually get: the six stocks are down a median 42% this year, while the S&P 500 is up 7%. Five of the six have moved more closely with Bitcoin than the S&P 500 has over the past three months.

The launch is the first real test of a new SEC rule meant to bring listed shares onto blockchains. So will Wall Street follow, or is this a crypto-only market for now?

A tokenized stock is a blockchain token that tracks a listed share. ICE set up the venture after it invested in OKX in March at a $25 billion valuation. On OKXICE, each token is backed by a real US share, and customers hold it in a self-custody wallet inside the OKX app.

The venue runs under an exemption the SEC issued on September 17. It lets blockchain venues trade tokenized versions of listed US shares without registering as an exchange, for up to five years. Companies are not asked to opt in. Instead, each one gets 30 days after being notified to object, and silence counts as consent.

Most said no. Applied Optoelectronics said it was worried about pricing, liquidity and volatility, and that investors might think it backed the product. The OKXICE launch page confirms the November 23 start, with more than 70 assets, 59 of them exchange-traded funds from Direxion.

We pulled daily prices for the six launch stocks from Yahoo Finance. Then we measured how far each has moved in 2026 and how closely its daily moves tracked Bitcoin over the past 90 trading days.

So a US investor who wanted Apple or Nvidia around the clock gets, for now, five stocks that rise and fall with the crypto market. ICE’s own shares, by comparison, are up 8% this year and barely track Bitcoin at all, with a correlation of 0.08.

The objections don’t mean Wall Street is ignoring tokenization. It is building its own version first.

In March, the SEC approved a Nasdaq pilot that puts tokenized and regular shares in the same order book. The Depository Trust & Clearing Corporation (DTCC), which settles most US stock trades, runs a tokenization service with 20 participants and 34 securities. In September, Nasdaq agreed to invest $100 million in Payward, the parent of crypto exchange Kraken, to build infrastructure for tokenized shares.

The difference is control. In those projects, exchanges and clearing houses run the system, and trading stays tied to regular market hours and rules. OKXICE offers 24/7 trading, which companies have pushed back on: a share that trades all weekend leaves no quiet time to handle bad news. Nasdaq has also warned that splitting trading across separate venues could thin out liquidity.

So the answer is “not yet.” Wall Street is likely to reach tokenized stocks through its own exchanges and DTCC before it lets crypto venues list its shares.

Robinhood is the launch name with the most at stake: it already sells tokenized stocks to European customers. Its 24/7 contract on Hyperliquid, a crypto exchange that lists perpetual futures on US stocks, trades at $70.32.
