Dually Noted: The Reasons Why Some Pickup Trucks and Vans Come with Dual Rear Wheels

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Dually pickup trucks and vans may look tough, but these models have four rear wheels and tires for reasons that have nothing to do with vanity.

The term “dual rear wheels,” as applied to full-size pickup trucks and vans, raises as many questions as it answers. Namely, one: Don’t all vehicles have two rear wheels? Good work, detective. The dual part, however, refers to the number of rear wheels on each side. Confusing? Sure. But consider yourself a member of an elite club. Send away for your decoder ring now.

You’ll often see so-equipped vehicles referred to in official specs or on a build sheet as DRW, as opposed to single-rear-wheel (SRW) pickups and vans. Out in the real world, you’re likely to observe dually pickups towing big, heavy trailers, often using a gooseneck or fifth-wheel hitch.

DRW vans are usually the longest and tallest, used for both cargo and passenger hauling. Simply put, DRW vehicles are meant to handle big loads.

Automakers go the DRW route on configurations of their heavy-duty trucks and vans designed for hauling and/or towing heavy loads. Having those extra tires means more contact patch over which to distribute the weight. It’s the same basic principle that’s used on semi trucks—having 18 or so wheels spread across the semi tractor and its trailer allows the rig to carry literal tons.

On dually trucks, the wider stance helps increase stability as well. You can think of the two extra wheels like outriggers, keeping the truck better planted in the face of crosswinds or in turns.

The main drawbacks of a dually setup are somewhat obvious. You have to worry about six tires instead of four, which in turn makes tire replacement more expensive. For dually trucks, the extra wheels sit outside the normal pickup-bed envelope, necessitating flared fenders to cover the extra rubber.

Maneuvering a big SRW truck or van is difficult enough. Predictably, the additional width of a DRW vehicle only adds to the challenge. It’s why many dually trucks end up with a blemish or two on at least one rear fender. The fenders of newer dually pickups are made from lighter-weight fiberglass instead of steel, so they’re less likely to bend and more prone to cracking when they meet a stationary object.

This isn’t the case with most dually full-size vans. So-equipped vans add their additional tires inboard, which eliminates the need for flared rear fenders. The consequence of this arrangement is a slight reduction in outright cargo space due to the wheel wells extending further into the cabin.

Ever wonder why the front wheels of a dually truck or van have a severe outward dish to them? It’s so that all the wheels match and are therefore interchangeable should you need to use the spare. This dished design allows the rear wheels on one side to mate up with each other, which enables the use of two rear tires on both sides of the vehicle.

The need for a dually comes down to capability. DRWs may be necessary to comfortably pull a fully laden horse trailer, a giant camper, various types of farm equipment, or the crops they help sow and reap. To get an idea of how much more a dually can handle, an SRW heavy-duty truck may be rated for a little more than 20,000 pounds of trailer weight on a bumper-mounted hitch, while the DRW variant of the same basic truck with a gooseneck hitch can pull almost twice the weight.

The difference isn’t as great with dual-rear-wheel versus SRW vans, mostly because both use bumper-mounted hitches. A DRW van may be able to tow as much as 50 percent more weight than a comparable SRW version. With some vans, towing capacity actually goes down when you add two rear wheels. Why go the dually route, then? Because that extra rubber is advantageous to rear-end stability—particularly when a van’s loaded up at, or near, its maximum payload capacity.

Dually setups are available on heavy-duty versions of full-size pickups and some large vans. Those looking for a pickup truck with a bed can choose this setup on the Chevrolet Silverado HD, GMC Sierra HD, Ram HD, and Ford F-series Super Duty.

In the realm of enclosed passenger and cargo vans, only the Ford Transit and Mercedes-Benz Sprinter offer available DRW configurations. You’ll also often find DRWs in use on cutaway and chassis-cab versions of these trucks and vans, which are used in the aftermarket to build things such as box trucks, tow trucks, and RVs.

In some international markets, where full-size pickups are much less common, automakers produce DRW versions of their sub-full-size pickups.

As with any towing or hauling exercise, make sure you understand the limits of your specific vehicle before loading it up or hitching a trailer to the back. Consider what you’re likely to tow or haul when shopping for your workhorse, and follow the manufacturer’s guidance on loading so you stay safe and avoid putting too much strain on the vehicle.

Dually is a term used for a pickup truck or van with dual rear wheels (DRW).

A dually pickup truck or van has two wheels and tires on each side of its rear axle for a grand total of four rear wheels and tires.

You do not need a CDL to drive a full-size pickup truck or an enclosed cargo or passenger van with dual rear wheels (DRWs).

Pedants will note that even a rear-wheel-drive dually relies on its engine to power four wheels (the four back ones). In a technical sense, however, a rear-drive dually is a two-wheel-drive vehicle, since power is sent to the drive wheels via two axle shafts. Similarly, though a four-wheel-drive-equipped dually may power a total of six wheels, the engine’s power only reaches the drive wheels via a total of four axle shafts (two at each end).
