Does AI Process Information Like the Human Cerebellum?

“So God created mankind in his own image…” —Genesis 1:27

I hypothesize that the human cerebellum created AI in its own image.

Since the time of the ancient Greeks, the cerebellum has been known to control motor movements in both humans and other animals. The human cerebellum has long been known to control, sequence, and refine motor movements like juggling, cursive writing, or guitar playing. It is also known that of the total 86 billion neurons (cells) in the brain, 80 percent, or 69 billion neurons, reside in the cerebellum, which makes it highly ironic that the word cerebellum translates from Latin into lesser brain or little brain. Because of these long-known associations of the cerebellum with motor movements, it was reasoned that motor control and refined motor movements must require an extraordinarily large number of the brain’s total neurons, which is only partially true.

There are two major types of cells in the cerebellum: the Purkinje neurons, which are the most dendritically complex in the entire brain, and the granule neurons, which are far more numerous but interact with Purkinje cells. Two other kinds of neurons that influence the functions of the cerebellum, mossy and climbing fibers, appear to interact for precise timing of sequential behaviors and the ability of the cerebellum to adapt to a variety of environmental stimuli (like rewards). For example, when in a dyadic interaction, we listen to another person speaking, and we "time" when it is appropriate to reply—that is, when it seems they are finished and it is our time to reply. Meanwhile, most amazingly, we are simultaneously listening to what is being said and preparing what to say in an appropriate response. This timing is controlled by the human cerebellum, which is why the cerebellum’s evolution was so visible to natural selection. The cerebellum, in part, enhanced social interactions. Also, imagine how timing in music affects the listener. We might listen to the intimate pauses in a performance, which often creates stimulating emotional reactions to a virtuoso as opposed to a beginner. We would also know the appropriate timing for clapping (e.g., Broersen & De Zeeuw, 2024).

Through repetition, the cerebellum also allows an automaticity of movements that allows the conscious brain (cortex) to offload thinking about movements, which then allows the cortex to be free to think about other things. Beginning in the late 1970s, the hypothesis arose that the human cerebellum might also be involved in higher-order cognitive processes such as thinking, reasoning, and creativity. Thus, the original Darwinian adaptive purpose of the cerebellum was, indeed, for the control, sequencing, and refinement of motor movements. Through evolutionary time, these same neurons came to perform an additional function, that is, the control, sequencing, and refinement of thoughts and ideas (this additional function is called an exaptation).

Interestingly, it may be no mere coincidence that modern AI processing has clear parallels to the human cerebellum’s processing of information, as the human cerebellum played an essential and critical role in the development of AI. Hence, AI was created in the image of the cerebellum’s complex cognitive processes. For example, both systems learn regularities from sequences of information, both use existing context to generate predictions, and both represent those learned regularities or patterns internally. These implicit models are not subject to conscious awareness or intention in either the biologically based cerebellum or electronically based AI. Both systems also improve their models by using the discrepancies between predictions and outcomes. Finally, both are characterized by a process of amortization; that is, iterative computations are compiled and incrementally improved. In the cerebellum, repetition converts conscious, effortful processing into automatic, unconscious processing, which allows the recombination of various previously learned models into novel models, and that is the essence of creativity.

As I have and others have previously written (e.g., Coolidge, 2021; Vandervert & Manto, 2025), the automaticity in movement and cognition created by the cerebellum frees the traditional executive functions of the frontal lobes to “wander and wonder,” thus often giving rise to novel ideas, intuitions, insight, and satori. It is also interesting that AI produces the same output (novel ideas), albeit in a non-biologically based manner; however, it is often subject to hallucinations, erroneous information, and fictional references. When I am asked about my overall opinion of AI, I say that it is yet another disruptive technology, and it is obviously not finished disrupting. Just as cell phones mostly eliminated cameras, landlines, and the need to talk to others live, AI is likely to have similar extinction capabilities that may not be all that predictable. We live in great hope but not without some fears.
