This Is What Christmas Dinner Would Look Like in ‘A Christmas Carol’

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

I'll give you Mr. Scrooge, the Founder of the Feast! Here are all the foods Charles Dickens wrote about in A Christmas Carol.

Charles Dickens’s A Christmas Carol occupies a particularly important place in my heart. My grandma gifted me her copy when I was in junior high, and I’ve returned to it nearly every Christmas since, each year finding something in the story that speaks to me in a new way.

And although I’m generally suspicious of film adaptations of the books I love, there are two versions I treasure every bit as much as the original story. I fell in love with them as a child, probably around the same time. The first was The Muppet Christmas Carol, which combined two of my favorite things: the Muppets and jaunty show tunes. I watched it incessantly throughout the Christmas season, and the tradition has carried on with my own children, who now know every song and every word of dialogue.

The other was the 1984 adaptation starring George C. Scott. I love its surreal, slightly unsettling atmosphere, the magnificent strangeness of the Ghost of Christmas Present and the genuinely frightening revelation of Ignorance and Want. And after all that darkness, Scrooge’s redemption is almost unbearably moving.

With another film adaptation arriving this year (Ebenezer), it’s clear that Dickens’s masterpiece has kept its hold on us. And because the food is an enormous part of what makes the story’s celebrations so memorable, I wanted to take a closer look at the happy commotion of the Cratchits’ kitchen, Fezziwig’s Christmas party and the astonishing abundance surrounding the Ghost of Christmas Present. Nearly two centuries later, those descriptions still give us a remarkably complete picture of what Christmas tasted like in Dickens’s world.

The Cratchits’ Christmas goose is one of the most eagerly anticipated meals in literature. As the family busies itself with the accompaniments, the younger children can hardly contain their excitement. When Mrs. Cratchit finally brings the bird to the table, Dickens describes it arriving with a great flourish and the stuffing spilling out as it’s carved.

Goose was a traditional Christmas centerpiece in Victorian England, particularly for families who couldn’t afford a turkey. Its rich, dark meat was well-suited to roasting, and sage-and-onion stuffing was a familiar accompaniment.

Bob declares that no goose has ever been so tender and delicious, and the family agrees wholeheartedly. The bird may be small for so many people, but the family’s excitement is anything but.

While the goose roasts, the Cratchit household is busy! Peter tends to the mashed potatoes, Belinda sweetens the apple sauce, and Mrs. Cratchit prepares the gravy. Nearly two centuries later, the menu would look perfectly at home on a modern Christmas table.

Apple sauce was an especially good companion to roast goose, with its tart sweetness balancing the bird’s rich, fatty meat. The mashed potatoes and gravy rounded out the meal, providing plenty of comfort alongside the centerpiece.

For all his considerable literary talents, Charles Dickens also had a good understanding of the anxiety that comes with serving a dessert you’re not entirely sure has turned out.

While Mrs. Cratchit retrieves her Christmas plum pudding from the kitchen, the family imagines every possible disaster. Perhaps it isn’t cooked through, or perhaps it will break when she turns it out. The younger children even worry that someone might have stolen it. Fortunately, Mrs. Cratchit returns triumphant with a pudding Dickens describes as a “speckled cannon-ball,” blazing with brandy and decorated with holly.

Traditionally, plum pudding was a dense mixture of dried fruit, suet, spices and other ingredients, boiled for hours in a cloth. Despite the name, it didn’t necessarily contain plums; the term was historically used for dried fruits such as raisins.

Bob declares the pudding Mrs. Cratchit’s greatest success since their marriage, and nobody dreams of disagreeing. The pudding may be modest in size, but to the Cratchits, it’s a triumph, and Mrs. Cratchit deserves every word of their praise.

Few characters in A Christmas Carol embody the spirit of Christmas quite like old Mr. Fezziwig. When the Ghost of Christmas Past takes Scrooge back to the Christmas party hosted by his former employer, we’re treated to a lively evening of dancing, music, laughter and an impressive assortment of refreshments, including mince pies.

These little pastries had been associated with English Christmas celebrations for generations. Earlier versions of mincemeat often contained actual meat alongside dried fruit, suet and spices. By Dickens’s time, sweeter fillings were increasingly common, though meat hadn’t disappeared from every recipe.

I rather like imagining Fezziwig presiding over the festivities, dispensing good cheer as generously as the food. His party is one of the few memories that briefly softens Scrooge, and it’s easy to understand why.

After the Cratchit family finishes dinner, apples and oranges are brought out, chestnuts are placed on the fire, and Bob serves a warm drink of gin and lemon. With the excitement of the goose and Christmas pudding behind them, the family settles in for the evening.

Roasted chestnuts were a familiar winter treat in Victorian London, sold hot by street vendors and enjoyed at home. Oranges were especially welcome during the winter months, when fresh fruit was less readily available.

Dickens mentions that the family has only two tumblers and a custard cup without a handle, a detail I love for its specificity. The bustle of dinner has given way to the quieter pleasure of sitting together, and Dickens allows us to linger with the family a little longer before the evening draws to a close.

Christmas celebrations in Dickens’s England didn’t necessarily end on Dec. 25. Twelfth Night, traditionally observed on Jan. 5 or 6, marked the close of the Twelve Days of Christmas, often with a party and an elaborate cake. Twelfth cakes were rich with dried fruit and spices, beautifully decorated and sometimes baked with a hidden bean. Whoever found the bean in their slice might be crowned king or queen of the festivities, an arrangement that presumably made everyone a little more attentive to their dessert.

Dickens details “immense twelfth-cakes” among the extraordinary foods surrounding the Ghost of Christmas Present. The tradition was already evolving by 1843, but the cakes were still familiar enough to belong on his magnificent collection of Christmas indulgences.

Dickens includes entire barrels of oysters among the Ghost of Christmas Present’s holiday foods, which may seem an extravagant addition to modern readers.

In Victorian London, though, oysters were commonly eaten, including by working-class people. They were sold by street vendors, served in taverns and prepared in everything from pies to stews. Britain’s oyster fisheries were once enormously productive, although overharvesting and pollution eventually led to their decline.

Dickens’s Ghost certainly had no shortage of delights surrounding him, but the barrels of oysters wouldn’t necessarily have struck his readers as the most impressive things in the room.

When Scrooge wakes on Christmas morning and realizes he’s been given another chance, one of his first acts is to purchase an enormous turkey for the Cratchits.

He sends a boy to the poulterer’s shop for the prize bird, giddy at the thought of how surprised the family will be. Dickens describes Scrooge’s delight so vividly that it’s difficult not to share his excitement.

Roast turkey was becoming fashionable for Christmas dinner in Victorian England, particularly among wealthier households. Compared with the Cratchits’ modest goose, Scrooge’s gift is a magnificent extravagance.

I especially love that he arranges for the bird to be delivered anonymously. He’s discovered the joy of doing something generous without expecting anything in return, and he can barely contain himself.

At the end of the story, Scrooge promises Bob Cratchit a raise and suggests they discuss the particulars over a bowl of smoking bishop. It’s a peculiar name for what was essentially a warm, spiced wine punch.

Smoking bishop was traditionally prepared with red wine or port, roasted citrus, sugar and spices. The ingredients were warmed together to make a fragrant cocktail similar to modern mulled wine. It belonged to a family of similarly named punches, including smoking cardinal and smoking pope. Victorian drinkers evidently enjoyed a little ecclesiastical creativity with their beverages.

I love that Dickens saves this particular drink for the end. We’ve seen the Cratchits gather around their modest dinner and the Ghost of Christmas Present preside over a feast of extraordinary abundance. Now Scrooge, who once wanted nothing to do with either, is inviting Bob Cratchit to share a bowl of punch. Of all the wonderful things on Dickens’s Christmas menu, that invitation may be the most satisfying.
