Tiger Woods Was Right About $750 Million LIV Decision

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

The most expensive decision in golf history turned out to be the smartest move Tiger Woods ever made. A reported $750 million offer to join the breakaway tour sat on the table, yet Woods walked away. Now that LIV Golf has officially folded, the man who refused the cash stands vindicated. The league that promised to upend the sport died because it could not pay enough to keep its stars.

The collapse happened fast. Lawyers for the tour announced in New Jersey that LIV would liquidate its assets rather than try to restructure. This move came after BC Partners Credit pulled a planned $300 million investment that was supposed to save the league for 2027. The Saudi Public Investment Fund had already stopped its annual billion-dollar funding stream in April. Without that cash, the "LIV 2.0" plan died before it could start.

Tiger Woods understood something the investors missed. He knew the game relies on earning your spot, not buying it. The Athletic's Ian O'Connor wrote about this in a column on Friday, noting that Woods questioned the whole idea of guaranteed money. "What is the incentive to go out there and earn it in the dirt?" Tiger Woods once asked. That phrase, "the dirt," represents the work players do to find their swing before sunrise or after sunset.

LIV tried to skip the grind. They offered nine-figure deals to players before they even hit a ball in a tournament. Woods, whose net worth of roughly $1.5 billion, did not need the money to feel successful. He saw the risk in a tour built on checks instead of competition. O'Connor noted that Woods was not sure how the game would work in LIV, pointing out that "dirt" is where the dream chasers find the essence of the sport.

Other top players saw the same problem. Rory McIlroy said the financial model was doomed from the start. He told Today's Golfer that it was impossible to get enough players to sign up for a plan with smaller prize money and no upfront payments. "It was always going to be very hard for them to get enough players to sign up for what they were proposing which was smaller prize money, no upfront payments," he said.

McIlroy pointed out that if the league could not make a $6 billion deal work, it certainly could not make a $300 million deal work. Jon Rahm, the tour's most successful defector, also rejected the new terms. He told reporters at the Abu Dhabi Aldar Championship that he liked the original idea but hated the changes in version 2.0. "Why I rejected the terms? As much as I liked the project and the idea on, let's say, LIV 1.0, I wasn't thrilled with the changes they were doing to 2.0, and I just wasn't willing to follow that path," Rahm said.

Sergio Garcia, Tyrrell Hatton, Ian Poulter, and Louis Oosthuizen also bailed on the tour. They realized the new plan offered nothing special. With these stars gone, the league lost its core value.

The end of LIV leaves many players looking for a place to play. The PGA Tour offered a one-time path back for defectors in January, but only Brooks Koepka took it. The offer required a $5 million charitable donation and the forfeiture of equity. Bryson DeChambeau and Cameron Smith declined the deal. Patrick Reed left LIV earlier and will return to the PGA Tour in 2027 without needing a special deal.

Rory McIlroy believes the DP World Tour could step in to help. He suggested that funneling former LIV players through that tour to qualify would make it incredibly strong. "It would be an amazing thing for this tour if all those guys were funneled through the DP World Tour and were made to qualify that way," he said. He noted that only a handful of cards would be available, making the competition fierce.

With the league shutting down, the focus shifts to who plays where next year. The Returning Member Program remains closed for most, and the DP World Tour stands ready to absorb the talent.
