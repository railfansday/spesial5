What happens when engineers grind up eggshells and squeeze them into a lightweight metal?

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Eggshell powder pressed into magnesium bonded to the metal and made tiny samples tougher to crush.

Most eggshells end up in the trash or the compost bin. Yet a shell is almost pure calcium carbonate, the same calcium compound that metal makers normally get from mined rock.

Engineers have now packed ground-up kitchen eggshells into a block of magnesium metal and pressed it through a spinning die. The shell powder spread evenly through the metal.

Part of it also reacted with the magnesium around it, which bonded shell to metal chemically. That makes a kitchen scrap a workable ingredient for a lightweight metal, at least in a small lab test.

The work came from the lab of Bharat Gwalani, an assistant professor of materials science and engineering at North Carolina State University.

It was a first test on small rods, and the finished metal wasn’t measurably harder than plain magnesium. Its gain in strength showed up only in microscopic crush tests.

Magnesium is a light metal. A recent antenna design for Mars helicopters used a magnesium frame.

“Calcium is added to magnesium to improve its mechanical properties,” Gwalani said. “We wanted to see if we could use biogenic waste – eggshells – to produce the necessary calcium materials during the manufacturing process.”

Biogenic waste just means leftovers from living things. Eggshells already appear on lists of food leftovers that could be recovered and reused, and other labs have turned food waste into plastic.

Industry makes one of those compounds, calcium oxide, by heating limestone in industrial kilns until it gives off carbon dioxide.

Earlier teams had already blended eggshell powder into magnesium, either by melting the metal or by stirring the powder into its surface.

However, little was known about what happens to the shell inside the metal during a friction-based press like this one.

Gwalani’s co-authors included researchers at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory, where first author Aniruddha Malakar now works, and at the Indian Institute of Technology Delhi.

The eggshells came from ordinary households. The team washed them, dried them in an oven at about 300°F (150°C) for four hours, and ground them with a mortar and pestle.

Then they cut small grooves, each about an eighth of an inch (3 mm) wide, into a short magnesium cylinder about an inch (25.4 mm) across. The eggshell powder filled the grooves in the metal and made up roughly 8.4 percent of the block by volume.

That’s a pinch of shell in a lot of metal.

A die spinning 300 times a minute pressed into the block at about 3 inches (75 mm) a minute and squeezed it out as a rod. Friction and squeezing heated the metal to roughly 930°F (500°C), hot enough to soften it without melting it.

Most of the shell particles measured 2 to 4 micrometers across, far thinner than a human hair. They were spread evenly through the rod.

Metal is made of tiny crystals called grains. In a plain magnesium rod made the same way, the grains averaged 16 micrometers across. With eggshell mixed in, they averaged 8.

Smaller grains usually make a metal stronger. Even so, the eggshell rod wasn’t measurably harder than the plain one when the team pressed a diamond tip into its surface.

The eggshell’s effect appeared only after the metal started to give way. The researchers carved microscopic pillars out of grains pointing in the same direction in both metals. Then they crushed them.

Both kinds of pillar began to give way at about the same stress. Once they did, the eggshell pillars kept resisting harder as they squashed.

Their peak strength reached about 32,600 pounds per square inch (225 megapascals), compared with about 29,000 pounds per square inch (200 megapascals) for plain magnesium.

The team thinks the shell bits help tiny flaws in the metal’s crystals pile up as it deforms. The more of those flaws that build up, the harder the metal is to squash further.

The second finding was chemical. Calcium carbonate usually breaks apart into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide at about 1,650 to 1,740°F (900 to 950°C) under normal air pressure.

Inside the press, the metal reached only about 930°F (500°C). Several of the smallest shell fragments broke down, at least partly. The team established this by measuring calcium, oxygen and carbon across individual particles.

The researchers think the spots where shell met metal got much hotter than the overall reading. They suspect sudden swings in pressure helped too.

Near the edges of some particles, the measurements pointed to magnesium oxide. The likeliest source, the team concluded, was carbon dioxide from the broken-down shell reacting with the surrounding magnesium.

The reaction was patchy, though. Larger particles, a few micrometers or more across, stayed mostly unchanged. The finished rod held a mix of untouched shell, partly broken-down shell and areas rich in calcium oxide.

One compound didn’t form. That compound, Mg2Ca, is a compound of magnesium and calcium that can strengthen magnesium alloys, and the team didn’t detect it. The calcium stayed mostly locked up as calcium oxide.

The authors wrote that their goal was to understand what happens inside the metal, not to make the strongest possible material.

They tested only one spin speed and one feed rate. The team also didn’t measure whether the process uses less energy than making calcium compounds from rock, although Gwalani said it should.

“There are fewer steps,” Gwalani said. “You have a reliable, sustainable supply chain. Eggshells are inexpensive.”

Gwalani’s group in NC State’s Department of Materials Science and Engineering has also used the same kind of press to work magnetic powder into scrap aluminum.

The next step is to push the chemistry further. The authors suggested heating the shell powder before it goes in, running the press longer or hotter, or working in a sealed chamber with less oxygen.

Any of those might free the calcium from its oxide and get it into the magnesium itself, the step that this first test didn’t reach.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Magnesium and Alloys.
