Sony Defeats $2.6 Billion Class-Action Lawsuit Over Claimed PlayStation Store Monopoly

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Since 2022, Sony has been facing the British equivalent of a class action lawsuit that could have cost the company as much as $2.6 billion had it lost. It, however, did not. As reported byÂ The Game Business, the lawsuit brought by consumer advocate Alex Neill claimed that Sony was operating a “near monopoly” via the PlayStation Store, and sued the Japanese company on behalf of nine million UK PlayStation owners, but today the claims were rejected, and the case has been dismissed.

The reported financial goal of the lawsuit has wildly swung about over the years, cited as $5.9 billion in 2022, then $7.9 billion by 2023, and then down to $2.6 billion in May of this year. Whatever the number, the case being made was that by not allowing third-party stores to sell digital PlayStation games, Sony effectively has a monopoly from which it extracts 30 percent of every sale. This, said Neill and the PlayStation You Owe Us campaign, was an “abuse” of Sony’s “dominant position,” forcing customers to pay “too much for digital games and in-game content purchased through the PlayStation Store.”

A previous attempt to have the case thrown out was rejected in 2023, and then things looked far worse for Sony when it lost a very similar case in California in May of this year, to the tune of $7.8 billion. And there’s the rather pertinent point that Sony absolutely does dominate sales of digital games on PlayStation, and that’s only going to get a whole lot worse as the megacorp phases out physical media entirely. Yet, following May’s trial, London’s Competition Appeal Tribunal has rejected the argument and ruled that, as reported by TGB, “the claim failed to establish that PlayStation infringed competition law.”

The argument against a monopoly was based on the wider competition of Xbox, Nintendo, and PC, alongside the (current) option to buy physical versions of games. Allowing third-party stores onto the PlayStation could, the court said, impact security, which interestingly was the failed argument Google made in its battle against Epic. The tribunal also said Neill had failed to prove that Sony’s profits were “excessive,” likely given that 30 percent cuts are pretty much an industry standard.

However, Sony is still facing similar cases in Holland, Portugal, and Mexico, and it seems they could fall either way.
