OpenAI Wants to Rebrand AI Data Centers, Use Them to 'Protect the Grid'

OpenAI wants its massive AI data centers to help keep the lights on when electricity grids come under pressure. And, apparently, it would also prefer that people stop calling them AI data centers.

The ChatGPT developer announced its Grid Protectors Program on Monday, a national initiative designed to let future AI facilities reduce electricity consumption during emergencies and periods of high demand, potentially lowering costs for other customers.

Under the plan, OpenAI would coordinate with utility companies to determine how much electricity its facilities could temporarily stop using, how quickly they could respond, and for how long. The company is also exploring on-site batteries and power generation to help manage demand.

“Families and small businesses should not be asked to subsidize a compute campus’s electricity needs,” OpenAI said in its announcement.

The program addresses one of the biggest complaints surrounding the AI infrastructure boom: companies are building increasingly power-hungry facilities while residents worry about paying higher—and water—electricity bills to support them.

OpenAI is using a planned facility in Effingham County, Georgia, as its first example. The company has signed a 25-year agreement with Georgia Power covering approximately 3,200 megawatts of electricity demand, with up to 1,000 megawatts available for temporary reductions during periods of high demand.

Under the approved agreement, OpenAI must cover the full infrastructure costs required to serve its facility, rather than passing those expenses on to existing customers.

Georgia Power also projects approximately $950 million in annual customer savings beginning in 2029, equivalent to at least $15 per month for a typical residential customer. However, those estimates cover the utility's broader portfolio of large electricity customers and expected growth, not just OpenAI's project.

The Georgia facility is still under development, and the savings remain projections. OpenAI has not yet disclosed binding demand-reduction commitments for other facilities under its national program.

OpenAI's electricity initiative also arrives after reports of a massive PR campaign to change what people call the facilities in the first place to erase from collective memory the negative sentiment towards AI data centers.

According to Axios, OpenAI is preparing a post-midterm policy and PR campaign that includes rebranding data centers as "AI compute campuses," an effort to make the infrastructure sound more appealing to communities, effectively trying to solve a problem by changing the problem’s name.

The company has already started using the terminology. Its Grid Protectors announcement repeatedly refers to future facilities as "compute campuses," although it occasionally uses the more familiar term "data centers."

An OpenAI spokesperson confirmed the naming strategy to Business Insider, arguing that the term better reflects the facilities' broader role in computing, energy infrastructure, and skilled employment.

The reported rebranding comes as opposition to AI infrastructure projects has become a political issue across the United States. Pennsylvania introduced new restrictions on large data centers in August, including provisions designed to protect residents from higher electricity costs. Other states are following.

OpenAI and six other major technology companies also signed a White House pledge in March to pay for the electricity and grid upgrades their AI projects require.

The new program goes further by proposing that AI facilities could become useful to utilities rather than simply adding pressure to their systems. But the national framework is still largely an ambition, with individual agreements to be negotiated.

OpenAI's Georgia project will provide an early test of that approach. Electricity delivery to the planned 3.2-gigawatt facility is scheduled to begin in 2028 and expand in phases through 2032.
