Cattails send oxygen into marsh mud, where it can change the soil chemistry

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Cattails grow in mud with almost no oxygen, but their roots bring some in. A new study found that growing roots release oxygen even at night.

The roots of cattails can grow in oxygen-poor marsh mud. To keep these roots alive, the plant carries air down from its leaves.

Part of that oxygen then escapes into the surrounding mud.

It has generally been assumed that this leak occurs mostly in daylight. During the day, the leaves produce oxygen and their pores stay open to the air.

A laboratory study of narrowleaf cattails has found that daylight altered the leak only slightly. Instead, oxygen appeared wherever a new root or underground stem was growing. It remained there through the night as well.

The findings suggest that the season of root growth may matter more for marsh chemistry than the time of day.

The study was conducted by Ilana Stein and Zoe Cardon of the Ecosystems Center. This center is part of the Marine Biological Laboratory (MBL) in Woods Hole, Massachusetts.

Four plants were examined one at a time in boxes of marsh mud. No measurements were made in a marsh itself.

Flooding leaves the soil of a wetland without oxygen. Most wetland plants cope with this through spongy tissue in their leaves, stems and roots that is filled with air.

This tissue serves as a kind of snorkel for the plant. Oxygen travels through it from the air above the water to the root tips below.

Narrowleaf cattail is particularly well adapted to flooded ground. Air spaces were found by earlier studies to make up as much as 74% of its leaf tissue.

In the roots, the figure reached as much as 32%.

Some of the oxygen that arrives is used by the roots and the rest can leak away. This leaked oxygen allows microbes that depend on it to survive in mud where they otherwise could not.

Certain microbes consume methane before the gas can enter the plant and escape through its leaves. Others break down organic matter in the mud and could reduce how much carbon the soil stores.

The same leak is also valuable to people. Cattails are planted in constructed wetlands that treat wastewater and are used to clean up contaminated soil. Both of these uses rely on the oxygen released by their roots.

Cattails and mud were collected by Stein and Cardon from a marsh along the Parker River in northeastern Massachusetts. This marsh forms part of the Plum Island Ecosystems Long Term Ecological Research program.

Each plant was placed in a narrow clear plastic box filled with live mud from the same marsh. The box measured about 15.5 inches tall and 8 inches wide (39 by 20 centimeters).

Much of one inside wall was covered with a film containing a dye that responds to oxygen. A camera photographed the film under blue light.

The colors in every image indicated how much oxygen was present in the mud behind the film.

These films were made by hand by Suzanne Thomas, who works as a senior research assistant at MBL.

Images were captured every few minutes over periods that usually lasted two to three days.

The box itself remained in darkness behind black cloth. Meanwhile, the leaves grew outside under a lamp that was switched on from 5 a.m. to 7 p.m.

A sensor attached to one leaf collected readings once every minute. It recorded how quickly the leaf absorbed carbon dioxide and how widely its pores had opened.

The team could therefore compare the activity of the leaves with the oxygen in the mud.

Mud that held no roots also held no oxygen. This changed as soon as part of a cattail grew into it.

Cattails spread by means of horizontal underground stems known as rhizomes. New roots then emerge from these stems.

Whenever a new rhizome or root entered a patch of mud, the oxygen level there rose rapidly from zero. It climbed to between 25% and 35% of the amount found in water that is fully saturated with air.

This oxygen did not persist for long. Around a rhizome, it disappeared after about three days on average. Around a main root, it lasted about four days.

According to the authors, a seal may be partly responsible. Older sections of a cattail root develop a barrier that is thought to keep oxygen inside. This would allow more of it to reach the growing tip.

Stein and Cardon wrote that this barrier and the steady demand for oxygen in the mud may explain the decline.

In many cases, oxygen returned to the mud a second time. Fine side roots broke through the sealed surface of a main root and oxygen spread through the mud around them.

These later pools of oxygen persisted for about three weeks on average. In one plant, they lasted longer than five weeks.

An earlier study had found that cattail side roots do not develop the barrier that seals a main root.

As a result, a single root released oxygen into the mud on two occasions. The first release lasted a few days near the tip and the second continued for weeks along the sides.

Earlier studies of rice and a salt marsh cordgrass had reported strong control of the leak by day and night. In the cattails, light did have some effect and this was mostly seen around the side roots.

Across three plants, the gap between the highest and lowest oxygen levels of each day averaged about 3% of saturation. No daily pattern could be detected in the fourth plant.

New growth therefore changed oxygen levels far more than the cycle of day and night.

The oxygen in the mud also did not follow the activity of the leaves. It increased during the first two to three hours of light. For the rest of the day, it declined even though the leaves were becoming more active.

After the lights were turned off, the oxygen fell for about two hours. It then rose slowly until the following morning.

Mud consumes oxygen quickly on its own. Oxygen-rich water was injected into mud that held no roots. The oxygen was gone within about an hour and a half.

The roots must therefore have continued to release oxygen in the dark.

The authors wrote that it remains unclear how cattails achieve this without exhausting the oxygen in their own air spaces.

One explanation they proposed is that the leaf pores did not close completely at night. Air could then have continued to enter the plant.

Methane, soil carbon, and microbes were not examined in the study.

The study also followed just four plants of a single species. None of them was kept in its box for more than two months.

If growth is what controls the oxygen, then the next question is when cattails grow underground.

Stein is a postdoctoral researcher in Cardon’s lab. She said the results turned the team’s attention to the timing of root growth across the year.

Earlier field data and models suggest that cattail rhizomes gain the most mass in mid to late summer. This follows the expansion of the leaves.

The authors wrote that oxygen from new rhizomes and their roots may be especially intense during that period.

Methane is released by wetlands naturally and oxygen around roots can reduce it.

“Natural greenhouse gas emissions have been happening forever,” said Cardon, a senior scientist at MBL.

She said the team hopes to identify natural processes that could act as levers on those emissions.

The cattails were not followed through a complete growing season or observed in a marsh. The authors wrote that understanding when rhizomes and roots grow through the year should become a priority.

In their view, such knowledge is needed to understand the chemistry of marshes where cattails dominate.

The full study was published in the journal New Phytologist.
