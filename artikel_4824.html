Then you check your child’s grade portal and realize that homework and assignments are missing, or test grades look like there was never any real studying done. Does this pattern sound familiar?

For many families, getting homework completed or preparing for a test or quiz is not just an academic challenge but, rather, a daily struggle involving motivation, time management, initiation or getting started, planning, and follow-through. Parents become frustrated because their children seem capable of doing the work but struggle to get started. Children become frustrated because they feel nagged, overwhelmed, or misunderstood.

When executive functioning skills are still developing, telling a child to "just get it done" isn’t addressing the struggles. Instead, we need to help children learn how to get started, manage their time, work through resistance, and follow a plan, even when they would rather be doing something else.

Here are a few strategies to try with your child or teen:

Not every child is ready to tackle demanding work the moment they walk through the door after school. Many children and teens have spent the entire school day working hard to manage their attention, behavior, emotions, and social interactions. By the time they get home, they are mentally exhausted.

Rather than expecting your child, who has spent a ton of mental, physical, and emotional energy all day, to be able to jump right into their homework assignments, build in a break for your child or teen so he or she can decompress for one hour. A snack, movement, music, time outdoors, or a little quiet time may help them transition from school to home. Set a timer and, after one hour, take out school materials with your child or teen in one consistent space in your home, and review the assignments that need to be completed.

What you are doing is building in a time to disconnect from school, then reconnect in a calm way and tackle the assignments that need to be completed. The goal is to find the sweet spot when your child is most ready to engage in undesirable tasks that require attention and follow-through to get them done.

Executive functioning practice does not have to feel like another lecture. Our children can be more engaged when they can participate in a challenge, solve a problem, or compete against their own performance. On a separate pad of paper or a large or small whiteboard, have your child write down the assignments that need to be completed for the night. Then, ask your child or teen to predict how long an assignment will take to complete and write it down. For example, you can ask your child or teen, "How long do you think these 12 math problems will take?" Set the timer and let your child go. When the timer rings, your child can then see how accurate he or she was about the duration of the assignment. If she finishes before the timer, awesome! If he needs more time, estimate how much longer based on how many problems were completed during the time frame. Set the timer again and go! Over time, your child or teen will be able to form a more realistic sense of how long an assignment will take to complete.

This process accomplishes yet another goal, and that is that it goes against the innately held idea that “My homework is gonna take forever!” This puts a time limit on homework assignments so that there is an end time. The goal is to finish the assignments rather than sitting at a desk or table for hours at a time with very little work actually completed.

Then number the assignments in the order to be completed. For example, will he want to start on the hardest assignment first? Will she want to start on the assignment that takes the shortest amount of time? Having a choice also helps initiation.

You can also turn planning into a challenge by encouraging your child or teen to get the work done so they can have downtime for the rest of the evening. Kids and teens alike are often surprised when a parent or executive functioning coach emphasizes the importance of downtime and the need for it. Ultimately, the focus is not on speed or perfection but, rather, on developing awareness, planning, and problem-solving.

You can guide your child or teen without making every decision for them. If the plan is unrealistic, help your child evaluate it. If she becomes stuck, offer a hint or a choice. But whenever possible, allow him to do the thinking. The more opportunities children have to formulate ideas, test strategies, and reflect on the results, the more practice they have to use their executive functioning skills.

Remember this: You are building skills for life. Executive functioning development takes time, and our kids and teens need opportunities to practice planning, managing time, initiating tasks, and following through. We can provide structure, encouragement, and accountability, but we also need to leave room for them to think, try, make mistakes, and learn from experience.
