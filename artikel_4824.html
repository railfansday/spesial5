When Keeping Your Distance Stops Protecting You

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Hallmark’s "Adopting St. Nick" features a single mother, Maren, and her adopted daughter, Leah, who “adopt” a senior resident for the holidays through a retirement community’s Christmas Companions program. Leah suspects that their companion, Kris, may be Santa. Spoiler alert—he is Santa. As I watched, however, my focus turned primarily to Reid, one of the retirement community directors. The ways in which he arranged his life so that he could quickly leave one community for the next made an impression.

Reid can pack his belongings in a day. His job takes him from one location to another, establishing retirement facilities before moving on to the next assignment. He has become comfortable with a life in which leaving is expected until he begins to realize that this departure this time might feel different.

Reid explains that his father was in the military, so his family moved frequently during his childhood. He rarely stayed anywhere long enough to make friends. When he finally did develop friendships, another move meant leaving them behind. His conclusion was understandable: It was easier to avoid making friends than to experience the pain of losing their everyday presence.

A child usually has little say in whether a family moves. Reid could not control where he lived or how long he stayed. Keeping his distance offered one way to make the next goodbye less painful and gave him an opportunity to control what he could. That strategy appears to have followed him into adulthood. His circumstances have changed, but his readiness to leave remains. He demonstrates how he maintains his distance from a community by not interacting with local businesses or residents until Maren shows him what his community has to offer. Viewers watch him discover his surroundings as well as discover his place within that community.

Attachment theory offers one way to think about how people approach closeness, dependence, and the possibility of separation. Adults higher in attachment avoidance often emphasize self-sufficiency and feel uncomfortable depending on others. They may minimize their need for support or create emotional distance when relationships feel threatening.

Researchers sometimes describe these responses as “deactivating strategies,” unconscious ways to cope with feeling connected to others. These involve directing attention away from attachment needs and suppressing feelings that might lead someone to seek closeness. A person may downplay how much a relationship matters or focus on managing difficulties alone. They may even look for opportunities to physically distance themselves from the developing relationship.

Reid’s story can be understood through this lens, although a fictional character’s account cannot establish an attachment orientation or explain its origins. His childhood friendships also tell us something different from his relationships with caregivers. Still, his reasoning illustrates a recognizable protective response: If getting close makes separation painful, remaining less attached can seem sensible. Over time, that response may become so familiar that a person rarely considers what it costs.

The detail about Reid’s belongings stayed with me. He has built a life that can be packed quickly and transported to the next place. Maren and Leah convince him to buy a Christmas tree and decorations, which look out of place in his mostly empty home. There is nothing inherently troubling about owning few possessions or enjoying work that involves travel. In Reid’s case, however, those choices fit the explanation he gives about relationships. He expects to move on, and he has organized his life around that expectation.

His work makes this an especially interesting situation. He helps establish retirement communities where people can develop routines, friendships, and a sense of home. We watch him eat dinner with residents of Jolly Acres in a community he helped build. Still, his role has an anticipated ending date. Once the facility is established, he leaves.

This arrangement allows him to contribute and interact with others while knowing that his time with them is temporary. He can become good at starting over without having to ask whether he wants to keep doing it, and he lets Maren know how he intends to move once Jolly Acres is up and running. His pattern of arriving, setting up a new retirement community, and then moving again has worked until this Christmas season, when leaving seems to be more complicated.

Through his time with Maren, Leah, and the retirement community, Reid develops relationships that begin to matter more than he expected. The change unfolds through participation. He works alongside people, spends time with them, and becomes part of their lives. Eventually, accepting another assignment means leaving people he wants to continue seeing. The next location has become less appealing because he recognizes what he would be giving up.

I found this more interesting than a simple story about a man meeting someone who persuades him to settle down. His connection with Maren matters, but so does his connection with Leah and the broader community. He’s built relationships that complicate his familiar plan, and his move away from this growing community of his is more difficult.

Maren and Leah are not responsible for repairing his attachment patterns. It’s on Reid to recognize his feelings and decide whether his usual response still serves him. The movie gives that recognition a holiday resolution. Outside a movie, recognizing a pattern can begin a longer process of practicing openness, communicating needs, and making room for dependence.

Both Hallmark movies I watched this weekend explored building community, but they approached it differently.

In "Who’s Coming for Christmas?" Julie deliberately seeks connection. She wants people around her Christmas table and makes invitations without knowing whether they will be accepted. Her challenge involves reaching out while she is still finding her place.

In "Adopting St. Nick," Reid discovers the significance of relationships that have developed through his everyday interactions. Building a personal community was never his goal, but the people he works with and encounters regularly have become important to him.

Julie must risk asking people into her life, whereas Reid has to acknowledge how much people already in his life matter. That recognition may be familiar. Sometimes, we understand the importance of our everyday relationships only when we imagine those people no longer being there.

Hallmark: With the help of the Christmas season, and some of Santa’s magic, Reid recognizes that the connections he’s made are too important to lose.

Psychology: Creating emotional distance can be an understandable form of protection. As we recognize its costs, it creates an opportunity to make different choices. Lasting change, however, takes more than one strong connection (or one Christmas season).

The verdict: The movie offers a useful picture of someone reconsidering a strategy that once made leaving easier.

Reid may still be able to pack his belongings in a day, but he may no longer be able to pack away the connections and leave them behind.
