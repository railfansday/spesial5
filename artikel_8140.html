‘Please Help Me.’ A Hiker’s Desperate Text Led to Her Dramatic Rescue on a Famed Colorado Trail

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

A Black Hawk helicopter. (Photo: patty_c / Getty Images)

A desperate text to 911 turned into a dramatic rescue in the Colorado high country after an early-season storm trapped a 37-year-old solo hiker in thigh-to-hip-deep snow on the Four Pass Loop on October 30.

The Four Pass Loop is a popular but rugged 27-mile route that travels through the Maroon Bells-Snowmass Wilderness while gaining about 8,000 feet of elevation. Most hikers tackle the route as a backpacking trip over the course of two to four days.

According to a release by Crested Butte Mountain Rescue (CBMR), the ordeal began when Pitkin County Emergency Dispatch received a text to 911 that read “please help me.” Before calling for help, the hiker had texted a family member that her phone’s battery was down to 10 percent. Shortly after that initial call for help, emergency services lost contact, leaving them with only a single coordinate to guide their search. With temperatures dipping into the teens, CBMR reached out to CareFlight of the Rockies to coordinate air support. The team also dispatched a ground team, reasoning the elevation and mountain conditions could make landing a helicopter impossible.

By 11 p.m., the helicopter team located the hiker attempting to climb over Frigid Air Pass, but they were unable to make a safe landing to extract her from the scene.

About four hours later, the first two party members of the CBMR ground team reached the pass and located the hiker struggling to make it over the pass near where the helicopter had spotted her. But neither air support nor ground support could reach the hiker: “The steep terrain, deep snow, and darkness presented legitimate avalanche concerns,” CBMR wrote. “The first two rescuers initially attempted to descend toward the subject but encountered concerning conditions. Recognizing the potential hazard, they made the decision to wait for the remaining team members before committing to the descent.”

When the remaining members of the CBMR ground team reached the pass at about 5:30 a.m., two team members descended somewhere between 300 to 400 feet to reach the hiker. Upon their arrival, they realized that her condition was potentially life-threatening since she was no longer ambulatory, she had cold-related injuries, and was showing signs of hypothermia. The rescue team placed the hiker in a bivy before realizing that the best way to extract her would be via a helicopter hoist rescue.

CBMR coordinated with the Colorado Search and Rescue Association (CSAR), who contacted the Colorado Army National Guard to dispatch a UH-60 Black Hawk helicopter from Gypsum. A helicopter lowered a rescue technician to the hiker, then hoisted both party members into the helicopter.

After the hiker was successfully transferred to the airport and flown to the UCHealth Burn and Frostbite Center in Aurora, the National Guard returned to the Frigid Air Pass to remove the remaining rescuers due to the pass’s dangerous conditions.

In the wake of the incident, CBMR reminded hikers to take extra precautions before heading into the backcountry since high-altitude rescues can be dangerous for rescuers, too. (“So throw that extra puffy in your pack!” the team wrote.)

The team noted that rescue came at the end of a particularly challenging week for CBMR, which responded to three incidents, two of them fatal.
