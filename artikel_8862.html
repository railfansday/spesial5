New runners always ask me, “What’s the best running shoe?” But there’s truly not one trainer that outperforms them all. Instead of recommending one single shoe, I consider the runner’s experience level and what they’re training for.

Maybe you signed up for your first 5K or you’re a more experienced runner setting your sights on training for your first marathon. Or maybe you’re trying to get faster or just want a new pair of kicks to start run/walking. From beginner to vet, road to trail, neutral to stability, there’s a shoe out there for everyone.

Men kind of have the upper hand in terms of running shoes because most models are designed for men’s feet by default. Even on unisex racing shoes, runners with smaller ankles (typically women) often need to use special tricks like the heel lock lacing technique to tighten the fit because they were built based on a men’s last with a wider collar.

That doesn’t mean you can zone out when it comes to finding the best fit. We have a handy guide to help you find your approximate size before shopping. There’s also width to consider, as well as intention: Do you want a grippy, durable trail shoe or a lightweight trainer for tempo runs?

Here, we break it down for you by naming our top 10 running shoes for men and provide guidance on what features you should consider.

The Peg is back—and our testing proves it was worth the wait. The newest iteration is snappier and more responsive, thanks to a full-length Air Zoom unit that had one tester say, “the design leans into speed.” The shoe is a major improvement compared to its predecessor, which wasn’t necessarily bad; the Peg 41’s ride was just a little lackluster according to our testers’ feedback.

Nike stepped in to make sure the Pegasus 42 would have more rebound. The last is also modified to accommodate wider feet.

“The Nike Pegasus 42 is a solid and stable everyday trainer. Back in 1990 when I was running cross country in high school, my very first shoe was the Nike Air Pegasus and they have made some excellent changes over the past 35 years,” said tester Steven Blasi.

“The Pegasus 42 is nothing like my Pegasus from my high school days,” he continued. “The shoe is not too hard, not too soft, it is just right. The slight rocker bottom of the shoe made for a nice and smooth transition from heel to forefoot and the [Air Zoom unit] added the extra push.”

With the Ellipse you get an affordable model that packs incredible comfort and bounce. New Balance Senior Global Project Manager Paul Zielinski explained to Runner’s World how the brand set out to capture the communal aspect of our sport in a shoe. Think of the Ellipse as your “running group model,” the shoe’s ride meant to embody the rush of going on a fun run with friends.

The shoe has a retooled version of the brand’s Fresh Foam X; it has new geometry to make it springier. Though the shoe feels slightly bulky—it’s not intended for speedwork—its midsole produces a trampoline-like bounce that energizes your run.

“I like the foam,” said shoe tester and Director of News Andrew Daniels. “Some of the shoes I ran in most recently feel more firm than these, like a gym mat. This isn’t *super* sponge-y, but more in that sweet spot of just-right firm and just-right squishy, where it stays pretty bouncy for most of the run and provides good cushioning on the landings.”

Our men and women testers were split on the Ghost 18. Men liked the shoe’s ride and comfy fit, saying that even though the foam isn’t as responsive as other daily trainers they’ve tested, the Ghost 18 is a reliable shoe for logging easy workouts.

“[The Ghost 18] is well-cushioned, especially in the heel,” said Photo Director John Hamilton. “Really noticed it on a long downhill at the end of a longer run. The foam was supportive and adequate especially for slower paces but lacked a ‘pop’ when picking up the pace.”

Hamilton also noted the toe area felt slightly narrow and that a wider width would remedy this hitch (the shoe is available in wide and extra wide, as well as narrow widths).

On the other end, the air mesh upper was uncomfortable for our women testers, who complained they got hot spots under the big toe area. It’s funny to say an iteration of a shoe works better for men’s feet, but that seems to be the case with our testing—which is why the Ghost 18 made this list as Best Daily Trainer.

“I really like easy cruising, when I lay back on my heel and float,” said Runner-in-Chief Jeff Dengate. “I think that’s where this shoe works best, and probably why it’s going to work for newer runners and the majority of us who heel strike.”

Dengate said in February 2025 that the Adizero Evo SL was the shoe of the year, and that he’d feast on it if something better came along. What would lead him to make such a bold claim? For starters, the Lightstrike Pro foam in its midsole is the same cushioning Adidas used in its incredibly effective competition-grade marathon shoe, the Adios Pro 4. The Evo SL drops the Pro 4’s carbon-fiber plate in favor of a nylon shank, lending stability in a more affordable way. Because of its best value attributes (you get a heckuva lot for just $150) and dynamic construction, the shoe has been a mainstay on our lists. Dengate is definitely saved from snacking on shoelaces.

The shoe has some minor drawbacks: The tongue tends to go askew mid-run, and the fit was narrow for some of our testers. These were, by the testers’ admissions, pet peeves, though. They all said they’d continue running in the shoe, especially for speedy efforts.

“I like the lightweight feel of these sneakers,” said tester Ahmad Norman. “This helps me glide and turnover my stride at a rapid pace. I can see that training in these sneakers will most certainly increase my tempo and bring down my splits per mile.”

We love the Cloudmonster 3 at any pace, from casual walks to 4:30 strides. Our testers were impressed with the firm—but soft—Helion cushioning that provided a responsive ride for almost any kind of effort.

The shoe has On’s triple-layer CloudTec cushioning, which makes the bounce and support not much of a mystery when you see the system of holes on the shoe’s rocker shape. A Speedboard promotes propulsion and smooth push-off.

“When I slid my foot into these shoes to try on at the pickup I was instantly blown away with the shear joy it was to have these shoes on my feet,” said shoe tester Dave Brattley. “Needless to say there were some high expectations for them to perform just as well during run and they didn’t disappoint! Fit and feel, check, forefoot cushioning that’s soft but you can still feel the road, check, heel cushioning while walking, check, and durability, check! There wasn’t anything that felt off about these shoes.”

The Guide 19 is basically the same—thank goodness!—as its predecessor. It features the brand’s CenterPath technology, which adds a broader base and higher sidewalls to create a more cradled fit that provides support without constriction. Wide-footed testers love how this feature also creates an accommodating fit in the midfoot.

Pwrrun foam provides max-cushioning, making the shoe suitable for long runs and recovery days. The shoe’s rocker profile promotes fast turnover.

“Like its predecessor, this shoe made me feel good before even starting my runs,” said tester Dave Block. “I just love the comfort and feel! The shoe is very soft and slips on easily. I can’t think of any other shoe that feels this great, with the possible exception of the Adidas Pureboost, but that’s going back several years.”

The Triumph 24 is a true triumph for Saucony. The max-cushioned shoe is a mere 8.8 ounces in a men’s size 9. That’s lighter than our favorite daily trainer, the Ghost 18. The shoe’s new IncrediLux foam provides an incredibly soft—but supportive—platform.

The foam also delivers high rebound, resulting in runs that start off slow then gradually go uptempo because of the shoe’s blend of comfort and high energy return.

Testers loved the fit and feel of the trainer. For runner Phil Nicolosi, it was all about the journey and not about the stats on his watch.

“I rated the Saucony Triumph 24 very highly overall because it was one of the most comfortable max-cushioned shoes I have worn,” he said. “The fit was excellent, the upper was comfortable, and the cushioning made it easy on my aging body. It was especially good for scenic, easy runs when I was not worried about pace and just wanted to enjoy being out there.”

The Clifton is the epitome of a Hoka trainer: soft and supportive cushioning; padded, comfy, accommodating upper; rocker geometry that smooths heel-to-toe transitions. Known for its max-cushioned models, Hoka released two Cliftons this year. The Clifton Pro is an amped-up version of the Clifton 11. But in terms of recommending the best men’s shoes, I’m sticking with the original Clifton.

The Clifton 11 is like running with an old friend. The daily trainer’s rep is up there with the Nike Peg and Brooks Ghost for its overall comfort and responsive ride. The shoe has a compression-molded EVA midsole (CMEVA). Even though it’s comparatively not as bouncy as other trainers with superior foams, you still get high-energy return for daily miles and excellent support running long. It’s also a favorite for run/walking.

The shoe worn by the fastest man, Sabastian Sawe, was the Adidas Adios Pro Evo 3. That shoe sold out in record time—at a pretty penny for $500. The next best thing? The Adidas Adios Pro 5, which is about half the price and in stock now.

The Pro 5 features a new carbon form in the shoe’s midsole; in place of rods, which mapped out the pad of your foot like your metatarsals, Adidas introduces a carbon rim. According to the brand, the rim results in 12.5 percent weight savings. Our testers found the new tech even more explosive than the Pro 4. The ride is snappy and delivers an aggressive but stable bounce thanks to the updated Lightstrike Pro foam, which is slightly firmer.

Dengate praised the shoe’s performance during a speed workout—even though he got dropped. “I loved that I could feel the foam compress and load up with each footstrike, yet feel bouncy and responsive at toe-off,” he said. “These shoes wanted more than my legs could give.”

The Experience Wild 3 has supportive cushioning without limiting ground feel, 3.5mm lugs that bite into loose terrain, and a comfortable, accommodating fit that allows you to splay your toes when hitting the trail.

With a 4mm drop, it’s a good option for runners not ready to commit to zero-drop but who still want to dabble in low-drop. The upper has a protective toe sweep and gaiter attachments.

“[The Experience Wild 3] takes the shoe to the next level with a redesigned upper that fits more generously but doesn’t make you feel like you are swimming around, plusher cushioning that absorbs firmer ground yet remains stable, and a lug pattern on the MaxTrac outsole that I felt was superior to each of the previous models in terms of grip,” said tester John Mikovits.

The shoe is also available with a Vibram Megagrip outsole.

Fit and comfort go hand-in-hand. You don’t want to constantly think of how loose or tight your shoes feel as you run. We recommend looking for a thumbnail’s width of toe room between the tip of your longest toe and the nose of the shoe, which leaves space for your feet to swell as you run.

Generally speaking, your running shoe should be a half size up compared to your regular street shoes. When in doubt, I generally recommend going in for a fitting and getting expert help so you're sure you find your perfect size.

Be mindful of heel rubbing and slipping. You can use the heel lock lacing technique for a snugger fit around the collar. Some runners have a high instep as well. If you feel pressure in the lacing area, you may need a shoe with a wider width.

It’s normal to have some pronation as you run. If your foot rolls excessively inward, however, you may be susceptible to knee and lower back injuries. See a specialist, like a podiatrist or physical therapist, for a gait evaluation. Special insoles and shoes with medial posts can help severe overpronators with erratic foot rolling.

If you have mild pronation or late-onset pronation, some stability shoes have holistic features, like guide rails, that act as subtle bumpers to help you when your form gets sloppy.

We recommend the Brooks Ghost 18 for beginner runners because it provides Goldilocks cushioning: not too soft, nor too hard. It’s a great starting point to see if you sway firm and lightweight or soft and max-cushioned.

Runners prefer lightweight shoes with a thinner midsole for faster efforts. These past years, however, have proven you don’t have to sacrifice stack height for speed thanks to the arrival of super foams. Still, some runners may prefer a simpler, more grounded lightweight racing flat for track workouts or hard tempos.

Max-cushioned trainers are great for long runs and recovery runs postrace, when you’re gradually adding miles again after taking time off from running.

Why should I pay more than $200 for a pair of running shoes?

Top-of-the-line running shoes made for competition feature premium materials, which generally force their prices well over the $200 line. Super foams enhance the running experience; paired with a carbon-fiber plate, this combination can improve your running economy.

These shoes are an investment and a novelty. Not everyone will experience a “4 percent” improvement. And even though super shoes eight years later are made of more durable materials (the recommended usage used to be 100 miles per pair), shelf life is still dependent on the wearer and their wear pattern.

You can also find top quality shoes for a lesser price. Training shoes, like the Saucony Endorphin Speed 6, are constructed with premium foams and have a much lower price tag than their carbon-fiber counterparts.

Can a running shoe actually make me faster?

When Nike released the original Vaporfly in 2017, many runners misinterpreted the brand's “4 percent” claim to mean running 4 percent faster, rather than improving energy efficiency. The combination of responsive super foam with a carbon-fiber plate to work as a stabilizer (the plate actually works in tandem with the foam and isn’t the root cause for propulsion) helps the wearer run more efficiently, using less oxygen and less energy to maintain their speed.

Regardless of what a brand claims, though, the only universal truth about a shoe’s impact on running performance is that no two runners will see the same benefits. A shoe is a tool: It can help you train to become stronger and improve your stride. Lightweight shoes with responsive foams can make you feel swifter and deliver more rebound, but you are the one getting faster.
