The surprising reason women seem better at multitasking than men

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Men and women appear to be similarly skilled at juggling multiple activities, but new research has uncovered one striking difference. In an experiment designed to recreate the demands of everyday life, men ignored conversational questions more than twice as often as women while performing just as well on the other tasks.

Whether driving, working, doing household chores, or enjoying leisure activities, people frequently find themselves managing several tasks at once or switching between them. Although women are widely believed to be better at multitasking than men, previous research has found only minor and inconsistent differences between the sexes, raising doubts about the accuracy of this popular assumption.

With support from the Bial Foundation, researchers André and Diana Szameitat (from Brunel University of London and City St George's, University of London, UK, respectively) set out to determine whether meaningful differences exist in multitasking ability and what might explain the widespread belief that women have an advantage.

In their study, "Men Talk Less Than Women During Multitasking," published in the scientific journal Psychological Research, the researchers developed an experiment involving five different activities. The setup was designed to capture the complexity of everyday multitasking more accurately than most previous studies.

The first experiment involved 41 men and 37 women. Participants were asked to follow a recipe in a kitchen setting, look up phone numbers, match numbers with letters, watch a slideshow for specific words, and answer conversational questions. A new question was posed every 20 seconds (e.g., "Would you rather lose all of your money and valuables or all of the pictures you have ever taken, and why?").

In a second experiment, the researchers recruited 160 observers to determine whether differences in multitasking behavior were noticeable to others. Without being given prior information about the participants or findings, the observers watched video recordings of the experiments and assessed how well the participants performed.

One Surprising Difference Between Men and Women

Men and women achieved comparable results on four of the five activities. Conversation, however, revealed a notable exception. Men left conversational questions unanswered more than twice as often as women. When men did answer, their responses were just as fast and of comparable quality to those given by women.

The researchers suggest that women may, on average, be more inclined to engage in communication during social interactions. However, the study did not directly investigate this possibility, so the explanation should be treated with caution. The results are also consistent with evolutionary theories suggesting that women may have a greater tendency to engage in conversation.

How Conversation Shapes Perceptions of Ability

The differences in conversational engagement appeared to have a considerable impact on how others judged the participants. Observers rated men as having less control over the activities, performing more poorly, putting in less effort, and being less alert than women. They also perceived men as less happy and as deriving less enjoyment from the experience.

Rather than revealing a broad difference in multitasking ability between men and women, the research points to a much more specific behavioral distinction: men are more likely to leave conversations unanswered when managing several activities simultaneously.

Because conversational engagement is easily noticed by others, this difference may create the impression that men are less effective at multitasking, even when their performance on other activities is comparable to that of women. The findings offer a potential explanation for how the popular stereotype that women are better multitaskers developed and why it continues to persist.

"Taken together, our data confirm that there are no substantial sex differences in cognitive visual-manual tasks, but that significant sex differences do exist in the ability to hold a conversation while multitasking," explains André Szameitat. This is "an ability highly salient in everyday life and, thus, could explain the development of the widespread public stereotype that women are better at multitasking than men," the researcher concludes.
