Most "smart contract audit checklist" posts are a list of vulnerabilities someone else already found. They are useless in the first twenty minutes of a review, because you are not looking for vulnerabilities yet — you are trying to build a map of where the money is and who is allowed to move it.

This is the sequence I run when a contract lands in front of me and I have twenty minutes before I have to decide whether it deserves a week. It is ordered by yield per minute, not by taxonomy. Two of the steps below are the ones that paid for themselves: the state-key inventory and the deployed-artifact check.

Before a single line of logic: is the source in front of me the bytecode on chain?

Three things bite here and none of them are in the Solidity file:

Write down: address, implementation, admin, upgrade mechanism, and whether the upgrade path has a timelock. Three minutes, and it frames everything after.

Do not read the code top to bottom. Answer four questions and draw them:

If the answer to (4) can be changed by someone who did not deposit — a donation, a rebase, a fee sent directly to the contract — the accounting is manipulable. That single check is the root of the entire share-inflation family.

The map also tells you which 200 lines matter. In a 20,000-line repo you will spend your twenty minutes in maybe four functions, and the map is how you find them.

This is the step most reviewers skip, and it is where the highest-severity bugs live in non-EVM systems and in EVM contracts that emulate mappings with hashes.

List every storage key the contract writes, and for each one ask: what is the uniqueness domain of this key, and who controls each component?

For each hit, classify every field that goes into the hash:

Any row with a check in the last column that appears in a uniqueness key is a replay bug waiting to be written up. The canonical failure is a claim guard keyed on sha256(sender, txid, amount, timestamp, recipient, reward, data): seven inputs, four of them free, so the same transfer can be claimed once per distinct combination.

The fix is always the same shape: key on the minimum immutable identity, validate the rest against it, and add a global invariant that total payouts per identity never exceed the attested amount.

If the contract has shares, lpTokens, or any "amount × total / supply" formula, this is where you look next. In order:

None of this needs the whole codebase. It needs the four functions from the money map.

Access control. Every onlyOwner / onlyRole / require(msg.sender == ...) — list what it can do. The dangerous ones are setFee, setOracle, setLimit, upgradeTo, sweep, pause, and anything that changes who is allowed to withdraw. If an owner can set a fee to 100%, the protocol's real risk model is "trust the owner", and the report should say so plainly rather than pretending it is decentralised.

Pause and liveness. Can withdrawals be stopped? By whom, for how long, and is there a user exit that bypasses the pause? "Temporary freezing of funds" is a legitimate Medium on most programs, and a pause with no exit is a finding, not a feature.

Loops over user-controlled arrays. This is the quiet killer:

If the loop breaks on the first request it cannot afford, a single large request at the head of the queue blocks every request behind it — including ones that would fit. In a live pool I looked at, the head request was roughly ten times the pool's free balance and the next request was larger still; the loop exited immediately and 12.6 ETH of queued withdrawals sat unpaid behind a 0.5 ETH balance. No cancel function, no partial fill, no reordering. One depositor can grief the entire exit queue for the price of one transaction.

The fix is continue with a running balance, or partial fills, plus a cancel path. If you see break inside a user-ordered queue, that is your finding — you can stop looking.

You now have a list. Do not write the report yet — write the smallest thing that turns a suspicion into a fact.

Twenty minutes is not enough to audit a protocol. It is more than enough to decide whether the protocol has a shape worth auditing, and it is usually enough to find the one bug that matters — because the bugs that matter live in the replay key, the share formula, the queue loop, and the owner's powers, and those are four places, not four thousand.

If you only have five minutes, do 1, 3, and 5. That is where the money is.
