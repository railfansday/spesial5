Hard Times and the Practice of Staying Engaged

Dedicated to Wendell Berry and The Berry Center, whose work continues to inspire care for the land, for community, and for the life we share.

In Standing by Words, Wendell Berry writes, “The impeded stream is the one that sings” (Berry, 1983). I keep returning to this line. Picture a stream running over a smooth, open bed. The water moves easily and makes almost no sound. Put stones in its path, and the water has to find its way around and over them, and that’s when we hear it. The resistance gives the stream its voice.

Berry’s image speaks to the way we live. He sees uncertainty, resistance, and inspiration as essential companions on any meaningful journey. Inspiration opens up possibility. When life becomes hard, our first instinct is usually to want the difficulty gone. We want certainty back. We want the stones out of the stream. Berry offers another way to see it: The moments of not knowing are where wisdom, creativity, and purpose take shape. The challenge is to stay engaged with what baffles and resists us long enough for it to shape us.

Staying engaged needs something to guide us, something to keep us practising, and something to renew us when we’re tired. In therapy, I talk about these as the three M’s of intentional living: a Motto, a Mantra, and a Muse. Each one answers one of Berry’s companions. A motto gives us direction when we’re uncertain. A mantra keeps us steady as we work through resistance. A muse inspires us and reminds us what’s possible.

The three M’s are personal, and the words people choose depend on what they’re facing. The following sections show how a motto, a mantra, and a muse take shape amid self-doubt, a strained relationship, and grief. These are struggles people commonly bring to therapy.

When we can’t see what lies ahead, we lose our bearings. A motto is a statement of principle that restores them, giving us a direction to walk in even as uncertainty remains.

For someone living with self-doubt, “My voice is worth hearing” affirms self-worth. When boundaries are strained, “I protect my peace” honours the right to inner calm. In grief, “I carry love forward” acknowledges the loss while embracing the love that remains. A good motto is short enough to remember and strong enough to stand up to doubt.

Where the motto gives direction, the mantra sustains the practice. We meet resistance in the present moment, in the conversation we’re dreading or the morning that feels too heavy to begin. A mantra is the phrase we bring into those moments and repeat when we most need steadiness.

“I trust my own voice today” helps someone with self-doubt speak up. “I honour my limits with kindness” affirms self-respect without hostility. “I carry their love in my heart” becomes a daily act of remembrance. Each repetition strengthens conviction, and over time the mantra becomes part of the inner language that steadies us. This is the impeded stream finding its way, one stone at a time.

A muse is our source of inspiration. It’s often found in a memory, a ritual, or a symbol: a teacher who encouraged us, a practice that restores balance, a place that brings rest.

In self-doubt, a muse might be the memory of being supported. When strengthening boundaries, it might be a place, real or remembered, that offers safety. In grief, a family ritual, a piece of music or a well-worn object can become a bridge between what was and what continues.

The words that steady us rarely emerge fully formed but slowly surface through reflection, often by noticing what we already value and naming it. We all go through seasons when our confidence dips, when relationships test us, or when we feel the weight of grief. Berry’s insight is that these are the seasons when our lives take their deepest form. A motto, a mantra, and a muse help us move through them with intention. They keep us engaged with what unsettles and resists us, so we can learn what those difficulties have to teach. Like Berry’s stream, we find our voice by meeting what stands in our way.
