Posted November 5, 2026 | Reviewed by Gary Drevitch

Our relationships are the source of some of our greatest joys and deepest pain. When our relationships are thriving, we tend to thrive, and when they’re strained, we suffer. In fact, many of the most pressing problems, not only in our personal lives but also in the larger world, such as interpersonal abuse, war, racism, climate change, and animal exploitation, are driven by dysfunctional ways of relating: to other people, to animals and the environment, and even to ourselves. So building relational literacy—the understanding of and ability to practice healthy ways of relating—can help transform these problems.

Yet the people who most need to build relational literacy are also probably the least likely to pick up a self-help book or go to therapy. They may, however, happily spend an evening engrossed in a novel or bingeing on Netflix.

What if we could build relational skills through reading or watching enjoyable stories?

Stories, whether written or on-screen, teach us things, from how a courtroom works to what healthy communication looks like. Research suggests that stories also shape our beliefs and attitudes, for better or worse. And because every story features characters, and characters are always relating—to others, and at the very least to themselves—every story is teaching us how to relate. The only question is whether we’re learning skillful or harmful ways of relating.

Consider the all-too-common bar scene in which an affronted man “communicates with his fists” by beating up a stranger who was making eyes at his partner. Or the many revenge movies in which an aggrieved parent (often a father) seeks not only to catch, but to brutalize, the person who kidnapped their child. In contrast, series like This Is Us portray complex relationships and characters who are nuanced and vulnerable, who turn toward rather than against or away from each other, and who repair after a relational breach.

Stories can also help us recognize difficult-to-identify dynamics, such as those that arise with addiction or domestic abuse. We often see more clearly when we look at a situation from the outside, as observers of someone else’s experience. For example, the film When a Man Loves a Woman reveals how alcohol dependence impacts a family system, while the novel and movie It Ends With Us reveal how gaslighting and trauma bonding operate in an abusive relationship.

If you’re building relational skills through such means as reading nonfiction or taking courses, fiction can be a powerful supplement. Stories can bring the principles and tools described in nonfiction to life, showing what they look like and how they can be applied. This is why, after writing seven nonfiction books, several of which are about relationships, I wrote a novel. I wanted readers to be able to see relational concepts in action while also being entertained.

To get more out of novels and shows as tools for relational learning, you can use a list of questions for post-reading or -viewing. You can tailor the list to areas you want to grow in or concepts you’re learning through therapy or nonfiction (for example, attachment styles, relational repair, or the Gottmans’ “four horsemen”), and use it for personal reflection or for discussion with someone you’ve read or watched with.

I’ve always believed that a great story leaves the reader or viewer a little better off than they were before. Whether you’re a therapist working with clients or an individual wanting to build your relational skills, consider adding stories to your relational toolkit. Stories not only teach us; they can help us have fun in the process of learning.
