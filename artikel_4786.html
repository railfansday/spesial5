Mental Health Care Has a Time Problem

We tell people to make time for their mental health as if time were theirs to make.

For someone paid by the hour, a therapy appointment can cost wages as well as a copay. For a parent or caregiver, getting help may require arranging care for someone else first. Even when treatment is technically available, it may not be available when we can actually use it.

The World Health Organization is asking that people’s lived experiences shape mental health services. We could start by taking seriously an experience that is easy to mistake for an excuse: “I don’t have time.”

The latest National Survey on Drug Use and Health, released in July, found that 49.1 percent of American adults with mental illness received no mental health treatment in 2025. Among untreated adults who felt they needed care, 48.3 percent said they did not have enough time. Cost was an even more common barrier (61.4 percent).

We need policies that protect time for mental health care. But what are people supposed to do while those protections remain out of reach, or when care isn’t available?

My interest in that question began at Plum Village, a mindfulness practice center founded by Thích Nhất Hạnh. During a stay there, I proudly told a monk named Brother Treasure that I’d just meditated for three hours.

He smiled, “How about three breaths? That’s all that you need to tune into the present moment.”

I was so embarrassed. But that exchange helped shape my research as a psychologist: What if well-being didn’t have to compete with the rest of our lives for time?

At Berkeley, my colleagues and I studied an exercise called self-compassionate touch: resting your hands over your heart and belly while responding to yourself with a kind inner voice. We randomly assigned 135 college students to practice either this exercise or a finger-tapping control group for 20 seconds a day for a month.

Compared to daily finger-tapping, just 20 seconds a day of self-compassionate touch was associated with greater improvements in self-compassion, stress, and mental health. The effects were comparable in size to those found in much more time-intensive interventions.

I call practices like these micropractices. They take 30 seconds or less, or can be woven into something you’re already doing. Noticing your footsteps on the way to the bus. Taking three slow, deep breaths before opening your inbox. Doing a yoga pose between tasks.

Although we extended this research on 20-second self-compassionate touch in a six-month study of 497 U.S. adults, published last month, my research alone can’t tell us how broadly this idea applies. But as I started looking beyond my own research, I found further evidence that practices can have measurable effects in seconds. In a separate experiment, another research group found that 20 seconds of self-soothing touch reduced the stress hormone cortisol following a stressful experience. And recent studies show that 30 seconds of slow, deep breathing can reduce anxiety, slow heart rate, and increase alpha and theta brain-wave activity associated with calm and focused attention.

More broadly, I kept running into studies that challenged an assumption I had largely taken for granted: that more time means more benefit.

An analysis of 203 randomized controlled trials on meditation found duration of sessions wasn’t associated with better mental health outcomes.

A study of 21,088 mindfulness app users objectively tracked session duration and frequency. The pattern was similar: Consistent practice was associated with reduced stress; longer sessions were not.

And it’s not just meditation. In an analysis of 137 trials of touch interventions and practices involving 12,966 people, more sessions were associated with better mental health outcomes. But longer sessions? No extra benefit. In fact, when it came to lowering cortisol, and even reducing blood pressure, longer sessions were less effective than shorter ones.

Breathwork points in the same direction. An analysis of 12 randomized controlled trials of breathwork interventions (like slow deep breathing) totaling 785 adults found that reductions in stress were not associated with session length.

And sometimes, breaking the same amount of movement into smaller pieces can be more beneficial than doing it all at once. In one study, six five-minute walks spread across the day improved mood, fatigue, and food cravings more than a single 30-minute walk.

None of this means that 30 seconds is always enough, or that longer practices have no value. But many of us only have a 30-second window—or at least a lot more of them: between Zoom meetings, in the checkout line, at a red light, or while waiting for the microwave to ring. And if the choice is between 30 seconds and nothing at all, I’d bet on 30 seconds.

The point is not that complex suffering has a 30-second solution. Nor is this a substitute for psychotherapy, medication, or any longer practice that is already helping. It’s more like brushing your teeth: It doesn’t replace the dentist, but it helps you keep things in check. In the same way, these tiny practices can give you a way of not being left empty-handed when the system isn’t there.

Lack of time, however, isn’t merely a personal problem.

A 2024 study found that after states adopted paid-sick-leave mandates, Medicaid-financed prescriptions for mental health conditions increased by about 6 percent per year. Protecting people’s time can help them use care. And we can also design forms of support that demand less of that time. These aren’t competing approaches. We need both.

Institutions need to do more than tell us to take care of ourselves. A company that offers employees a breathing exercise should protect time to attend a therapy appointment. A health system that offers a self-guided resource should provide a clear route to a clinician.

The best self-help doesn’t teach people that they should be able to handle everything themselves. It creates more moments in which help is possible while leaving the door open to more.

We need to move beyond reminding people that their mental health matters. We need to take “I don’t have time” seriously. People deserve care that fits the lives they actually have. Sometimes that means systems that protect an hour for therapy. Sometimes it means discovering that a meaningful practice doesn’t require another hour, or even another minute.

It can begin with 30 seconds you already have.
