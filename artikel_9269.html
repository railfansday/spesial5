The Unretirement Movement

More people of a certain age are holding jobs rather than waiting for their Social Security checks to arrive in the mail. The demographics of the American workforce are changing, and for the better, as it becomes more diverse in terms of age.

A 2026 AARP survey of adults age 50 and older found that over six months, 7 percent of retirees “unretired,” meaning they reentered the labor force. Not surprisingly, most of them reported that their primary reason for returning to work was to make money.

Financial problems most commonly push retirees back into the workforce: Spiraling healthcare costs or a health-related emergency are primary reasons that retirees return to work either part-time or full-time. Recent inflation spikes have also driven many older Americans to look for work, as the rising cost of living has reduced spending power. What might have seemed like a sizable nest egg has shrunk for many Americans, and the thought of running out of money is understandably terrifying.

Purpose, Structure, and Social Connection

Not just financial necessity but simply wanting to stay busy or engaged is behind the unretirement movement. While money is the major factor in returning to the workforce in some way, many retirees have found their post-career lives are not the nirvana they had expected. Americans as a whole have a strong work ethic, and full-time leisure is not as fulfilling or rewarding for those who have been productive for decades. Research indicates that engaging in work provides routine, purpose, and social connection, which can boost physical and mental well-being.

A growing labor shortage in many industries may help drive the unretirement movement and options for retirees who prefer to work on their own terms. Older adults are, among many other things, selling health insurance, becoming real estate agents, tutoring and substitute teaching, and working in retail or food service in their spare time.

Our knowledge economy also allows retirees to leverage the skills and wisdom they have gained over the years by serving as life coaches, youth mentors, community outreach specialists, and caregiving companions. According to Pivoters, which connects people over age 55 with employers looking for experienced talent, the United States has more than "100 million people 55 and over, most of whom need to work for financial reasons and want to work for the longevity, health, and social benefits of work.”

Baby boomers, now in their 60s and 70s, are not surprisingly leading the unretirement movement. In their book The Talent Revolution: Longevity and the Future of Work, Lisa Taylor and Fern Lebo examine the gradual sidelining of boomers in the American workforce. In the 1980s and 1990s, boomers were undoubtedly an economic powerhouse; however, a couple of decades later, society thinks they have little to contribute. The authors argue that digital technology created a false perception of this generation as obsolete—akin to viewing them as beta-max. The authors see older adults as essential to the future workplace, and longevity may pave the way for the coming “talent revolution.” “We identify boomers as revolutionaries—a population of disruptors that is altering career patterns, creating new expectations, and demanding inclusion,” they write.

Pairing Older Workers’ Wisdom with Younger Workers’

Chip Conley has conceived perhaps the most compelling and welcoming workplace scenario for older adults. The notion of modern elderhood has been simmering for some time, but Conley has shown how it could serve as a platform for age-friendly work. In his early 50s, Conley sold his business and wasn’t sure what to do next when he unexpectedly found a new gig as the designated “elder” for Airbnb, the online vacation rental marketplace. At that company, like most tech-driven ones, digital intelligence was the primary job qualification, putting young people with little managerial experience in major positions of power.

Conley wrote in his book Wisdom at Work, "Here exists a generation of older workers with invaluable skills—high emotional intelligence, good judgment born out of decades of experience, specialized knowledge, and a vast network of contacts—who could pair with these ambitious millennials to create businesses that are built to endure.” Conley advised older workers to repurpose their experience by seeking recognition as wise elders in organizations, like village elders in traditional societies who wielded authority based on the wisdom that came with age. Wise words indeed.
