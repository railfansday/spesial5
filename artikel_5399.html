Kevin Warsh has Fed staff building Wall Street-style contingencies for AI outcomes: From a growth boom to bad actors attacking critical infrastructure

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

The role of the Federal Reserve in the AI boom has, so far, been undefined: Will the central bank be reactive, sitting on the sidelines until data impacting its mandate demands action? Or will it seek to get ahead of economic shifts driven by transformative technology? The Fed’s new chairman, Kevin Warsh, doesn’t seem content to watch from the sidelines.Speaking yesterday in San Francisco at the Productivity and Jobs Task Force Conference, hosted by the Federal Reserve Board and the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, Warsh said the Fed is conducting contingency planning.

“The Board’s staff has begun to evaluate various contingencies, applying stylized, model-based frameworks to generate scenarios,” Warsh said. “These are illustrative scenarios, not forecasts. Moreover, many other outcomes—probably including one that will prove most accurate—could be drawn.”

Of course, the Fed’s expertise and analysis of AI are nothing new: it already publishes datasets monitoring everything from AI adoption rates by firm size to the impact of AI-related investment on the economy.

But Warsh hinted at work that establishes what outcomes may look like, rather than observing current trends. He continued: “As policymakers, we have to be mindful that great opportunities rarely come risk-free. And the risks in this case, if overlooked, can be highly disruptive and economically costly. SI [Super intelligence] empowers all, including bad actors, so we are thinking especially of critical infrastructures and their potential vulnerabilities.”

Before his return to the central bank (Warsh was the Fed’s youngest governor in history, previously serving between 2006 and 2011), hawkish analysts had been somewhat alarmed by how bullish the incoming chairman had been about AI.

But since his nomination by President Trump this spring, Warsh has struck a more speculative tone. “Our aim today is a humble one,” he said yesterday, “to ask the right questions.”

Warsh outlined multiple risks, for instance the threat posed to critical infrastructure if the technology falls into the wrong hands. He also said that widespread automation could increase unemployment. AI could also “disappoint,” he said, if expected returns fall flat, leading to an economic slowdown.

There’s also opportunity, he said: “SI leads to an increase in productivity and economic gains that are shared broadly across labor and the owners of capital. In that event, real GDP growth rises persistently above its long-run average, and the U.S. stays close to full employment.”

If contingency planning across a spectrum of outcomes sounds familiar, that’s because it’s a staple Wall Street habit. Warsh is a former Morgan Stanley vice president and executive director.

You don’t have to look far for examples: JPMorgan pulled together a war room in January last year following the re-election of President Trump, analyzing and evaluating the policies Trump had espoused while out on the campaign trail.

Bank of America and Citigroup reportedly set up similar internal functions to support investors.

Later that year, David Garfield, the co-CEO of consulting firm AlixPartners, told Fortune that his team was working with major companies across every industry to build tariff war rooms: mechanisms for responding to the shifting policy landscape navigated over the past two years.

Warsh’s planning is not a response to an immediate policy question, rather an issue of preparedness. “While I’m not here to offer predictions, I’m not exactly impartial about American leadership in a time so filled with both challenge and possibility,” Warsh said. His ultimate goal is to create a dynamic economy for future generations, and promote “human flourishing.”
