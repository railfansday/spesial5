Brava's Ghost Feeds Finds Nearly 1,200 Organizations Exposed to Log Injection

Security logs are supposed to be the record of truth inside a company, the place investigators turn when they need to know what really happened. New research from Brava Security suggests that outside parties can write into that record without permission. The company says its findings show that more than 2,300 exposed log collectors, belonging to nearly 1,200 organizations, accepted security events from outside parties without asking for any credentials.

The work is part of a research project Brava Security calls "Ghost Feeds," and it focuses on the logging infrastructure businesses depend on to detect threats, investigate breaches and make security decisions. According to the company, the central concern is that attackers may be able to manipulate the very evidence security teams rely on. That concern grows as more organizations hand investigative work to security tools powered by artificial intelligence (AI), which makes the integrity of the underlying telemetry even more critical.

To measure the scope of the problem, Brava Security's researchers sent a single harmless event, uniquely marked so it could be identified, to exposed log collectors across seven products. More than 2,300 endpoints accepted that event without authentication. The company says those endpoints represent nearly 1,200 distinct organizations, and it cautions that the real total could be higher because owners were identified for only about one-third of the endpoints.

Results differed from one platform to the next. Brava Security reports that roughly three-quarters of the exposed Elasticsearch clusters that responded accepted the test event. Nearly two-thirds of sampled TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) Syslog collectors did the same, as did 43% of identified exposed Fluent Bit endpoints. The researchers also note that, depending on their security configuration, Graylog GELF (Graylog Extended Log Format) inputs may accept events without authenticating senders.

Splunk presented a different picture. Its HTTP (Hypertext Transfer Protocol) Event Collector rejected 58% of the test writes, which the company attributes to token-based authentication requirements. In Brava Security's laboratory testing, however, the primary forwarding port on a standard Splunk installation could accept unauthenticated events in plain text. Brava Security reports that more than 100,000 internet hosts listen on that port number, and 42% of a sample simply accepted unauthenticated plain-text events.

The project started with a single decoy system. Brava Security placed a decoy SIEM (security information and event management) system on the public internet with no legitimate organizational traffic flowing into it. Within approximately two hours, unknown parties had begun reading it, and attempts to inject fabricated events soon followed. Those events were crafted to look as though they came from systems the senders did not control, and the activity continued for at least two weeks.

"Security teams cannot afford to assume that everything appearing in their SIEM is trustworthy," said Idan Basre, CEO and Co-Founder of Brava Security. "If an attacker can influence the evidence an organization uses to investigate incidents, they can potentially change the outcome of an investigation before anyone realizes the underlying data has been compromised."

Brava Security's researchers went beyond false alerts to show how injected data can affect operations and budgets. In one laboratory test, 13 cents worth of bandwidth produced enough data to exceed a Splunk daily license allowance by 2.39 times in 141 seconds. In certain Splunk Enterprise licensing configurations, repeated licensing violations can eventually disable search functionality while indexing continues, which could leave a security team unable to investigate an incident.

A separate demonstration showed that flooding a logging system with junk data could push legitimate events outside their retention window just 17 seconds after an index rollover. The researchers also showed how an attacker's email could travel through a legitimate, authenticated connector and then appear in the SIEM as company infrastructure.

The research arrives as many companies delegate more of their security investigations to AI agents. Brava Security tested three popular open-source AI SOC (security operations center) tools against the same suspicious login scenario. All three tools correctly identified the activity at first. After the researchers injected ten fabricated Windows events into the logs, all three missed the suspicious login.

For organizations, the lesson is that automated conclusions depend on the records underneath them. "AI investigators are only as reliable as the evidence they analyze," said Itay Gabbay, CTO and Co-Founder of Brava Security. "When attackers can write directly into the systems that supply that evidence, they gain an opportunity to influence what security teams and their AI agents see, investigate and ultimately dismiss."

Alongside the findings, Brava Security says it is giving organizations a practical way to check their own exposure. The company has released ghost-telemetry-check, a free, open-source tool that tests whether logging pipelines accept unauthenticated events. The release also includes detection rules for Splunk, Microsoft Sentinel and other platforms, and the company says the full ghost-feeds repository is public for teams that want to review their telemetry infrastructure.

Brava Security notes that the study was carried out without using credentials against any tested system and without accessing target organizations' data. The findings were published in aggregate, and no individual organization was identified, leaving businesses with a clear view of the risk and a free starting point for testing their own logging setups.
