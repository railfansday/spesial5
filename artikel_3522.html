Nearly a Decade Ago My Friend’s Private Plane Fell Out of the Sky. Then, in Florida This Month, It Happened to Another Friend.

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

When I was in my late 20s, I lived in the worst building in the best part of SoHo. My apartment above the French restaurant Raoul’s had been created by merging two units, so it was quite spacious. When I decided to move in with my boyfriend in Los Angeles, I still planned to spend a quarter of my time in New York and wasn’t about to give up this gem. But I figured I could find a roommate who would be psyched I wouldn’t be there a ton, and keep the smaller bedroom for myself.

The writer Deborah Schoeneman introduced me to her best friend from Cornell University, who she said would be the perfect roommate. Jen Blumin was a Sex and the City character come to life: a tall, gorgeous blonde who managed a high-end event space with a private pool a few blocks away, mostly for corporate sponsors. I figured she wouldn’t be home much while I was, and told her if she had $1,800 a month, she had a deal. The day she moved in, she gingerly walked up the four flights of stairs carrying a large wrought-iron cage containing a shrieking bird. “Did I not tell you I had a cockatoo?” she asked with such insouciant camp, such mock innocence, that I just had to laugh.

Schoeneman, Grigoriadis, and Blumin in 2010 at Round Hill Resort.

Thus began one of my life’s great friendships, proceeding through many more ripostes, a decade of shared living space, and the birth of two kids each. Jen became wildly successful by turning vacant institutional buildings into heart-stopping event spaces, and even landed the contract for New York Fashion Week at the old post office across from Pennsylvania Station. The love of her life and father of her children, the architect James Ramsey, conceived of an idea with Dan Barasch, another Cornell chum, to create a “low line” under the old Delancy Street trolley terminal, and for a while it seemed like that was going to happen. But the relationship fell apart, and then things move very fast in my memory: Jen started to say she disliked New York City and wanted to live full time in the North Fork, a new boyfriend showed up, now she was moving to Puerto Rico for the climate and tax breaks, then she and the boyfriend were buying a private plane to fly direct from Puerto Rico to Islip, NY.

When I heard about this new acquisition by my far-more successful friend, I have to admit I thought it sounded rad. Private planes occupy an almost mystical place in our culture. You’re up in the skies, free of seatmates chomping tuna sandwiches or annoyances like last-minute checked bags. You’re watching the clouds drift by, close enough to touch God. So what if her plane wasn’t the fanciest! It was still glamorous. As former Vanity Fair editor Tina Brown wrote on her Substack, everyone wants to be your friend when you have a plane: “The late TV legend Barbara Walters used to lament the necessity to vacation every year with the crass termagant CEO of a bra company just because said termagant happened to own her own plane.”

For the wealthy, private planes, which have become increasingly popular as a mode of transportation in recent years, aren’t only the ultimate in seclusion, but also the ultimate in spontaneity. You’re in a power position that’s completely the reverse of commercial flights, which reduce you to helpless nose-boned chattel. If you’re paying the bill, most decisions are controlled by you: You decide when you leave, who to invite, what to eat (certainly not pretzels and ginger ale), and whether there’s space for your three bulldogs.

But the day after Mother’s Day in 2017—which Jen memorialized by sending a group text to myself and the core girlfriends with a snapshot of her two toddlers on a big rock in Puerto Rico—Jen’s private plane fell out of the sky. She was flying with her children about 15 miles east of Eleuthera, an island in the Bahamas, in the Bermuda Triangle. None of them were found.

I think a lot about Jen, but rarely more than now, nearly a decade after her death. That’s because this month, the same set of New York friends—loosely—are mourning another person who died with his family, including two more children under five years old. Adam Robinson, who chartered the Learjet that dove into a Florida road on November 1, leaving hypersurreal audio and video recordings that are now seared into the nation’s collective memory, arrived in New York a little later than Jen. I met him in the mid-2000s, but he seemed to be cut from the same cloth as she was: a tall, gregarious Lehman Brothers trader who had inherited a loft on lower Broadway from our mutual friends, the founders of the website College Humor (there was a lot of apartment swapping in lower Manhattan back then).

Adam was the definition of a “good guy”: smart, kind, and wry at the right moments. He had the open-hearted self-possession of someone who had traveled widely, but now he was in New York City to start his career, which, like so many of us, involved being hyper-social. He was not a close friend, but I was always happy to see him, and would usually spend 10 minutes at a crowded party chatting before moving on (this means a lot if you, like me, ascribe to the Fitzgeraldian notion that large parties provide intimacy, and prefer not to waste your breath in this rarefied milieu on dolts). A few guys in the crowd were into email marketing, and I heard that Adam had started a business in that industry but didn’t know much about his life after he moved to Austin, nor that he, too, had become quite successful.

It’s eerie that Adam’s private plane was headed to Eleuthra, the same area where Jen disappeared. But beyond that coincidence, what I keep returning to is how Jen and Adam’s stage in life had so many similarities: both in their 40s, too early to retire, they had nevertheless entered what you could call the “second phase” of success. They weren’t only trying to make money but to enjoy the money they’d made, and for them, that meant devising a new kind of life. Adam had moved from Austin to Aspen and wanted to pursue more outdoor adventures, some of which involved destinations hard to reach by commercial flights. Jen didn’t need to take her fatal private journey—JetBlue flies direct from San Juan to JFK—but having her own plane definitely came with bragging rights, though neither was the type to post a “flashing a peace sign on a PJ” semi-candid shot on Instagram.

And yet the private plane, so much a symbol of freedom and success for both Jen and Adam, led to their demise. This type of aviation is far less regulated than commercial planes, which hew to “strict government regulations enumerated in hundreds of pages of fine print…everything from the stowing of tray tables to announcements concerning lavatory smoke detectors,” as Jeff Wise wrote in Vanity Fair in 2025. That may be okay when you’re traveling in a $75 million G800, or as a guest of Drake in his $185 million plane, or with Kylie Jenner in her $73 million Bombardier, but when you’re on a small plane, it matters. Wise notes in his story that since 2020, almost half of the private plane crashes in the US have been Citations, which are on the smaller side.

And when traveling in a plane that lacks the sophistication of a commercial jet, whether you’re JFK Jr., Aaliyah, or just two people that everyone in a certain downtown Manhattan set knew in the mid-aughts, weather happens. Bad decisions in the cockpit compound, if the pilots even have time to make them. And then everything you’ve worked so hard for is lost, with the wreckage extending to family members and best friends left behind. “If I was Adam, I would rather be on the plane than on the ground” is the refrain I keep hearing from my friends this week, meaning that they’d rather perish with their families than live without them. Success and its luxuries are wonderful things. But they don’t guarantee you’ll get to come home.
