High Sierra Storm — John Muir Trail Day 20

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

The rain fell off-and-on throughout the night.  It was a light rain, as I discovered when I left my cabin to pee.  It felt like the threat of a serious storm may have been overblown, to coin a pun.

Lying warm in my cozy bed in Red’s Meadow, I tried to discern whether I was dreaming (asleep) or daydreaming (awake).  I was thinking about the pleasures of life and the inevitability of death.  Lighthearted stuff like that.  Maybe I was dreaming and asleep, and death is really not inevitable after all?  The advice I found on the cabin wall was a bit more down-to-earth!

Red’s Meadow Rules might apply anywhere, right?

When I saw that it was 6:30, I thought, “Well, breakfast starts at 7 a.m., so I had better get going.”   I got dressed, got ready, and got mostly packed.  I assembled the remainder of my resupply, what I was not going to take with me.  This was mostly a lot of M&M weight and one excess freeze-dried packet.  Then I headed off to the restaurant, where I was first in line for breakfast.

Being first in the morning?  That was new for me!  I got a couple of eggs, bacon, a couple of pancakes (of which I ate only one), coffee, and orange juice.  It all seemed very civilized as I chatted with the people at the table next to me.  They were from my home state, from Seattle.  She was a baker and he was a high-rise window cleaner.

After a stop at the privy, and a stop to return my key, I got back to my room.  At 8:00, I prepared to depart.  I recognized that my hiking gloves were in bad shape, but a pair I found abandoned in the laundry were three sizes too small.  So I stuck with what I had.

The gloves would not last beyond the trip, but were still usable. You can see I faithfully splinted my mallet finger.

Just about then the rain picked up.  So, in my cozy little room, I put on my dry rain poncho.

Putting on a poncho is easy with a friend to help.  Alone, it can be more challenging.  The main problem is that you cannot see your own back.  You presume that you’re covering everything properly, but since you can’t see it, you can’t be certain.  But I thought I got it.

I headed down the stairs outside my cabin and out into the rain.

This was a popular hiking area where a lot of different trails intermingle and crisscross.  It was therefore potentially easy to get a little lost.  I saw a workable route on my app that went past the Devil’s Postpile, so I took that one.  Why not check it out?

As I approached it, the first people I met said, “Oh, the Devil’s Postpile entails a long hike up to a high point.”

I thought, “I don’t really have time to do that.”

Then the next group I met said, “Oh, it’s a short little hop just up to the top of that hill there and down.”

Once again, I marveled at how different the perceptions of different people can be, when describing the same thing.  I figured, “When am I ever going to be here again?  I may as well go see the Devil’s Postpile.”  So, I started off.  It was an uphill climb, but it was short.  The second description was more accurate.

Where the mostly-hexagonal basalt columns stuck out from the hillside was most dramatic.  On the top they formed a sort of tiled floor, like finely paved flagstones of basalt that fit together perfectly.  I took some pictures, then I came down.

Devil’s Postpile, showing a wet paved floor

Overall, it was less than overwhelming, but still interesting.  As I proceeded on my way, I came to a trail crossing.   To test the direction, I went straight ahead for about 100 yards and then checked my FarOut app.  It indicated that I was going the wrong way.  So, I went back to the crossing and took the other trail.

Subsequently, I looked at the map of the area and realized I would have traveled a shorter distance by going in my original direction.  The app added about a half mile to my day.  Not a big deal, but why?

At that point getting to my destination, Garnet Lake, represented an elevation gain of over 3,500 feet, and an elevation loss of 1,500 feet.  It looked like a lot of up and down, and overall, a really, hard day.  But I was feeling strong, and I was passing a lot of younger people on the trail.

The route was very good, not super steep, so that the elevation gains were manageable.  I hiked them without stopping and catching my breath every 60 steps.  Surprised at how fast I was moving, and how much progress I was making, I started to think, “Well, my goal is Garnet Lake.  That’s my planned schedule.  But maybe I can go the extra two-and-a-half miles up to Thousand Island Lake?  This will put me ahead of schedule in the days ahead.”

Stream and Meadow en Route to Garnet Lake (Storm Threatening)

As I was thinking all these thoughts about the future, about the huge miles I was going to pound out, the weather let up.  I was not so foolish as to take off the rain poncho yet, but I doffed the hood so I could see better and feel the breeze and hear better.

Up to this point I had been leapfrogging a group of four guys.  This time, now that I could hear, I asked the lead fellow what their story was.  He told me, in a British accent, “We are here section hiking, doing a LASH (Long Ass Section Hike) on the John Muir Trail, finally finishing it in fact.  We are doing it with our dad; it is just our dad and his three sons.”

I was moved by that, particularly as someone alienated from his own two sisters, to see their family unity.  It warmed my heart, actually, and before hiking on I told them so.

I had set one p.m. as the target time to have my lunch.  Yet around 12:15, the clouds thinned so that the sun was strong enough to cast somewhat of a shadow through them.  Just then, during that period of slight sunshine, I had a chance to have my lunch on a rock overlooking a lovely lake.  But no, I remained determined I was going to wait until one o’clock.

The rain had (temporarily) stopped when I passed this lake

The trail turned sharply downhill to another lake, and I realized that most of my 1,500-foot elevation loss on the day would be descents down to lakes.  This time, when I got down to  the lakeshore, it was finally lunchtime, and I stopped to eat near the shore.  Now there was no longer any sign of the sun, and the clouds were massing, but I parked my gear, took out my food, and sat on a rock overlooking the lake.

My lunch site (before the weather turned)

I had selected a tamale for lunch, a favorite high-calorie treat.  As I ate my tamale, it got a little chillier, a little windier, and I started to feel the first few drops of rain.  I finished up the tamale, ate a Snickers bar, ate some jerky, and by then a little more rain was falling.  So I cut short the lunch, finished up what I had taken out, and quickly packed it up.  Before returning to the trail, I put my rain gear back on, no longer hopeful that I was done needing it for the day.

At that point I still felt strong, and I was still thinking I might make it to Thousand Island Lake.  If not, well, my original destination at Garnet Lake would be fine too.

On the trail in my poncho, long before it got really bad…

As I continued hiking, the rain fell harder and heavier, and the wind increased steadily.  Visibility dropped and the rain turned into a true downpour.  While I was dry enough under the poncho, my arms and hands, holding my poles, were sopping wet.  The same was true of my legs and feet.  They were all getting cold.  There was so much water on the trail I could no longer avoid walking through mud puddles – the whole trail was a mud puddle!

A the wind increased, the rain was not falling straight down, but falling at a wind-blown angle.  Also, because the mountains shape the wind, it came from different angles at different moments.  As I gained elevation, it also became gustier, buffeting me in my rain poncho that served almost like a large wet sail.

As the wind blew harder, I could hear it even with my hood on, as a roaring sound.  Sometimes it would abate for a few minutes, only to resume with increased force.  At one point, I heard an especially loud blast howling toward me and just had time to turn my back to brace myself when it hit.  I am sure that gust was at least 50 MPH and possibly more.  The rain was relentless too.  This was a true High Sierra storm!

Shortly after that, three people passed by me.  They dropped a quick “Hello” as they went by, nice enough, but they were clearly focused on the trail.  I saw that they had ropes, and ice axes; clearly, they were climbers.  I wondered what an ice axe is for in the summer when there’s no ice?  As they were not moving that much faster than I was, I resolved to speed up a little to keep pace with them.  Actually, I was a little worried about my safety, and I thought it might be good to remain in shouting distance, just in case…

I kept up for about a mile, all of us fighting the wind and rain, but then they turned left at a fork in the trail and I continued right towards Garnet Lake.  I was alone again.

I passed another couple of people, going down, and I asked, “Are we crazy, are we literally insane to be out here?”  Through the steady downpour, they looked at me like I really was crazy.

Taking pictures of green rocks in the middle of a rainstorm? Maybe I really am insane!

At one point, I passed a tent pitched by the side of the path.  It looked so cozy and nice, I felt a pang of jealousy.  But I kept going.

The problem is, you don’t know when it’s going to end, right?  I mean, it could clear up in an hour.  Or maybe two hours?  Or maybe five hours?  Or maybe 12 hours?  In each of those cases, if you knew, really knew, then the decision “What should I do?” would be so much clearer.  If “one hour” then you should keep going,  it will be over soon.  If “12 hours” then you should find the next reasonably good camp site and hunker down until the morning.

The next guy coming down helped me to decide.  He said, “You know, the weather conditions are even worse higher up, and it’s more exposed.”  That meant at my presumed destination, Garnet Lake, the weather would be worse, finding a camp site would be harder, and, in the wind, pitching my tent would be a nightmare.

A little further on, then, I pulled aside, off the trail.  I spotted a father and his son there, also pulled over, just about 10 yards away from me.  I started talking with them.

The son was in his early teens, and it was his first hiking trip!  So, I explained to him the concept of Type Two fun and assured him he was getting GREAT stories to tell his pals when he got back to school.  They were very much thinking about turning around, and going back down.  I understood; it was miserable!  They’d started from a meadow below, and were now thinking of just going back to that meadow, where there was even a chance of getting a shuttle to town.  They decided to turn back.

Before leaving, however, the father pointed out that a little deeper in the shelter of the woods there was a potential tent site.  I went and looked at it.  It seemed drained.  I mean, obviously it was soaking wet, but it didn’t have a puddle in it.  I thought, “Okay, forget Garnet Lake, I’m going to try this.”

Here’s the problem with rain.  Hiking in the rain isn’t actually so bad, if you have rain gear and stay warm by moving steadily.  But making camp or breaking camp in the rain is a misery.  So it was in this case.

I was trying to stay as dry as possible, trying to create a little dry haven under the trees.  But the rain was still pouring down.  I did my best, but the dry haven became a wet mess in a very short time.  As fast as I could go was nowhere near fast enough.  The ground sheet, which I staked immediately, so it wouldn’t go flying in the wind, was soaking wet well before I put the tent down over it.  Then the tent was sopping wet by the time I got the rain fly over it.  That created my dry space, but it wasn’t a dry space anymore, because it had been rained on heavily for, I don’t know, five minutes?  Eight minutes?  Maybe even 10 minutes?

However long it took to pitch the tent, my next move was to shove my whole soaking wet backpack in there.  Very few items, like my completely soaking rain gear and my hiking poles, did not go in.  The last soggy item to go inside the tent was… me.

Once inside, I was dismayed to see there were big puddles of water all over the floor of the tent.  I was really worried; I wondered if I had torn or compromised my tent floor and water was leaking in that way.  I came inside with my shoes on initially, urgently escaping the rain.  But this was stupid, because I hadn’t set down my sleeping pad to cushion the floor.  In a site like this, that I had not scoured for rocks and twigs beforehand, there was a real danger of tearing the tent floor.

What to do?  I took my bandana and soaked it up on the tent floor, then reached outside the tent flap and squeezed it out like a sponge.  I did this again and again and again.  Eventually I saw that the amount of water on the tent floor was decreasing, and more water did not seem to be coming in.  That was a relief!  I continued until the floor was only wet, and no longer soaking.

Then I lay there for a while, just resting and listening to the storm still gushing water and roaring wind outside.  But I realized, there was a certain danger of hypothermia if I continued in my state of paralysis.  I roused myself then and got my shoes off and into the covered vestibule; I got my soaking wet socks out there too.

The great news was that between my rain poncho, and the plastic bag liner inside my pack, the pack contents were all dry.  I took off my shirt and my shorts, and I put on a dry shirt from inside my pack, and dry pants, and fresh socks.  Now I was feeling better.

I did not worry about bears.  If they wanted to eat me or my food in this weather, they were welcome to us.  In the tent I opened my bear cannister and selected a packet of freeze-dried dinner for cold soaking.  It was a chicken alfredo, or something like that, a chicken in a white sauce with noodles.  It ranked among the blandest meals I ever ate, but it was calories.  I forced myself to eat every bite of it.

I stretched out my pad and pulled my dry sleeping bag out of its stuff sack.  To stay warm, I put my puffy on, my shirt on, my pants on, and my wool socks on.  After about an hour or two, the heaviest rain did start to abate, though it didn’t end.  To the sound of the wind searching and seeking and moving through the mountaintops, I dozed off.

When I woke again at 9 p.m., the downpour had been over for a couple of hours, the wind was down, but it was still occasionally dripping rain.  The temperature had dropped, but wearing dry clothes I was warm enough.

I realized that instead of the vision of hiking beyond my intended destination, I was now a couple of miles short of where I intended to camp.  If the rain resumed in the morning, I was not going to leave my tent.  However, if it was dry, I would face a long day.

As I dozed off again, I listened to the sound of raindrops falling on my tent from the pine needles overhead.  To my surprise, I found it soothing and I slept.
