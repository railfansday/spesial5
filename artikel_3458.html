20 Years Ago, Nintendo’s Awkwardly Named Console Defied All Its Biggest Doubters

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Nintendo is a force in the video game industry, currently riding high with the Switch 2, but it has had dips and missteps like any other company. After strong performances during the 8-bit and 16-bit eras, its performance started to slip with the powerful Nintendo 64, which gave unexpected ground to the PlayStation console that Nintendo itself inspired Sony to build. Despite the GameCube’s impressive tech, the hardware continued to bleed market share to new competitors, and it was clear something needed to change to avoid a fate worse than Sega.

Enter the Wii. Nintendo’s little white box with controllers that looked like television remotes, complete with detachable “dongles,” wound up being just the savior the company needed. But it faced several uphill battles, and the first one was its unconventional name.

When Nintendo first started talking about its next generation of hardware, the Wii was known by the codename “Revolution.” It’s the kind of term that hints at the future the Wii actually made possible. For me, it conjured up imagery of sleek hardware, perhaps silver and with the sort of aesthetic we saw from Sony with its early mock-ups of the PlayStation 3. (Remember the boomerang-shaped controller?) That’s the sort of thing I expected from the system that eventually became the Wii. Instead we got something closer to the banana patent.

By the time preorders became available, Nintendo had finally revealed its very simple final name. Somewhere online, I learned that people who wanted one (a group I was very much a part of) could head to the local shop and stand in line for the chance to preorder it. But there was a problem: those preorders wouldn’t open until 10AM on a weekday morning, while kids were in school and employees were on the clock.

I was an employee by then, and I didn’t have enough warning to request the day off in advance. But I had a reasonably understanding boss, who agreed that the other people in the service department could field customer inquiries while I went to stand out in the cold for a chance to preorder Nintendo’s next console from the EBGames store that had recently opened in town.

The day arrived and I stood in that line, shivering alongside other Nintendo fans my age and older. A few Sony Bend employees were among us, every bit as eager to get their hands on the next console from Nintendo. I felt a bit silly to be there, but the thought of missing out overrode my embarrassment. Thankfully, I’d arrived in time. I was one of the last few people in line who got to sign that magical sheet of paper. Most of the other people behind me were turned away.

When I showed up at work, my boss wanted to know if I had been successful. I had, I assured him. Then I had to endure ribbing from my coworkers, who thought it was hilarious that I would bother to miss work for something with such a dumb name. In their defense, I worked in a computer store. They wouldn’t have been on my side no matter the console.

Someone in Nintendo of America’s marketing department must have had some idea what sort of ridicule fans like me would face, but I imagine they didn’t have a lot of input. Reggie Fils-Aimé, the president and CEO of North American operations at the time, did at least insist on making Wii Sports the pack-in game, so the decisions that mattered most were still made correctly. One wonders what role he played in the commercials, now iconic, which featured two Japanese guys showing up at someone’s door and saying “Wii would like to play.”

If I’m being honest, the name “Wii” feels a bit silly to me even now. It is bested in Nintendo history only by the name of its successor, which sounds like a noise an ambulance might make.

With a name like Wii, the puns write themselves. They even led to one tragic marketing stunt. I’ve never forgotten reading about some radio jockeys who (when the system was still all but impossible to find in local stores) secured a unit and then held a disastrous on-air contest in Sacramento. Whoever could drink the most water within a narrow window—and who proved they could “hold their Wii”—would win the hardware. A woman emerged the victor and left with a Wii, but didn’t live long enough to enjoy it. It turns out you can drown by drinking too much water, even when you drink it intentionally.

Today, when we talk about the Wii, we often remember some of the games it made possible. We recall the impact it had on the industry at large. Microsoft and Sony were initially too cool and mature to play in Nintendo’s juvenile sandbox. After they realized they were behind the curve, they produced the Kinect camera and Move controllers. Everyone wanted to have a boat in the “blue ocean” of gaming that Nintendo proved existed. The people in nursing homes and elementary schools who incorporated the Wii into their fitness routines didn’t care about the hardware’s name; they only cared that they were having a good time. Kids who asked for a Wii for Christmas didn’t mind that their older brother’s friends made fun of them for getting that instead of Grand Theft Auto. They were too busy having fun with Super Mario Galaxy.

Ridiculous names have always been a part of console ownership, and Nintendo bears some responsibility for that. Wii was and is a rather silly name. But we’re still talking about the console today, and mostly for the right reasons. Sometimes, silly names work.
