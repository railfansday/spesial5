Lufthansa Management Threatens To Massively Shrink Airline, Lay Off Employees

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Lufthansa is the “flagship” airline of Lufthansa Group, yet somehow, is the least profitable airline in the whole organization. 2026 was supposed to be an exciting turnaround year for Lufthansa, as it’s even the carrier’s 100th anniversary.

Unfortunately things haven’t turned out so great. Earlier this year, the carrier dealt with massive labor issues, and back-to-back-to-back strikes. Just a few months ago, the company’s senior management claimed it wanted to turn over a new leaf with labor relations, and stop fighting with employees, so that it could find long term solutions.

How is that working out? Well, Lufthansa pilots are just days from possibly being able to strike again, no agreement has been reached for long term peace with employees, and management is threatening to massively shrink its mainline operation. In other words, it’s just another day at Lufthansa!

Lufthansa and its pilots have been in negotiations for quite some time over the future of the company. As you’d expect, broadly speaking, negotiations revolve around pay and future flying opportunities. As you’d expect at any airline, employees want as much flying as possible, and as big of a fleet as possible, since that’s what maximizes their compensation.

The issue with Lufthansa Group management is that the company’s solution to everything is to simply create new, low cost subsidiaries. And if Lufthansa Group management doesn’t like the terms with its subsidiaries, then it just shuts down the subsidiaries and creates new ones.

So that brings us to the current issue. Essentially, Lufthansa’s problem is that the airline doesn’t make money on short haul flights, given high costs and intense competition. So we’ve seen the carrier increasingly outsourcing this flying to Lufthansa City Airlines, a subsidiary with lower operating costs, operating the same kinds of planes as you’ll see in the mainline fleet.

Lufthansa is asking its pilots to accept wage freezes, and is promising a certain level of career development opportunities. Pilots aren’t happy with what’s being proposed, so what’s Lufthansa’s solution?

Management is reportedly proposing a new plan to the supervisory board, which would see the core Lufthansa brand massively reduce its regional flying, and largely outsource this to lower cost operations, like Lufthansa City Airlines. The idea would be to make Lufthansa primarily a long haul airline, and then have much of the short haul flying be operated by a subsidiary.

Lufthansa’s mainline fleet currently consists of 260+ planes, with another 120+ planes on order. One proposal would see the mainline fleet reduced to 100 long haul aircraft and 100 short haul aircraft, with other planes being assigned to Lufthansa City Airlines.

One radical proposal could even see Lufthansa exclusively have a long haul fleet and ditch short haul flying, though it’s noted that this isn’t the most likely scenario, and it could take several years for something like that to be implemented.

Keep in mind that going back to 2017, Lufthansa had guaranteed pilots a minimum fleet size of 325 aircraft, but it terminated that agreement at the end of 2021. So it’s really hard for employees to trust any promises by management, when they can just be terminated.

There’s no denying that labor relations can be incredibly challenging, and that the economics of intra-Europe flying are also tough, when you have competitors like Ryanair, though Lufthansa doesn’t face nearly as much competition on that front as other carriers. Then again, Frankfurt (FRA) and Munich (MUC) aren’t exactly massively leisure destinations either.

Most airline employees want their company to succeed, and just want to be compensated fairly. Admittedly economics don’t always align. But what I find incredibly toxic is the approach taken by Lufthansa management. Let’s compare it to a marriage (which I think is a fair way to view the relationship between management and employees, since they’re “stuck” with one another).

Lufthansa’s approach is to constantly threaten employees by saying that “if you don’t do what we want, we’ll just get rid of you.” That’s simply not a good faith way to approach things. Why would Lufthansa employees trust management, when they’ve repeatedly torn up contracts, and shown employees they simply don’t care? It would be like threatening to leave your spouse every time you have an issue, rather than trying to find a solution.

Nothing against Lufthansa Group CEO Carsten Spohr personally, but I don’t understand how the board thinks he’s the best person for the job. Where’s the accountability for the endless management mistakes that have horribly impacted Lufthansa’s bottom line? Lufthansa’s employees are paying the price for the lack of success that comes as a result of all of Lufthansa’s errors, from the botched Allegris rollout, to Lufthansa’s very bad luck with new aircraft orders.

And before anyone says “oh, but the unions,” contrast what has happened at Lufthansa Group to what has happened at Air France-KLM, under the leadership of Ben Smith. Smith has now been CEO at Air France for over eight years, and there hasn’t been a single strike under his leadership. Under the previous Air France-KLM CEO, there were endless strikes, and the situation got so bad that senior executives were being chased up a fence and having their clothes ripped off.

This shows that good labor relations absolutely can happen, balancing the needs of employees and the company. But it requires showing employees that you actually care about the company and their future. What was one of Smith’s first orders of business when he took the job at Air France-KLM? He eliminated Joon, the low cost carrier at the airline that was supposed to basically be a labor arbitrage opportunity. That was a way to show employees how much he respected them, and that he wanted a fair agreement, rather than one that would undermine them.

Meanwhile Spohr’s solution to literally everything is to just threaten to start new subsidiaries or shut down existing subsidiaries. The irony is that Spohr started his career as a pilot, so you’d think he’d have respect for employees, having been on the other side. But clearly once he became senior management, his love for aviation turned into disdain for employees.

A few months ago, Lufthansa management claimed it wanted to turn over a new leaf when it comes to labor relations. That didn’t last long, because now the airline is back to its “do what we say or we’ll create a new subsidiary” approach.

I don’t have a strong opinion on the exact negotiations as such. Obviously it’s a tough time for aviation, and intra-Europe flying is challenging. But if you ask me, the toxicity that has been created by Lufthansa management due to the lack of good faith negotiations just can’t be undone.

If your only solution to everything is “oh, we’ll create a new subsidiary,” and if that doesn’t work, maybe it’s time for a new strategy. The fact that American CEO Robert Isom and Lufthansa Group CEO Carsten Spohr still have their jobs are two of the industry’s greatest mysteries.
