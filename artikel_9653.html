I Judged Dozens of AI Product Entries. Here's What the Best Ones Did Differently

The AI entries that scored highest weren’t the ones with the most impressive models. They were the ones that made it easy to believe them.

Last cycle, I judged dozens of AI product entries for the BIG Awards for Business, run by the Business Intelligence Group. I scored them one at a time against the same criteria and kept notes on what separated the entries that were easy to score well from the ones that left me guessing.

I’ve spent the last five years in project management and process improvement across manufacturing, healthcare technology and consulting, and lately my focus has been AI products. Judging turned out to be one of the most concentrated product lessons I’ve had. Each entry gets a limited, focused read, which is roughly the attention your pitch gets anywhere: a demo day, a steering committee, a customer call. Here is what scoring taught me about pitching AI products. It’s drawn from patterns across many entries, and the details of any single one stay where they belong.

The strongest entries opened with a problem stated precisely enough to be tested: who was losing what, how often and at what cost. The model showed up later, as the means. The weakest entries opened with the technology and worked backward to a problem, and the difference showed within the first paragraph.

This is an old product habit that AI makes people forget. When the technology is new and exciting, teams lead with it. But a judge, like a customer, is still buying a solved problem.

[A specific team, in a specific workflow] was losing [a measurable thing] at [a rate], and that cost them [a consequence].

Here’s what that looks like (a composite, not a real entry):

Claims processors at mid-size insurers were re-keying about 30% of incoming documents by hand, which added roughly two days to every payout and drove most customer complaints.

If a pitch can’t fill in that sentence, the demo that follows has nothing to stick to.

Entries split cleanly into two groups: those that claimed and those that showed. “We improve efficiency by 40%” immediately raises the question: measured how, over what period, against what baseline? Entries that walked through how they measured scored far better than entries that just stated a number, even when the stated number was bigger.

Weak: “Our AI makes support teams 40% faster.”

Strong: “Average ticket handling time dropped from 12 minutes to 7, measured over six weeks on about 2,000 tickets and compared with the same team’s previous quarter.”

The second claim is smaller, but you can believe it.

My process-improvement background probably biased me here, but I don’t think unfairly. In quality work, a claim without a measurement system behind it isn’t a claim; it’s a hope. For AI, that means a baseline, a sample, a clear definition of an error, and a before-and-after you can stand behind. If an entry told me how they knew the model was working, I trusted the rest of their numbers. If it didn’t, I trusted none of them.

The most counterintuitive pattern was that entries that volunteered their weaknesses scored better than entries that claimed to have none.

An illustrative example of what that sounds like:

The model misreads handwritten forms about 1 time in 20. Anything below 85% confidence goes to a human reviewer, which catches most of those errors before they reach a customer.

That one line answers the question a judge is already forming. An entry that claims the model is always right does the opposite: it sends the reader hunting for the catch.

AI products make mistakes. Judges, buyers and executives all know this. What marks a mature team is not a product that’s never wrong, but knowing where it’s weak and having a plan for it. This is also a stakeholder-management lesson: people don’t lose trust in products that fail predictably and get caught; they lose trust in teams that seem surprised.

Entries that showed a real person’s workday gave me something I could picture being used. Entries that showcased the model’s capabilities read like a science project. Often the product underneath was equally capable; only the framing differed.

In practice, that means showing the user opening the tool, doing their real task and reaching the outcome, with the AI working inside that flow. Only then show what the AI did underneath. The order matters because it answers the adoption question before anyone asks it, and adoption is where AI products fail in the real world, more often than on model accuracy.

Every judge quietly asks the same question: do the economics work? AI has real running costs (compute, model usage, human review time), and entries that had worked through them could answer in a sentence. Entries that hadn’t either skipped the topic or got caught out by it.

The memorable entries had a one-line answer ready. An illustrative version:

Each user costs us about $18 a month to serve, replaces roughly six hours of manual work, and we price at $49, which leaves a margin competitors can’t easily undercut.

If you’re pitching an AI product anywhere, to judges, investors or your own steering committee, prepare that line. It’s the question nobody forgets to ask.

I’d like to say I spotted these patterns from the first entry. I didn’t.

In my first few entries, I caught myself reacting to the biggest numbers before I had checked where they came from. It took a handful of entries that explained their measurement properly before I noticed how many others hadn’t, and I started asking the same question of every entry: how do you know?

The harder part was recognizing the same habits in my own work. Looking back at proposals and status updates I’ve written as a project manager, I’ve led with the solution I was excited about and explained the problem only when someone asked. I’ve also shared improvement figures without spelling out the baseline behind them. Nobody called it out at the time, but reading dozens of entries made it obvious how that comes across to the person on the other side of the table.

Judging didn’t teach me anything I hadn’t been told before. It showed me what those mistakes look like when you’re the one trying to decide whether to believe them.

Stepping out of the judge’s seat, these habits carried straight into my own work: state the problem before the solution, attach every claim to a measurement, volunteer the failure modes and rehearse the one-line cost answer. None of them are specific to AI, and that’s the deeper lesson. The technology changed what we’re building; the discipline of pitching it well hasn’t changed at all.

Next cycle, I hope to see fewer entries asking me to be impressed, and more that simply make it easy to believe them.
