The core rule of local inference is simple.

You cannot talk about local AI without talking about quantization.

It is the single technique that turns a 1.6 terabyte download into something that fits on a desk.

But first - what the heck is quantization anyway?

Every model is a giant collection of numbers called weights. At training time each weight is usually stored in 16-bit or 32-bit floating point.

Quantization stores those same weights with fewer bits. Fewer bits per weight means less memory, less data to move per token, and therefore faster generation on bandwidth-bound hardware like Apple Silicon.

Push the bit count too low and the model starts to forget facts, lose track of long instructions, and make sloppy tool calls.

Add roughly 10 to 30 percent on top for the KV cache, runtime buffers and the operating system.

A 70-billion-parameter model at 4.5 bits per weight needs about 39 GB for weights alone.

The key difference from older 4-bit schemes is quantization-aware training.

When a lab trains the model to live in 4 bits from the start, the quality loss largely disappears.

GGUF is the file format of llama.cpp, and by extension Ollama, LM Studio and Jan. Its quant names look cryptic, but they follow a pattern.

The "K" means super-blocks with nested scales, and the S, M and L suffixes mean small, medium and large. The M and L variants keep sensitive tensors such as attention and output layers at higher precision.

When you see "i1-GGUF" in a repository name, such as the mradermacher releases, that means imatrix-calibrated files.

Unsloth's Dynamic 2.0 quants (names like UD-Q4_K_XL or UD-IQ1_S) do not give every layer the same precision.

They keep critical layers at higher bit widths and crush the bulk of the MoE experts harder.

That is how Unsloth lists a 1-bit Mistral Large 4 at about 233 GB and a 1-bit Kimi K3 at about 594 GB, as reported in an M5 Ultra hosting guide.

Unsloth itself claims around 91 percent top-1 agreement for its smallest Mistral Large 4 quant, which is a vendor claim, so test it on your own prompts.

Apple's MLX framework has its own group-wise quantization at 3, 4, 6 and 8 bits, published under the mlx-community organisation on Hugging Face.

On Apple Silicon, MLX is often noticeably faster than llama.cpp for the same model.

One measured comparison on an M5 Ultra found DeepSeek V4 Flash at about 40 tokens per second under llama.cpp and about 53 tokens per second under MLX, according to an M5 Ultra benchmark.

That is roughly a third more speed from a software switch.

The KV cache stores the attention keys and values for every token in your context.

At a million tokens of context it can rival the weights in size.

Both llama.cpp and MLX can store the KV cache in 8-bit or 4-bit.

An 8-bit KV cache is nearly free in quality and halves context memory, while 4-bit is more aggressive and worth testing on long-document tasks first.

DeepSeek says V4.1 Flash cuts its KV cache to about a quarter of V4 Flash's, and hybrid linear-attention designs like Qwen's Gated DeltaNet and Kimi's Delta Attention shrink it further.

Dense models below 40B: stay at Q5_K_M or higher.

Mid-size MoE models: Q4_K_M or MLX 4-bit.

Trillion-parameter MoE models: whatever fits, because a 2-bit giant often beats an 8-bit dwarf.

They come straight out of the file sizes of the models that matter in late 2026.

Kimi K3.1 Pro is a 2.8-trillion-parameter model that downloads at about 1.56 TB in its native MXFP4 format, as confirmed in a Kimi K3.1 Pro weights report.

Mistral Large 4 is about 1,060 GB in official FP8, and Muse Spark Open needs about 1.63 TB in full BF16.

A single 512 GB Mac Studio cannot hold any of these at high precision.

Two machines (1,024 GB) hold some of them at 4-bit.

Four machines (2,048 GB) hold every one of them at its native or near-native precision, with room left over.

That headroom is not waste. It goes to three jobs:

Four is also the natural size for a Thunderbolt 5 mesh.

Each Mac Studio can cable directly to the other three, so every node is one hop from every other node, and there is no switch to buy or fail.

Apple added RDMA over Thunderbolt 5 in macOS 26.2, and Jeff Geerling's cluster test showed exo with RDMA speeding up as nodes were added while llama.cpp over TCP slowed down.

On Qwen3 235B, exo went from 19.5 tokens per second on one node to 31.9 on four, according to AppleInsider's write-up.

With the 2026 M5 Ultra, each node brings 1.2 TB/s of memory bandwidth.

Four of them reach about 4.8 TB/s in aggregate, a figure exo Labs highlights in its four Mac cluster announcement.

Why 512 GB nodes rather than eight 256 GB nodes?

TechRepublic notes that two 256 GB Mac Studios cost about $700 less than one 512 GB machine, so smaller nodes are cheaper per gigabyte.

But eight nodes cannot form a direct mesh, every extra hop adds synchronisation latency, and every extra box adds cables, power bricks and failure points.

The 2026 mid-size models are astonishingly good.

Qwen3.8-27B posts 61.7 on SWE-bench Pro, and its 4-bit build is only about 17 GB, according to a Qwen3.8-27B analysis.

Because the floor is about running the best small models well, not just running them.

One hundred and twenty-eight is the sweet spot.

Is 100 agents realistic on four Mac Studios?

Research agents spend much of their time waiting on tools - Lean proof checks, Python experiments, quantum simulators, literature search - so far fewer than 100 are generating tokens at any instant.

The fast, small-active workers (8B to 16B active for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, 6B for Qwen3.8-Flash-Next) carry the volume, and the heavy reasoners are called only when a decision needs them.

Agent swarms at this scale are already real.

AI sovereignty means you, your company or your country can keep using AI on your own terms, no matter what a vendor, a regulator abroad or a supply shock decides.

Owning the weights and the hardware is the most direct way to get there.

It is also the only way to ensure your company is running irrespective of internet outages, natural disasters, international calamities or war.

I am not a lawyer, and you should get legal advice for your jurisdiction.

But I strongly believe that every serious organisation should hold at least one capable model it controls completely.

A local model on a badly managed machine is just a new place for data to leak.

The supply chain is the most overlooked risk.

While researching this article I saw dozens of unofficial re-uploads of flagship models from accounts that have nothing to do with the original labs.

Ransomware is moving laterally through your network.

Your analyst pastes an obfuscated PowerShell loader into an AI assistant and asks what it does.

Safety-tuned models are trained to refuse requests that look like hacking.

The trouble is that defending against an attack looks exactly like studying one.

Those are reasonable choices for public chatbots.

But in an incident room, a false refusal can cost you minutes, and minutes are what separate a contained incident from a company-wide outage.

At the same time, capability is clearly there.

GLM-5.3 scores 84.5 on CyberGym, a vulnerability-discovery benchmark, on its model card.

The knowledge exists inside these models; alignment decides whether your defenders can reach it.

An uncensored local model does all of this without arguing, without sending your malware samples or breach details to a third party, and without a rate limit in the middle of the night.

During a breach, you may not be able to trust your own network, your SaaS accounts or your internet connection.

A model running on an isolated Mac Studio or a disconnected workstation keeps working when everything else is suspect.

And sending live incident data to an external API during a breach is a disclosure decision your legal team should make deliberately - not something an analyst should do at 3 a.m. by accident.

Uncensored models are powerful tools, so treat them like any other powerful tool.

Isolate the machine on a dedicated analysis network with no route to production.

Log every prompt and response for later review.

Write an acceptable-use policy that covers defensive work, authorised testing scope and prohibited uses.

Keep an aligned model for everyone else. The uncensored model belongs in the security toolkit, not on every desk.

Vet the abliterated build using the provenance checklist above.

The community already publishes hundreds of abliterated models, browsable in catalogues such as Featherless's abliterated catalogue, including some aimed at security work, but you must test any such model yourself.

Good news - the defenders now have frontier-grade help that never says no.

Bad news - so do the attackers, which is exactly why your team cannot afford to work with one hand tied behind its back.
