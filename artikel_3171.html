‘The Gilded Age’ Co-Showrunner Sonja Warfield Reveals How Audra McDonald “Planted The Seed” For “Core Conflict” Of Peggy’s Wedding In Episode 403

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

SPOILER ALERT: This post contains spoilers for The Gilded Age Season 4 Episode 3, titled “Ain’t That Good News.”

Wedding bells are also ringing for the new Dr. (Jordan Donica) and Mrs. William Kirkland (Denée Benton) in The Gilded Age.

The newlywed couple, who met early on in Season 3 of Julian Fellowes’ period drama series, walked down the aisle and officially sealed the deal on their marriage as of Season 4’s third episode, though not without some road bumps along the way.

Elizabeth Kirkland (Phylicia Rashad) takes particular issue with Peggy’s parents gifting their daughter the broomstick they jumped over for their wedding celebration, because Dorothy Scott (Audra McDonald) could not legally marry Arthur Scott (John Douglas Thompson) due to his enslavement. “We were on set filming Season 3, and Audra McDonald turned to me and she said, ‘When they get married next season, I want them to jump over the broom because Elizabeth will hate that,’” co-showrunner Sonja Warfield told Deadline. “And so Audra planted that seed, and so that was going to be their core conflict.”

William’s mother, who hasn’t exactly warmed up to Peggy since the journalist and doctor started their courtship, doesn’t want her son and future daughter-in-law to incorporate the tradition because of its roots in slavery, marking commitment for those in bondage whose unions would not be legally recognized.

“We found out historically that it’s kind of inaccurate. Yes, enslaved people were doing that, but in that time post emancipation, they really weren’t, because then they could legally be married and so the tradition came back really in the 20th century,” Warfield continued. “We got to take some poetic license, artistic license and keep it because we were trying some other things, but really it needed to be that because that represents the division between the families,” Warfield said. “Elizabeth does not want to remember anything from enslavement period, while Peggy wants to pay homage to her family, to her dad and her parents. It is completely like diametrically opposed. So that made for great dramatic conflict.”

Another wrinkle in the plans for the nuptials comes in the form of the Fisk Jubilee singers, led by Dorothy and Peggy’s cousin Ava (Adrienne Warren), who attends Peggy’s pre-wedding tea and announces that she’s brought the choir to sing hymns at the ceremony in Newport, Rhode Island. Elizabeth is not fond of this idea either, nor, at first, is her husband (Brian Stokes Mitchell), who ultimately presides over Peggy and William’s ceremony. Much like in the show, this addition was not originally in the plans for Season 4’s third episode.

“I believe Sally [Richardson Whitfield] talked to Dr. [Erica] Dunbar because Sally wanted to add more to the wedding, which I was all for. At first, we had to be budget conscious, but then we got some more budget, so then we were able to add the Fisk Jubilee singers,” Warfield said. “I thought it need[ed] to be important to Peggy in a meaningful way, and so then we get to introduce cousin Ava as one of the singers.”

Warfield described the broom as something “tangible” to be angry about regarding Peggy instead of just saying she doesn’t like her soon-to-be daughter-in-law, giving actress Phylicia Rashad plenty of opportunities to “annihilate just with a look.” Meanwhile, the defensiveness Dorothy feels for her daughter is just one of many emotions on the big day.

“Dorothy is more than proud; there’s probably everything in it. There’s pride. There’s relief. There’s joy. There’s a little sadness because she has to let go. She’s got to let go in a way that she hasn’t had to. [Dorothy] probably had more time to cling to [Peggy] because of Peggy’s setting out on her own in the beginning, and so all of that is swirled into one,” McDonald told Deadline. “In some ways she starts to feel a little more nostalgic for and a little more romance toward Arthur again. They feel more like a unit now, given how separated they all were in Seasons 1 and 2. So, to see that bond come back together, she’s feeling that, and Peggy is finally getting the joy that she deserves. She’s emerging into this powerful young modern Black woman. That’s a fulfilling thing to see. Her ancestors are definitely smiling down on her.”

“She’s wearing the crown that her ancestors bought and paid for. So that’s a lot of beautiful things come to fruition. In some ways, it may be why Dorothy is so stringent and so unwilling to back down about the broom,” McDonald continued. “That’s honoring the ancestors. Like ‘Yes, you did buy and pay for this crown, and we are honoring you and saying we see you, we thank you, and we give gratitude to and we remember you. That’s why I think Dorothy’s willing to do a knockdown, hat-pulling, hair-pulling fight to get to this moment. That’s why it’s such a beautiful moment when it happens.”

In an interview paired with McDonald, co-star Cynthia Nixon called Peggy’s wedding “the wedding to end all weddings” and she traced the other ceremonies that have speckled The Gilded Age so far.

“Every one is different. We’ve had Gladys’s wedding with the bride sobbing down the aisle. We’ve had Enid’s and Oscar’s wedding, which is sort of hilarious. We’ve had Ada and Luke’s wedding, which is late in life and full of anguish because you think Agnes isn’t going to come, and it’s more small and modest,” she said. “The wedding of Peggy and Dr. Kirkland, is this fruition of this tremendous love and the most opulent and beautiful, but also heartfelt and full of tradition and singing and the beautiful attire and the stained glass, and it just it is the apex of all the weddings we’ve ever had on the show.”

McDonald shared one last special association with production behind such a major moment in the show.

“The days that we were shooting it, because it took a long time to shoot, it was the night that the Knicks won. So, we literally had to stop shooting for a while to watch those final moments,” the Tony winner said. “Everybody was stationed at phones and Sally Richardson was like, ‘We can’t keep going. Let’s turn on some TVs.’ And we’re all watching, and then screaming our heads off for I don’t know how late it was. And then we went back to finishing the wedding. We did end up watching the sunrise that night, but it was a worthy night. It was kind of magical that it happened on the night of the of the wedding.”
