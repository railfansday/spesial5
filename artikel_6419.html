AI is revealing a hidden world of animal behavior

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Artificial intelligence is revealing subtle patterns in animal behavior that scientists have long overlooked, opening new possibilities for research.

For decades, scientists studying mice in laboratory mazes have largely tracked their movements as single dots on video screens.

That dot shows how far the animal went and how fast, but it can’t tell a mouse frozen in fear from one that has fallen asleep or stopped to groom.

Artificial intelligence now lets researchers track the whole body instead, from the curve of the spine to the position of each limb.

A new review of the field finds that this shift has changed what behavior research can measure.

But one of the major obstacles left is something no camera can fix: scientists still don’t share clear definitions for many of the behaviors they study.

The review comes from Ke Chen, Yaning Han, and colleagues. They work at the Shenzhen Institutes of Advanced Technology of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, two Chinese universities, and a Guangzhou biotech company.

The team’s research summarizes published research rather than reporting new experiments. Most examples involve laboratory mice and rats.

In classic behavior experiments, researchers place an animal in a simple, controlled space and record a handful of numbers.

A Y-shaped maze can test memory by measuring how often a mouse chooses the correct path to food.

In an open-field test, a mouse explores an empty arena. Researchers count how often it ventures into the exposed middle and treat that as a sign of anxiety.

Other setups, such as the Barnes maze, work on the same principle.

Tests like these are easy to standardize. But researchers using them see little of what an animal does on its own. Carrying a mouse from its home cage into an unfamiliar apparatus may also change how it behaves.

The authors described a bigger problem in the gap between these tests and brain research.

Today’s probes can record from thousands of brain cells at once, yet those recordings are often compared against a single number, such as speed or time spent in one zone.

The newer approach uses video, and AI software learns to mark chosen points on the animal’s body in every frame, turning a running mouse into a moving stick figure. Researchers call this pose estimation.

One of the most widely used programs, an open-source tool called DeepLabCut, needs only a modest number of hand-labeled images to get started.

By one estimate cited in a 2021 review, carefully labeling every frame of a one-hour video takes 22 person-hours.

Some labs also add infrared cameras or ultrasonic microphones that pick up calls too high for human ears.

A second layer of software sorts the tracked movements into behaviors. Some programs learn from clips people have already labeled, while others group similar movements without labels.

The label-free approach has turned up movements lasting less than a second that human observers had missed. Still, a pattern a computer can separate isn’t automatically a meaningful behavior, the authors wrote.

Some of the tools the review describes came from teams that included its own authors.

Chen and Kang Huang work for Guangdong BayOne Biotech, which holds patents related to a behavior-analysis technology called BehaviorAtlas. The other three authors reported no financial stake in the company.

Tracking one animal at a time is the simpler job. Once several mice share a space, the software also has to keep track of which mouse is which, and lab mice make that hard.

Many lab mice come from inbred strains or the same litter, so they look nearly identical. Curling up changes a mouse’s outline, and gnawing can leave patches of missing fur.

In the review’s comparison of tracking tools, a program called SLEAP achieved roughly 90% of its maximum tracking accuracy using only about 200 labeled video frames.

Even so, it mixed up the identities of mice about 23 times in every 100,000 video frames, compared with fewer than once for fruit flies.

A single swap matters more than it sounds. If it goes uncorrected, every later measurement for those animals can end up credited to the wrong mouse.

Implanted radio tags tell individuals apart reliably, and farms use the same kind of tag to monitor how much feed and water livestock take in. But implanting a tag is invasive, and the discomfort may change how the animal behaves.

The authors asked how long and how sharply a mouse has to swerve before its path counts as a left or right turn, rather than walking forward.

Without an agreed answer, researchers may rely on their own judgment.

Broader labels are harder. Anxiety-like behavior can mean less time on the open arms of a raised, cross-shaped maze, or a mouse that hugs the walls of an open arena.

Both carry the same name, but the authors wrote that the two tests may capture different things.

Social behavior adds another layer. With two mice, it’s unclear at what distance one counts as approaching the other.

When a third mouse enters the picture, two animals might gang up on one, or one might intervene in a conflict between the others.

In mouse models of Parkinson’s disease, a few points on one forelimb may be enough to spot a tremor.

Researchers running a more detailed study might need to separate normal grasping, shaky grasping and tremor at rest.

Because labs tailor their setups this way, their data often can’t be combined or compared. A shared data format called Neurodata Without Borders is an important first step, the authors wrote, but labs have taken it up unevenly.

The review’s more speculative ideas start with what the authors call “behavioral nucleotides.” In DNA, chemical units called nucleotides line up in sequences that carry genetic information.

The authors suggested describing each sampled moment of movement as a list of numbers and treating it as one candidate unit of behavior.

Strung together by rules they call behavioral syntax, those units would build into larger patterns tied to functions such as foraging or avoiding danger.

If that works, researchers could borrow tools built for analyzing DNA, such as lining up two sequences to compare them.

Researchers might also make short-range forecasts, stated as probabilities, of what an animal will do next.

The idea will only be useful if the units can be defined the same way every time, and chopping smooth movement into steps may lose some gradual changes.

The team also proposed “bilingual robotic animals,” robots that send precise, repeatable signals to live animals. Changing one signal at a time could slowly build a rough dictionary of animal body language.

But a robot can look, move and smell different from a real rat, so an animal may react to its strangeness rather than to the signal.

It’s still an open question whether any of this will help human patients.

Better tracking may make preclinical research, the lab work that comes before testing in people, more precise and easier to repeat.

That promise is limited by species differences, the authors wrote, and by the loose fit between lab tests and human psychiatric conditions.

Computer-generated animals could ease some of the need for live experiments. Virtual mice built from anatomical 3D models can produce training video with the labels already attached.

The National Institutes of Health has also emphasized research methods that reduce animal use.

In one study, software trained on synthetic video estimated poses about as well as software trained on hand-labeled footage.

Other studies measured different things, which makes their numbers hard to compare.

The authors recommended creating shared collections of real-world test videos so researchers can compare how well AI tools perform, whether they were trained on real footage, computer-generated videos or both.

The full study was published in the journal The Innovation Reviews.
