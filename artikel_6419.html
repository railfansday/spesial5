Glue used in batteries makes recycling hard, so engineers designed one that comes off in water

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Battery recycling is harder because of the fluorine glue in each cell. A citric acid version dissolves in water and lasted longer in tests.

The glue inside a lithium-ion battery is a tiny share of its weight, yet it shapes how the battery gets built and how hard it is to take it apart for recycling.

In most batteries that glue contains fluorine, and removing it at the end of a battery’s life takes high heat or corrosive chemicals.

However, the new glue is made from two common materials and citric acid, the acid in citrus fruit, and it comes off in plain water.

Test batteries built with it held their charge as well as standard ones, and in extended runs they held it longer. If that holds up at factory scale, the glue would take a toxic solvent out of battery making and simplify recycling.

Engineers at the University of California San Diego developed the glue in Zheng Chen’s Sustainable Materials and Energy Lab. They worked with John Muldoon of Toyota Research Institute of North America and colleagues at Arizona State University.

Every test ran in small lab cells, from watch-battery size up to flat pouches about 1.3 inches (3.3 centimeters) across. Those are far smaller than the packs in electric cars.

Inside a battery, the glue binds a powder that stores charge to a thin metal foil. The standard glue is a fluorine-containing plastic, and it has to be dissolved in a toxic solvent before it can be mixed with that powder.

Once the mix is spread on the foil, big ovens dry the solvent off, and it has to be captured and cleaned for reuse.

Standard-glue electrodes dried off under vacuum at 248°F (120°C) for 12 hours. New-glue electrodes dried in an ordinary oven at 140°F (60°C) for four hours.

“Those extra steps significantly increase the cost and energy use – as well as the environmental impact – of battery manufacturing,” said Jiao Lin, a postdoctoral researcher in Chen’s lab and the study’s first author.

The fluorine glue is also very stable. That helps a battery last, but it means the glue leaves residue that only high heat or harsh chemicals will strip from the material before recycling.

“But that same stability also makes batteries much harder to recycle,” Chen said.

Water-based glues already exist. Carboxymethyl cellulose, a plant-derived thickener found in foods and paper products, holds together the graphite side of some commercial batteries.

On their own, though, glues like this tend to crack as they dry, which leaves the coating brittle. Chen’s team mixed that thickener with polyacrylic acid, a plastic that spreads evenly and bends.

Then they added citric acid, which bonds to both and links them into a single network. Lithium hydroxide kept the mix only mildly acidic, so it wouldn’t corrode the aluminum foil.

The glue made up just 2 percent of each electrode’s weight. Electrodes made with either ingredient alone, or with the two mixed without citric acid, cracked or pulled away from the foil as they dried. The citric acid version dried smooth.

When the team stuck tape on the coating and peeled it off, almost nothing came away. Standard-glue electrodes lost material after only a few peels. The new coating also stayed on when the foil was twisted.

Most test cells used lithium iron phosphate, a battery material that standard recycling methods earn little or no money from. At the start, cells with either glue stored nearly the same charge per gram.

After 1,000 rounds of charging and draining, cells with the new glue had 75.7 percent of their starting capacity. Cells with the standard glue lost capacity faster, especially after about 400 rounds.

So the water-soluble glue didn’t shorten the battery’s working life. In these tests, it added some.

A pouch cell built like a commercial battery, with graphite on the opposite side, kept 76.4 percent of its capacity after 1,600 rounds.

A stack of several layers did a little worse than a single layer, 74.5 percent to 83.5 percent after 700 rounds, which the authors traced to uneven conditions inside the stack.

The glue also worked with other electrode materials, including a nickel-rich one and silicon.

After 100 rounds of use, the team took cells apart. For the standard-glue electrodes, the team used the toxic solvent to lift the coating off.

New-glue electrodes soaked in purified water until the glue dissolved and the coating came cleanly off the foil.

Recovered material from the standard-glue cells still carried fluorine on its surface, left over from the liquid inside the battery. Material from the new-glue cells showed no detectable fluorine.

The team then restored the recovered lithium iron phosphate in a way that keeps it intact instead of breaking it down to raw metals.

Cells built from it kept more of their capacity, and handled fast charging better, than cells built from material recycled out of standard-glue batteries.

Graphite that was recovered in the same way worked again in new cells through 420 rounds.

The team also estimated the environmental cost of a battery cell. By that estimate, building one with the new glue would produce 6.9 percent less greenhouse gas and use 9.3 percent less energy than building one with the fluorine glue.

Recycling it would use 10 percent less energy and release 7.2 percent less greenhouse gas.

The authors call those figures a comparison of scenarios, not measurements from a working plant. The numbers would shift with how much solvent a factory recovers and how it captures fluorine, though the authors say the new glue would still come out ahead.

The new glue doesn’t remove all the fluorine from a battery. The salt in the battery’s liquid contains fluorine too, so the team calculates that the new glue cuts a cell’s total fluorine by about 30 percent.

Lin and Chen are inventors on a patent application for the glue, and Chen’s team plans to commercialize it with industry partners.

Whether the glue works as well when made by the ton and built into full-size batteries is an open question. Scaling up production is the team’s next step, along with adjusting the recipe for other battery types.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Communications.
