Why are human brains so big? Hundreds of genetic switches may explain

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Scientists identified hundreds of genetic switches that may help explain why human brains grow much larger than those of other great apes.

We share many of our genes with chimpanzees and gorillas, but our brains grow much larger. A new study suggests that part of the difference may come from how brain cells use those shared genes.

Researchers in Dresden grew brain tissue from stem cells belonging to all three species.

They found nearly 600 short stretches of DNA that were more accessible in human cells, many linked to genes that help cells take shape.

Mareike Albert led the work at the Center for Regenerative Therapies Dresden, part of TUD Dresden University of Technology. Her group studies gene activity as the brain develops.

“Changes in brain size actually arise during development. This is when the majority of neurons are generated. But developing brain tissue of non-human great apes is not available for research, and for good ethical reasons,” said Albert.

To study those early stages, the team grew tiny balls of tissue called cerebral organoids.

With the right chemical signals, stem cells arrange themselves into tissue that copies some features of early brain development.

Study co-author Madeline Lancaster first grew cerebral organoids in 2013. She now runs a lab at the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology.

The researchers used one gorilla stem cell line, one chimpanzee line and three human lines. They examined the organoids after eight weeks.

They also studied donated human fetal cortex, the brain’s outer region, from weeks 13 and 14 of pregnancy. This let them check how closely the organoids resembled developing human tissue.

The team focused on stem cells called basal radial glia. These cells divide deep within developing brain tissue and extend long arms toward its surface.

Researchers stained the outside of each organoid with a fluorescent dye, allowing them to identify cells whose long extensions reached the surface.

A cell sorter then separated the tissue into four groups.

These included the branching stem cells, related stem cells anchored near the fluid-filled center, cells that produce neurons, and neurons themselves.

The researchers checked which parts of the cells’ DNA were open and accessible to the machinery that reads it. Across the three species, they identified 225,395 stretches they could compare.

Of those, 599 were more accessible in human branching stem cells than in their chimpanzee and gorilla counterparts. All 599 were also accessible in the donated human tissue.

Theresa Schütze, who led the experimental work, explained how these stretches can affect genes.

“These genetic switches are short DNA sequences that control how active a gene is. You can think of them as sort of dimmers that turn genes up or down,” said Schütze.

“We found nearly 600 switches that are more active in human neural stem cells than in those of chimpanzees and gorillas,” Schütze said.

About 63% overlapped DNA regions already known to regulate gene activity. A few belonged to regions that changed unusually quickly after the human lineage split from other great apes.

A switch can control a gene some distance away along the DNA.

Using maps of how DNA folds inside cells, the researchers linked the 599 stretches to 589 possible target genes.

These genes help build the forebrain, attach cells to their neighbors, guide cells into position and grow nerve cell branches.

The team made its ape sequencing data available through the public Gene Expression Omnibus archive.

The researchers then examined whether these differences in DNA accessibility were reflected in gene activity.

They found 939 genes whose activity differed between human and chimpanzee branching stem cells, including 465 that were more active in humans.

Many of those more active genes help shape cells. To explore that pattern, the team gave each cell type a score based on the activity of its shape-related genes.

In chimpanzees, scores rose from the simpler, anchored stem cells to neurons. Human branching stem cells scored almost as highly as neurons.

That stood out because neurons develop elaborate shapes over months as they grow toward their targets. The human stem cells were using many of the same genes while they were still dividing.

The team then checked where else those 465 genes were active. In developing mouse brains, most worked in neurons.

In human and macaque fetal cortex, they worked in both neurons and stem cells. These were old, widely shared genes, rather than genes found only in humans.

The researchers think human branching stem cells have adopted part of the program that shapes neurons, a process called co-option.

This may help explain their more elaborate shapes and adds to earlier research focused on human-specific genes.

“It is not necessarily different genes that make a human brain special. As the nearly identical genomes suggest, we use largely the same toolkit as apes, just with different settings,” Albert said.

The team chose two of the 589 candidate genes for further tests. One, FAM107A, makes a protein that binds to actin, part of the internal scaffolding that supports a cell’s shape.

The other, CNGA3, helps form an ion channel best known for its role in the eye’s color-sensing cells. Chimpanzee organoids showed very little activity from this gene.

Its protein was plentiful in human radial glia, scarce in gorilla cells and undetectable in chimpanzee cells.

To test the switches themselves, researchers placed human and chimpanzee versions in mouse cells beside a reporter gene. A glow showed when a switch was working.

The human FAM107A switch produced a glow; the chimpanzee version did not. Both versions of the CNGA3 switch worked, but the human version worked more strongly.

The human CNGA3 switch carries a single DNA letter change that creates landing spots for 11 control proteins.

When researchers removed either gene from human organoids, fewer stem cells developed complex shapes.

Cells missing FAM107A also grew fewer branches from their cell bodies, and the organoids contained fewer young neurons.

Adding extra human FAM107A to chimpanzee organoids also made cells simpler. That suggests the amount matters: increasing this gene’s activity does not necessarily produce more branches.

Organoids capture only part of brain development.

They grow a thin version of the outer stem cell layer that develops during the second trimester, where much of the human cortex’s growth takes place.

The links between individual switches and genes also need more work.

When researchers reduced the activity of the FAM107A switch, rather than removing the gene, the gene’s activity barely changed.

Other switches may help keep it working. The result shows why changing one switch can have a different effect from removing an entire gene.

Testing two genes offers clues about how these cells develop, but it does not explain the contribution of all 589 candidates. Nor does the study measure how many extra neurons these differences might add to a human cortex.

The researchers suggest that better growth conditions, including a protein called LIF, might help organoids develop a fuller outer stem cell layer.

That would give scientists a better way to study a stage of development that matters greatly for brain size.

The findings suggest that the evolution of the human brain may depend not only on which genes we inherited, but also on how those genes are used during development.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Communications.
