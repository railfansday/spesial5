Bitcoin’s next macro test will come from a familiar combination: what Americans are spending and how Federal Reserve officials are thinking about interest rates. The week of November 16 brings October retail sales and the minutes of the Fed’s latest policy meeting, both relevant to a market already digesting persistent inflation.

The dates are confirmed on the agencies’ calendars. Their significance is more conditional. A release moves markets through the information it adds relative to expectations, and through how investors change their outlook for borrowing costs and risk.

The Census Bureau’s schedule places October’s advance retail and food-services sales report on November 17 at 8:30 a.m. Eastern Time, or 13:30 GMT. That gives investors a new reading on a substantial part of household spending.

Strong sales can support the view that demand remains resilient. Depending on the inflation backdrop, that may also reduce the pressure on policymakers to loosen financial conditions. Weaker sales can suggest cooling demand, but a sufficiently poor reading could bring concerns about growth rather than an uncomplicated boost to risk assets.

The survey’s definitions add a useful caution: dollar values are expressed in current prices. A rise in spending is not automatically an equivalent increase in the quantity of goods purchased. The categories and revisions matter alongside the headline monthly change.

The Federal Reserve calendar schedules minutes from the October 27–28 meeting for November 18 at 2 p.m. ET, or 19:00 GMT. They should provide more detail about the discussion behind that decision.

The October 28 statement held the federal-funds target range at 3.75%–4% by a 10–2 vote. Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack preferred a quarter-point increase. That disagreement makes the breadth of concerns about inflation and the conditions for another move especially relevant.

Minutes are a retrospective account, however. They describe deliberations before the latest inflation release and before this week’s retail figures. A forceful passage may explain October’s debate without settling what officials will decide after receiving newer information.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported a 0.4% monthly rise in consumer prices for October, with prices excluding food and energy up 0.3%. NewsBTC’s coverage of the October inflation report and Bitcoin’s rate outlook explains why that release renewed attention on monetary policy.

There is also a data limitation worth preserving. BLS could not publish most October year-on-year changes because the corresponding 2025 survey data were unavailable following a lapse in government funding. The monthly numbers remain usable, but an invented annual comparison would give a false sense of precision.

For the coming releases, the more useful question is whether the new evidence reinforces persistent demand and inflation, or points toward a meaningful slowdown. Those interpretations can produce different reactions even when a headline number looks superficially positive.

Changes in expected interest rates affect the appeal of cash and bonds, financing costs and the willingness to hold volatile assets. Those channels offer a framework for interpreting Bitcoin’s response; they are not a mechanical rule that every stronger economic report must send its price lower.

NewsBTC’s account of the recent weekend sell-off also highlights conditions within crypto itself. Liquidity and positioning can amplify a macro surprise or overwhelm the first reaction.

The clearest reading will compare the actual data, Treasury-market response and sustained trading after the initial move. This week’s calendar creates two well-defined information points. It does not predetermine the next Bitcoin trend.
