Is Berkshire Hathaway Stock a Buy if the Fed Keeps Raising Rates?

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Berkshire Hathaway has both opportunities and challenges in a high-rate environment.

By Ryan Vanzo – Nov 25, 2026 at 4:30PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Berkshire Hathaway ›

Interest rates are on the rise. At least that's the expectation for most experts. "The debate has shifted from whether the Fed hikes in December to how aggressively it moves," warns economist Anthony Smith.

Rising rates have various impacts on different stocks. Some companies experience a net benefit. Others are clear losers in a rising rate environment. Most, however, have a mix of benefits and drawbacks.

Berkshire Hathaway (BRKA +0.54%)(BRKB +0.37%) is one of the market's most fascinating cases. The conglomerate owns dozens of businesses across dozens of sectors. Breaking down exactly how the company will fare in a rising rate environment can be difficult.

But there are three major moving pieces to pay attention to right now. Let’s look at each.

Warren Buffett is no longer the CEO of Berkshire. But in his final years, he amassed a sizable cash position for the company. In 2016, Berkshire had less than $100 billion in cash. When Buffett stepped aside last year, he left the firm with a record cash position of more than $350 billion.

Investors were curious to see what would happen to all this capital once Greg Abel took over as CEO. So far, Abel has accumulated even more cash. Berkshire's cash pile reached $377.4 billion last quarter.

For every 0.25% increase in interest rates, Berkshire will earn nearly $1 billion in additional interest income annually. So while elevated cash may or may not be in the best interests of shareholders long term, higher interest rates should directly result in higher interest income.

Berkshire is heavily diversified. But at its core sits a portfolio of insurance operations that generate investable cash. When rates rise, insurance operators typically generate more cash to invest -- a positive result for Berkshire shareholders.

"Modestly rising interest rates are generally positive for the insurance industry," concludes the U.S. Office of Financial Research. "When rates rise at a reasonable pace, portfolio yields also rise. With these new, higher-yielding corporate and other bond purchases, insurers' investment earnings also increase."

Again, Berkshire is so diversified that the benefits its insurance operations experience from higher interest rates may be offset elsewhere. But investors can generally expect better interest income from the company's insurance businesses -- similar to what Berkshire will experience for its cash position.

Most of Berkshire's value isn't tied to insurance profits or interest on its cash position. Warren Buffett's primary wealth-creation strategy has been his investing acumen.

Over more than 60 years, Buffett generated compound annual returns of nearly 20%. That's almost double what the S&P 500 index averaged over the same period. That resulted in a cumulative gain of more than 6,000,000% compared to only around 46,000% for the market overall.

Buffett's investing relies on two key levers: public investments and private transactions.

Research on how interest rates impact public stock prices and private market valuations is mixed. Companies that now need to borrow at higher interest rates -- including unprofitable businesses, capital-intensive enterprises, and companies with distant cash flows -- may see their valuations suffer. Certain financial, energy, and tech businesses, meanwhile, may benefit for a variety of reasons.

In short, it's hard to predict how markets in general will respond to changing interest rates. Berkshire may see its stock portfolio perform well or poorly. Similarly, while the company may find more attractive deals -- either on public or private markets -- it may have to shoulder higher debt costs to finance the purchase if cash isn't used. If cash is used, Berkshire forgoes the potential for greater interest income.

All of this means that for a diversified investment conglomerate like Berkshire, investors shouldn't put too much emphasis on the current rate environment. Undoubtedly, Berkshire will be impacted by changing rates. But it has the potential to benefit or suffer in nearly any rate environment. The key to Berkshire's success will remain its long-term investment performance, not near-term shifts in interest rates.
