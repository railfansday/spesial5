How buyer objections shape listing strategy in 2027

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

A veteran listing agent argues the strongest listing strategy starts by identifying likely buyer objections before marketing begins. He outlines pre-listing steps including permits, condition, insurance costs, and active competition to set pricing expectations and reduce renegotiation.  AI Summary

The longer I work in real estate, the more convinced I become that some of the most important work in selling a home happens before the first photograph is taken. I’ve represented sellers through very different housing markets, and one thing hasn’t changed: Even the best marketing campaign can’t overcome every concern a buyer may have.

Photography, descriptions and online exposure matter. But they’re only part of the equation.

Before listing a home, I try to answer a simple question: What might cause a qualified buyer to hesitate, negotiate aggressively or walk away? I’d rather uncover those concerns before losing a good buyer.

Earlier this year, I listed a four-bedroom pool home in Oakland Park, Florida, that needed considerable updating. Frankly, I was concerned about how buyers would react to its condition. It wasn’t turnkey, but it had real strengths: an attractive location and a walkable neighborhood.

We priced the property at $660,000. Within four days, we received five offers, and the home sold above asking price. The transaction was reported by Paul Owers for Homes.com in May 2026.

That experience reinforced something I’ve observed repeatedly: Buyers will accept imperfections when they believe the overall value makes sense. Not every home needs extensive improvements before selling. Conversely, a desirable neighborhood doesn’t excuse unrealistic pricing. Our job is to recognize a property’s strengths and shortcomings, then help sellers decide how to proceed.

I wonder whether our focus on marketing sometimes crowds out essential preparation. We can produce photographs, videos and descriptions quickly. But if we haven’t researched the property, understood the competition or identified potential insurance concerns, we may be beautifully marketing a problem we should have investigated first.

Today’s buyers face affordability challenges, costly financing and, in many markets, more competing homes. Here in South Florida, insurance costs, property taxes, maintenance and condition can significantly influence purchasing decisions. A house may appear affordable, but the monthly cost of ownership can tell a different story.

As listing agents, we need to look at properties through the buyer’s eyes. Could an older roof create insurance or financing problems? Are improvements properly permitted? What might surface during an inspection? What else could buyers purchase for the same money?

We won’t always have every answer. But we should know which questions to ask.

I like to start with the property itself.

That doesn’t necessarily mean expensive improvements are needed. But it calls for an honest conversation about price, condition and expectations. I don’t pretend we can anticipate every problem. Yet experience helps us recognize what might give buyers second thoughts.

One of the harder parts of representing sellers is telling homeowners something they’d rather not hear. Perhaps their anticipated price isn’t supported by the market. Maybe an expensive renovation won’t produce the return they expect. Those conversations aren’t easy.

For many sellers, their home represents years of memories, hard work and financial investment. We owe them empathy, but also honesty. I’d rather have that conversation before listing than watch a seller become discouraged after weeks of limited activity and repeated price reductions.

Technology can help with this preparation. I use artificial intelligence to research properties, study competing listings and organize information. But technology doesn’t replace walking through a property, asking difficult questions or recognizing when something deserves further investigation.

Sometimes you have to put the computer aside, visit the property and bring in someone who knows more about a particular problem than you do.

There’s wisdom in acknowledging another professional’s expertise. As we approach 2027, I believe listing agents should spend less time emphasizing how widely they can advertise a home and more time explaining how they’ve prepared it for the market.

Good marketing gets buyers through the door. What we’ve done before they arrive can make a real difference in what happens next. Even a well-prepared listing can encounter unexpected problems. There are no guarantees in this business. But sellers deserve to know we’ve done our homework, considered the competition and anticipated difficult questions.

For me, it comes down to this: The best listing strategy doesn’t begin by asking how we can make a home look more appealing. It begins by understanding what might keep a buyer from saying yes.

Gary Lanham is a broker associate with LoKation Real Estate and an AI-certified Realtor specializing in residential listings in the Fort Lauderdale area. With 22 years of real estate experience, he founded Lanham & Associates, acquired by Coldwell Banker Realty in 2014. A member of the Master Brokers Forum, Lanham writes and teaches about listing strategy, real estate technology and professional representation.
