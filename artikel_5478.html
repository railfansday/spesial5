10 milestones in Nvidia's growth, from the NV1 to $3 trillion in market value

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Free daily briefing on global business news.

This Nvidia timeline traces 10 milestones in the chipmaker's rise, from its 1993 founding and GeForce graphics cards to CUDA, AI data centers and trillion-dollar market value

Nvidia $NVDA started in April 1993 in Santa Clara, California, as a company building 3D graphics for PCs. The founders were Jensen Huang, Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem. On June 18, 2024, it overtook Microsoft $MSFT as the most valuable listed company. Company rankings by market value change daily. That makes the June 2024 milestone a dated one, not a permanent title. The two dates sit 31 years apart.

The link between those two dates is the graphics chip itself. Hardware designed to draw 3D scenes for games turned out to suit other kinds of computing work. Nvidia's CUDA software, introduced in 2006, let developers use its GPUs for general-purpose programs. In 2012, AlexNet showed that the same chips could speed up deep learning. Those two steps pointed the company toward AI systems for data centers. After OpenAI released ChatGPT in November 2022, demand for Nvidia's data center chips rose.

Not every step moved the company forward. Its first product, released in 1995, did not succeed commercially. The company changed its approach to graphics afterward. A planned acquisition was abandoned after regulators raised objections. Its purchase of Mellanox, by contrast, added data center networking technology. Those setbacks and deals sit in the timeline alongside product launches and market value figures.

This Nvidia timeline covers 10 milestones, from the 1993 founding to the June 2024 day the chipmaker passed Microsoft in market value. It is a selection, not a complete history of the company. The entries run in chronological order and are not ranked. The early entries cover graphics chips for PCs and a game console. The middle entries cover the software and research that opened GPUs to new uses. The later entries cover data center hardware, acquisitions, export restrictions and the market value steps that followed. Market value claims in the timeline are tied to the dates they occurred. Each entry stands on its own, with the date, product, deal or figure that defined it.

Nvidia $NVDA was founded in April 1993 by three co-founders: Jensen Huang, Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem. The company was based in Santa Clara, California.

Its early work centered on 3D graphics for personal computers.

The NV1 was Nvidia $NVDA's first product, and it reached the market in 1995. It did not succeed commercially.

The chip arrived about two years after the company's founding. It was Nvidia's first attempt to sell graphics hardware to buyers.

That attempt did not catch on. The commercial failure led Nvidia to change its approach to graphics.

Nvidia $NVDA went public on Nasdaq $NDAQ on January 22, 1999, at $12 a share. In October of that year, it launched the GeForce 256 and marketed the chip as the first GPU, or graphics processing unit.

The listing came less than six years after the company's April 1993 founding. Once its shares began trading on Nasdaq, Nvidia became a publicly traded company.

The product milestone followed about nine months after the listing. In October 1999, Nvidia released the GeForce 256, a graphics chip for PCs.

Nvidia promoted the GeForce 256 as the first GPU. That label was the company's own marketing claim. Nvidia used the term to present the chip as a new kind of graphics product.

The two 1999 events differed in kind. The IPO changed how Nvidia was owned and financed. The GeForce 256 gave the company a new chip and a name for the category it claimed that chip started.

Nvidia $NVDA supplied the graphics processor for Microsoft $MSFT's original Xbox, the game console that launched in 2001. The deal added a console business to the chipmaker's existing sales of graphics cards for PCs.

With the Xbox, Nvidia's graphics hardware reached players through two kinds of products. One was a graphics card installed in a personal computer. The other was a home console sold under Microsoft's brand.

Nvidia $NVDA announced CUDA in November 2006. The platform lets developers run general-purpose programs on Nvidia GPUs, not only graphics code.

The announcement came alongside the GeForce 8800 GTX, a graphics card built on Nvidia's G80 chip. Nvidia introduced the software platform and the new card at the same time.

A GPU had been designed to render images for games. CUDA gave programmers a way to write software that put an Nvidia GPU to work on tasks other than drawing a picture on a screen. That widened the range of jobs the company's hardware could take on.

The GeForce 8800 GTX was a graphics card for PC gaming. Paired with CUDA, the launch addressed two audiences. One was buyers looking for a graphics card. The other was developers who wanted to use a GPU for their own programs.

CUDA's role grew in the years after 2006. It later became the software base for much of AI computing.

That later role traces back to the November 2006 announcement. It marked the point when Nvidia began offering its GPUs for general-purpose computing as well as for graphics.

AlexNet, a neural network built by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton, won the 2012 ImageNet competition. The three researchers trained it on two Nvidia $NVDA GTX 580 GPUs.

AlexNet was a deep learning model. Its ImageNet result showed that GPUs could speed up that kind of work.

The 2012 win linked Nvidia's graphics processors to deep learning research. The link came through the work of Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton, who chose a pair of the chipmaker's GPUs to train their network.

In April 2016, Nvidia $NVDA announced the Tesla $TSLA P100, a GPU built on its Pascal architecture. That same month, it introduced the DGX-1, an AI system. Together, the two launches marked the chipmaker's turn toward the data center.

The two products sat at different levels. The Tesla P100 was a single chip. The DGX-1 was a complete system built for AI work. With it, Nvidia offered a finished machine as well as the processors that go inside one.

In August 2016, Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang delivered a DGX-1 to OpenAI. The handover came four months after the system's introduction. It put one of the new machines in the hands of an organization working on artificial intelligence.

Pascal did not stay Nvidia's newest design for long. In 2017, the GPU maker followed it with Volta, an architecture that added tensor cores.

Nvidia $NVDA agreed in March 2019 to buy Mellanox for $6.9 billion. The purchase closed in April 2020 and gave the chipmaker data center networking technology.

About 13 months passed between the announcement and the close. The deal took a business built around graphics processors into the networking side of the data center.

A much larger bid followed. In September 2020, five months after the Mellanox purchase closed, Nvidia announced a $40 billion deal to buy Arm.

That agreement never closed. It stayed pending for about 17 months. In February 2022, the companies abandoned it after regulators raised objections.

The two deals ended differently. Mellanox became part of Nvidia, and its networking technology stayed with the chipmaker. The Arm bid ended without Nvidia acquiring the company.

Nvidia $NVDA announced the H100, a data center GPU built on its Hopper architecture, in March 2022. Demand for Nvidia's data center chips rose after OpenAI released ChatGPT in November 2022.

Hopper is the name of the architecture. H100 is the name of the data center GPU built on it. Nvidia announced the chip in March, before ChatGPT reached the public that November.

ChatGPT was an OpenAI product, not an Nvidia one. The demand that rose after its release was for Nvidia's data center chips. The H100 belongs to that category.

U.S. export rules also limited where those chips could be sold. In October 2022, the U.S. imposed restrictions on exports of advanced chips to China. The rules covered the A100 and the H100, two of Nvidia's data center GPUs.

The H100 therefore came under U.S. export restrictions for China in the same year Nvidia announced it.

Nvidia followed Hopper with another architecture. In March 2024, the company announced Blackwell.

This stretch of Nvidia history runs from the H100 announcement in March 2022 to the Blackwell announcement in March 2024. The October 2022 export restrictions and the November 2022 release of ChatGPT fall between those two dates.

Nvidia $NVDA's market value first passed $1 trillion in May 2023. It crossed $2 trillion in February 2024 and $3 trillion in June 2024. On June 18, 2024, the chipmaker overtook Microsoft $MSFT as the most valuable listed company.

Market value, also called market capitalization, is a company's share price multiplied by its number of shares outstanding. It moves with the stock on every trading day. Each threshold records how investors priced Nvidia on a given date. It is not a fixed measure of the business.

Market value is also separate from a company's sales or profit. A higher market value does not mean a company sold more or earned more than another. It reflects the price investors paid for its shares, multiplied across all of them. When Nvidia passed Microsoft, the comparison was between two stock market valuations on the same day.

The word "listed" matters in that comparison. The ranking covers companies whose shares trade on public stock exchanges. A privately held company has no daily share price. It cannot be measured on the same daily basis.

The two trillion-dollar steps took different lengths of time. Nvidia needed about nine months to go from $1 trillion in May 2023 to $2 trillion in February 2024. The climb from $2 trillion to $3 trillion took about four months and ended in June 2024. Each step added the same $1 trillion in market value. The second step took less than half as long as the first.

The Microsoft milestone came in the same month as the $3 trillion crossing. The lead was a position held on a particular trading day. A company that ranks first on one day can fall behind on the next as share prices move.

The dates mark first crossings only. They show when Nvidia's value reached each level, not whether it stayed above that level afterward. Round numbers such as $1 trillion are reference points for share-price valuation. They are not figures the company reported in its earnings.
