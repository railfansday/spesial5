A company has 500,000 support tickets sitting in Snowflake. Years of troubleshooting notes, product defects, workarounds, resolutions, and occasional messages that just say "fixed after restart." Very helpful, thanks.

Someone wants an AI assistant that can search those tickets and suggest resolutions. The architecture looks straightforward. Take the documents, generate embeddings, store vectors, retrieve similar records, and let an LLM write the answer.

A few SQL queries later, you have a demo.

Then someone asks which embedding model you picked.

And there it is. We've designed an intelligent search system using the same decision-making process people use to buy televisions. Snowflake Cortex offers eight built-in text embedding models through AI_EMBED. Some produce 768-dimensional vectors, others 1,024. Some are English-focused. Others support multiple languages. Context windows vary considerably.

I've spent about eight years working with Snowflake, and one lesson from enterprise data engineering keeps showing up: the technically impressive option isn't necessarily the right one for your workload. Embedding models are no exception.

According to Snowflake's AI_EMBED documentation, the built-in text embedding lineup includes:

Snowflake also supports image embeddings through voyage-multimodal-3, but that's a different use case. We're keeping this investigation focused on text.

Eight models sound like eight chances to get it right. Or eight chances to explain to your architect why you chose one based on a name.

A 1,024-dimensional embedding contains more numbers than a 768-dimensional embedding. More numbers must mean more meaning. Unfortunately, vector search doesn't work like upgrading a laptop from 16 GB to 32 GB of RAM.

An embedding model learns a representation of language. Its dimensions are coordinates in that learned space, not a score for intelligence.

A larger representation might capture useful distinctions. It might also deliver no measurable improvement on your particular documents.

Imagine three fictional incident records:

"Why did settlement reconciliation fail after the column changed?"

A useful model should recognize that A and B are relevant, while C is mostly unrelated.

But here's the interesting part. Suppose a 768-dimensional model puts B first, while a 1,024-dimensional model puts A first.

You can't answer that from the dimension count. Maybe both are valid. Maybe one describes the exact failure while the other concerns an older version of the pipeline. Maybe neither contains the accepted resolution.

The model's job isn't to produce a beautiful vector.

For an English-only knowledge base containing relatively short documents, I'd start with a compact model such as Arctic Embed M v1.5 as a baseline.

Then I'd test whether a larger model improves retrieval.

Some tickets are in English. Others are in Spanish, French, Japanese, or a combination of languages.

"El proceso de conciliación falló después de modificar una columna."

"Why did reconciliation fail after the column update?"

Ideally, your retrieval system can connect these without requiring every ticket to be translated first. This is where models such as multilingual-e5-large, Arctic Embed L v2.0, and Voyage Multilingual 2 become candidates.

But multilingual support comes with an uncomfortable question. Does supporting multiple languages mean equally good retrieval in every language?

A model can support multilingual embeddings and still perform differently across languages, technical domains, and query styles.

An error such as ERR_RECON_042 should remain ERR_RECON_042, regardless of the surrounding language.

A model that understands a Spanish sentence but loses the exact error identifier hasn't solved the whole problem. I'd also separate two questions: can the model retrieve semantically similar information across languages, and does the retrieved information actually answer the user's question?

They're related, but they're not identical. Multilingual support earns a model a place in the evaluation. It doesn't automatically win the contract.

Consider a 40-page incident report. It contains the original failure, several failed fixes, the successful workaround, and a postmortem written two weeks later.

A model with a short context window can't embed the whole document in one pass. A model supporting longer inputs gives you more room.

Arctic Embed L v2.0 8K supports longer inputs than its standard version. Voyage Multilingual 2 supports up to 32,000 tokens. At first glance, longer sounds better. Fewer chunks. Less processing logic. Everybody goes home early.

That 40-page report isn't necessarily about one thing.

The first section describes ingestion. The second discusses schema evolution. The third explains a permissions issue. The fourth says the original root-cause theory was wrong. Put everything into one vector and you're asking the model to summarize several distinct concepts into a single location in embedding space.

Now imagine a user searching specifically for the permissions issue.

Will the document-level vector represent that section strongly enough? Maybe. But I wouldn't bet the retrieval architecture on it.

Chunking exists for a reason. Sometimes a focused 300-token chunk retrieves better than an entire document represented by one embedding. Sometimes larger context helps preserve the explanation that a smaller chunk would lose.

The answer depends on the structure of the content.

I'd compare different chunking strategies before spending time arguing about context-window sizes. The bigger window may be useful. It isn't permission to stop thinking about document structure.

Here's my favorite trap in semantic search.

The system retrieves a document discussing reconciliation errors.

The correct document contains ERR_RECON_042, but a different document talks about similar failures in more natural language. The embedding model prefers the descriptive document. Now the LLM receives the wrong evidence and writes a perfectly polished answer.

We've successfully automated being confidently incorrect.

This is why I wouldn't build enterprise retrieval around vector similarity alone. Exact identifiers, error codes, schema names, version numbers, and product IDs often deserve lexical matching. Semantic search helps when people describe the same problem differently. Keyword search helps when the exact characters matter. Both can be useful.

Cortex Search supports hybrid retrieval, making it worth evaluating for applications that need semantic and lexical matching together.

The right question isn't always "Which embedding model should I use?"

Sometimes it's "Why am I asking an embedding model to solve a problem that exact matching handles better?" A screwdriver isn't a bad tool because it struggles with nails.

Let's say you've embedded two million documents using snowflake-arctic-embed-m-v1.5.

Six months later, a new model looks promising. Someone changes the model name used to generate query embeddings.

The stored documents were embedded with the old model. Your queries now use a different model. Even if two models return vectors with identical dimensions, their embedding spaces aren't necessarily compatible. And if one returns 768 dimensions while the other returns 1,024, the mismatch is even more obvious.

So switching models isn't just a configuration change.

You may need to regenerate embeddings for the corpus, maintain parallel indexes, measure retrieval changes, and coordinate the cutover.

This may sound like too much metadata for a vector. Until someone asks why search behavior changed last Tuesday. Then suddenly everyone loves metadata.

If I were evaluating embedding models for a real enterprise application, I wouldn't start with a leaderboard.

I'd start with a set of questions and known relevant documents. For an initial experiment, something like 100–200 representative queries could help expose failure patterns, although broader testing would be needed before making general performance claims.

I'd include straightforward questions, ambiguous queries, similar-but-wrong incidents, multilingual examples, and cases with no valid answer. Then I'd compare candidate models against the same data.

Did the correct evidence make the top five?

How high did the first relevant result appear?

Were the most relevant results ranked appropriately?

How quickly can the model process a search query?

What does it cost to embed the document collection?

How expensive is a future model migration?

What kinds of questions does the model get wrong?

I'd measure actual costs using Snowflake's current pricing and usage information rather than infer them from vector dimensions. And I'd pay special attention to the failures.

Suppose Model A retrieves the correct document for 94 out of 100 questions. Model B succeeds on 96.

A routine FAQ? Or an incident where retrieving an outdated fix could cause another production outage?

Raw accuracy alone doesn't tell you which failure matters more.

That's the problem with declaring winners before understanding the workload.

There's a practical issue worth mentioning. You can have unused Snowflake trial credits and still be blocked from calling certain Cortex embedding functions.

For example, a trial account may return an error indicating that an internal embedding function isn't available. That isn't proof that the SQL syntax is wrong or the requested model doesn't exist.

Account eligibility, regional availability, permissions, and service access all matter. Before writing a benchmark, verify that the account can actually invoke the candidate models. It's a small step that can save an afternoon of debugging the wrong thing.

Snowflake Cortex makes generating embeddings easy. Choosing the right model is still your responsibility.

Eight models don't mean eight levels of intelligence. A larger vector doesn't guarantee a better match. A longer context window doesn't eliminate chunking. And multilingual support doesn't guarantee that every language or technical domain performs equally well.

A model can retrieve something semantically similar and still miss the evidence your application needs. That's the part worth testing.

If I had to make the decision tomorrow, I'd start with a simple baseline, build a representative evaluation set, and bring in more capable models only when they solve a measured problem.

Because the embedding model isn't supposed to win a beauty contest.

It's supposed to find the right document. And when it doesn't, I'd much rather know that before the LLM writes three paragraphs explaining the wrong answer.
