Ballet training may shape how the brain responds to other people’s emotions

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Ballet dancers show different brain responses to emotional faces, without identifying emotions more accurately than people without training.

Ballet dancers spend years training their bodies to move with precision.

A new study suggests that the same training is also linked to how the brain responds when a dancer sees fear or happiness on someone’s face.

When ballet dancers looked at photos of fearful, happy, and neutral faces, their brain activity differed from that of people with no dance training.

The differences appeared in brain areas that process vision and in areas that process touch.

Beatriz Calvo-Merino worked with lead author Vasiliki Meletaki and Bettina Forster at City St George’s, University of London’s Centre for Cognition and Neuroscience.

“The impact of dance expertise goes beyond movement and taps into social perception mechanisms, such as face processing and emotions,” said Calvo-Merino.

The team compared 26 professional or trainee ballet dancers with 26 people of similar age and sex who had no dance experience.

The dancers had been dancing for about 17 years on average.

Both groups judged the emotions about equally well, but their brain signals differed in timing and pattern.

Earlier brain-scanning studies, including work by Calvo-Merino, found increased activity in the brains of expert dancers when they watched familiar dance movements.

Nearly all of that research looked at how dancers perceive movement and bodies, the authors wrote.

The team used faces to test whether dancers’ brains also differ in other, everyday kinds of perception.

The test is based on an idea about emotion.

According to this idea, people perceive someone else’s emotion partly through their own body’s sense of that emotion, and not through vision alone.

Researchers describe this as an embodied view of emotion.

Earlier experiments found that the brain area receiving touch and movement signals from the body is involved when people look at emotional faces. That area is the somatosensory cortex.

Professional dancers develop a refined awareness of their own bodies through long, precise training, the authors wrote.

Dance training might also refine the body’s part in perceiving emotion, the team reasoned. If so, dancers’ brains should respond differently to emotional faces as well as to dance.

Each volunteer wore a cap fitted with 64 electrodes. The electrodes recorded the brain’s electrical activity, a method called EEG.

The faces came from a photo collection called the Karolinska Directed Emotional Faces. The team used 60 black-and-white photos, half of them male faces.

Each face stayed on screen for 600 milliseconds, a little over half a second.

In one task, a question about emotion sometimes followed a face, such as “Is s/he fearful?” In the other task, used as a comparison, the question asked whether the face was male or female.

Half of the time, a blunt-tipped device gave a brief touch to the tip of the volunteer’s left index finger.

The touch came 105 milliseconds after a face appeared, and volunteers were told to ignore it.

The touch served as a probe. The team recorded the brain’s response to a face with a touch and to a face alone, then subtracted the second recording from the first.

The subtraction helped isolate the brain’s response to touch. Differences between facial expressions could then indicate that emotional processing was influencing that response.

In the dancers, the touch response differed between fearful and neutral faces at the earliest stage the team analyzed.

That stage was about 143 to 173 milliseconds after the face appeared, less than a fifth of a second.

The response to happy faces didn’t differ from the response to neutral faces at that stage. The early effect was specific to fearful expressions, the authors wrote.

In non-dancers, the touch response didn’t differ by emotion at that first stage.

Their touch responses did differ by emotion at later stages, but at a narrower set of recording sites than in dancers.

“What is particularly interesting is that dance expertise appears to influence how the somatosensory cortex responds to emotions expressed by others,” Forster said.

“This suggests that our own bodily experiences can shape how the brain processes other people’s emotions, providing further evidence for the embodied nature of emotion perception.”

Electrodes over the visual areas at the back of the head recorded a second set of differences.

One difference appeared between about 150 and 200 milliseconds after a face came on screen. In dancers, visual responses to neutral faces differed from responses to both fearful and happy faces.

In non-dancers, visual responses at that stage were different for each of the three expressions. Those differences were also stronger on the right side of the brain, a pattern that earlier face studies have typically reported.

In dancers, the differences were spread across both sides. The authors called the two patterns distinct styles of telling emotions apart.

Both groups processed fearful faces in a distinct way. The authors linked that result to the priority the brain is known to give fear.

The authors also wrote, as an anecdote, that fear is an important tool in dance training. Dancers learn to feel comfortable performing in front of an audience, for example.

The study found no clear advantage in dancers’ ability to identify facial emotions.

During the recordings, dancers answered 94% of the emotion questions correctly. Non-dancers answered 95% of them correctly, a gap too small to rule out chance.

Dancers did score higher on a questionnaire about awareness of signals from inside the body. Their average score was 3.69, compared with 2.71 for non-dancers.

Body awareness may be part of how dancers process other people’s emotions, the authors suggested. They proposed exploring that relationship further.

Each group had 26 people, a size chosen to match earlier studies. The authors sugg that future studies should calculate in advance how many participants they need.

Scalp recordings also couldn’t show precisely where in the brain the differing touch responses arose.

The study compared the two groups at a single point in time, so it can’t show how the differences developed.

The next question is whether the brain differences are accompanied by differences in behavior.

Future studies could add behavioral tests of emotion recognition, the authors wrote.

New studies could also explore how the touch area’s response to emotion changes across stages of dance training, from advanced trainees to professional performers.

The full study was published in the journal Cerebral Cortex.
