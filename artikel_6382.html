A man using a metal detector in Germany found the biggest stash of Roman silver coins from Emperor Hadrian ever discovered

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

A metal detectorist discovered 934 Roman silver coins near Cologne, Germany, uncovering the country's largest known hoard from Hadrian's reign.

Oliver Riedl had already dug up 15 Roman silver coins in a field near Wesseling, south of Cologne, when his metal detector kept signaling. He immediately notified the regional heritage office.

The excavation that followed recovered 934 Roman silver denarii, about 6.8 pounds (3.1 kilograms) of silver. It is the largest coin hoard from the reign of Emperor Hadrian ever found in Germany.

The LVR Office for Archaeological Heritage Management in the Rhineland (LVR-ABR) announced the find on September 22, 2026.

Riedl, an amateur metal detectorist, was searching the field with official permission.

“For me it was clear that something bigger was lying in the ground here,” Riedl said. “So I notified the office immediately.”

The LVR-ABR‘s branch in Nideggen responded immediately and excavated the site. To study the find more closely, archaeologists lifted it in a block of soil and X-rayed it at the LVR-LandesMuseum Bonn‘s restoration workshop.

Dr. Erich Claßen is the state archaeologist and head of the LVR-ABR.

“This makes it the largest coin hoard from this period in Germany, and worldwide it is the fifth largest,” Claßen explained.

The latest coin in the hoard was minted in AD 124 or 125. The archaeologists think the coins were buried in the second quarter of the second century, under Hadrian, who ruled from AD 117 to 138.

Most of the coins date to the Flavian emperors, who ruled from AD 69 to 96. Among them is a denarius of Domitian, the last emperor of that dynasty.

The oldest coin was struck in 148 BC, during the Roman Republic. It was more than 270 years old when it went into the ground.

The fortune was probably assembled around AD 100 and buried on purpose decades later.

In that period, the Rhine formed the frontier of the Roman province of Lower Germany. Its defenses, the Lower German Limes, ran about 250 miles (400 kilometers) from south of Bonn to the North Sea, according to UNESCO.

From the late first century BC, that frontier protected the Roman Empire for more than 450 years. Its remains include legionary fortresses, forts, watchtowers, roads and towns.

Dr. Rahel Otte, a research associate at the LVR-ABR, studied the coins.

“At that time, an ordinary soldier in the legion earned 300 denarii a year,” Otte noted.

About half of that pay was withheld for food and equipment. To save 934 denarii, a legionary would have had to set aside all of his available money for about six years, Otte calculated.

Who amassed this considerable fortune remains unclear. The owner may have lived on one of the Roman country estates in the surrounding area.

“We can of course only speculate about the motives, but very probably someone wanted to keep this money safe. After all, there were no banks back then,” Claßen observed.

Why the silver stayed in the ground is also unknown. The owner may have died without passing on what they knew about the coins.

The soil block also held fragments of a buried clay pot and remains of straw.

“The coins were probably originally buried in a pot padded with straw,” Otte explained.

Years of farming had destroyed the pot and scattered the coins through the topsoil.

“But in our investigations we were able to find and recover all of the coins,” Otte stressed.

“Mr. Riedl really behaved in an exemplary way,” Claßen remarked. “Thanks to his care, we were able to recover the hoard properly and completely,” he added.

Under the monument protection law of North Rhine-Westphalia, the hoard now belongs to the state. Because Riedl acted lawfully, the state ministry responsible for heritage will give him a reward.

Metal detecting in the Rhineland requires a permit from the heritage authorities and the landowner’s consent, whatever a person is searching for.

Applicants first attend an information session run by the LVR-ABR and mark the areas they want to search on a map. Detectors are allowed only on arable fields, not on meadows or in forests.

Simon Matzerath chairs the West and South German Association for Archaeological Research. About 300 archaeologists from across Germany saw the hoard at the association’s annual conference in Bonn.

“The private use of metal detectors can be an incredible support for science if everyone follows the rules, but unfortunately we also often have to deal with so-called illegal digs,” Matzerath said.

The hoard will go on public display for the first time at the LVR-LandesMuseum Bonn.

The information was obtained from the LVR Office for Archaeological Heritage Management in the Rhineland.
