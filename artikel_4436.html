8 JRPGs That Came Back From the Dead

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Murillo Zerbinatto is a JRPG enthusiast, writer, and editor. He has played over 300 RPGs during his gaming career, with 90% of them being JRPGs.

It feels like we have been living through a game renaissance for quite some time. While development costs continue to inflate, particularly in the Western market, one way to keep the fanbase engaged with a developer and its IPs is to bet on smaller games, or even on ports, remasters, and remakes.

Another approach is to play on older fans' nostalgia, investing in older IPs while modernizing certain titles to attract newcomers at the same time. One genre that does this frequently is JRPGs, and as you can see in the following list, there are several JRPGs that pretty much came back from the dead, titles that had not shown their face in years and returned either as a remake or a brand new entry.

When Square Enix started with their HD-2D games, it was pure happiness. A rescue of the pixel art that the developer understands so well, but better than ever, paired with traditional gameplay. What I didn't expect, and I think nobody did, was that they would leverage this graphical engine to remake some older titles, and among these, Live A Live.

Live A Live was a JRPG released in 1994 for the Super Famicom exclusively in Japan, so imagine everyone's surprise when a modern remake was announced for 2022. Seriously, no one could have expected that. But there is a good reason, and aptly so, why Live A Live is timeless and was so well received by the modern audience 28 years after its original release.

Lunar was one of the JRPGs Game Arts released for the Sega CD, and it quickly earned a reputation as one of the finest JRPGs of the fourth generation. The first two mainline entries also quickly followed on PS1, reaching a much larger audience and becoming classic titles for the console. Portables followed, including Lunar Legend and the critically offensive Lunar Dragon Song.

In 2009, the series tried a comeback with Lunar: Silver Star Harmony, a remake of the first game for the PSP, and while critics loved it, it didn't give the franchise a second wind. Well, me, you, and our moms thought Lunar was over. But then, out of nowhere, GungHo announced Lunar Remastered Collection, a compilation of Lunar: Silver Star Story Complete and Lunar 2: Eternal Blue Complete, bringing the franchise back after 26 years without a sign of life.

Nihon Falcom is really focusing its efforts on bringing its entire lineup to modern platforms.

NISA is one of the oldest Japanese developers around, but the studio really found its footing after releasing the tactical La Pucelle: Tactics. It went on to mimic that gameplay style across several other franchises, mainly the well-known Disgaea. In 2004, it released Phantom Brave, another tactical JRPG following the same NISA blueprint, but with its own distinct identity and slightly different gameplay.

Phantom Brave was a solid game. I enjoyed it, but I really thought it was a one-and-done deal. I was pretty surprised when NISA announced Phantom Brave: The Lost Hero, not only a game set in the same universe, but a direct narrative sequel. Okay, luckily the game included a recap because releasing a sequel to a 20-year-old game was wild, but it is what it is.

I have mentioned Brigandine a few times here on the site, including in my review of Brigandine Abyss. I discovered this franchise serendipitously, and it took a long time to find other people who were also familiar with it. Truth be told, I only found them when I started frequenting the depths of Reddit. I had even forgotten about it, only to be reminded when I stumbled upon Brigandine: The Legend of Runersia.

Reviving an old IP or a cult classic happens every now and then, as you can see with other titles here. But Brigandine was not a cult hit, a classic, or even mildly famous. It was obscure among obscure titles, so why Happinet thought of bringing it back baffles me, but they did. Brigandine: The Legend of Forsena came out in 1998 and Runersia in 2020. The game apparently did well enough to warrant a new mainline entry, Brigandine Abyss, released just this year.

There are more than a few JRPGs that are still stuck on older systems and deserve a port to modern platforms.

I have always preferred fantasy games over sci-fi, but if there was one franchise where mechas stole my heart, or in this case, Wanzers, it was Front Mission. I played and replayed Front Mission 3 so many times and never grew tired of it, especially since the game featured branching narrative paths and a degree of RNG when learning skills, making every playthrough feel fresh. Then came the fourth mainline entry, Scars of War, Front Mission Evolved, and ugh, Left Alive, which come on, is not a Front Mission game, so let us dismiss it.

Well, in 2020, Forever Entertainment said it was working on a remake of an established Square Enix franchise that had multiple releases. My guess was either Parasite Eve or Front Mission, and since the former is tied up in licensing issues, yeah, it was Front Mission. The first remake launched in 2022, the second in 2023, and the third in 2025. I don't know if the franchise will continue, but it was great to revisit it through modern releases.

I know this entry is a bit far-fetched and even a tad insulting. After all, calling Valkyrie Elysium a mainline Valkyrie Profile entry is a stretch, but closing our eyes doesn't make it less true. After years without a new entry, Valkyrie Elysium was announced and left many fans excited, especially the nostalgic ones who adored the mysterious adventures of Lenneth back on the PS1.

What we got, however, wasn't what we wanted. It is not a bad game per se. It's just too middle-of-the-road and doesn't reflect what Valkyrie Profile represented, whether in the first game, Silmeria, or even Covenant of the Plume. Still, Valkyrie Elysium arrived in 2022, 13 years after the last mainline entry, so it fits the topic. I'm also not considering the mobile game Valkyrie Anatomia.

The producer is giving us hope, or at least some more coping issues.

Mana was always such a cozy, colorful, and friendly series. I loved playing Secret of Mana with my brother or transferring my character from Legend of Mana onto a memory card to bring to my friend's house. Good times. Even so, for one reason or another, it never achieved massive mainstream success. In 2007, Heroes of Mana came out for the Nintendo DS, followed in 2014 by Rise of Mana, a mobile-first game that received a Vita port. It was an oddity.

The franchise faded for a bit, but returned with the remake Adventures of Mana, followed by Secret of Mana and then Trials of Mana. The success of the remakes, especially Trials, signaled something to Square Enix, because it didn't take long for them to produce a brand-new mainline title in Visions of Mana. Personally, I loved Visions! It was a modernized version of everything I liked about Mana. I don't know why the game didn't achieve the success it deserved, but it may have been the last time we saw a mainline Mana entry.

You have probably heard someone say that Suikoden II is their favorite JRPG of all time. It was one of the most remarkable titles on the PS1 and built a deeply dedicated fanbase. Three more mainline entries later, the series began to fade into oblivion, still receiving a few spinoffs like Suikoden Tierkreis in 2009 and the Japan-only Gensō Suikoden: Tsumugareshi Hyakunen no Toki in 2012, which was its latest release. It was a strange era for both the series and Konami, which had stepped away from the video game industry for a while.

Then, completely out of nowhere, Konami announced Suikoden I & II HD Remaster: Gate Rune and Dunan Unification Wars during Tokyo Game Show 2022. It was a pleasant surprise, made even sweeter with the arrival of Suikoden Star Leap, which released in 2026. And okay, I know it is a mobile game, but those who played it (still locked in Japan) say it feels like a full-fledged Suikoden game, so who knows, maybe this will convince Konami to produce an offline version of Star Leap or a brand-new mainline entry altogether.
