The 1977 Mazda Cosmo Deserves Better

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

From the archive: It’s a lot more car than the sales figures would have you believe.

From the April 1977 issue of Car and Driver.

Peel the name tags off the Mazda Cosmo, and it could easily pass for an American car. The two-rotor Wankel is as smooth and vibra­tionless as any V-8, the car is easily as quiet inside as a typical intermediate sedan, and the Cosmo’s body even has the same sort of creaky flexibility that we’ve come to expect of Detroit cars. If we didn’t know better, we might think this was a new subcompact Buick or perhaps a miniature Camaro. Never would we confuse it with a Toyota or Datsun.

The Cosmo is something of a mystery car. It was designed with the United States in mind—a fast, smooth sports coupe for a country that swallowed Datsun Z-cars as fast as the ships could unload them at the ports. But for some reason, the Cosmo never struck the American fancy. Maybe it was the bad publicity heaped on the rotary engine; per­haps the dull colors and unfortunate combi­nation of mag wheels and white-wall tires turned off the sporty-car buyer. And we can’t entirely disregard the thought that a coupe with a Vega silhouette, Oldsmobile waterfall grille, and Plymouth Cricket taillights may sim­ply be the wrong thing to wave in American faces. But whatever the reason, Cosmos tend to languish in the showrooms.

That’s the situation in the U.S. In Japan, the Cosmo is last season’s smash hit, named Car of the Year by one of the country’s auto mag­azines and the next best thing in Tokyo to driving a foreign car. (The Japanese, like many Americans, tend to think that anything imported is probably better and certainly more fun than the made-at-home equivalent). The car looks great in its own neighborhood. It has slightly different—but not much small­er—bumpers and the mandatory mirrors perched out on the front fenders halfway to the headlights. But apart from those two de­tails, the Japanese-market Cosmo looks the same as the one we tend to ignore here.

We humbly submit, at this point, that the Cosmo deserves another look, if for no other reason than it features one commodity in de­cidedly short supply elsewhere—horsepower. This car is quick. The engine is wide awake above 2000 rpm, and it climbs to 7000 with­out a backward glance. Freeways abound with legions of 350 four-barrels that won’t match the Cosmo’s pace. And as amazing as it may seem, none of them can come close to the Mazda’s gas mileage. The 1977 model with the standard five-speed trans­mission has been weaned down to 32 mpg highway and 20 mpg city for a combined rating of 25 mpg in the EPA mileage sweep­stakes. That is excellent economy for a car of this performance level.

While the Cosmo is surprisingly economical, it is by no means austere. The interior offers about the same amount of useful space for four occupants as a Camaro, and the trunk, because it stows its spare more ef­ficiently than the Chevrolet, is at least as good. Yet overall, the Mazda Cosmo is about the same size as a Corvette, actually slightly smaller.

The quality of the interior is also on par with that of Detroit models. The dash is molded vinyl with a minimum of seams, and shows the driver a full complement of instruments set in a field of imitation wood. Velour cloth covers the seats and door panels. There is even a trapezoid opera window to delight the double-knit crowd. And if that doesn’t knock them dead at the Donut Center, show them the wire-spoked steering wheel.

There is just one aspect of the Mazda’s er­satz Detroit personality with which we take is­sue: It’s a smooth-road car. On ribbons of un­broken concrete the Cosmo simply glides along, but when the going gets rough, so does the ride. The front wheels steer notice­ably over bumps, and the suspension travel is limited enough so that the wheels are occa­sionally jerked clear of the road on surfaces that are not really that rough. The balance of the car, particularly on smooth roads, is rea­sonable however.

At least the Cosmo’s ailments are the kind that can be fixed, certainly by a second look at the factory and perhaps even with a few bolt-on aftermarket parts. We would start with the shocks and see what happens. The rotary engine, on the other hand, is just fine as it is. And Mazda provides an incredible warranty—­five years or 75,000 miles—to make sure it stays that way. Nobody can dislike a car that is guaranteed to keep running even after the loan is paid off.
