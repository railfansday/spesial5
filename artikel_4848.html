Rejecting Oppression, Embracing Joy

What makes a literary character—or a living person you’ve just met—seem intensely human? Besides the movements people make and the sensations they describe, their attitudes can show their humanity. In literature, characters who resist social oppression make more compelling protagonists than those who quietly occupy a society's bottom rungs. Most inspiring of all may be characters shocked by abuse because, despite poverty, sexism, and racism, they believe they deserve joyful lives.

In a recent study of over 400 readers, 19 percent reported “experiential crossing” in which the language and characters of a book they were reading entered their daily lives. “It’s as if the character has started to narrate my world,” wrote one participant (Alderson-Day et al. 2017). Getting to know literary characters can broaden readers’ understandings of human life if those characters’ experiences differ from readers' own.

When characters’ experiences match those of readers, their crossover into readers’ worlds can feel life-affirming, especially if their experiences rarely appear in published fiction. At a book signing in Cambridge, a woman thanked author Junot Díaz for creating Beli, a central character in his novel The Brief Wondrous Life of Oscar Wao. “I had a life a lot like Beli’s,” the reader told him. Tears kept her from saying more (Díaz 2018).

Born in the Dominican Republic, Belicia (Beli) Cabral is sold as a child after her mother dies and her father is imprisoned for offending the dictator, Rafael Trujillo. During her nine years as a slave, her “father” burns her back with hot oil when she tries to attend school. After a distant relative rescues Beli, she grows into a tall, strikingly beautiful young woman. A relationship with Trujillo’s brother-in-law wrecks her life, however, when his henchmen force her to have an abortion and beat her nearly to death. She escapes to New Jersey, where she marries, but her husband abandons her and their two children. To support her son and daughter, she works several minimum-wage jobs, persisting even after she develops breast cancer. Beli is abusive and violent not just to her children but to neighbors, shoppers, and police officers. Throughout her life, she retains her sense of self-worth, refusing to believe she should be exploited because she is female, Dominican, and Black.

Recent studies affirm that the suppressed emotions caused by social inequality harm people’s physical and mental health (Kiehl 2024). The effects of racism have received more attention than those of social class, whose influence many people still deny (Kiehl 2024). Beli and readers who see their lives in hers experience disrespect caused by sexism as well. Her ability to inspire readers stems not from her suffering, however, but from her sense that she is inferior to no one and deserves a good life.

Junot Díaz’s character-narrator, Yunior, hints that Beli can’t see disaster coming because even after being enslaved and burned, she can’t imagine how badly people will treat her. “Like everybody in this damn story,” he writes, “she underestimated the depth of the shit she was in” (Díaz 2007). Beli’s refusal to accept that she is in danger shows not just her sense of self-worth but her belief (at least in her youth) in other people’s goodness. Yunior illustrates her capacity for joy, which blazes forth in her dancing. A complex character, Beli terrorizes her neighborhood and hurts her children, but her humanity is never in doubt. Her will to survive and her rejection of abuse ensure that whether or not readers see themselves in her, she will enter and inhabit their lives.

Like lay readers, psychologists can learn from imagining Beli because in creating characters, authors build models of minds (Alderson-Day et al. 2017; Bernini 2021). Participants in psychological experiments still come disproportionately from “WEIRD” (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) populations, especially North American and European universities (Henrich et al. 2010). Novels like Díaz’s Oscar Wao guide readers to imagine the mental world of a woman who experiences horrors but retains her self-worth and desire for joy. Until scientific studies can include more of the world’s population, literature can complement information revealed through experiments by making a wider range of human experiences imaginable. Most human of all may be the belief that no matter how other people see you, you deserve to thrive.
