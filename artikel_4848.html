3 Anxiety Resets From an OCD Therapist With OCD

“Sorry, my hand is shaking,” my client says.

We are in the middle of an exposure exercise for his obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). He experiences terrifying intrusive thoughts about harming his loved ones, and my job is to help him confront those fears without resorting to compulsions. My client is holding a knife to the small of my back.

“I know it's hard, but stay with the exposure for another minute,” I reply.

As I sit with my client and allow the anxiety of the moment to wash over him, I ask my client to rate his distress on a scale of 1 to 10.

I am an OCD therapist, and I practice an objectively strange type of therapy. I help my clients engage in exposures—brave acts that fly in the face of their fears—while resisting their compulsions.

I love being an OCD therapist. Exposure and response prevention (ERP) is vastly preferable to trying to talk my clients out of their fears. Reassurance rarely works for long. The only thing that can convince my client that he won’t hurt me is being in a position where he could.

My clients are kindhearted, earnest humans who suffer from the same disorder that made me feel alone and scared for most of my 20s. The fear of harming someone does not equate to the intent to harm someone. I myself grappled mightily with this confusing thought pattern and learned to trust myself again.

But there are moments when my professionalism and empathy come up against a formidable foe: my own nervous system.

Sometimes, my nervous system forgets that I am safe. Sometimes my nervous system doesn’t understand time and place and, most importantly for the knife exposure, context. Despite all the work I've done, I still experience fear, anxiety, doubt, and the occasional panic attack.

And my experience is not unique. Many therapists are wounded healers. We love this work because we have lived this work.

One thing I've learned about mental health recovery is that we are never done. The goal isn't to eliminate anxiety forever. It's to develop the tools to move through it without allowing it to control our lives.

When I cannot stay present and grounded for my own therapy session, I count on three resourcing techniques that I learned from eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR), a therapy technique that I underwent as a patient. When my body forgets my professional training, I use techniques from EMDR to guide myself back to the present moment.

Butterfly tapping is a technique that involves gently alternating taps on either side of the body. It's a subtle movement you can try almost anywhere, including at a crowded party or during a stressful conversation. (Or, in my case, in a knife exposure with my client)

Cross your arms against your chest and place each hand on the opposite collarbone. Now, gently and rhythmically begin alternating taps from one side to the other.

Continue for a short period, noticing the rhythm and how your body responds.

The alternating stimulation and repetitive movement may help you feel more grounded. There is no need to force yourself to feel calm or continue if the exercise feels uncomfortable.

Sometimes, a moment of physical connection is all you need to find your footing again.

Your mind can be remarkably unkind, especially when you feel anxious. Trauma can encode negative thought patterns into your brain, telling you things like “I cannot handle feeling uncomfortable” or “I am not strong enough.”

Before heading into a stressful situation, you can choose a phrase that reminds you of how you want to respond when discomfort arises.

Repeat your phrase gently when you need a reminder.

I like to take a photo of my mantra and set it as my phone's home screen because I tend to pick up my phone and glance at it when I'm uncomfortable.

An important distinction for anyone with OCD: A mantra should offer encouragement, not become a ritual you must repeat until your fear disappears. The aim is to develop tolerance for uncomfortable feelings, not compulsively reassure ourselves that nothing bad will happen.

EMDR can open up strong emotions, but it also guides people on how to temporarily put them away.

For this exercise, you may close your eyes, if appropriate in the situation, and imagine a container that can hold the feelings or concerns you aren't ready to address.

It can be a chest, lockbox, jar, drawer, or anything you can vividly picture.

Now imagine placing a troubling experience inside that container. You can return to the experience later, when you have the time and resources for processing.

I use this for a nervous system spike during my therapy workday. My job is to stay present for my client, so I put any reactions in a container and set them aside temporarily.

The goal isn't to suppress feelings or avoid necessary emotional work. If you have OCD, be mindful that this exercise doesn't become another way to neutralize intrusive thoughts or escape uncertainty.

Instead, think of it as giving yourself permission to choose when and how to address an experience that deserves your attention.

As an OCD therapist, I have learned that recovery doesn't mean becoming a person who never experiences anxiety.

In fact, the entire premise of ERP is that we can learn to face discomfort without relying on compulsions to make it disappear.

The three techniques can offer support when anxiety feels overwhelming, but they aren't substitutes for treatment. No anxiety reset should become a ritual that you perform perfectly.

By the end of our session, my client's hand is no longer shaking. He tells me his distress has fallen to a “2 out of 10.”

“Good job staying with the discomfort,” I say, mostly to him, but also a bit to myself.

Here we are, two imperfect nervous systems, finding our way back to the present moment and moving forward with recovery.
