Exclusive: The World’s Top Players Look Back on a Wild Year in Men’s Tennis

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

It was Friday the 13th, and Turin, Italy, the site of the Nitto ATP Finals, glowed with cobalt light as a fingernail moon hung in the air. It looked almost like a Tintoretto painting.

At the Piazza San Carlo, a blue carpet had been laid out lengthwise for the world’s eight best (and currently active) men’s tennis players as fans called out for autographs and selfies. Carlos Alcaraz in Louis Vuitton and Ben Shelton in Ralph Lauren walked among them; missing was the home favorite, Jannik Sinner, who qualified for the field but is sidelined with a knee injury. The scene delivered a surreal, Hollywood-esque half-hour of public stagecraft—paparazzi flashbulbs, police escorts—with all of it designed to celebrate the end of a very notable, and sometimes odd, year in men’s pro tennis.

Speaking of the dreamlike: the evening included an ATP-commissioned portrait session with the photographer Szilveszter Makó, a man who has skyrocketed to industry prominence for having shot multiple covers this year—including for the American, British and Italian editions of Vogue. Makó’s aesthetic itself is rather painterly; photographs as aged frescoes, both cerebral and unusual. Within Turin’s Inalpi Arena, he forged two vignettes with his signature box sets. We stepped in to take a look as Arthur Fils, wearing Balenciaga, was instructed to stand tall and lift his right foot, balancing it on the heel. (“It’s kind of weird, no?” Fils asked, before leaning in.) Makó lensed seven of the eight athletes; due to extenuating circumstances, Alcaraz was unable to stop by the makeshift studio.

But, no matter. In a mad dash between the Principi di Piemonte Hotel, the Piazza San Carlo, a jam-packed van ride across gridlocked roads, and, finally, the Arena, we caught up with all eight players, who together offer an apt representation of the deep and diverse present state of the world’s elite-most men’s tennis. What was discussed: everything from self-reflections to coaching change-ups to cellys to the popularity of the Maldives.

2026 was the biggest year of Zverev’s career: he won his maiden Slam at Roland-Garros, then followed it up with a victory at the US Open. The German also reached the world number 1 spot for the first time (it’s worth noting here that Zverev also holds a super-rare tennis title: an Olympic gold medal, from Tokyo). At 29, he is a half-generation older than the sport’s current dominant duopoly of Sinner and Alcaraz, but if he keeps his current level of play up, he’ll make a strong case for another “Big Three”(the nickname assigned to Roger Federer, Rafael Nadal and Novak Djokovic during their long run of supremacy). Despite the settlement of a domestic abuse lawsuit involving his ex-partner Brenda Patea, a new case was brought to light just a few days ago: the tennis writer Ben Rothenberg reported that Patea has sued Zverev for public misrepresentations. It’s been a career year, but it has to be said that Zverev remains a polarizing figure.

The finest Canadian tuxedo I’ve ever seen.

When you wear head-to-toe denim, yes. You’ve had an incredible year. What worked—and is working—best for you mentally, looking back on it now?

I made a decision with my team last year to change my game style—changing the way we play. I think it was quite a bold move at the age of 29. But we said: it had been working, I’ve had a great career, but it wasn’t working at the final stage of Grand Slams. It hadn’t worked to achieve the dreams and the goals we had. So, sticking to the new game plan, I think that was the biggest thing. Sticking to it and being brave enough to accept it. I think, in the end, it paid off.

Definitely. Two Slams this year, the number one ranking. Are you happy with your game right now, or is there anything you want to improve in 2027?

No, I don’t think I’m complete at all. There are always things you have to improve—and at the end of the day, everybody else is improving, too, so you have to keep with it to stay relevant.

You had some frustration a few weeks ago in Paris in having to defend your number one ranking. Are you still reckoning with that?

I think in Paris it was all just quite new, and I was mentally a little bit tired. There were a lot of new things. And of course, it’s a different kind of pressure. But, I think it’s all settled down, and I’m very happy to be here among the best in the world. It’s an honor.

What’s your vacation plan once the season ends?

Alcaraz—men’s tennis’s brightest star—nicely bookended an otherwise turbulent 2026. The Spaniard, 23, won his first Australian Open all the way back in January, and has enjoyed a surge of recent title wins (in Vienna, Tokyo, and Paris) after returning to the tour from a bout with wrist tenosynovitis that kept him offline for nearly five months. His return at the US Open—where he reached the quarterfinals—brought with it a much-hyped collaboration with Nike’s Cactus Jack line and a tousled new hairstyle. One interesting note going into Turin: Alcaraz has not yet won an ATP Finals, and will be hungry to add that to his resume.

What’s your best 2026 memory off of the court?

Oh, well, I have a couple, to be honest. One of the highlights I had this year was attending the World Cup final. Seeing Spain win the World Cup was unbelievable. So yeah, I’m going to say that! Ferran Torres’s goal, that was the turning point of his career. From that, I would say his stock is really high.

You’ve come back from injury, and you’ve had a strong tail-end of the year, winning Tokyo and Paris. After being hurt, did your mentality change at all? Any hesitation?

I knew that, coming back, I needed matches. I needed competition. I need to feel I was back in the rhythm of competing, and I’m still [building that back]. I need to go out there and feel those nerves of competition. I’m still working on being strong for these difficulties.

With the season ending, what will you try to do in the offseason?

Well, I am not allowed to play golf yet, because my team [won’t let me]. Probably the first thing I’m going to do after the season is over is play golf.

Shelton, 24, had two standout moments on the court in 2026: he defended his Canadian Open title and reached his first Major final at the US Open. Though he lost to Zverev there, it was still a considerable level-up for the Florida-born phenom. Shelton made recent industry news by tapping Juan Carlos Ferrero—Alcaraz’s former coach—to join his team in 2027 (Shelton’s father, Bryan, will remain his coach as well).

You’re bringing on Juan Carlos Ferrero as a coach. Talk us through that decision.

Yeah. Even in the early stages, I think we’ve done a good job of putting together a schedule where I will have two coaches. I don’t want either of them to have to travel 40 weeks a year. So I think we’re putting together a really good formula where they’re both traveling about half the weeks. That’s the ideal scenario for us.

What’s the strongest, most resonant moment you’re taking with you from 2026?

Defending my title in Canada is one. I thought that was the best tennis I’ve played in my career. I didn’t drop a set all week. I was clinical. It was fun for me to see that I can play at that level. But, I have to say, walking out on court for a US Open final and feeling the energy of that stadium on that day, even though I took an L, was pretty special.

I’m just going to play it by ear. I might go on vacation with Arthur Fils, but we’ll see.

The tennis world is happy to see Medvedev, 30, back in elite form. After a less-than-stellar 2025, the lanky Russian has been playing significantly better in 2026, having won four titles and returned to the top 10. Yes, he still has outbursts, and yes, crowds will either go with him on them or boo relentlessly. But that’s just part of the Medvedev Experience. What’s helped on a deeper level: coaching change-ups, and a newfound self-assurance. Don’t forget: Medvedev is a Slam winner (US Open, 2021), a former world number one, and an ATP Finals winner (2020).

Looking back at 2026, what’s the most significant thing you fixed?

What I was looking for was probably finding confidence in myself. Sometimes that requires changing things, and I wanted to take a bit more responsibility. That was probably the biggest fix I managed to make, closer to the end of the year.

Was that rediscovering your old confidence, or was it something new?

Rediscovering in terms of tennis, yes. But more in terms of… I want to say, it’s more like confidence in a tennis life path. I was listening to my old coaches a lot rather than trusting myself fully. So there was always a bit of back-and-forth. Where do I trust them? Where do I trust myself? And now it’s more about trusting myself fully, and then discussing that trust with the team, if that makes sense.

You’re known for your heated interactions with crowds. At this stage in your career, how much of that is just a reaction versus a choice?

It’s never a choice. It’s always a natural reaction.

Any indulgences you’re looking forward to after the season ends?

I have a photo shoot with Lacoste, and then I’m in the Maldives the next day. I would love to go to Hawaii, I don’t know, or wherever. But it’s way too far from where I live.

Cobolli, 24, had the best year of his young career in 2026. His breakthrough: reaching the final of the French Open, where he lost to Zverev in five sets. In Turin, the Italian naturally received the loudest cheers on the blue carpet. With Sinner absent, he’s the home favorite. Cobolli is well-liked on social media, too; the ATP sometimes jokingly calls him their “admin.” He’s had some struggles with fatigue as the season nears its conclusion, but here’s hoping the Florentine can turn on the jets in Piedmont.

At Wimbledon this year, you made some headlines for doing Matheus Cunha’s “surfing” celebration. Should tennis have more cellys?

No, you know why? Two weeks ago, Cunha beat AS Roma. He beat my team, so I’m really pissed with him. [Laughs] But no… I think [for celebrations] if I have something on my mind, I just do it.

You’re the home favorite in Turin. What does that feel like?

It means a lot to me to play here and to win in front of the Italian crowd. Of course, we miss Jannik a lot. He deserves to be here. So I play for him, for the country, for my team, for myself, and for my family. I just want to play for everybody!

Fils, the 22-year-old Frenchman, struggled in the beginning of the year with a back injury that forced him out of the Australian and French Opens. But he had a strong enough summer to qualify for the year-end tournament, winning his first Masters 1000-level title at the Cincinnati Open. In tennis circles, many believe Fils has the natural talent to eventually challenge the Tour’s stalwart top three Sinner, Alcaraz and Zverev. But he’s already matched them if not exceeded them in aura: in Turin, Fils stepped out on the blue carpet in a full Balenciaga look—he has a deal with the brand—and space-like sunglasses. Audiences went wild.

What are you most happy with regarding your play this year?

I’m serving better, moving better. I mean, I knew I was a great competitor. But I didn’t know I could be that good in competitive moments.

When the season is said and done, what’s going to be your first indulgence?

I’m going on holiday in the States, so I think I’m going to go with… having some ribs.

Tell us a little about working with Balenciaga.

They’re changing [internally] now, I know. But I love the brand. It was kind of a dream when I was a kid to sign one day with a huge fashion label. I feel very comfortable wearing them. It’s a new world for me, but I enjoy it.

Tiafoe, 28, has reached the ATP Finals for the first time. It’s testament to his grit and commitment—what he calls a re-established sense of “showing up” day in and day out. “Big Foe,” as he’s endearingly called, has had a productive year; he won his first 500-level tournament in Halle, reached the semis at the US Open, and recorded the fourth-most match wins of any man on Tour. He was all smiles in an expertly tailored Giorgio Armani double-breasted suit on the blue carpet, too.

You’re having a great season. What are you most happy with, not necessarily numbers wise, but in terms of your play?

I think I just found a flow state. I found this peace, and I’m showing up every day. I think sometimes you try to figure out this whole thing of how to get to X to Y to Z, or how to do X, Y and Z together. And it’s kind of just: you show up, man.

Every day looks different, but you just go out there and give your best and be okay with the result. Every time I’ve been able to lay it out there and lost, I was cool with it, rather than having regret that I could have done a little more. I was also focused on my preparation, and once preparation meets opportunity, you get the results.

During the long season, what was a favorite memory away from the court?

I saw the Chris Brown and Usher concert in D.C. and it was out of control. One of the biggest things was getting to meet Matthew McConaughey at the US Open. He’s a special individual.

What are you looking forward to during the break?

Jodar, the youngest man in the Turin field, has been lightning in a bottle this year. He broke men’s tennis’s record for the quickness of his ascent: from his tour debut to hitting the top 10, Jodar needed only 296 days. With that in mind, the Spaniard can be forgiven for speed bumps along the way: a highly publicized outburst at a ball kid during the Shanghai Masters, and a gamesmanship debate around equipment changes. He’s among the youngest of tennis’s new guard—a quarter-generation behind Alcaraz and Sinner, even—and his future feels bright.

So much has been said about the speed of your year, and how quickly you’ve risen. Is there anything you wish would slow down?

Well, I think how it works is that if you do well on Tour, you’re going to have more expectations. That’s something you just have to deal with when you’re doing well. Not every day you’re going to be in a happy mood. But at the end of the day, you just have to handle the situation in the best way possible, and keep focusing on and trying to get better at the game.

What do you do with your limited free time?

I think I’d say spending some time at home and doing some activities in Madrid. Probably, the thing I want to do most is go to the Bernabéu to watch Real Madrid. That’s when I disconnect completely from tennis, and focus on the football game.
