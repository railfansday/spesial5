After 470 Episodes, ABC’s Best Show Is a Surprising Streaming Hit on Apple TV

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

By rights, Grey's Anatomy should be on death's door, flatlining on one of Grey Sloan Memorial Hospital's beds. Since its debut in 2005, there have been 39 main cast members, only three of which — Chandra Wilson (Miranda Bailey), James Pickens Jr. (Richard Webber) and, albeit intermittently now, Ellen Pompeo (Meredith Grey) — have remained with the show since its first episode; 20 characters that have left, either of their own volition or in a body bag; multiple instances of ambulance crashes, hospital shootings, hospital explosions, and plane crashes. If doctors aren't at a bedside operating, they're in a backroom "operating" (wink wink). Yet year after year, Grey's Anatomy keeps coming back strong, including on Apple TV, where it's a surprising hit. Only it isn't as surprising as you might think.

The fight to just get Grey's Anatomy on the air was hard enough: network president Steve McPherson repeatedly challenged show creator Shonda Rhimes on every aspect of the show. It perhaps should have been expected, given Rhimes was proposing a dramatic shift in the medical drama genre. The ensemble was racially diverse, with Rhimes insistent on using a color-blind casting technique, creating diversity based on talent and chemistry as opposed to an all-white cast with minor roles for other ethnicities. The main characters weren't just doctors, but rather a group of interns, low-ranking personnel learning what it takes to be a doctor, and making mistakes along the way. Perhaps the worst was the utterly game-changing take on female sexuality, with showrunner Krista Vernoff citing, "He [McPherson] just didn't get it; he didn't like it... I don't think he liked the [idea of] ambitious women having sex unapologetically."

But the creative team behind Grey's Anatomy had an ally in ABC's then-head of drama, Suzanne Patmore Gibbs, and a first season, consisting of only 9 episodes, would begin airing on March 27th, 2005, following Desperate Housewives. Still, there was little to no faith in the show, with the writers asked to clear out their offices when the season was finished (per Variety). To say they misjudged the series is an understatement, with the premiere netting massive ratings, numbers that Pompeo said were, "so huge the first time we aired it was a big f**k you to McPherson!"

Initially, the appeal was in the show's soap-like aspects of sex; relatable, fast dialogue; the eschewing of traditional scoring in lieu of indie-pop and alternative music; and, of course, Patrick Dempsey's "McDreamy." What kept viewers around, however, was the series' unconventional take on the character's themselves. These weren't seasoned doctors and eager-beaver interns, but flawed, real people, with real and relatable relationships. They weren't even always particularly likable, but they were always interesting, and they all had their time in the spotlight. And all this without ignoring the wildly inaccurate but also wildly entertaining medical cases, "every kind of crazy accident that had ever caused terrible harm to any human ever."

Grey's Anatomy has never strayed from its core focus on the people. This would be in spite of the times that arguably would have ended any other show. Fan favorite Cristina Yang (Sandra Oh) left for Switzerland to run a research institute, a job offer from ex-fiancé Preston Burke (Isaiah Washington) in the Season 10 finale, while Derek Shepherd is tragically killed in Season 11 by a semi-truck. Yet the series largely doesn't shy away from the effects these losses take on those that remain, and while they may upset the fan base for a time, they come back because the characters hurt just as much as they do.

The show is very much a "next man up," with the constant turnover allowing new faces to come in to fill the void, and because they are given screen time to develop, viewers have a chance to get to know them intimately. Storylines, which maintain cultural relevance, range from heartbreaking losses to against-all-odds successful surgeries to the outright hilarious, like the man who swallowed 10 doll heads, creating a severe bowel obstruction ("Because eleven would have been too much"), a genuinely well-balanced formula that has consistently worked for 21 years.

And it's that consistency that makes the streaming success of Grey's Anatomy unsurprising. At 470 episodes, it's easy to throw on for a binge session ranging from a few hours to an entire week. You don't have to know what's going on with the characters wherever you decide to start, with the series bearing a familiarity across episodes (but it's more enjoyable if you do). And as with most series of its age, later generations, wooed by social media clips and the like, experience it anew, while those who started watching in 2005 get to relive the best, the most nerve-wracking, and the saddest moments. Grey's Anatomy is also buoyed by the fact that it has remained a constant presence on streaming, allowing new and old fans the avenue and time to watch all 23 seasons without fear that the series will be pulled. That's what being a "surprise streaming hit" gets you.
