Highly heritable, polygenic and easily measured, adult height has long been the model trait in human genetics1,2. Although the landscape of height-associated common genetic variation has been studied extensively2, rare variation remains relatively unexplored1. Here, using rare protein-altering variants in a discovery set of 826,066 exomes, we identify 207 height-associated genes, 98% of which replicate in an additional 624,567 individuals. The rarest and most deleterious class of variation, singleton (frequency < 0.0001%) putative loss-of-function variants, implicated 17 genes with large effects on height ranging from −17cm (ACAN) to +11 cm (FBN1) per allele, 52× larger than the average effect of common height-associated variants and comparable to the 1% tails of a common variant polygenic score. Several genes (TET1, DTL and IGF2BP2) have effect sizes that are at least as large as established Mendelian height genes, yet lack documented stature or skeletal growth syndromes, particularly for genes associated with increased height. Our large-scale sequencing study elucidates the genetic architecture and allelic heterogeneity of rare variants in height, providing unique insights into their large effects—often similar to or larger than recognized Mendelian syndromes—and their impact on trait prediction, clinical evaluation, and discovery power to facilitate drug target prioritization.

Although height was described as a heritable trait as early as 18863, its genetic architecture started to emerge only in the late twentieth century when researchers began identifying the genetic causes of Mendelian syndromes with a substantial stature component4,5,6,7. Non-syndromic genetic modulators of height remained unclear until 2007, when genome-wide association studies (GWASs) identified the first common variants associated with height8. Fifteen years later, a GWAS of 5.3 million individuals identified 12,111 independent variants that collectively accounted for 40% of the variance in height across individuals of European descent2. As with other traits, height GWASs predominantly implicated common non-coding variants with modest effects (<1 cm)2, complicating efforts to link associations directly to specific genes and functional mechanisms.

By contrast, sequencing-based studies of rare variation can directly implicate causal genes by identifying large-effect putative loss-of-function (pLoF) and missense variants9. To systematically assess the contribution of rare coding variation to height, we performed a multiancestry exome sequencing analysis of adult height in 1,450,633 individuals across 12 cohorts (Supplementary Table 1), in which we (1) identified 17 height-associated genes through a burden of singleton pLoFs; (2) identified additional height-associated genes by including nonsynonymous variants up to 1% alternate allele frequency (AAF) and applying non-burden tests that do not assume variant effects act in the same direction; and (3) estimated the impact of rare protein-altering variation on genetic-based height prediction.

Using 826,066 individuals from six cohorts (Supplementary Table 1), we performed three discovery association analyses of adult height: (1) imputed common variants (AAF > 0.01); (2) rare exome variants (AAF ≤ 0.01, alternate allele count (AAC) ≥ 10); and (3) gene-based tests grouping rare coding variants. We used a single unified Bonferroni significance threshold of 1.75 × 10−9 across all three analyses (Methods). For gene-based analyses, we used a gene-wide omnibus statistic, gene-P10, that aggregates evidence across burden tests and two other gene-based tests, variant-level aggregate Cauchy association tests (ACAT-V)11 and optimal sequence kernel association tests (SKAT-O)12 (Methods and Supplementary Fig. 1). We used six additional cohorts with 624,567 individuals for replication (Supplementary Table 1) and assessed the impact of rare variants on height prediction using a subset (n = 242,679).

Common variant analysis identified 3,034 conditionally independent height associations (Supplementary Table 2) that collectively explain around 25% of the observed variation in height (Methods and Supplementary Note). We conditioned on these 3,034 common variants to ensure rare variant and gene-based associations were independent of common variant signals (Supplementary Note and Supplementary Fig. 2). We identified 105 rare nonsynonymous variants in 87 genes (median AAF = 2.16 × 10−3; Supplementary Table 3) and 207 genes associated with height (gene-P < 1.75 × 10−9; Extended Data Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 4). In total, 82 out of the 87 genes with significant rare nonsynonymous variant associations are also among the 207 genes identified through gene-based tests.

As singleton pLoFs represent the rarest and most deleterious class of variants, we first focused on this subset. Singleton pLoF burden tests identified 17 genes, 11 height decreasing and 6 height increasing, with a mean absolute effect of 8.92 cm per allele (Fig. 1, Table 1 and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6), about 52× larger than the average GWAS variant (\(\overline{|{\beta }_{\mathrm{GWAS}}|}\) = 0.17 cm) and around 44× larger (range 14.4× to 78.3×) when comparing effects per gene (Methods and Supplementary Table 7). Effects ranged from a 16.6-cm decrease in stature for ACAN singleton pLoF carriers to an 11.1-cm increase in stature for FBN1 singleton pLoF carriers. The effect of carrying a singleton pLoF in one of these 17 genes approaches the cumulative effect observed in the 1% tails of a common variant polygenic score (PGS) (top 1%, +9.60 cm; bottom 1%, −9.12 cm; Fig. 2a, Methods and Supplementary Tables 8 and 9). These incredibly rare pLoFs, cumulatively carried by fewer than 0.1% of individuals (744 carriers among 826,066 individuals), have a larger impact on height than all but the most extreme combinations of common variants.

These 17 genes belong to well-established biological processes and pathways affecting height, including extracellular matrix organization and skeletal/growth pathways such as growth hormone/insulin-like growth factor (GH/IGF), WNT/β-catenin and transforming growth factor-β1,13,14,15 (TGFβ) (Table 1 and Extended Data Table 1).

Incorporating more common variation into burden tests can increase statistical power for gene discovery by adding variant carriers, although at the cost of attenuated effect sizes. Expanding our analysis to include increasingly frequent pLoFs, followed by including missense variants, identified an additional 162 genes (Extended Data Fig. 1).

Among pLoFs, relaxing the burden test AAF threshold from singletons (about 0.00006%) to 1% identified 76 genes, 62 not discovered with pLoF singletons alone. On average, burden tests aggregating more common pLoFs had a 3.96-cm effect—less than half the 8.92-cm effect of pLoF singletons, but still 24× larger than the average GWAS association (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Table 10). Among the 15 genes with pLoF singleton associations that remained significant at AAF < 0.01%, we observed a 35% attenuation in the absolute effect size (from 8.71 cm to 5.68 cm), suggesting that a substantial fraction of more frequent pLoF-annotated variants may not cause complete loss of function. Two genes—CHD8 (associated with autism spectrum disorder and intellectual disability) and COL1A1 (associated with Ehlers–Danlos syndrome16 and osteogenesis imperfecta)—were identified only through singleton pLoF burden tests. When more frequent pLoFs were included, burden test effect sizes fell by 78% for COL1A1 and 83% for CHD8 and were no longer statistically significant (Fig. 1a). By contrast, three genes with absolute effects on height of >1 s.d. (7.99 cm) reached significance only with higher-frequency pLoFs: SHOX17 in the fibroblast growth factor pathway (β = −12.05 cm, P = 2.85 × 10−16); transcriptional regulator ERF (β = −8.67 cm, P = 4.41 × 10−21); and HMGA2, the insulin-like growth factor pathway gene underlying the first height GWAS discovery8 (β = −8.11 cm, P = 6.11 × 10−13). These three genes each had few singleton pLoFs (1, 6, and 16 carriers, respectively); aggregating singletons with additional rare pLoFs was therefore essential to achieve significance.

This expanded set of height-associated pLoF burden genes highlighted additional canonical growth pathways and height biology (for example, the fibroblast growth factor pathway; Extended Data Table 1), as well as additional members from the same gene families and pathways implicated by pLoF singleton genes (Supplementary Table 11).

Whereas pLoFs represent the rarest and most deleterious class of coding variation, missense variants represent the most frequent class of protein-altering variants9,18,19. If missense variants ablate protein function to a similar extent as pLoFs, we might expect both increased statistical power and similar effect sizes when combining both in burden tests. Conversely, if missense variants only partially diminish protein function or exert opposing effects through gain-of-function (GoF) mechanisms, burden effect sizes will be attenuated.

We combined pLoFs with three sets of missense variants: (1) all missense variants; (2) missense variants inconsistently classified as deleterious by in silico algorithms (possibly deleterious); and (3) missense variants confidently classified as deleterious by in silico algorithms (deleterious; Methods), collectively identifying 154 genes (85 not discovered by pLoFs alone; Fig. 1c). When combined with pLoF variants, successively filtering rare missense variants for increasing deleteriousness increased discovery power, yielding 96, 116, and 132 genes for each of the above groups, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 1b).

Among the 14 genes with both significant singleton pLoF and singleton pLoF + deleterious missense burden associations (Table 1), the absolute effect size decreased 28.5% on average when adding deleterious missense singletons (8.70 cm versus 6.22 cm; Fig. 1b). This observation holds broadly across height-associated genes, with diminished pLoF + missense effect sizes reflecting both that missense variants account for most rare allele carriers (68.5% in singleton and 84.1% in AAF < 1% burden tests) and that the effect of missense variants is, on average, substantially smaller than that of pLoFs (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Table 10). Despite diminished effect sizes, the higher allele counts contributed by missense variants substantially increased overall discovery power, more than doubling the number of associated height genes.

Of the 85 genes not discovered by pLoFs alone, UHRF2 (singleton pLoF + deleterious missense variants: β = −4.74 cm, P = 3.6 × 10−12)—an E3 ubiquitin ligase regulating the cell cycle20—and PTPN11 (pLoF + deleterious missense variants AAF < 0.01%: β = −4.00 cm, P = 2.5 × 10−32)—a signalling protein involved in development and hormone response through the RAS–MAPK and PI3K–AKT signalling pathways21—had the largest effect sizes.

We found six genes (ESR1, FGFR3, HSD11B2, MTMR11, PTPN11 and RPL5) that exhibited the strongest association from missense-only burden tests, and pLoF burden tests were, at best, only nominally significant (Supplementary Table 13). Four genes (FGFR3, HSD11B2, PTPN11 and RPL5) had opposite directions of effect between pLoF and missense burden tests, although the pLoF estimates were not statistically significant. Notably, three genes—FGFR3 (refs. 7,22), PTPN11 (ref. 23) and ESR1 (ref. 24)—have previously reported GoF mutations that drive syndromic conditions with notable skeletal growth phenotypes, including achondroplasia (FGFR3)22 and Noonan syndrome (PTPN11)23. Given the known GoF mechanisms for three of these genes, the other missense-driven genes may also affect height through GoF mechanisms.

The new genes identified by incorporating missense variants further underscore previously implicated pathways, biological functions and gene families (Supplementary Table 11) but also implicate different pathways (for example, Sonic Hedgehog signalling) and biological processes, including sex hormone regulation and carbohydrate transport, storage and metabolism (Extended Data Table 1).

Although burden tests identified 179 height-associated genes (Extended Data Fig. 1a), they were not designed to identify genes of which variants with effects exist in opposite directions or where most variants have no effect. For these situations, SKAT-O12 and ACAT-V11 were developed.

Collectively, ACAT-V and SKAT-O identified 28 genes not detected by burden tests (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Before conditional analysis, ACAT-V and SKAT-O associations were more likely than burden test associations to be driven by linkage disequilibrium with common variants (Supplementary Note, Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 12). Consistent with the underlying model, SKAT-O associations highlight genes harbouring variants with opposing effects on height. For example, in PTCH1, a transmembrane protein receptor involved in Hedgehog signalling25, the SKAT-O association (P = 1.94 × 10−15) was driven by two variants: a missense variant associated with increased height (9:95458026G>A; p.T1052M; β = +1.00 cm, P = 4.8 × 10−11; Supplementary Table 3) and a splice acceptor variant associated with decreased height (9:95485875C>T; β = −4.28 cm, P = 1.9 × 10−9). Meanwhile, ACAT-V identified genes like FBN2 (P = 1.72 × 10−12), a component of microfibrils that stores and regulates growth factors including TGFβ26, in which the association was driven by one significant missense variant (5:128332993G>T; p.H1381N; β = +1.07 cm, P = 9.8 × 10−14; AAF = 0.48%; Supplementary Table 3).

Although gene-based tests are useful for boosting rare variant discovery power, we identified 121 significant rare (AAF < 1%) exome variants after conditioning on common GWAS variants (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Tables 3 and 14 and Supplementary Note). Of these 121 variants, 105 were nonsynonymous (85 missense, 17 pLoFs and 3 in-frame insertions or deletions (indels)) and implicated 87 genes, 82 of which overlapped genes implicated by gene-based tests (60 from burden tests; 22 genes from ACAT-V or SKAT-O).

For the 82 genes identified by both single variants and gene-based tests, we compared the consistency of effect sizes between burden test and significant individual variants (Methods). Focusing on the 79 rare nonsynonymous variants (59 missense, 17 pLoFs, 3 in-frame indels) in 60 genes with a significant burden test, we found that 52 (66%) had larger effect sizes than the largest burden test effect. The neurodevelopmental-disorder-associated gene KMT2B27 had the largest discrepancy, with a nearly 20-fold difference between variant 19:35720959C>T (p.R538C; β = −7.67 cm, P = 2.6 × 10−10, AAF = 2.61 × 10−5) and the largest burden test effect (β = −0.38 cm, P = 5.0×10−10; pLoF and possibly deleterious missense variants with AAF < 0.01%).

Two genes with GoF missense variants, FGFR3 and PTPN11, have multiple missense variants with some of the largest observed effects on height (Fig. 2b). Among the six exome-wide significant variants in FGFR3, a pathogenic GoF missense variant causing achondroplasia7 (4:1804392G>A; p.G380R; P = 3.22 × 10−64) decreases height by 29.78 cm, about 13× larger than the largest FGFR3 burden test effect (β = −2.31 cm, P = 2 × 10−41; deleterious missense variants AAF < 0.0001; Extended Data Fig. 2). PTPN11 also contains two significant pathogenic GoF missense variants23,28: p.R265Q (12:112472981G>A; β = −12.26 cm, P = 6 × 10−19) and p.N308D (12:112477719A>G; β = −14.21 cm, P = 4 × 10−14), with effects around 3× larger than the largest burden signal (β = −4.12 cm, P = 3.6 × 10−33; deleterious missense variants with AAF < 0.0001). These examples emphasize the challenge that allelic heterogeneity presents for interpreting and using gene-level burden effect sizes. We discuss the implications of effect size heterogeneity on phenotypic prediction below (see the ‘Heritability, enrichment and prediction’ section).

Recent studies29,30 demonstrated that non-additive models can identify associations missed by standard approaches; we therefore tested rare coding variants under a recessive model. This identified only one association missed by additive tests: the CFTR ΔF508 mutation that causes cystic fibrosis31 was not associated with height under an additive model (βadd = −0.05 cm; Padd = 0.14), but was associated with a 5.87-cm height reduction in homozygotes in the recessive model (Prec = 5 × 10−10; replication and additional details are provided in the Supplementary Note). This result is consistent with both the recessive nature of cystic fibrosis mutations and the established impact of cystic fibrosis on growth and stature. The inability of cystic fibrosis treatments to fully restore growth trajectory and stature, along with diminished IGF1 levels exhibited by newborns with cystic fibrosis, are findings compatible with a direct effect of CFTR on height through fetal and/or neonatal growth pathways32,33.

We sought to understand the differences in genetic architecture across genetic ancestry groups by stratifying single-variant and gene-based tests by ancestry (Methods). Consistent with previous findings, we detected no effect-size heterogeneity by genetic ancestry34. However, we identified several ancestry-specific signals resulting from differences in AAF and sample size. Most associations specific to a single ancestry were identified in the European subset, which accounts for most participants (n = 640,240, 78%; Supplementary Table 15 and Supplementary Note). One notable exception is a rare HHIP p.V496E substitution associated with decreased height exclusively in the admixed American subpopulation (AMR AAF = 0.05%, P = 1.52 × 10−9, β = −3.96 cm; Supplementary Table 15 and Supplementary Note). This variant is observed only in individuals from the Mexico City Prospective Study35. We speculate that the p.V496E variant is a GoF missense variant, as rare HHIP pLoFs appear to be associated with increased height (pLoF singleton β = +9.92 cm, P = 3.72 × 10−7). Similarly, we replicate a known signal at a FBN1 missense variant36 (p.E1297G; β = −2.12 cm, P = 2.9 × 10−17; Supplementary Table 16) that is substantially more common in admixed Americans (about 1% versus 0.004% in Europeans) and occurs almost exclusively on Indigenous haplotypes (Supplementary Fig. 4). These discoveries underscore the value of sequencing diverse populations.

Height GWASs14 have consistently observed enrichment of Mendelian syndrome genes among height-associated common variant loci37; we observed similar enrichment in genes implicated through rare variation. Forty-three out of 76 genes (57%) with significant pLoF burden signals at AAF < 1% and 102 out of 207 (49%) height-associated genes in total were documented in the Mendelian syndrome in Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) database, significantly more than expected by chance (odds ratio (OR) = 4.46, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 2.76–7.26, P = 1.83 × 10−10; Fisher’s exact test). Of the 43 Mendelian genes, 21 had annotated stature or skeletal growth features2, an 8.37-fold enrichment (95% CI = 4.34–16.10, P = 2.56 × 10−10; Fisher’s exact test). Genes with pLoF burden height associations and a Mendelian syndrome listed in OMIM are over 5× more likely to be associated with decreased height (36 out of 43, 83%). Among the 11 pLoF singleton genes with height-decreasing effects, 9 were in OMIM and 8 list stature or skeletal growth among the clinical characteristics. By contrast, among the six with height-increasing effects, three are in OMIM, but only FBN1 includes height among the clinical characteristics. Our results were insufficient to determine whether increased height is under-reported as a syndromic feature or whether genes leading to increased height are less likely to cause other clinical features and therefore cause fewer recognized syndromes (Supplementary Note).

Previous studies estimated that the gene nearest to a GWAS peak is the causal gene in around 70% of instances9,38. We observed strong overlap between large-effect rare variant burden associations and GWAS peaks, although this overlap diminishes noticeably with the stringency of the burden definition and variant frequency. Of pLoF singleton genes, 82% (14 out of 17) are the closest gene to a GWAS peak, compared with only 60% (92 out of 154) of pLoF + missense burden genes. In total, 28 out of 207 genes are not among the top 10 nearest genes to any common variant signal. Notably, 4 genes are >1 Mb from the nearest common variant association signal: FIBIN, HHIPL1, PTPN11 and WLS. Even compared with the 12,111 index variants from ref. 2, PTPN11 is not among the 10 nearest genes to any GWAS peak, with the closest signal 393 kb away (Supplementary Table 4).

To assess the validity of our results, we performed replication analyses in six independent cohorts comprising an additional 624,567 individuals (Supplementary Table 1). Despite the 24.4% smaller sample size, 203 out of 207 (98.1%) genes and 113 out of 125 (90.4%) single variants replicated with nominal significance (α = 0.05; Methods, Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplementary Tables 3 and 4 and Supplementary Note), demonstrating both high consistency of height associations and the robustness of our Bonferroni threshold. Furthermore, replication rates were consistent across gene-based tests—BURDEN-ACAT, 98.1% (155 out of 158); SBAT, 93.8% (166 out of 177); SKAT-O, 95.3% (164 out of 172); and ACAT-V, 94.9% (94 out of 99; Supplementary Fig. 5b–e). Replication performance for single variants improved to 96.1% (98 out of 102) after restricting to variants with over 80% estimated replication power (Supplementary Fig. 5f). Irrespective of power, all 179 burden genes and all but one variant had consistent directions of effect between discovery and replication analyses (Supplementary Note). Furthermore, gene-based tests maintained high replication rates well above our study-wide P-value threshold (Supplementary Note and Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). For example, 91.6% of the 368 genes reaching a discovery gene-P < 2.5 × 10−6—roughly equivalent to one test per gene—replicated with nominal significance (P < 0.05) and would implicate an additional 137 genes compared to our experiment-wide Bonferroni threshold (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Replication rates for single variants and singleton burden tests declined similarly, requiring respective P-value thresholds of 4 × 10−6 and 1 × 10−6 to achieve a comparable 91.6% replication rate when restricted to tests with over 80% power (Supplementary Figs. 5f and 6).

Finally, because burden tests were more likely to have negative (height-decreasing) effect estimates, we compared the ratio of effect directions across significance thresholds (Supplementary Note and Extended Data Fig. 3). The excess of height-decreasing burden effects persists well below the Bonferroni significance threshold, with the degree of imbalance varying by both significance threshold and variant class. These patterns suggest considerable rare variant signal yet to be discovered.

Rare variants are frequently offered as an answer to the ‘missing heritability’ problem in complex trait genetics39. Here we estimated the cumulative impact of rare coding variation on height at the population level by calculating the proportion of phenotypic variance attributable to burdens of rare coding variants and assessing the potential of large-effect height-associated coding variants to improve height predictions.

First, burden heritability regression40 estimated the burden of rare nonsynonymous variants explain 3.97% (95% CI = 3.77–4.17%) of phenotypic variance (Extended Data Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 17 and Supplementary Note), nearly identical to the previously published estimate of 3.82% (95% CI = 3.55–4.09%)40. pLoFs with AAF < 0.001% accounted for the largest fraction of burden heritability, consistent with the large effects we observed for these extremely rare, damaging variants. This probably underestimates the true contribution of rare coding variants because the burden approach underestimates heritability in the presence of effect size heterogeneity40. Together, the most significant burden test for each of the 179 burden genes explains around 1.1% of phenotypic variance in the replication data, nearly 30% of the total estimated contribution of the burden of rare variants to adult height (Extended Data Fig. 5).

We next tested for enrichment of rare, deleterious variants in individuals whose height was poorly predicted by common variants in our replication data (n = 242,679), as rare variants unaccounted for in common variant prediction may contribute to the discrepancy between observed and genetically predicted height41,42,43. Individuals deviating from their common variant predicted height by >2.5 s.d. (±17–20 cm) were 17.2-fold (95% CI = 9.89–28.34, P = 1.12 × 10−16, Fisher’s exact test) enriched for carrying a singleton pLoF in one of the 17 singleton pLoF genes (Fig. 3a, Methods and Supplementary Table 18). By contrast, individuals at the extreme ends of the height distribution (>2.5 s.d.) were only 4.9-fold enriched (P = 1.89 × 10−7, Fisher’s exact test; Supplementary Table 19 and Supplementary Note) for pLoF singletons in these genes, consistent with previous results42,43.

We next focused on the 337 individuals in the replication data carrying a pLoF singleton in one of the 17 genes with significant pLoF singleton burden (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 5). In these individuals, adding burden effects to a common variant prediction model (Methods) improved the R2 from 0.55 to 0.72 (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Note). This brings the performance in line with common variant prediction across the entire replication sample (R2 = 0.73). Including pLoF singleton burden effects into prediction also reduced the s.d. of the error distribution by 1.55 cm, signifying more consistent predictions; however, improvement varied substantially by gene (Fig. 3c,d and Extended Data Fig. 6).

Given the large effects of rare missense variants in FGFR3 and PTPN11 (Fig. 2b), we evaluated the performance of several rare variant-based prediction models compared with standard common variant PGS in these variant carriers (Fig. 3d and Methods). Among 29 carriers of five rare deleterious missense variants in these two genes in the replication dataset, the prediction R2 dropped slightly from 0.158 to 0.155 when adding a PGS built from deleterious missense (AAF < 1%) burden effect sizes (Supplementary Fig. 7). By contrast, the prediction R2 increased to 0.67 when adding a PGS based on individually significant rare variants. This difference in prediction performance was especially pronounced among the six carriers of FGFR3 p.G380R (4:1804392:G:A; βdiscovery = −29.8 cm, P = 3.2 × 10−64; Fig. 2b)—a ClinVar pathogenic variant reported for achondroplasia7 (Fig. 3d))—for whom height prediction improved by an average of 26.1 cm after accounting for the variant. Aggregate burden effects are largely unsuitable in prediction for genes with substantial effect size heterogeneity, such as FGFR3 and PTPN11 (Supplementary Note). Still, individual rare variants are difficult to incorporate in prediction, as variants are frequently not observed in reference samples or effects are estimated from few observations. Overall, we observe that PGSs that supplement common variants with both aggregate rare variant effects and individual rare variants with extreme effects perform best for height prediction (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Finally, population-based data can provide a useful orthogonal resource to aid clinical variant interpretation19. We assessed whether our association results could inform pathogenicity classification of ClinVar variants with conflicting classifications of pathogenicity (CCOPs) or variants of uncertain significance (VUSs). To maximize power for these ultrarare variants, we performed a meta-analysis of our discovery and replication data (Supplementary Tables 20 and 21 and Extended Data Fig. 7) for the 5,233 rare variants in our 207 height-associated genes that were evaluated in ClinVar for links to stature-related syndromes (Methods). In total, 39 variants were significantly associated with height, including 20 VUSs/CCOPs. Of those 20 variants, one—an ADAMTS10 splice region variant—was associated with increased height (β = +2.3 cm, P = 3.9 × 10−15), the opposite direction of ADAMTS10’s burden test effects (Supplementary Table 6). The pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) category contained more significant height-associated variants than expected by chance (P = 6 × 10−12, binomial test), as did the CCOP and benign/likely benign categories (Supplementary Table 22).

We next looked for variants absent from ClinVar with absolute effect sizes greater than 7.99 cm (1 s.d.), an effect size greater than 77% of the stature-related P/LP variants in our 207 genes (Fig. 4). A total of 11 VUS/CCOP variants (AAC ≥ 10) had absolute effect sizes exceeding 7.99 cm, with P values ranging from 1.6 × 10−4 to 1.1 × 10−31 (five with P < 1.75 × 10−9; Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 20). Although none of these missense variants achieved genome-wide significance, two (p.G221R and p.R921Q) are experimentally validated loss-of-function variants44. Here we highlight large-effect variants lacking clear clinical annotations (either unannotated or classified as VUSs/CCOPs) that exemplify how population-scale association analyses provide relevant data that clinical geneticists can use to evaluate potentially disease-causing variants.

We undertook an exome-wide association study in 1,450,633 individuals, identifying 207 height-associated genes after conditioning on common variants—115 more than an earlier large-scale sequencing study9. In 17 genes, singleton pLoFs—the rarest and most deleterious variant class—were associated with absolute effects on height 52× larger than the average common variant (8.92 cm versus 0.17 cm). Although we noted substantial overlap with Mendelian syndromes, fewer than 25% (44 out of 207) of genes include a stature or skeletal growth component in their OMIM description2, and only 16% of height-increasing genes (9 out of 57) are both included in OMIM and annotated with stature or skeletal growth phenotypes. Collectively, rare variant associations showed strong concordance with genes implicated by common variant associations and reflected established biological processes influencing height, including growth factor signalling, extracellular matrix organization and transcriptional regulation.

Our results illustrate classic trade-offs in genetic association studies between effect size and allele frequency. Expanding from singletons to low-frequency variants up to 1%, and from pLoFs to missense variants, increased the number of implicated genes from 17 to 179 but greatly diminished the average absolute effect size. Tests that more explicitly accommodate effect size heterogeneity12 or small proportions of causal variants11 within each gene increased gene discovery another 16%, bringing the total to 207 genes. As no single analysis captured all of the implicated genes, we maximized gene discovery by combining gene-based P values from multiple models using a well-calibrated omnibus gene-based test10.

The genes we found to be associated with height include those in well-understood pathways but also implicate potentially new areas of height biology. For example, in the GH/IGF pathway, growth hormone (GH1) signals for IGF1 production, stimulating skeletal growth through cartilage generation in the growth plate45. While Mendelian syndromes with altered stature as a major feature are linked to eight members of the GH/IGF pathway—GH1, GHR, GHRHR, GHSR, IGF1, IGF1R, IGFLAS and PAPPA2 (ref. 46)—our results include seven of these eight (Supplementary Table 23), and implicate three others (PAPPA, STC2 and GHRH). Furthermore, for several genes that were previously considered to have autosomal recessive modes of inheritance (GHRHR, IGFALS, IGF1 and PAPPA2), our results show that loss-of-function and deleterious missense variants have apparent effects in the heterozygous state (Supplementary Table 23). Finally, while GH1–IGF1 is generally considered the major driver of post-natal growth and development, the IGF2 pathway is believed to act mainly during prenatal development with uncertain effects on adult height47. Human genetic evidence for a role of the IGF2 pathway in height has previously been limited to extreme short stature (>3 s.d. below average) in Silver–Russell syndrome, caused by disruptions in the IGF2 gene itself (although not implicated here)48. Our results include association in an additional IGF2 pathway gene: IGF2BP2 through singleton pLoFs (β = −8.67 cm, P = 1.52 × 10−10; Table 1), an mRNA-binding protein that stabilizes IGF2 mRNA, providing additional evidence for a developmental role of IGF signalling in adult height.

Accurate complex trait prediction is a key aspiration of human genetics. Current prediction efforts focus on common variants with effects that are individually small but substantial in aggregate. Supplementing this approach by adding both rare variants with individually large effects and burden tests provides substantial predictive value for carriers. Among 337 carriers across our 17 singleton pLoF genes, adding rare variant effects to common variant prediction models improved the prediction performance to nearly that of common variants in the full population (R2 = 0.72 in carriers versus 0.73 across the full sample). However, as these variants are so rare (<0.1% of individuals), this has a limited impact at the population level.

FGFR3 and PTPN11 frequently arose as interesting case studies throughout this work. First, they represent the unusual case in which rare missense variants collectively yielded a significant burden association with no contribution from pLoFs. They reduce height through GoF mechanisms22 and are known causes of achondroplasia7 and Noonan syndrome23, respectively. However, their burden effects were relatively modest (3–15× smaller; −2.31 cm for FGFR3; −4.12 cm for PTPN11) compared with the effects of a few individual variants: −29.8 cm effect for FGFR3 p.G380R; −14.2 cm for PTPN11 p.N308D; and −12.2 cm for PTPN11 p.R265Q. For most other genes, the effect sizes were more consistent for missense and pLoFs and combining them yielded the most statistically powerful analysis.

Collectively, this work substantially expands the breadth of rare genetic associations for adult height. Beyond directly implicating 207 genes, we demonstrate how rare variant gene discovery at scale can bridge the gap between common variant GWAS and Mendelian disease genetics, providing valuable data for identifying functional genes and interpreting observed variation in clinical settings.

Our discovery analysis comprised six exome-sequenced cohorts jointly called on an Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) modified xGen exome capture from the RGC-ME study49: the glaucoma-ascertained African Descent and Glaucoma Evaluation Study III (ADAGES; n = 1,941)50; the Mexico City Prospective Study (MCPS; n = 138,667), comprising residents from the Iztapalapa and Coyoacán districts of Mexico City35; the population-based UK Biobank (UKB; n = 452,583); and three health-system cohorts: Mount Sinai BioMe Biobank (BioMe; n = 26,850), Geisinger Health System DiscovEHR (GHS; n = 165,080)51 and Penn Medicine BioBank (PMBB; n = 40,945)52.

We performed a meta-analysis across six replication cohorts not part of the RGC-ME49. Four cohorts were exome sequenced using a TWIST capture: the household-based prospective ‘BangladEsh Longitudinal Investigation of Emerging Vascular and nonvascular Events’ (BELIEVE; n = 53,830) Study53 and three hospital-based cohorts: The Biobank at Colorado Center for Personalized Medicine (CCPM; n = 48,799)54, Mayo Clinic Project Generation (n = 72,472) and University of California Los Angeles (UCLA) at LA Study (ATLAS) Precision Health Biobank (UCLA ATLAS; n = 38,406). The longitudinal All of Us (AoU) research programme was whole-genome sequenced; participant-level phenotypic and genetic data cannot be removed from the trusted research environment and we therefore did not use AoU data for prediction analyses (AoU; n = 381,888)55. The prospective, population-based Malmö Diet and Cancer Study (MDCS; n = 29,172) was exome sequenced by IDT exome capture but could not be jointly called in the RGC-ME due to regulatory restrictions.

We calculated an individual’s standing height by first converting all height measurements to centimetres, removing measurements <75 cm or taken before age 18, and then taking the median across the remaining height measurements. We excluded individuals for whom their genetically predicted sex differed from their self-reported sex as well as 76 bone marrow transplant recipients for whom their DNA was collected from blood. Within each cohort, we applied rank inverse normal transformation separately for each sex and used this rank inverse normal transformed height phenotype in genetic association analyses. To approximate height effect sizes in centimetres, we multiplied effect sizes (in s.d. units) by 7.994 cm, the sex-stratified, sample-size-weighted s.d. in the discovery cohorts.

All genomic data analysed here was generated as previous described9,49,55. In brief, exome capture was performed using IDT (ADAGES III, BioMe, GHS, MCPS, MDCS, PMBB and UKB) or TWIST (BELIEVE, CCPM, Mayo Clinic, UCLA ATLAS) capture reagents, sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 or NovaSeq 6000 system, and sequencing reads were aligned to GRCh38. AoU used DNA extracted at the Mayo Clinic (blood or saliva) and sequenced at Baylor College of Medicine, Broad Institute, or University of Washington: sequencing centre and DNA source were included as batch covariates55 (Supplementary Fig. 9). PLINK (v.1.9)56 converted the pVCF to PLINK files for downstream analyses. Quality control included sample/variant missingness of <10%, ±10 bp buffer window around target regions, heterozygosity Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) P > 1 × 10−100, excess heterozygosity HWE P > 1 × 10−30 and setting male heterozygous genotypes in the non-pseudoautosomal region (non-PAR) of chromosome X to missing using the PLINK256 option --set-hh-missing. We implemented a previously described support vector machine (SVM)-based quality control protocol to identify likely artefactual variants9 and then used these predictions to create individual-level covariates for GWAS.

Variant Effect Predictor (VEP)57 v.100.4 (Ensembl58 release 100) annotated variants on human protein-coding transcripts and assigned a single consequence per variant based on the canonical transcript defined using MANE58, APPRIS59 and Ensembl canonical tags as previously described58.

We used REGENIE’s60 linear whole-genome regression test to perform three genetic analyses on chromosomes 1–22 and X in the discovery: (1) a TOPMed61 imputed common variant GWAS (AAF > 0.01; n = 19,527,870 variants); (2) a rare variant exome analysis (AAF ≤ 0.01, AAC ≥ 10; n = 9,086,709 variants excluding those tested in the imputed variant GWAS); and (3) a gene-based association analysis of rare coding variation (n = 19,107 gene-level omnibus tests). We set a single unified Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold of P < 1.75 × 10−9 across all three analyses, accounting for all common, rare and gene-based tests performed (0.05/28,633,686 tests). A discussion of calibration in these results is provided in the Supplementary Note.

For the non-PARs of chromosome X, we assumed a dosage-compensation model in which homozygous reference male individuals are coded as 0 and hemizygous male individuals are coded as 2 (and heterozygous male individuals were set to missing). In step 1 of REGENIE (that is, prediction of individual trait values based on genetic data), we included genotyped variants with an AAF > 1%, <10% missingness, HWE P > 10−15 and linkage-disequilibrium (LD) pruning (1,000 variant windows, 100 variant sliding windows and an r2 threshold of 0.1). The covariates used in REGENIE steps 1 and 2 are described in Supplementary Table 24.

To identify conditionally independent common GWAS variants, we performed an iterative conditional meta-analysis using REGENIE’s leave-only-same-chromosome-in (LOSCI) option on TOPMed-imputed dosage data. LOSCI conditions the variant/gene being tested only on the same-chromosome variants to avoid model overfitting. In each iteration, distance-based clumping (1 Mb minimum) identified the lead variant (P < 1.75 × 10−9), which became covariates in subsequent iterations. After 22 iterations, no variants reached significance (P < 1.75 × 10−9), yielding 3,034 independent variants used to condition all rare variant and gene-based association tests (REGENIE’s model includes each variant individually).

REGENIE collapses qualified (based on AAF and annotation) rare coding variants by gene and assigning each individual’s genotype as the maximum of their alternate alleles (2 for individuals who are homozygous alternate carriers for a qualified variant; 1 for heterozygous carriers of any qualified variant; and 0 for individuals who are homozygous reference for all qualifying variants). Burden tests were performed on these genotypes whereby effect sizes represent the per-allele effect of carrying a qualified variant. pLoFs comprised frameshift, stop gain, splice-donor or splice-acceptor variants. Missense variants were evaluated using six in silico predictors: ESM-1v62, SIFT63, PolyPhen2+HumDiv64, PolyPhen2+HumVar64, MutationTaster65 and LRT66. Variants were selected for inclusion in burden tests based on AAF, status as pLoF and—for missense variants—how many algorithms predicted the variant to be deleterious. Missense variants were classified as deleterious if predicted pathogenic by ESM-1v (using a threshold of 0.78) or all five other algorithms; and possibly deleterious if predicted to be not pathogenic by ESM-1v but predicted to be pathogenic by 1–4 other algorithms. Burden tests were then performed using every combination of five different AAF thresholds (singleton (4.17 × 10−7), 0.0001, 0.001, 0.005 and 0.01) and 7 different combinations of variant class: (1) pLoF; (2) pLoF + deleterious missense + stop lost + start lost; (3) pLoF + at least possibly deleterious missense + stop lost + start lost; (4) pLoF + all missense + stop lost + start lost; (5) deleterious missense + stop lost + start lost; (6) at least possibly deleterious missense + stop lost + start lost; and (7) all missense + stop lost + start lost—yielding 35 burden tests.

To properly adjust for multiple correlated tests across a large number of genes, we used gene-P10, an omnibus gene-based test framework that uses ACAT67 to combine P values from four complementary rare variant gene-based tests—BURDEN-ACAT10, Sparse Burden Association Test (SBAT)10, SKAT-O12 and ACAT-V11—into a single P value per gene (Supplementary Fig. 1) as previously described10. After identifying genes associated through gene-P, we quantified the number of significant associations from these four underlying tests (Extended Data Fig. 1a). We classified genes into two groups: burden-associated (P < 1.75 × 10−9 in BURDEN-ACAT or SBAT) or non-burden-associated (P < 1.75 × 10−9 in ACAT-V or SKAT-O and P ≥ 1.75 × 10−9 in both BURDEN-ACAT and SBAT).

We used a previously described method for genetic ancestry assignment49. In brief, using a diverse reference panel comprising 11,354 individuals from 95 populations, we calculated the proportion of haplotype sharing of each sample and assigned them to one of six genetic ancestries (African (AFR), Indigenous American (AMR), East Asian (EAS), European (EUR), Middle Eastern (MEA), and South Asian (SAS)) if they had greater than 50% of that genetic ancestry; those with no majority ancestry were assigned to an ‘OTH’ group. We recognize that these commonly used labels oversimplify an individual’s genetic, cultural and environmental diversity, and use them only out of convenience to properly adjust association tests for genetic ancestry.

In both imputed and sequenced data, we assigned each variant a single AAF representing the maximum AAF across six fine-scale ancestries. On the basis of the fine-scale ancestry maximum AAF, we classified variants as common (AAF ≥ 1%; n = 19,514,636 in imputed data with INFO ≥ 0.3 in all discovery cohorts) or rare (AAF < 1%; n = 44,600,231 in exome data). When a variant was rare (<1%) in the exome, but common in the imputed data (≥1%), we classified them as common (n = 335,522). Singletons in the discovery analysis were defined as those with only a single AAC for variants on chromosomes 1–22 and the X chromosome’s PAR, and a single hemizygous or heterozygous genotype in the X chromosome’s non-PAR. REGENIE used these AAFs and singleton definitions when writing burden test genotypes and running the gene-based tests (options --aaf-file and --set-singleton). For replication analyses, we used the same AAFs as in the discovery analysis but included an additional 12,777,247 unique variants in the replication data. Singletons in the replication comprised variants that were either (1) a singleton in the discovery or (2) a singleton in the replication that was absent from the discovery.

We built a common variant PGS using PLINK56 from 3,039 common (Supplementary Table 8) variants from two sources: first, 3,034 conditionally independent common variants identified through iterative conditional meta-analysis (Methods and Supplementary Table 2) and second, five common variants identified from the exome and not tested with imputed data. The common variant PGS was computed as a weighted sum of alternate alleles, where the magnitude and direction of the weight was based on the estimated variant effect size (in s.d. units) from a joint model in the discovery (n = 826,066; Supplementary Table 1) of all 3,039 variants (Supplementary Table 8) and the same covariates (Supplementary Table 24) used in the association analyses (Supplementary Note).

We constructed four versions of rare variant PGS (rvPGS), based on the 179 genes with a significant gene-P and either a significant BURDEN-ACAT or SBAT test, weighted using s.d. effect sizes: (1) rvPGSSNP: all 77 significant (P < 1.75 × 10−9, AAC ≥ 10) rare variants in these genes (after LD clumping for variants with R2 < 0.2), weighted by the single-variant effect size; (2) rvPGSpLoF: all 179 pLoF (AAF < 1%, AAC ≥ 10) burden associations weighted by effect size; (3) rvPGSmissense: all 179 deleterious missense (AAF < 1%, AAC ≥ 10) burden associations weighted by effect size; and (4) rvPGSsynonymous: all 179 synonymous (AAF < 1%, AAC ≥ 10) burden associations weighted by effect size. To assess whether height-associated genes identified through rare variant burden testing were enriched for biologically relevant annotations—OMIM disease genes (all and stature/skeletal growth-related, curated in ref. 2), constrained genes and nearest gene to a GWAS signal—we used two-sided Fisher’s exact tests to test for enrichment; when odds ratios were infinite due to zero count cells, we applied a continuity correction of +1 to all cells.
