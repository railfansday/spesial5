Who actually arrived: telling a reader, a crawler, and your own robot apart in one event table

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

You ship a small page. You add a counter. A week later the counter says thirteen visitors, and you start making decisions from it — this copy works, that aggregator sends traffic, the landing page is worth improving.

Then you look at the rows and find that four of the thirteen were not people at all. Two were your own deploy check. One was a crawler. One was a link-preview bot from a chat app, fetching your title so a stranger could see a card. You did not lose the visitors; you never had them. The number was never a count of people. It was a count of requests, and requests are made by programs.

This article is about the small event table that fixes that, the classification ladder that makes its numbers defensible, and the specific ways those numbers lie even after you build it properly. It is written for the case most solo builders are actually in: no analytics vendor, no consent banner, one table you control, and a need to answer "did anyone come, and did they do anything" without fooling yourself.

Start with the honest description of the data. Each row records that something fetched a URL at a time, with a referrer it claimed, from an address your server saw, with a user agent it volunteered. Everything about a person is inference on top of that.

So the table should hold what you can defend, and nothing that pretends to know more:

Do not delete your own traffic; flag it. Internal rows are the audit trail that your deploy, your check script and your monitoring all still work. Delete them and the next time the counter reads zero you cannot tell whether the page is fine or the instrumentation died. Keep the row, exclude it by is_internal = 0 in every query, and require a reason string so a later you can disagree with the classification.

The temptation is a single boolean, is_bot. That boolean will be wrong in both directions: it will mark a curious reader who pastes a link into a terminal as a robot, and it will let a headless browser through because it had no user agent at all.

The reason strings are the part that pays off months later. When you reclassify, you reclassify by reason, not by guessing which rows were which:

Every deploy you open the page to see whether the stylesheet loaded. Every check script you will write over the next month (see the companion piece on schedules and checks) touches the same route. Left alone, that is a steady drip of "visitors" that scales with how much you work — which is precisely backwards, because the busiest weeks look like the most successful ones.

The failure mode to plan against: an internal exclusion that quietly catches real people. That happens when the rule is broad — "user agent contains curl" — and someone genuinely arrives from a terminal. The reason string is what saves you: when the totals look wrong, sort by reason and read the rows.

Reverse DNS is not the answer either — it is slow, frequently absent, and still spoofable in the direction that matters. If you need proof, verify that a claimed crawler address belongs to the crawler's published ranges, and cache the result. Otherwise accept that you are estimating, and keep the reason string so the estimate is auditable.

There is one more crawler class worth its own bucket because it confuses funnel math: the preview fetcher. When someone shares your link in a chat app, the app fetches the page to render a card. That request is a real request, sometimes from a real person's account, and it produces a view with no click, no scroll, and no second request. If your page's job is to get someone to press a button, a preview fetch is a false positive on the first funnel step, and it arrives exactly when you have shared the link somewhere — which is when you are most eager to read the numbers.

Two habits make "nothing happened" a claim instead of a guess.

Measure twice: at the start and the end of the work. A job that reads the table at 06:54 and reports "zero new" is making a statement about a window it never defined. Read again at 07:01, and now the statement is: from row 687 to row 687, over the interval between two timestamps. Nine quiet minutes. That is a fact, and it is falsifiable.

This is not pedantry. Events land between runs. A visitor who arrives two minutes after one run's last read and before the next run's scheduled time is invisible to both the "no traffic" report and the "we had traffic" report, and you will spend an afternoon trying to explain why a channel appears to do nothing while the totals keep growing:

Watermark by id, not by timestamp. WHERE created_at > last_seen skips rows that share a second with the watermark — and in a burst, several rows do. Ids from a monotonically increasing sequence give you an exact, tie-free boundary. If you do not have one, take the timestamp as a boundary and the max id at that timestamp.

Store UTC; display local. One field, one timezone, converted at the edge. The alternative is a database whose meaning depends on which report you are reading, and a bug you will find at the worst possible hour.

You will have days that look like this: thirteen view rows, two click rows. The arithmetic that leaps to mind is 15.4%, and it is worse than useless — it invites a decision about copy from a sample where one more click would move the number eight points.

The rule I use: report counts until n is large enough to make a rate meaningful, and always name the denominator. "Two clicks from thirteen arrivals, over 24 hours, two of the arrivals on different days" is honest. "15% click-through" implies a stability the data does not have.

The same caution applies to deduplication. Counting rows counts events; counting distinct ip_hash counts something like sessions. They answer different questions, and the gap between them is information: thirteen rows from thirteen hashes is thirteen separate arrivals; thirteen rows from three hashes is one enthusiast and a refresh button. Choose deliberately, and print both.

The referrer header is the only free signal that says how someone arrived, and browsers strip it constantly: cross-origin navigation without a referrer policy, links opened from apps, and anything typed or pasted. So a blank referrer is not "direct traffic". It is "unknown", and labelling it "direct" in a report is how people conclude that their search traffic is worthless.

And when a path has views but the next step in the funnel has never a single row from any referrer, that is a finding of its own — though not the finding you want. It can be a copy problem (nobody wanted to press it) or an instrumentation problem (the button's event never fires). The table alone cannot tell you which; you find out by being one of the arrivals and checking that your own click produced a row.

That check — do my own events appear, and with the shape I expect — is worth running after every change to the page. An event table nobody has verified against their own behaviour is a table of wishes.

The one addition that pays for itself: the same classification on the downstream event as on the arrival, so the funnel's steps are computed over the same population. If arrivals exclude robots and clicks do not, your conversion rate silently includes robots clicking.

Aggregating early feels tidy and is a one-way door. Daily rollups answer "how many on Tuesday" and cannot answer "did the arrivals from that listing click", which is the question you will actually have in three weeks.

Keep raw rows long enough to cover the questions you can currently imagine asking — a few months for a personal project — then roll up to daily counts by path, event, referrer class, plus the distinct-person count. Aggregate by reason as well as flag, so a later reclassification does not require the raw rows you just deleted.

None of this makes the table correct. It makes it auditable — which is the only property that matters, because the moment you trust a number you cannot explain, you have stopped measuring and started guessing with extra steps.
