The world's biggest investors are discovering Europe's holiday parks, where valuable properties don't always mean profitable businesses.

For many Americans, holiday parks are an unfamiliar corner of the travel industry. Across Europe, however, millions of families spend their vacations in forest cottages, seaside villages, and campsites packed with swimming pools, restaurants, and entertainment. Most drive just a few hours from home, returning to the same spots year after year.

These properties sit somewhere between hotels, vacation rentals, and full-service resorts. Some of the companies that run them have grown into massive accommodation operators while staying largely under the radar outside Europe.

Take Pierre & Vacances–Center Parcs. The French group runs forest resorts, seaside apartments, city aparthotels, and campsites across roughly 330 European locations. It manages more than 45,000 units, hosts nearly 8 million guests a year, and brings in about €2 billion ($2.2 billion) in annual revenue.

European holiday parks have become one of the most actively traded accommodation assets in travel, and two deals announced within months of each other show why. Mubadala Capital has agreed to buy Pierre & Vacances–Center Parcs for roughly €900 million ($1 billion) in ordinary equity, with shareholders holding 80.13% already backing the offer and regulatory sign-off still pending. Separately, the British and Irish Center Parcs business — a different company running six villages — is moving into a Brookfield-managed investment vehicle under binding agreements announced October 22, in a recapitalization reportedly valuing it at about £4.5 billion ($6 billion). The logic behind the buying is the local travel market: EU residents took 1.2 billion trips in 2025, 70% of them domestic, and campsites and caravan parks logged 413 million guest nights, up 28.5% since 2015, outpacing hotels and short-term rentals. New supply is hard to add, with tighter zoning and tougher financing, so existing parks with permits, infrastructure, repeat customers, and land command a premium. Pierre & Vacances shows the other side of the trade. Roughly 85% of its holiday-residence portfolio was leased as of September 2025, with €3.2 billion ($3.6 billion) in discounted lease liabilities and about €474 million ($530 million) in rent paid in fiscal 2025 — fixed payments that don't move when demand softens. Management plans to grow beyond 50,000 units by 2030, with 60% of new additions from asset-light management and franchise contracts, and targets adjusted EBITDA of €270 million ($302 million) by 2030, roughly 46% above its fiscal 2026 forecast.
