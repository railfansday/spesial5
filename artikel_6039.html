American Airlines’ Lag Linked To Flyers Unphased By Delta & United’s High Fares, Analyst Says

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

American Airlines is struggling to match the financial performance of its two biggest domestic rivals, and passengers' willingness to pay higher fares to fly Delta Air Lines and United Airlines may be a major factor. According to Dan McKenzie, an analyst at Seaport Research Partners, both carriers command considerably higher fares than American on comparable flights, according to The New York Times.

McKenzie estimates that American needs approximately $4.5 billion in additional annual revenue to match its competitors' profit margins. With surging jet fuel costs pressuring airline profitability, the ability to command higher fares without significantly compromising demand has become increasingly important. American's latest disappointing financial results illustrate the scale of that challenge.

McKenzie's analysis found that Delta commands fares that are 12% above the industry average on similar flights, compared with 9% at United and just 3% at American. To illustrate, on a flight with an industry-average fare of $500, Delta could command $560 and United $545 without impacting demand, compared with $515 at American. Across millions of passengers, those differences quickly accumulate.

Interestingly, American achieved the strongest passenger yield growth of the three carriers during the third quarter of 2026. However, an analysis of their respective financial results reveals that American also experienced the largest decline in load factor. Consequently, its passenger revenue per available seat mile (PRASM) increased more slowly than at either Delta or United.

While American's faster capacity growth also contributed to its declining load factor, the results partly reflect a deliberate commercial strategy. The airline historically prioritized filling its aircraft, but is increasingly focused on improving yields. Speaking during American's third-quarter earnings call, Chief Commercial Officer Nat Pieper explained the trade-off.

"We have prioritized yield over load factor, proactively securing meaningfully higher yields in exchange for slightly lower loads."

Delta and United have spent years differentiating their onboard products, recognizing the growing demand for premium travel. That strategy is delivering substantial financial returns. In the third quarter, United's premium and loyalty revenues both rose 17% year over year. Meanwhile, Delta's premium ticket revenue actually surpassed main cabin ticket revenue, with the latter accounting for less than 40% of its adjusted operating revenue.

Nowhere is this more evident than with corporate travelers, whose purchasing decisions are often influenced more by schedule convenience, operational reliability, and loyalty benefits than by fares. Delta topped the Business Travel News' 2026 Airline Survey for the 16th consecutive year, outperforming its competitors in eight of the 11 categories assessed. Despite substantial improvements, American remained the lowest-ranked qualifying airline.

Network composition is another important differentiator. Delta and United have greater exposure to affluent coastal markets, with both offering nearly twice as many departing seats from the most affluent, New York, as American. This also underscores the commercial significance of American's intensifying battle with United at Chicago O'Hare, where higher frequency and connectivity could help it secure more lucrative corporate travel contracts.

American Airlines is abandoning its legal challenge over Chicago's gate redistribution, but its battle with United is far from over.

American is pursuing a comprehensive transformation of its passenger experience, reversing several previous management decisions. These include reintroducing narrowbody seatback screens and substantially expanding premium seating. However, several of its most significant product enhancements will take years to implement across the fleet, delaying their full commercial impact.

Increase from 25% to approximately 40% of seats

800+ narrowbodies; new deliveries from 2028

1,000+ mainline aircraft; rollout from 2027

Boeing 787-9s, Airbus A321XLRs and 777 retrofits

All 20 aircraft by 2027; premium seating increasing from 38% to 44%

There are encouraging signs that American's strategy is gaining traction. Its premium revenue increased 22.9% year-on-year in the third quarter, while managed corporate revenue increased 27.6%, marking its sixth consecutive quarter of double-digit growth. American's corporate travel satisfaction score has also improved this year, suggesting its efforts to rebuild relationships are paying dividends.

However, American is pursuing a moving target. While it prepares to begin installing Starlink in 2027, United already offers the service on more than half of its daily flights. And while Delta offers its personalized Delta Sync seatback entertainment experience on more than 900 aircraft, American will only begin its seatback screen rollout from 2028. So, as its rivals continue to reinvest profits in product differentiation, American's ultimate challenge will be persuading passengers that its flights are equally worth paying more for.
