Even in destinations that are overall very safe, petty theft is a risk. For instance, many European cities are pickpocketing hotspots, and crowded tourist attractions like Christmas markets make ideal hunting grounds for thieves.

Thankfully, there are steps you can take to protect yourself. I’ve gathered real-life anecdotes from my fellow travelers at TPG so you know what to look for, and our top tips to help you protect yourself.

The first and easiest precaution you can take is to minimize your loss if you do get robbed.

That means leaving your flashy jewelry or designer-brand luggage at home, as these can often attract thieves. And be sure to make copies of crucial documents and store them in a safe digital place, where you can easily access them if your hard copies get lost.

What about travel insurance? Some policies may cover theft, so check the coverage offered by your credit card. You may want to consider buying additional coverage for particularly costly items.

This one can be tricky, but do your best not to advertise your status as a tourist. In Europe, for instance, dressing in neutral clothing can help you blend in with the locals (meanwhile, bright colors and American sports apparel scream “foreigner”).

Check a map and figure out where you’re going before leaving your hotel to avoid wandering around looking clueless. Familiarize yourself with your destination’s public transport system; thieves often prowl transportation hubs in search of tourists who are out of their element.

The more distracted you are, the easier it is to get separated you from your valuables. Instead of getting absorbed in your phone, keep your gaze up and walk with purpose. Plus, this will help you soak in your surroundings and make the most of your trip.

Public transportation is a pickpocket’s playground, especially lines that cover tourist draws like Rome’s notorious 64 bus and Lisbon’s iconic yellow Tram 28.

Senior editor Lyndsey Matthews says, “When I was 11, I was in London and saw two teens try to pickpocket my grandma on the way out of the Tube.” Thankfully, all they got was some dirty tissues and seashells.

Keep your bags close and in sight while riding public transport. If you’re carrying a daypack, turn it around and carry it on your chest with the straps looped around your shoulders. Be particularly cautious at train, tram and subway stops, where thieves can dash on, grab your suitcase and bolt before the doors shut again.

Thieves often work together, with one creating a spectacle or disturbance — a spilled drink, an argument, a stumble on the metro — while the other preys on distracted bystanders.

Senior editor Colin Hogan once lost his wallet to a pickpocket in Montreal: “She was working alongside a street performer, and as the performer would gather people into a circle around him to view the performance, she would come behind and steal things from bags or pockets while they were distracted.”

If something happens, resist the urge to stare. Stay focused on yourself and your belongings instead.

You’ve probably heard not to leave your wallet in your back pocket, where it will be easy pickings. But skilled thieves can navigate front pockets with ease, or snatch purses and disappear into the crowd before you can blink.

Lead writer Katie Genter has a relative who got robbed on public transportation in Paris: “She was carrying one of those huge purse bags without a zipper on top,” she says, which would be child’s play for most pickpockets.

To reduce your risk, stick to bags that zip closed, and consider clipping the zippers together for an added layer of protection. Opt for a crossbody bag or fanny pack, which is harder to snatch than a shoulder bag, or a money belt you can keep under your clothes.

As travelers have gotten smarter, so have criminals.

One popular tactic is to slice through the strap of a crossbody bag, then make off with it. Director of content strategy Carly Helfand says, “I’ve had my bag sliced from the bottom on the Prague metro and the phone stolen out of it!”

Consider investing in a theft-resistant bag made of tougher material that’s harder for thieves to cut into. I’m a big fan of this one, which also has locking zippers, a cut-resistant strap and RFID-blocking credit card slots.

Remember, you don’t need to make your bag impenetrable. Simply making it harder to access is often enough to convince opportunistic thieves to move on in search of easier targets.

It goes without saying to not leave anything valuable at your seat while you run to the restroom or grab napkins from the bar. But thieves may be bold enough to snatch items from right in front of you, or even out of your hands.

Director of UX Research Mindy Pastrovich says that in Cape Town, “my friend was talking to a guy who asked for her number, and when she took out her phone to give it to him, he stole it and ran away.”

Stay aware of your surroundings. Keep a firm grip on your phone or camera when you’re using it (a wrist strap or lanyard can help), and tuck them out of sight when you’re not. If you sit down in any public place, like a hotel lobby or train car, loop your bag strap around your chair leg or clip your suitcase to the luggage rack.

It can be tempting to keep all your valuables on your person, where you can keep track of them constantly. But even the most vigilant travelers can fall prey to clever thieves.

TPG assistant marketing manager Paige Stepanian has a friend who got pickpocketed on the Barcelona metro. “She had her phone in a fanny pack that was at the front of her, facing into a group of friends. It happened so fast, by the time we realized her phone was gone the person who took it was already off the train!”

In fact, your items are generally safer in your hotel room than your day pack. Keep what you need in a money belt or theft-resistant crossbody purse, and leave the rest in your room. It’s always a good idea to leave valuables out of sight, or better yet, lock them in the hotel safe or your suitcase.

Scams and confidence games can take unexpected forms. I recently heard of a couple friends of a friend who, while stopped in an airport, were approached by a man offering to give them directions. Meanwhile, his accomplice was walking off with the travelers’ luggage.

Other scams may involve someone putting jewelry or accessories on you without your permission or handing you a drink you didn’t ask for — then demanding payment later. Be wary of anyone who is overly friendly, and set boundaries politely but firmly.

On a recent trip to Zanzibar, I couldn’t even take a casual stroll on the beach without at least one person trying to give me a tide pool tour — which I’m sure he would have charged handsomely for, had I not resolutely walked off and left him in the shallows.

If you’re traveling by car, never leave valuables in view when you park, even if you think you’ll be right back.

My friend once had her purse stolen from a rental car in Hawaii while she was stopped at a scenic overlook for a quick photo. Not only did she lose all her money, she also had a heck of a time getting home without an ID.

In areas where break-ins are common, it’s a good idea to empty the car and leave the doors unlocked any time you leave. That way, if a thief decides to take a look inside, you won’t have to deal with the cost and headache of repairing a broken window.

When senior content strategist Gabrielle Bernardini was in Cabo, Mexico, a gas station attendant asked her for her credit card. “I gave it to him, and he handed it to someone next to him, who then ran into the convenience store. Next, I got an alert from Amex that about $4,000 was charged to my card.”

Thankfully, she contacted Amex right away and was able to get the fraudulent charge wiped in about a week.

Despite all the horror stories I’ve mentioned, theft is still relatively uncommon. I’ve been to Europe more times than I can count and never had anything stolen (knock on wood).

Following the tips above won’t guarantee you don’t fall victim to petty theft, but it will significantly slash your risk.
