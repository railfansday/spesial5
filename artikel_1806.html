Mauricio Pochettino at Manchester United: Will Things Finally Change at Old Trafford?

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

When Mauricio Pochettino walks into Anfield for his first match as Manchester United manager, he will immediately be confronted by the scale of the challenge ahead. There are easier ways to begin a reign than an away trip to Liverpool, especially for a club that has spent much of the past decade searching for stability and finding very little of it.

The symbolism is obvious. Liverpool have spent recent years building coherent teams, developing clear identities and competing regularly for major trophies, even if they did hit a few bumps on the road themselves. Manchester United have spent the same period cycling through managers, rebuilding projects and tactical resets. Pochettino’s appointment represents another attempt to break that pattern, but it would be a mistake to believe the Argentine’s task begins and ends with improving results.

His predecessors discovered that managing the team is only part of the job. The harder challenge is changing the culture and structure of a club that has repeatedly undermined its own attempts at renewal.

Pochettino was right to push back against the idea that Manchester United’s problems can be reduced to distance covered or sprint numbers.

The statistics are alarming. United have been outrun in most of their league matches and rank near the bottom of the division in several physical metrics. Yet simply demanding greater effort is unlikely to transform a struggling team overnight.

What United have lacked for years is collective purpose. Too often the team has looked like a group of talented individuals operating with different interpretations of the same game plan. When Pochettino talks about players running together and understanding their roles, he is really describing something more fundamental than fitness. He is talking about creating a collective identity.

That was one of the defining strengths of his best Tottenham side. They were physically outstanding, but their success came from organisation and belief as much as athleticism. Every player understood what was expected of him, and the team functioned as a coherent unit.

Manchester United have not consistently looked like that under any manager for a very long time.

The obvious counterargument is that other highly regarded coaches have arrived at Old Trafford with similarly ambitious ideas.

Erik ten Hag was respected throughout Europe. Ruben Amorim arrived with a reputation as one of the continent’s brightest tactical minds. Others before them possessed impressive credentials too.

What makes Pochettino different is the nature of his previous achievements.

Unlike some elite managers, he has rarely inherited finished products. His reputation was built on developing teams, improving players and creating environments where younger footballers could thrive. His Tottenham side became one of Europe’s best teams despite operating with fewer resources than many of their rivals, while his spell at Chelsea demonstrated an ability to stabilise a chaotic dressing room and extract improvement from a talented but unbalanced squad.

That experience feels particularly relevant at Manchester United. This is not a club requiring minor adjustments; it is a club still searching for a clear football identity. Pochettino has spent much of his career building exactly that.

Even the best coaches struggle when recruitment and management pull in different directions, and that, among other things is where Manchester United have repeatedly run into trouble.

Successive managers have inherited squads constructed for different styles of football. Players signed for one coach have remained when another arrived with entirely different ideas. The result has been an increasingly awkward collection of footballers who often appear unsuited to the system being asked of them.

If Pochettino is to succeed, that cycle cannot continue.

The club’s hierarchy must show the discipline to recruit according to a long-term strategy rather than chasing short-term fixes. That requires difficult decisions, particularly regarding players who may still carry reputations but no longer fit the demands of modern elite football.

Tottenham’s rise under Pochettino was possible because the squad gradually became an extension of the manager’s ideas. Manchester United have not reached that point under any coach since Sir Alex Ferguson.

Without alignment between the manager and recruitment department, even the strongest project stalls, and eventually fails.

Perhaps the greatest danger is expecting immediate transformation.

The temptation after years of disappointment will be to judge Pochettino’s reign through the lens of short-term results. That is understandable, especially when the club sits far below the standards many expect from them.

Yet rebuilding rarely follows a straight line. Pochettino’s first Tottenham team finished fifth before progressing into genuine title challengers, and Champions League finalists. Many of the players who later became stars required time and patience. The same principle is likely to apply at Old Trafford.

His opening fixture at Liverpool serves as a reminder of how much work remains to be done. Whatever happens at Anfield, it should not define the project. The more important question is whether United begin to look like a team with direction.

For all the doom that surrounds Manchester United, the club remains one of football’s great institutions. The fanbase remains enormous, the resources remain substantial, and the attraction of the badge has not disappeared yet.

Pochettino himself acknowledged that reality when discussing the scale of the support he encounters around the world. That is why the job continues to draw elite managerial candidates despite the obvious risks. The potential rewards remain extraordinary. The challenge is converting potential into reality.

The honest answer is yes, but only under conditions that have often been absent at Manchester United.

Pochettino has the coaching pedigree. He has the experience of handling demanding environments. He has a proven track record of improving players and building teams. Whether that is enough depends largely on whether the club has learned from the mistakes that consumed previous managers.

If United continue changing direction every couple of years, no coach will succeed. If they provide Pochettino with time, authority and recruitment aligned to a coherent football vision, then he may have a better chance than most of restoring the club to relevance at the top of the Premier League.

His first game – away to Liverpool – is obviously a daunting introduction to the task ahead. In many ways, it will be a good indicator of where Manchester United currently stand. They are beginning another chapter while measuring themselves against a rival that has spent years getting the fundamentals right.

Pochettino’s challenge is not simply to win matches. It is to ensure that, a few years from now, Manchester United are finally discussing how to compete for titles again rather than debating the future of yet another manager.
