One Man Built Linux in a Bedroom. Another Blew Up Three Rockets. Both Are My Idols. Who's Yours? 🚀🐧

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

In 1991, a 21-year-old student in Helsinki posted a message online saying he was building a free operating system.

He called it "just a hobby" and said it wouldn't be "big and professional."

Today, Linux runs most of the world's servers, and the Android phone in your pocket is built on its kernel.

In 2008, a company was one launch away from going bankrupt. Its first three rockets had all failed. The founder put what was left of the money into a fourth attempt.

That founder was Elon Musk, and the company was SpaceX.

I'm a builder and developer shipping automated systems, web apps, and crawling tools like crawl.webdesignbe.com from Lagos—and these are my two idols. 👇

🐧 1. Linus Torvalds: Build the Thing Nobody Else Will

He didn't set out to change the world. He wanted a system that worked, so he built one.

Then, in 2005, the tool the Linux kernel team relied on, BitKeeper, withdrew its free license.

He started writing his own version control system on April 3, 2005, and it became usable within days.

Solve your own problem first. Other people might have the same problem—and often they'll use what you build.

Automate and ship lean. You don't need a massive team or bloated budget to build real, working infrastructure.

Be direct. Clarity is a form of respect, especially when shipping fast.

🚀 2. Elon Musk: Failing Is Not the End. Quitting Is.

A private company reaching orbit sounded absurd when SpaceX started.

Falcon 1 failed three times between 2006 and 2008.

The fourth flight, on September 28, 2008, finally made it to orbit.

Then, on December 21, 2015, a Falcon 9 first stage flew back and landed upright.

Reusable rockets went from an ambitious engineering goal to a reality.

Think big enough that others call it impossible. Whether it's launching rockets or building global systems from scratch, ambition sets the ceiling.

Treat failure as data, not as a verdict. Every broken build, rate limit, or failed test tells you what to fix next.

Don't stop after the first three failures. Persistence separates people who experiment from people who ship.

At first glance, they look like opposites.

Aspect Linus Torvalds Elon Musk Style Quiet, code-first Loud, vision-first Medium Software Hardware Superpower Simplicity and shipping Ambition and persistence

But there's one thing they have in common:

Both built the thing instead of waiting for permission.

Linus didn't wait for a better operating system. Elon didn't wait for a better rocket company.

Building automated web platforms, crawling pipelines (crawl.webdesignbe.com), and full-stack tools means I'm constantly trying to take a bit from each:

Linus's "just build it—and keep it simple."

Both men have public controversies and loud critics. I'm not saying either is perfect.

I admire their work and certain aspects of their mindset, not everything they've ever said or done.

I think that's a healthier way to have an idol: learn from someone's achievements without blindly following everything they do.
