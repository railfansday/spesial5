Fed up of Steam's lack of action regarding AI slop? Maybe you should take a look at the 'Games Without AI' event, instead

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Yesterday, a new Steam event went live that claims all games featured in it have avoided AI entirely during development. The event, called 'Games Without AI', has been set up by a third party rather than Valve - by Dev Land Marketing, All Ages Of Geek, and Dark Cocooning - to "support real creators" and runs from 25th November to 6th December.

"Behind every game, there are people, ideas, passion, and countless hours of creative work," said a mission statement. "We've carefully curated a selection of independent games that celebrate human creativity, craftsmanship, and collaboration, all made without generative AI. But Games Without AI is more than a festival. It's also about bringing developers, players, and creators together to build a community where we share knowledge, support one another, and grow together."

There are plenty of games featured in the event, and not just ones tied to the organisers, as the cynical-minded amongst you may expect. Ones that jump out to my eye are: Pidgeon Pocket, a 'cozy incremental' with pixel art and a flock of adorable pigeons that you need to upgrade; Bottled by Bears, which tasks you with collecting and discovering rare bees, bottling honey jars, and expanding your farm; and Arctic Awakening, which sort-of looks like Pacific Drive meets Subnautica, in the Arctic.

The event was posted to Reddit where commenters immidiately began accusing certain games of having used AI in their development - often without proof. One user, however, who claims to have been a developer trying to submit a game for inclusion in the event, offered some insight on how the selection process worked.

"As one of participants I can assure you that the qualification process was restrictive[ly] hard to bypass," they wrote. "We were all required to provide proofs of the game being made without AI and share as much of the process as possible. Then it was actually voted by community, which games are convicing enough so the whole process was intended to be as objective as possible. It's an event meant for developers who take pride in their games being made by humans so if any participant attends with ill intend of showing that AI generated game can get through... That would be very sad and shameful. From what I've seen, none of the selected games can be reasonable suspected of that. I also know some other participants personally and they are genuine zero-AI devs."

It's refreshing to see curation turned towards explicitly anti-AI games: between major publishers chasing the trend and smaller indie studios being caught out using the dubious tech, it's nice to see a library of games that promises that no AI was used during development. Given that Valve itself recently claimed it was up to developers themselves to ensure their games couldn't be ripped off by cheap AI tools, I'm happy to see an initiative rallying against the normalcy of all this.

The most recent Steam Next Fest had more than double the number of demos than last year, but that stat isn't what it seems on the surface: roughly 17 percent of those games listed the use of generative AI, but it seemed that many more games did not disclose the use of AI in their development. In June's event, more than a thousand games in Steam Next Fest featured a generative AI disclosure of some kind, which made up roughly 20 percent of all featured games.

Over the past 18 months or so, we've seen the issue of generative AI in games on Steam evolve quite a lot. AI tools have been used to clone games, resulting in a troubling trend of imitation games taking the spotlight away from real indie devs. Whilst Steam, as a storefront, has been steadfast in requiring games to declare their use of generative AI during development, there are loopholes: devs that claim they only use AI tools for "efficiency gains" can appear on the store without any declaration.

At the other end of the spectrum is Epic Games' Tim Sweeney, who last year criticised Valve's requirement to disclose generative AI, saying, "Steam and all digital marketplaces need to drop the 'Made with AI' label. It doesn't matter any more." The swell of users rejecting AI in games, though, seems to run counter to that argument. Recently, Big Walk developer House House revealed it used generative AI "sparingly" during the development of the co-op darling, and received a lot of backlash online.
