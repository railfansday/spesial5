The AI-assisted proof is 250,000 pages long, and mathematicians are trying to understand what it says.

The Riemann Hypothesis is one of math’s oldest and most famous unsolved problems. It was originally put forward as a problem in 1859 by Bernhard Riemann, and professional and amateur mathematicians alike have made thousands of attempts to solve it. Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge has apparently solved the problem with the help of AI, showing that in the battle of humans versus AI, the real winner might be both of them working together.

Alpöge made the announcement on Friday in a four-page document (PDF) on his personal web page.

“I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis,” Alpöge said in the announcement. “In fact, the method easily extends much further, resolving the so-called Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and I expect, with more time, the Extended Riemann Hypothesis too.”

The actual hypothesis itself isn’t the biggest deal since all it does is make it easier to see the distribution of prime numbers. However, math didn’t come to a screeching halt just because the hypothesis hadn’t been proven. There are hundreds of other math theorems along with algorithms and cryptographic encryption methods that operate based on the assumption that the Riemann Hypothesis is true.

The Riemann Hypothesis is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s seven Millennium Problems for this very reason. If Alpöge really did solve the Riemann Hypothesis with Claude’s help, it would remove a metaphorical logjam that’s been gumming up the works for over a century and a half. Most of those hundreds of math theorems would be proven as true overnight, and all of those algorithms and encryption methods would be validated. That would make solving the Riemann Hypothesis one of the greatest mathematical breakthroughs in history.

“It is certainly an exciting time,” Paul Milewski, head of the department of mathematics at Penn State, said in an email to CNET. “The Riemann Hypothesis (or any big theorem) is often not the ‘goal’ of mathematics. They are set up as grand challenges that require the development of new tools, new results along the way and new understanding. It is the ‘voyage’ and not the destination that matters the most.”

As such, the mathematics community wasted no time testing the proof to see whether it was true. Two groups — Lean Focused Research Organization and the Mathlib Initiative — independently verified the proof, with many other researchers running their own tests and coming to the same conclusion.

“We have full confidence that Alpöge’s proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct, and we congratulate him on the achievement,” the two groups said in a joint statement on Saturday.

This is certainly an exciting discovery, but it’s not without its obstacles. Alpöge’s proof was a staggering 250,000 pages of some of the most complex math ever written by a human or a machine. Alpöge provided 17 million lines of Lean code, a programming language specifically made for writing and checking proofs, and that’s how researchers were able to check the work and show it to be true.

The problem is that the average novel is about 300 pages, and that means that Alpöge’s AI-assisted proof is approximately 833 novels’ worth of complex math. For reference, the longest work ever produced is World-2023, which holds the Guinness World Record at 100,100 pages. That book is 19 feet tall, and Alpöge’s proof is two and a half times longer.

Despite the logic being verified as true through Lean code, Alpöge still has a major problem. So far, none of the mathematicians or researchers who have attempted to read it have been able to understand what it says.

“I do not understand, and I suspect no human completely understands,” Jon Keating, professor of mathematical physics at the University of Oxford, said in a Numberphile YouTube video. “When I look at that, I’m completely in awe and in wonderment.”

For the problem to be fully solved, the proof has to be read, analyzed and simplified so that it can be translated into readable mathematics, which Alpöge says in his announcement that he’s in the process of doing. All of this has to be done so that Alpöge can publish it in a peer-reviewed journal in such a way that his peers can actually read it. It is only after the mathematics community scrutinizes and accepts Alpöge’s proof that it is considered fully solved.

Aside from being nigh unreadable to humans, Alpöge has otherwise avoided the kinds of drama that other AI-assisted discoveries have faced in 2026.

OpenAI had to backtrack on its proof of the Navier-Stokes problem, which is another Millennium Problem, after the company couldn’t rule out that the AI models it used had access to data from other researchers. Somewhat ironically, one of those mathematicians whose work OpenAI may have  — knowingly or unknowingly — borrowed from was Levent Alpöge, as he and others were pretty close to solving the problem on their own.

Similarly, Claude was credited with finding a new novel enzyme that humans had never seen before. The problem is that computational biologist Mario Rodríguez Mestre had seen this enzyme before and was using Claude to study it further, so it’s uncertain if Claude actually found it independently or used data from Mestre’s research to find it.

The heavy assistance from AI has been met with mostly positive reactions, with most researchers agreeing that the AI usage is justified provided that the proof is proven to be true through peer review. Since no one was particularly close to solving the Riemann Hypothesis, there haven’t been any accusations of Alpöge or Claude stealing work like those prior examples. By and large, most mathematicians are excited about the discovery.

“Even before this, we have seen the power of AI, and this shows that AI trained on hundreds of years of human mathematics can perform amazing tasks,” said Penn State’s Milewski. “I would add that AI didn’t come up with the Riemann Hypothesis, nor did most of the work to prove it, and AI probably doesn’t ‘know’ why it was invented or why it’s important.”

CNET spoke with a few professors about this, and they all echoed Milewski’s sentiment, stating that AI was trained with centuries of human mathematical achievement, so a win for AI is a win for humans too. Jeffrey Lagarias, a professor of mathematics at the University of Michigan, told CNET that “I don’t think this particular proof would have been found without AI.”

Prasad Tetali, head of the department of mathematical sciences at Carnegie Mellon University, said: “It is easy to overestimate the role of AI, and underappreciate decades of human mathematician work that went into it. The AI here is really standing tall and wide on the shoulders of generations of human giants.”

On the AI side of things, influencers are attempting to use the news to hype up the capabilities of AI. Meta Chief AI Officer Alexandr Wang said in a post on X that solving the Riemann Hypothesis was a sign that AI superintelligence was close at hand and that humanity should be terrified of the “alien mind that we don’t understand.”

AI researcher Eliezer Yudkowsky likened the whole thing to a company making enriched uranium that’ll one day result in the end of the human species.

These warnings are hard to take seriously when one considers that Anthropic tried to make Claude solve the Riemann Hypothesis by itself back in August. Claude came up with some cool ideas but ultimately failed to solve the problem alone. For the time being, AI still needs a great deal of human guidance to make these massive discoveries, so the doom-and-gloom proselytizing comes off as a little premature.

To make things even more interesting, a competing Riemann Hypothesis proof was submitted by Alessandro Masullo just a few hours after Alpöge released his. This competing proof is 940 pages long, which is significantly shorter than Alpöge’s proof, and it was an all-human effort with no AI assistance. The proof is months in the making, with Masullo working with mathematician Brian Conrad to bring it up to spec before it hits peer review. When Alpöge posted his proof, Masullo and others thought it wise to submit theirs as well.

This started a “dueling proofs” storyline over the weekend, but it was short-lived. Shortly after Masullo’s proof was posted, multiple people found the types of flaws and omissions that often disprove Riemann Hypothesis proofs. Those issues are in the process of being corrected, and the proof is officially in mid-revision status.

Masullo kept the original proof posted (PDF) in its unedited form, but acknowledges that the proof was rushed to publication so that it could compete with Alpöge and Claude.

“The PDF below was posted on Nov. 20 on Ravi Vakil’s blog,” Masullo wrote on a Google site. “It was never meant to be shared publicly at this stage, and it has been posted in this draft form in response to current circumstances.”

It’s entirely possible that future revisions of Masullo’s work may solve the problems in the early draft and create a fully human proof of the Riemann Hypothesis without AI, but that isn’t happening anytime soon.

This is a lot of information about finding the Riemann Hypothesis, so it’s probably a good idea to briefly explain what it even is. In the 1730s, famous mathematician Leonhard Euler came up with a zeta function as a way to solve the Basel Problem, another mathematical curiosity about the exact sum of the reciprocals of squares. Euler was successful in this endeavor, and the zeta function became a part of mathematics.

Bernhard Riemann picked up where Euler left off and expanded the zeta function to use complex numbers — real numbers mixed with imaginary numbers — using a process called analytic continuation. Riemann used this expanded function to express how many prime numbers there were under a specific number and how tightly those prime numbers were packed together.

This is easy enough to do in your head with small numbers, like figuring out there are four prime numbers under 10, but when the biggest prime number is 41,024,320 digits long, having a function to do it for you is a lot more helpful.

The mechanics of Riemann’s expanded zeta function are quite complicated, and the math-focused YouTube channel Numberphile has an excellent explainer from a professional mathematician (with pictures) if you want to learn more.

The hypothesis concerns the solutions the function yields. Riemann’s version of the zeta function showed that if you plugged in an even negative number, like -2, -4 or -672, the function would always spit out a zero. These are referred to as trivial zeroes, and Riemann proved that this was always true in his original paper.

The Riemann Hypothesis is the conjecture that all the other numbers that would also make a zero — referred to as non-trivial zeroes — follow a very specific formula, which is “½ + it”, where “i” is an imaginary number and “t” is any real number, positive or negative. In short, Riemann believed that there was no other way to generate a zero with his zeta function other than even negative numbers (trivial zeroes) and numbers that followed the “½+it” formula (non-trivial zeroes). Riemann provided some examples of the hypothesis being right but never wrote a full proof of it.

It’s worth noting that not every number that follows the “½+it” formula produces a zero, but that all the numbers that produce a zero will use that formula. It’s kind of the same as saying that all squares are rectangles but not all rectangles are squares.

In the 167 years since the hypothesis was first published, mathematicians have found 12 trillion non-trivial zeroes, and every single one of them fits the above rules. Trillions of other numbers that don’t follow those rules have been tested to find a counterexample, since even one counterexample disproves the hypothesis immediately. Those efforts have produced no counterexamples at all.

This isn’t conclusive proof, but it’s such an overwhelming amount of evidence that mathematicians began assuming that the hypothesis was true, even if no one could prove it. Alpöge’s AI-assisted proof is the first to show in any context that the hypothesis is always true, which, according to Tetali, has implications in prime number understanding, number theory and other branches of mathematics.

“The big job on the math side is to find a human-understandable proof,” Lagarias said. “I would say this is certainly going to happen; there will be such understanding. Already there are several AI digests of the proof breaking it into its component pieces. I have seen one [that’s] 11 pages, 164 pages, 273 pages. There are great ideas to extract from them.”
