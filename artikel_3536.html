How Elizabeth Taylor’s Childhood and Career Were Defined by Her Overbearing Mother

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Elizabeth Taylor, like many other actors of her time, experienced the demands of Hollywood from a very young age. Her parents could never have imagined on February 27, 1932, just how much their newborn baby would achieve artistically. Sooner rather than later, however, they recognized her potential. Although she spent most of her life in Southern California in the heart of the movie industry, Taylor was born in Hampstead, England, to an American family consisting of her father, Francis Lenn Taylor, her mother, Sara Sothern, and their son, Howard, the movie star’s brother with whom she was close. They didn’t remain in Great Britain long, returning to the United States in 1939, shortly before World War II.

Sara Sothern with her children, Elizabeth and Howard Taylor.

The family sailed from London to New York and then settled in Los Angeles. By then, Taylor already had some experience in the performing arts, as she had been taking ballet lessons since she was just three years old. She made her debut in a recital that same year. Once in California, the father of the violet-eyed legend opened an art gallery featuring works imported from the UK. This would turn out to be an important step that brought the Taylors into the world of Hollywood. As biographer Alexander Walker would recount in Elizabeth: The Life of Elizabeth Taylor (Grove Press), the space “opened many doors for the Taylors, leading them directly into the society of money and prestige.”

Despite their differences, mother and daughter grew closer as they matured.

Sothern, the mother of this acting prodigy, saw her daughter as the perfect excuse to live the dream she didn’t fulfill because she was focused on caring for her family. If she couldn’t pursue an acting career, her daughter would. Seeing the effect that Taylor’s beauty had on others, Sothern set out to introduce her to people in the film industry. “Elizabeth Taylor probably wouldn’t have been an actress if it hadn’t been for her mother,” notes actor George Hamilton in the BBC documentary Elizabeth Taylor: Rebel Superstar. In this same documentary, Liz herself can be heard saying in an old recording: “My mother was an actress, but then she married my father, and he didn’t want her to be one, so she gave it up. I think she lived her life through me.”

Kate Andersen Brower, author of Elizabeth Taylor: The Grit and Glamour of an Icon (Harper Collins), explains that Sothern was behind the scenes trying to control every aspect of her daughter’s life in order to make her famous. This included sending Taylor to the same schools as the children of movie studio executives. Soon, thanks to a meeting with the fiancée of a high-ranking Universal Pictures exec who visited the gallery run by Taylor’s father, Elizabeth made her big-screen debut. Her early Hollywood years were propelled by connections her mother painstakingly built.

In 1949, Elizabeth Taylor and Sara Sothern pose at their home in Beverly Hills.

Taylor’s first movie was There’s One Born Every Minute, filmed when she was only 10 years old. Also starring Carl Switzer, who played Alfalfa in Our Gang, it was not a success, and Universal chose not to extend her option. Andersen Brower quotes the studio’s casting director: “The kid has nothing. Her eyes are too old; she doesn’t have the face of a kid.” Sara persisted, however, and through her husband, she met Sam Marx, a producer working on an MGM film, Lassie Come Home. Taylor would be cast in a small role.

Elizabeth Taylor and her mother in a 1948 photo taken in London.

Its success paved the way for another milestone in Taylor’s early career. At age 12, she landed the role of Velvet Brown in National Velvet, alongside Mickey Rooney and Angela Lansbury. Released in December 1944, it grossed almost $6 million worldwide, making it one of MGM’s top films of that period. Taylor signed a new contract with the studio, though she later complained about how she was treated by its head, Louis B. Mayer.

Elizabeth Taylor helps her mother prepare hot dogs and hamburgers at their home in Los Angeles in 1947.

At the same time, her father was both astonished by and jealous of his teenage daughter’s success. An alcoholic and physically abusive, on one occasion he punched her in the jaw, and for the rest of her life, she suffered from TMJ, or lockjaw. Andersen Brower writes, “She explained it away—as she always had—saying that her father was embarrassed that Taylor was the breadwinner of the family and that he felt emasculated.”

Sothern was a practicing Christian (though she was also interested in Judaism, which may have contributed to Taylor’s decision to convert to that religion so that she could marry Eddie Fisher, her fourth husband), and she taught her daughter from an early age that she could not sleep with any man until she was married to him. It’s a view that Taylor appears to have adopted: “Nothing comes off until the ring goes on,” she told gossip columnist Louella Parsons. Sothern, however, had her doubts. Before Taylor married Conrad “Nicky” Hilton in 1950, when she was just 18 years old, Sothern paid a doctor to confirm that her daughter was still a virgin and also to open her hymen to spare her pain during the first time she had sex.

Years later, Taylor would admit that her early years in the film industry were not easy. Her parents spent practically all the money she earned as a child actor. In a 1997 interview with Barbara Walters on 20/20, she described her childhood as “lonely.” She once confided to her close friend George Hamilton that she just wanted “to be a little girl again,” to relive those years that she never got to enjoy.
