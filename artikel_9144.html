NIKE rethinks air cushioning with digitally printed patterns inside its soles

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

instead of relying on conventional internal supports, the sportswear company uses selective lamination to control underfoot response, with the first running models expected in late 2027.

NIKE has found another use for ink, and this time it has little to do with logos or graphics. The company has introduced Tuned Air, a manufacturing process that uses digitally applied ink to determine how different sections of an air-filled sole behave under pressure. Presented on November 16, 2026, the technology changes the internal construction of NIKE’s familiar Air units rather than introducing an entirely new cushioning material. The idea is to control which areas compress, which remain firmer and how the sole responds to movement.

Traditional NIKE Air units rely on internal pins and rigid sections to maintain their shape, a construction method that also limits how their geometry can be adjusted. Tuned Air offers an alternative by allowing engineers to create different internal configurations without depending on those fixed structures. This means that the properties of an Air unit can be adjusted across its length, potentially producing different sensations beneath the heel and forefoot. Nike intends to adapt these configurations to different sports and athletes, although the company has yet to demonstrate how the technology performs in a commercially available shoe.

The process combines proprietary digital ink with selective lamination. Ink is applied to specific areas inside an Air unit, preventing those sections from bonding during manufacturing. Areas without ink, meanwhile, are joined together. By controlling this pattern, engineers can determine the geometry of the internal structure and influence how it compresses and rebounds. Rather than assembling a different mechanical configuration for every variation, NIKE can modify the printed pattern to produce different cushioning characteristics.

The inspiration came, somewhat unexpectedly, from the glossy colors of magazine covers. NIKE’s engineers began exploring how printing techniques could be adapted to create internal structures within an Air unit, eventually developing an ink-based process refined over more than a decade. The method also reduces the constraints traditionally associated with the height and shape of the units, giving designers greater freedom to experiment with their proportions. What looks like a relatively simple printed pattern is doing much of the work previously assigned to physical supports.

The manufacturing process also changes how NIKE develops its prototypes. According to the company, average development times for new Air units have fallen by 70 percent at its Advanced Product Creation Center. A prototype that previously took three weeks can now be produced in three days. This allows engineers to test more variations of internal geometry without having to redesign the entire structure each time. Nike also says the process makes it easier to reproduce specific cushioning properties across different shoe sizes, although these improvements remain company-reported figures.

The first Tuned Air running shoes are scheduled to arrive in late 2027, with applications in other sports and NIKE Sportswear planned to follow. The technology was previewed at NIKE’s World of Athletes event through a series of colorful components illustrating the possible internal configurations. No specific commercial models or independent performance results have been announced. For the moment, the most interesting part of NIKE’s latest footwear experiment is something that will be largely invisible once the shoe is on someone’s foot.
