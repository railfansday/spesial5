After $26 billion in gifts, MacKenzie Scott is still worth $27 billion—and just gave Atlanta students $8 million more

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Four years ago, MacKenzie Scott gave $8 million to Achieve Atlanta, a nonprofit that helps Atlanta public school students pay for and finish college. Last week, the organization announced she had done it again, with a second $8 million gift that brings her total support to $16 million.

It is one of two gifts Scott made to Georgia education nonprofits this month, together worth $12.5 million. The donor has given away more than $26 billion since her 2019 divorce from Amazon founder Jeff Bezos, yet is still worth more than $27 billion, largely because Amazon stock keeps rising.

For an Atlanta Public Schools graduate pursuing a bachelor’s degree, the need-based Achieve Atlanta scholarship can be worth up to $20,000 over four years. Students at two-year colleges and in technical programs can receive $1,500 a year for up to four semesters.

The nonprofit also helps students choose colleges, work through the application process, and find their first jobs. Since 2015, it has awarded more than $75 million in scholarships.

An independent evaluation by researchers affiliated with Georgia State University’s Georgia Policy Labs found that 91% of Achieve Atlanta scholars returned for a second semester of college. Among peers who also graduated from Atlanta Public Schools, had similar academic and demographic backgrounds, and enrolled in college without the program’s support, 77% did.

The gap narrows over time but holds. Six years after high school, 42% of scholars had graduated, compared with 36% of similar peers.

“The repeated investment by Scott is a testament to the success of Achieve Atlanta’s postsecondary success model,” the nonprofit said in announcing the gift.

Scott’s latest giving in Georgia also supports women returning to school later in life. The Athens-based Jeannette Rankin Foundation received $4.5 million, its largest single donation to date, according to The Atlanta Journal-Constitution. Karen Sterk, the foundation’s CEO, told the newspaper that its support could benefit more than a million women.

The two gifts fit a pattern. Scott’s higher education giving has largely bypassed elite universities in favor of HBCUs, community colleges, and tribal colleges, and her giving to historically Black colleges and universities alone has topped $1 billion, Fortune has reported.

When Scott and Bezos finalized their divorce in July 2019 after 25 years of marriage, she received roughly 4% of Amazon. The shares were worth about $37 billion then.

Since then, she has given away more than $26 billion through more than 2,700 gifts to nonprofits, according to Yield Giving, the organization she set up to make them. Forbes still estimates her net worth is more than $27 billion.

Her stake has shrunk. In the year through September 2025, Scott cut her Amazon holdings by 42%, according to a regulatory filing that did not say whether she sold, donated, or both.

But the shares she kept have kept climbing. Amazon stock gained more than 42% over the five years through July 2026, as Fortune previously reported. Those gains help explain why Scott is still worth tens of billions of dollars even as she gives away much of her fortune.

The company behind that fortune is now the largest in the U.S. by revenue. Amazon ranks No. 1 on the 2026 Fortune 500, ending Walmart’s 13-year run at the top.

Scott signed the Giving Pledge in 2019, committing to give away the majority of her wealth during her lifetime or in her will. Her largest gifts include $436 million to Habitat for Humanity, $281 million to Boys & Girls Clubs of America, and $275 million to Planned Parenthood.

Scott has also described what she wants her money to do while she still owns it. In a 2024 essay, she wrote that she asked her investment team to find funds and companies working on the same problems she supports through philanthropy. Her examples included affordable housing funds and digital health platforms for women.

When she makes a gift, she wrote, she would rather draw the money from those investments than from a stock portfolio “that increases the wealth and influence of leaders who already have it.” She wants to back ventures led by people from the communities they serve, or by founders who often struggle to raise capital, including women and people of color.

Her idea was that the same dollar could help twice: first as an investment in those ventures, and later as a donation to a nonprofit.

As Scott wrote in her Giving Pledge letter, “It will take time and effort and care. But I won’t wait. And I will keep at it until the safe is empty.”
