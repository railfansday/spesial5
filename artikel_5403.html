12 terms that show how the inflation rate is built and what it leaves out

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Baskets, price indexes, core measures and more: 12 terms from the Cleveland Fed explain how the inflation rate is built.

Most people look up the inflation rate and accept it as a plain fact about the economy. That habit matters more now that price growth has picked up. Consumer prices rose 2.9% from December 2023 to December 2024, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and BLS reports that the 12-month increase reached 3.8% by April 2026. Faster price growth takes more out of household budgets. When a number has that much effect on people's money, how it is made becomes the reader's business. Each term in the vocabulary of inflation marks a point where someone chose what to count, what to compare or what to ignore.

Nobody observes the inflation rate directly. It is the change between price readings taken at two points in time, such as a month, a quarter or a year apart, and without both readings there is no rate. A lapse in federal appropriations left October 2025 without survey data, and BLS reports that 12-month changes for most series went unpublished in October 2026. The need for a second reading applies to every other choice behind the number. A different mix of goods, a different span of time or a different set of excluded items can change the headline even when no price has changed. A reader who knows where those choices sit can tell a real shift in the cost of living from a result of the method.

The Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland set out to explain how inflation is gauged and to define the terms related to it, from the basket of goods behind every price index to median CPI, deflation and stagflation. Those terms fall into three groups. Some describe how the inflation number is built, and some describe how economists separate a lasting trend from a passing jolt. Others name extreme conditions, such as prices that rise out of control or fall for a long stretch. The value for a reader lies in knowing which question each term answers. With that knowledge, the next inflation headline can be read for what it actually says and for what it leaves out.

Inflation is measured from a chosen shopping list rather than from every price in the economy, so every reading carries the judgments built into that list. Measurement begins with a representative set of household purchases whose prices statisticians collect, and statisticians rebuild that set as spending habits change. The basket tries to capture what a typical household buys and also how much of each item it buys, and that quantity is an estimate. "The basket does not contain every good or service but is meant to be a good representation of both the types of items and the quantities of items that households typically consume," the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland said. When statisticians update that estimate of typical spending, the inflation reading can change even if no price tag does.

The weights shape the answer as well as the prices do, though in January 2026 the effect on the headline was small. Each item counts in the basket at its quantity times its price, and statisticians add those values across all items. BLS reports that the monthly increase in consumer prices for January 2026 was 0.37% under the new weights and 0.40% under the weights from 2023, using the same prices. Anyone who follows the headline can take some comfort from that, because refreshing the basket left the overall picture of the month intact. That total is an average across categories with very different results, and some of those categories shifted far more.

Under the newer weights, the increase for the groceries families buy every week was much sharper. The increase for fruits and vegetables in January 2026 was 1.01% under the new weights and 0.73% under the 2023 weights, according to BLS. The basket is a sample of typical consumption and not a full list of what anyone buys, so a household that spends heavily on produce faces a different inflation rate from the basket's average.

Reweighting changes only how much each price counts toward the total, never what a single price did. Gasoline was 2.52% cheaper that month under either set of weights, according to BLS. When a headline shifts after a basket update, the mix has changed, not the price at the pump.

A reader whose costs seem to outrun the official rate should look first at what they buy, because the official rate can describe the basket accurately and still not match that reader's spending.

Thousands of separate price tags say nothing about inflation until statisticians combine them into one figure that people can compare over time. A price index sets what the whole basket costs today against what it cost in a base period, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, and every inflation figure starts from that comparison. The result depends on a choice about whose purchases count. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports that its headline consumer price index follows how much prices move for shoppers in urban areas, averaged across all of them. The figure is precise about that average urban shopper and uncertain about any one family's costs.

Averaging is the purpose of an index, and it is also the reason large price shocks can sit unnoticed inside a calm headline. Across all items, the index rose 3.7% over the 12 months through September 2026, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, a moderate pace for a full year. Over the same year, gasoline cost 34.2% more. The headline blends gasoline with everything else households buy, so one steep increase moved the total far less than it moved the cost of filling a tank. A gap between the headline and particular purchases is therefore built into how an index works. A household that drives a lot faced a much steeper increase than the one reported in the news.

The price index gives a fair reading of the average household and a poor one for any single budget.

"Prices are high" and "inflation is high" sound like the same complaint, but they answer different questions. The Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland explains that inflation measures how fast an index climbs in percentage terms, and that the index on its own reflects what living costs. In October 2026, the index that tracks prices paid by city shoppers stood at 337.511, according to BLS. That figure tells readers how costly everyday life has become, not the speed at which costs are rising. A household that feels squeezed at the store is reacting to the level. A calmer pace of inflation does not bring that level down; only an actual drop in prices would.

The big index number is a record of the past, measured from a starting line that someone chose. Every index level compares today's overall prices with a base year, and for the consumer price index that base is the period from 1982 to 1984, according to BLS. The October 2026 reading is therefore a running total of price increases since the early 1980s, not a gauge of how fast prices are moving today. Its size means something only next to that chosen base, and the current direction of prices is visible only in how much the level grows from one period to the next.

Index points are a poor guide to how fast prices are rising. In a worked example, two items each had an index 18.0 points higher than before, according to BLS. For one item, that was an 8.0% increase, and for the other it was a 16.4% increase, because the two started from different levels. The same visible jump can hide very different speeds of price growth.

For any index, the question that reveals inflation is how much it changed in percentage terms, not how many points it moved.

Anyone measuring inflation first has to decide how far back to compare, and the same prices give a different rate depending on that decision. Year to year is one common span, and inflation can also be measured over others such as quarter to quarter or month to month, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland. No single span is the correct one, so a headline that reports "the" inflation rate gives readers one window among several. The yearly rate compares each month with the same month a year earlier. It shows how far prices moved over a full year but not the path they took in between, and a headline can mislead in that gap.

A violent swing in a single category can account for much of a sharp move in the yearly rate, because the overall rate blends every category in the basket. In July 2008, energy cost 29.3% more than a year earlier, according to BLS. Anyone checking the headline that month saw all items 5.6% pricier than a year before, which looked like a serious squeeze on household budgets. By December 2008, the same yearly comparison showed energy 21.3% cheaper than a year earlier. In about one season, a steep annual rise in energy had turned into a steep annual fall. When the yearly rate jumps or plunges, a reader should ask whether one volatile category accounts for the move before concluding that prices across the board have changed course.

A large price move stays in the yearly rate for a full 12 months, and after that it becomes the starting point for the next comparison. In July 2009, all items cost 2.1% less than a year earlier, according to BLS, and that comparison reached back to the month of the 2008 energy surge. Part of the drop came from measuring current prices against an unusually high starting point. A negative yearly rate therefore does not prove that prices are sliding now, since it can mean that last year's spike has become the point of comparison.

Before trusting what a yearly rate says about today, a reader should check what prices were doing a year earlier.

There is no single inflation rate, and a report about "inflation" rests on one index chosen from several. The Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland explains that different indexes weight different products, so they show price changes for households, for producers or for the whole economy. These indexes are not rival estimates of one true number. Each one gives its own answer to the question of whose prices count. Two indexes that the public treats as interchangeable can therefore report different results from the same price changes, and that gap comes from their design, not from an error.

In March 2026, software and computer accessories held a weight of 0.035% in core CPI, while the category's share of core PCE was 1.2%, according to the Federal Reserve Board. That category barely counts in one index and is a real input in the other. The two federal agencies build their weights from different kinds of data. The Bureau of Labor Statistics relies on what households report buying in its Consumer Expenditure Survey, while the Bureau of Economic Analysis relies on what companies tell the Census they shipped. One source tracks what people say they bought and the other tracks what businesses sent out, and the two do not agree. A jump in software prices therefore barely registers in the CPI but counts noticeably in PCE.

The two indexes also do not serve as independent checks on each other, because PCE takes its software prices from the CPI. The PCE price index for that category is derived from the CPI series for computer software and accessories, the Federal Reserve Board reports, so a problem in the CPI series passes into PCE without being caught. The CPI item behind that series, Computer Software and Blank Media, includes blank media, while the PCE category gives blank media no weight at all. A rise in memory product prices may therefore show up in a PCE category that contains no memory products. Correcting for that mismatch would lower the category's measured contribution to core goods inflation, but that correction is an estimate by Federal Reserve Board researchers Barbarino, Diercks and Miran, not an official revision by the Bureau of Economic Analysis or the Bureau of Labor Statistics, so the official numbers stand.

Whether goods inflation signals broad price pressure can depend on this single small category. Core goods PCE rose at a 5.5% annualized pace over the four months to March 2026 and at 2.7% without software, according to the Federal Reserve Board. Including software makes goods inflation look like a serious surge, while leaving it out shows a modest pace. Much of the alarm comes from a category whose measurement researchers question.

The number is not fake, but before reading a surge as economy-wide, a reader should ask which index produced it and what the category driving it contains.

The overall inflation rate cannot tell a short-lived increase in prices from one that will last, and underlying inflation is the measure built to separate the two. Policymakers use it to set aside temporary noise and see the price growth that persists. The Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland defines it as the price growth that would be left if brief jolts in the data could be stripped away. That condition makes underlying inflation an estimate rather than a direct reading. It also makes it another choice in how the number is built, because someone has to decide which price effects are short-lived and which will last.

Underlying inflation tracks a trend over the medium run, a span of several years, and not the bill a shopper pays this month. A household can pay far more for a single item this month while underlying inflation barely changes, and both readings are accurate because each answers a different question. When something a household buys becomes sharply more expensive, the useful question is whether the increase is temporary or part of a lasting trend. Only the lasting kind is meant to appear in the underlying rate.

Policymakers can respond differently depending on how they classify a rise. A rise they judge persistent may lead them to recommend raising interest rates, which makes borrowing more expensive for households and businesses. One they judge temporary may leave rates where they were.

For households, that classification matters because a rise that policymakers read as lasting can lead to higher borrowing costs.

The full consumer price index counts every sharp price swing, so a single month's reading can mislead anyone trying to judge where prices are heading. The Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland describes median CPI and core measures as practical ways to estimate underlying inflation, either by keeping only the middle price change or by dropping food and energy. Both approaches set aside real price changes on purpose. Readers should treat these measures as deliberate edits of the data, made by people who decided which prices to leave out.

The two measures define noise in opposite ways. Median CPI judges a price change by its size rather than by what was bought, and it excludes every change except the middle one. Whatever moved most sharply in a given month is set aside, whether it was gasoline or haircuts. Core measures make the reverse choice and decide in advance which items to ignore. Food and energy stay out even in a month when their prices hold steady, while a wild swing in some other item stays in. Each method aims at the same trend and misses different price changes, and each reflects a judgment about which prices deserve trust.

Leaving prices out is a choice, not a requirement. Some gauges of the underlying trend keep every item in the basket. Deciding what counts as noise is therefore a method someone picked, and the measurement itself does not demand it. When a report says underlying inflation is cooling or heating, the useful question is which method produced that reading and what it left out. A gap between a core reading and a median reading is not a contradiction to explain away. It shows that two reasonable definitions of noise caught different price changes.

Once the inflation number has been built and cleaned up, people want to know where it is headed, and the most familiar answer comes from the job market rather than from prices. The Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland explains that the Phillips curve links inflation and unemployment inversely, though other drivers can loosen the connection. In plain terms, price growth tends to be weaker when more people are out of work, and the idea carries that caveat from the start. It is named for the economist A. W. Phillips, which makes it an established economic concept that a reader can look up, not a market slogan. The relationship Phillips first described concerned wages, and later work extended it to price inflation. That means the jobs-to-prices link people cite today goes one step beyond what was first observed.

The trade is easiest to see in a hypothetical slowdown. Suppose unemployment stands at 4% at the start and 7% at the end, a shift that would put many more people out of work and dominate the news. In the curve's logic, inflation over the same stretch is 5% at the start and 2% at the end. Price growth would go from a pace that strains household budgets to a much gentler one. Those figures illustrate what the idea predicts and are not drawn from any real episode. They show the curve's ideal shape, not evidence that the economy behaves this way. Even in that tidy form the bargain is grim, because calmer prices come at the cost of lost jobs.

The job market is only one influence on prices, and that is why the tidy trade often fails to appear. "However, economic conditions are only one of the factors that determine inflation," the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland said. One of those other factors is what people expect inflation to be, and no jobs report measures that. Changes in exchange rates are another, and they have nothing to do with how many people are working.

Readers should treat a weak jobs report as a reason prices may rise more slowly, not as a forecast that prices will fall.

Every measure of inflation in this guide assumes that prices change slowly enough for a comparison with last year to tell people something useful. Hyperinflation is the case where that assumption fails. It is extreme, accelerating inflation, commonly defined as above 50% per month, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland. That definition counts inflation in months instead of years, so a shopper would face a sharply higher grocery bill before the next paycheck arrived. The word "accelerating" matters as much as the number, because in hyperinflation the pace of price increases typically keeps rising instead of holding steady at one high level. The term describes a breakdown in how prices behave, not just an unusually bad year for the cost of living.

In some of the most extreme cases, prices can double within days. At that speed, even a monthly inflation figure describes the past. By the time statisticians publish it, shoppers are already paying far more than it shows. For people living through hyperinflation, the inflation rate no longer helps them plan. It records how much buying power they have already lost.

Hyperinflation can sound like a problem from old economics textbooks, but its cases are recent. People in Yugoslavia lived through it in the 1990s, within living memory in Europe, and Venezuelans began living with it in 2017. Modern economies are not protected from it.

Baskets, weights and core measures help people understand prices only while prices change at a pace statisticians can measure, and hyperinflation is the point where that stops.

Falling prices look like good news to a shopper, but a lasting drop in prices is a warning about the economy. A price level that falls for a long stretch can lead people to cut back on what they buy, and that pullback can set off a cycle in which conditions keep getting worse, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland. Consumer spending supports businesses and the jobs they provide. A broad, sustained slide in prices therefore points to an economy in trouble rather than a season of bargains.

The danger of deflation is that it builds on itself instead of arriving as a single shock. Economists call this process a "deflationary spiral." Once prices start to fall, the decline can continue instead of settling at a new, lower level. The risk for a household lies in where that pattern leads over months and years, not in any single price tag that has dropped.

No single reading can show deflation, because it requires a fall in the overall price level that lasts. A one-month dip in an index does not qualify. A slowdown in inflation does not qualify either, because economists treat it as a separate condition with its own name, disinflation. The price level as a whole has to fall and keep falling.

Before calling a headline deflation, a reader should ask whether the overall price level has fallen and stayed lower over a long stretch.

When a news report says inflation is coming down, many people hear that prices are coming down, and disinflation is the term for the gap between those two ideas. Disinflation means the inflation rate slows, say from 6% to 4%, while prices still rise, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland. An inflation rate of 4% is still a positive rate. A household paying for groceries, rent and fuel still spends more than it did a year earlier, only by a smaller margin than it would at 6%. The improvement is in the pace of increases, not in what anything costs. A reader who hears only that the rate is falling can easily come away believing that the household budget is getting easier when it is only getting harder more slowly.

Disinflation offers narrow relief because it slows the rise in the overall price level without reversing it. The price level keeps going up for as long as disinflation lasts. The increases a household has already absorbed stay in place, and new ones keep arriving on top of them, only smaller. A family whose budget was stretched by last year's prices gets no money back when the rate cools. It simply faces smaller additions to costs that remain high. When a headline says inflation is easing, the useful question is which number is easing: the rate of increase or the prices themselves.

Disinflation describes a cost of living that is still going up, only more slowly.

Stagflation is the extreme in which households face fast-rising prices, a weak economy and scarce work at the same time. It is high inflation alongside a weak economy and rising unemployment, the combination that the Phillips curve leads people to expect will not occur. Americans have lived through a severe version of it. Consumer price inflation was above 10% during the recessions of 1973–1975 and 1980, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland. At that pace, the everyday purchases in a household's basket became sharply more expensive within a single year. Those double-digit increases took place during recessions, not booms, and that timing is what separates stagflation from ordinary overheating.

The usual trade-off suggests that households hit by a weak job market would at least find some relief at the checkout as price growth slowed. Under stagflation, households lose that relief, and people lose income just as everything costs more. In both U.S. episodes, the unemployment rate was rising rapidly while prices were rising at double-digit rates. Stagflation is defined by two readings at once, and no inflation figure, however carefully built, can identify it alone. A reader can recognize it only by setting the price figures beside the jobs figures.

A high inflation reading alongside rising layoffs is the one pairing the usual rules say should not happen, and Americans have paid for it twice.
