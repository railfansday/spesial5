Can the internet go down? 10 facts on how it breaks and why it holds together

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Can the internet go down? These 10 slides, from undersea cable cuts to routing errors, explain how outages happen and why the whole network rarely fails

The internet has no off switch. It is a collection of many independently run networks that agree to pass traffic to one another using shared rules. That design shapes the answer to a question people tend to ask after a major outage: can the internet go down?

The short answer is yes, in pieces. Parts of the internet fail regularly. A single website can vanish for an afternoon. A popular app can stop loading across a continent. A whole country can lose access when its government orders providers to cut service. The other kind of failure has never been recorded: a total, simultaneous shutdown of the internet worldwide. The gap between those two outcomes — a local or service outage versus a global collapse — is the thread running through this list.

The weak points sit at several layers. Some are physical, such as fiber-optic cables on the seabed and the electricity that powers data centers, cell towers and home routers. Others are logical. Routing systems tell networks where to send traffic, and the Domain Name System turns web addresses into the numbers computers use. A mistake in either can make working services unreachable.

Then there are deliberate disruptions. Attackers can flood a target with junk traffic until it stops responding. Governments can cut or slow national access. Concentration adds its own risk. Many unrelated websites rely on the same few content delivery and cloud providers. One fault at such a provider can take them offline together. The sun adds another variable. A strong solar storm raises open questions about long-distance power lines and other equipment.

The 10 slides that follow start with how the internet is built, then work through the failure points one at a time. Each one includes documented examples where they exist. The final slide explains the redundancy that usually keeps a failure contained. For businesses that depend on constant connectivity, these mechanisms show why an outage at one provider can halt logins or payments while the rest of the internet keeps running. For everyday users, they show why a site can be down for some people and working for others.

No single company, government or machine runs the internet. It is made up of separately managed networks that connect by following common technical standards, and control is spread across all of them.

Each of those networks is called an autonomous system. One might belong to a broadband provider, a mobile carrier, a university, a cloud company or a large corporation. Each operator runs its own equipment and decides how traffic moves inside its own network.

The common standards are protocols. The Internet Protocol gives every connected device an address. The Transmission Control Protocol helps data arrive complete and in the right order. Any network that follows these rules can exchange traffic with any other network that does the same.

Autonomous systems are identified by numbers. Five regional internet registries hand out those numbers, along with blocks of IP addresses: ARIN, RIPE NCC, APNIC, LACNIC and AFRINIC. The registries coordinate addressing. They do not carry traffic or control what flows across the networks they register.

The design traces back to packet-switching research in the 1960s. Paul Baran at the RAND Corporation in the U.S. and Donald Davies at the National Physical Laboratory in the U.K. developed the idea independently. A traditional phone call held open one dedicated circuit between two points. Packet switching instead breaks data into small chunks.

Each packet carries its own addressing information. It can travel separately and be reassembled at the destination. Packets from the same email or video stream do not have to follow the same path. If one route is congested or broken, traffic can move along another.

The ARPANET project, funded by the U.S. Defense Department's Advanced Research Projects Agency, put these ideas into practice. It began operating in 1969, first connecting computers at the University of California, Los Angeles and the Stanford Research Institute. ARPANET switched its hosts to TCP/IP on Jan. 1, 1983. Those protocols still underpin the internet.

That layout explains the pattern of most outages. No one network is the internet. A fault tends to stay with the provider, service or region where it started, while traffic elsewhere keeps flowing. Partial failures of this kind happen often. A complete, simultaneous worldwide shutdown has never been recorded.

A single incorrect Border Gateway Protocol announcement can redirect or drop traffic meant for networks that had nothing to do with the mistake. A fault inside one company's network can make a major service unreachable for users far beyond that company's own customers.

BGP is the system that independent networks use to tell each other which blocks of IP addresses they can reach. Each network announces the address ranges it handles. Its neighbors pass those announcements on to their own neighbors. Over time, every participating router builds a picture of which path leads to which destination.

The weakness lies in trust. BGP was first specified in 1989, when far fewer networks used it. The protocol has no built-in way to confirm that a network announcing a set of addresses is entitled to them. A router generally accepts what its neighbors tell it and passes it along.

Routers also prefer the most specific route available. If one network announces a large address block and another announces a narrower slice inside it, traffic for that slice follows the narrower announcement. That rule keeps routing precise. It also lets a bad announcement override a legitimate one.

A well-documented example came in 2008, when Pakistan Telecom tried to block YouTube inside Pakistan. It did this by announcing a route for part of YouTube's address space, intending to send domestic requests nowhere. The announcement leaked to its upstream provider, which passed it to the wider internet.

The false route was more specific than YouTube's own. Many networks around the world followed it as a result. YouTube became unreachable for many users outside Pakistan for a time, until the false route was withdrawn and filtered out.

Network operators now use route filtering and Resource Public Key Infrastructure, known as RPKI, to reduce these risks. RPKI lets address holders publish signed records stating which networks may originate their addresses. Other networks can then reject announcements that do not match those records.

Adoption of these safeguards is voluntary and uneven across networks. A misconfigured announcement can still spread before anyone catches it.

Undersea cables carry most international internet traffic between countries and continents. When one is cut on the seabed, connections across a whole region can slow down or drop out until a repair ship fixes it.

These cables are fiber-optic lines. They carry data as pulses of light through thin strands of glass wrapped in protective layers. Specialized ships lay them along surveyed routes on the ocean floor. Each cable ends at a coastal landing station, where it connects to networks on land.

Most damage is accidental. A ship dragging its anchor can snag a cable in shallow water. Bottom trawling and other fishing activity can catch and break one in the same way. Cables near the shore are often armored or buried for this reason. In deep water, they usually rest on the seabed with lighter protection.

Natural events pose a separate risk. The 2006 Hengchun earthquake off Taiwan damaged multiple undersea cables in the surrounding waters. Connectivity across parts of Asia was disrupted afterward. Cables running through the same stretch of sea can fail together. One earthquake can remove backup routes along with the main ones.

Fixing a cable is slow work. Operators first have to locate the fault. A dedicated repair ship then travels to the site. The crew raises the damaged section to the surface, splices in a replacement length and lowers the cable back down. Weather, water depth and the ship's distance from the fault all affect how long the job takes.

While a cable is out of service, network operators shift traffic onto other cables that still have spare capacity. Users in well-connected regions may notice slower speeds rather than a complete loss of access. Places served by only one or two cables have far fewer options. For them, a single cut can remove most international connectivity until repairs are finished.

Satellite links offer another path for data. They carry only a small share of international traffic compared with seabed fiber, however.

A cable cut is a regional problem, not a global one. It can isolate a country or slow service across part of a continent while networks elsewhere keep running.

When the Domain Name System fails, websites can look broken even though their servers are running normally. A browser cannot reach a site until it turns the site's name into a numeric address. DNS is the service that performs that lookup.

DNS works like a directory for the internet. A person types a name such as quartz.com. Computers, however, route traffic using IP addresses, which are strings of numbers. DNS connects the two. Without a working answer from DNS, a browser has nowhere to send its request.

A lookup passes through several layers. A device first asks a recursive resolver. That resolver is often run by an internet service provider or by a public service such as Google $GOOGL Public DNS or Cloudflare's 1.1.1.1. If the resolver does not already know the answer, it works down a chain. It queries the root servers, then the servers for a top-level domain such as .com. The last stop is the authoritative name servers for the domain itself. Those authoritative servers hold the official record.

That chain creates a weak point. Many companies hand their authoritative DNS to an outside provider. If that provider stops answering, resolvers cannot get the address for any domain it hosts. The websites, apps and servers behind those domains may be fully intact. Users still see error messages. Their devices simply cannot locate those servers.

Caching softens the effect for a while. Resolvers store recent answers for a period called the time to live, or TTL. The TTL is set in the domain's DNS records. Once a cached record expires, the resolver must ask again. During a provider outage, those fresh requests fail.

The disruption at Dyn on Oct. 21, 2016, followed this pattern. Dyn provided DNS for many well-known sites. An attack on its infrastructure left resolvers unable to get answers for the domains it served. Access to those sites was disrupted, mainly for users in North America and Europe. The sites' own servers were not the target.

DNS failures do not require an attack. A mistyped record can make a site unreachable. So can a configuration change pushed to the wrong place, or a domain registration allowed to lapse. In each case, the problem sits in the naming layer rather than in the site itself.

The usual defense is to spread DNS across more than one provider. A domain can list name servers run by two separate companies. If one stops responding, resolvers can try the other. That setup can turn a DNS failure into a slowdown or a partial outage instead of a complete loss of access.

A distributed denial-of-service attack, or DDoS, floods one target with more traffic than it can handle until it stops answering legitimate users. It knocks out a particular website, server or service. It does not shut down the internet as a whole.

The word "distributed" separates a DDoS from an ordinary denial-of-service attack. A single attacking machine can be identified and blocked by its address. A DDoS sends traffic from a large number of machines at once, often spread across many networks and countries. That makes hostile requests hard to separate from real visitors.

Attacks come in several forms. Volumetric attacks try to fill the target's bandwidth with raw data. Protocol attacks exhaust resources on servers, firewalls or load balancers by abusing the way connections are set up. Application-layer attacks send requests that look like normal page loads. Each request forces the target to do real work, and that work piles up.

Most of the machines in a large attack do not belong to the attacker. They are compromised devices grouped into a botnet and controlled remotely. Their owners usually do not notice. A hijacked device can keep working normally while it sends attack traffic on command.

The Mirai botnet showed how far this could reach. Mirai infected internet-connected devices such as IP cameras, digital video recorders and home routers. Many of those devices still used factory-default usernames and passwords. Mirai scanned the internet for them and logged in using lists of common credentials. It was used in the 2016 attack on the DNS provider Dyn.

Mirai's source code was posted publicly in 2016. Other attackers copied and adapted it. Variants built on it have continued to appear. The episode drew attention to cheap connected devices that often go without security updates.

Defenders respond by filtering traffic before it reaches the target. Specialized mitigation services absorb attack traffic across large networks and pass clean requests through. Network operators can also drop all traffic aimed at a victim's address further upstream. That stops the flood, but it also takes the victim offline.

The limits of a DDoS help answer whether the internet can go down. An attack on one service can disrupt everything that depends on that service. The attack still needs working networks to carry its traffic. A DDoS cannot switch the internet itself off.

A national government can take its own country offline. Most countries reach the global internet through a limited number of providers and international links. An official order to those companies can cut access for an entire population.

Egypt showed how this works in January 2011. During mass protests, authorities had the country's internet access cut. Most users inside Egypt lost their connections to the outside world. Websites hosted inside the country also became unreachable from abroad.

The shutdown starts as an administrative order rather than a technical attack. A government does not need to sabotage cables or break into servers. It instructs licensed operators to stop carrying traffic. In practice, providers can withdraw the routing announcements that tell other networks how to reach their customers. Once those announcements disappear, the rest of the internet no longer has a path into the country.

Concentration determines how easy this is. A country with a single state-controlled gateway can be disconnected quickly. A country with many competing providers and many cross-border links is harder to isolate. Each additional independent connection is another company that must receive and follow the order.

A full cutoff is the bluntest option. Throttling is a quieter alternative. Authorities slow connection speeds until video, voice calls or file uploads stop working, while basic access technically remains. Governments can also block specific apps or websites instead of the whole network. These partial measures can be harder for users to recognize as deliberate.

Mobile networks are part of the same picture. In many countries, people go online mainly through phones rather than fixed broadband. An order aimed at mobile operators can disconnect a large share of users without touching home or office lines.

A national shutdown is still a local event in global terms. Traffic between other countries keeps flowing, and the wider network routes around the missing region. The people inside the affected country bear the cost. Their businesses, banks, schools and families lose every service that depends on a connection.

When many websites rely on the same infrastructure company, one fault at that company can take unrelated sites offline together. Shared dependence on content delivery networks and cloud providers helps explain why large parts of the web sometimes fail at the same moment.

A content delivery network, or CDN, keeps copies of a website's pages, images and video on servers in many locations. When a reader asks for a page, the CDN sends it from a nearby server instead of from the site's own origin server. The data travels a shorter distance. Pages load faster as a result. The CDN also spreads incoming traffic across many machines, which helps a site cope with sudden surges in visitors.

Cloud providers play a related role. They rent out computing power, storage and databases. Companies can then run their services without building data centers of their own. Websites, apps and internal business tools often sit on rented infrastructure of this kind.

The trade-off is concentration. A small number of large providers carry much of this work. A news site, a retailer and a government portal may share nothing except the company standing between them and their readers. If that company's systems fail, all of them can go dark at the same time.

On June 8, 2021, a configuration issue at the CDN Fastly caused errors across sites that used its service. Reddit $RDDT was among the sites that went down. So was gov.uk, the U.K. government's main website. The disruption lasted a short period before Fastly restored service. During that window, visitors saw error messages where pages should have been.

To an ordinary user, an outage like this can look as if the internet itself has stopped. The underlying networks, cables and routing systems kept working. The fault sat in one layer of infrastructure that many sites happened to share.

That distinction sits at the center of whether the internet can go down. A CDN or cloud outage is a service-level failure, not a collapse of the network. Sites that did not route their traffic through Fastly were not affected by its fault.

Companies can limit this exposure by splitting traffic across more than one CDN or cloud provider. The approach is known as multi-CDN or multi-cloud. It adds cost and complexity. Each business weighs how much downtime it can accept against the expense of maintaining that extra redundancy.

A large power blackout can take the internet offline across a whole region. Data centers, cell towers, network switching sites and home routers all run on electricity. The network stops working wherever that supply fails for long enough.

The weakest point is often inside the home. A Wi-Fi router and the modem beside it go dark the moment the power cuts out. A laptop with a fully charged battery still cannot reach the internet if the box linking it to the provider has no electricity.

Mobile data can keep working for a while. Cell towers commonly carry battery backup, and some sites also have generators. Once those batteries drain and no generator is available, the tower drops off the network. Phones nearby then lose both calls and data, even if the handsets themselves are still charged.

The provider's own network has the same dependency. Cable, fiber and DSL systems rely on powered equipment in local exchanges, street cabinets and regional hubs. Some of that equipment has backup power and some does not. A household running its own generator can still lose service if a cabinet down the street goes dark.

Data centers are designed to ride through outages. They typically use uninterruptible power supplies, which draw on banks of batteries to bridge the first moments of a cut. Diesel generators then start and take over the load. That arrangement keeps servers running, but only as long as fuel lasts and deliveries can arrive.

A long or widespread blackout tests those limits. Roads may be blocked, fuel trucks may be delayed, and generators need maintenance to keep running. Cooling systems also draw heavy power, and servers cannot run safely once they overheat.

Even a severe regional blackout remains a local failure. The rest of the internet keeps running, and traffic flows around the affected area. For the people inside it, the result can look like the internet itself has gone down.

A strong geomagnetic storm could damage long-distance power lines and other equipment the internet depends on. The best-known historical example, the Carrington Event of 1859, disrupted telegraph systems in Europe and North America.

The event is named after Richard Carrington, a British astronomer who observed an intense solar flare on Sept. 1, 1859. A powerful geomagnetic storm reached Earth soon after. Telegraph operators reported sparks and failed lines. Some lines kept carrying messages after their batteries were disconnected. Auroras appeared much farther from the poles than usual.

Storms like this begin on the sun. A coronal mass ejection sends a cloud of charged, magnetized gas into space. If that cloud strikes Earth's magnetic field, it can set off a geomagnetic storm. The storm induces electric currents in long conductors on the ground. Power lines, pipelines and old telegraph wires all fit that description.

Power grids face the most direct risk among modern infrastructure. Induced currents can overheat large transformers, which are slow to replace. In March 1989, a geomagnetic storm triggered the collapse of the Hydro-Québec power grid in Canada. Millions of people lost electricity. For the internet, the main risk from a storm of that kind runs through the loss of power.

The cables that carry data are a separate question. Fiber-optic strands carry light, and light is not affected by induced currents. Long undersea cables differ in one respect. They contain copper conductors that power repeaters spaced along the seabed to boost the signal. Those powered components are the part of a subsea cable that a storm could affect.

Space weather also reaches equipment in orbit. In February 2022, SpaceX said a geomagnetic storm increased atmospheric drag on a batch of newly launched Starlink satellites. Many of them fell back into the atmosphere. GPS signals and high-frequency radio can also degrade during strong storms.

Whether a modern Carrington-scale storm would cause lasting damage to the internet remains an open question. No storm of that strength has been recorded since the modern network was built. The U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's Space Weather Prediction Center tracks solar activity. It rates geomagnetic storms on a five-level scale from G1 to G5. Spacecraft that watch the sun can spot a coronal mass ejection as it leaves. That gives grid and network operators advance warning before it arrives.

The internet rarely fails everywhere at once because its core pieces are duplicated and spread out. Redundant routes, many separate cable systems, multiple root server operators and distributed hosting usually keep a failure inside one region or one service.

Redundancy starts with the way networks connect to each other. Large networks typically buy connectivity from more than one upstream provider, a practice called multihoming. They also exchange traffic directly at internet exchange points, where many networks meet in one facility. When one link drops, traffic can shift to another path.

The physical layer follows the same logic. Many cable systems cross oceans and continents, and different groups of companies build and own them. Losing one route puts more load on the remaining ones. It does not usually cut a whole continent off from the rest of the world.

The Domain Name System applies the same principle at its top level. At the top of DNS sit 13 root server identities, labeled with the letters A through M. Each identity is a single name and address, but it is not a single machine. Independent organizations run them, including Verisign, NASA and the University of Southern California's Information Sciences Institute.

Anycast routing turns those 13 identities into a worldwide system. With anycast, the same address is announced from many locations at once. A query goes to whichever copy is closest in network terms. Many servers around the world answer for the root this way. If one site goes offline, routing sends its queries to another.

Caching adds a further layer of protection. DNS resolvers store answers for a set period of time. Most lookups never need to reach the root servers at all.

Hosting has spread out as well. Large sites often run copies of their content in data centers on several continents. If a fire or flood shuts one facility, the others can take over its traffic.

None of this makes outages impossible. Redundancy only protects the places where it has been built. Some regions have fewer routes than others. Shared dependencies can still tie together systems that look separate on paper. What the design does is make a simultaneous, total shutdown far harder to trigger than a local one.
