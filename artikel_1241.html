Six highlights from Parkinson’s disease research

Rachel Nuwer is a freelance writer based in New York City.

Personalized deep-brain stimulation improved the gait mechanics of people with Parkinson’s disease.Credit: K. H. Louie et al. Nature Med. 32, 2803–2814 (2026)

Deep-brain stimulation (DBS), in which electrodes are implanted in the brain to reduce the symptoms of certain neurological disorders, is often used to treat motor symptoms of Parkinson’s disease. But DBS does not usually improve gait, in part because it delivers a continuous current of electricity that does not account for the shifting demands of walking.

Researchers at the University of California, San Francisco, tested a DBS approach that adjusts stimulation during different phases of the gait cycle. Using neurostimulators implanted in five participants, the team recorded brain activity and delivered electrical stimulation as the individuals walked. The researchers analysed the brain-activity data to identify a personalized neural signature for the leg-swing phases of each participant. This was used to programme the device to recognize brain-activity patterns during walking, and to adjust stimulation automatically at specific phases of the gait cycle.

The researchers confirmed that the gait-synchronized stimulation improved the participants’ step symmetry and reduced the variability of their steps, compared with conventional continuous stimulation. Three participants also completed a multi-day trial at home, in which synchronized stimulation improved their gait mechanics in different ways. One person’s steps became significantly more even in length, for example; another person reported fewer falls.

The technique has at least one drawback: participants tended to report worse stiffness under synchronized rather than continuous stimulation. Still, according to the authors, the study demonstrates that DBS can be synchronized to walking dynamics, opening the door to more-personalized stimulation strategies for gait dysfunction.

Clumps of misfolded α-synuclein protein are a hallmark of Parkinson’s disease, yet there is still no way to image the pathological protein reliably in a live brain. This is in contrast to Alzheimer’s disease, for which diagnosis has been improved using specific tracers of amyloid and tau proteins in positron emission tomography (PET). The tracers bind to those proteins and carry a radioactive tag for imaging.

A team led by researchers at the University of Tübingen in Germany has now developed a PET system to trace α-synuclein. In laboratory tests, the tracer not only bound to α-synuclein with high affinity, but was also more selective for the protein than for other types of aggregate. In rodents and monkeys, the tracer entered the brain readily and cleared quickly.

In a first step towards human trials, the team gave the tracer to four people: one with Parkinson’s, two with multiple system atrophy (another condition characterized by α-synuclein accumulation) and one healthy volunteer. In the participants with diagnoses, the tracer generated signals in the brain regions expected to have α-synuclein pathology; in the healthy volunteer, it produced only a faint signal. The authors conclude that the PET technique is a promising way to image α-synuclein in the living brain.

The prevalence of Parkinson’s disease is rising worldwide, but researchers have struggled to understand whether this is being driven by ageing populations, by more people surviving after diagnosis or because more people are developing the disease. Scientists at Sorbonne University in Paris and the Karolinska Institute in Stockholm used national health registers of the entire populations of their countries to track the prevalence of Parkinson’s, multiple sclerosis and motor neuron disease over two decades.

The researchers found that the prevalence of all three conditions has risen steadily over time, but that the underlying drivers differed. For Parkinson’s, the number of new diagnoses each year stayed flat and, once adjusted for population ageing, declined slightly. This suggests that the rate of people developing the disease is not increasing. Although survival times had been getting longer, the trend was not sustained over the past ten years.

These findings are in contrast to those for the two other diseases. For multiple sclerosis, rising prevalence was driven mainly by improved survival, probably because of disease-modifying drugs, whereas the rise in prevalence of motor neuron disease seems to reflect an increase in cases. The researchers point out that their findings come from high-income countries with universal health care, so might not generalize to other places.

Scientists have long associated Parkinson’s disease with brain regions that control movement in specific body parts. Researchers at the Changping Laboratory in Beijing instead propose that Parkinson’s is a disorder of the somato-cognitive action network (SCAN), which coordinates whole-body action.

The researchers pooled brain scans from 863 people — including healthy volunteers as well as people with Parkinson’s and other movement disorders — and compared how their brain regions are wired together. They found that the people with Parkinson’s had excessive connectivity between deep-brain structures and the SCAN, and that the strength of those connections correlated with symptom severity. The team also showed that several of the most effective interventions for Parkinson’s — including transcranial magnetic stimulation — reduce SCAN hyperconnectivity.

In a randomized experiment, the authors assigned 36 people with Parkinson’s to receive two weeks of transcranial magnetic stimulation aimed either at the SCAN or at motor regions. Compared with conventional stimulation, SCAN-targeted stimulation roughly doubled the reduction in overall motor symptoms, including improving issues related to tremors, muscle rigidity and speed of movement. The authors propose that SCAN hyperconnectivity could be used as a diagnostic marker for Parkinson’s and that SCAN-targeted treatments could enhance patient outcomes.

Air pollution is a recognized risk factor for dementia, but its specific role in two related conditions — dementia with Lewy bodies and Parkinson’s disease with dementia — has been unclear. Researchers at Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore, Maryland, combined large-scale human data with mouse and cell experiments to show that particulate matter smaller than 2.5 micrometres in diameter (PM2.5) promotes abnormal aggregation of a protein called α-synuclein, which forms the Lewy bodies that underlie these dementias.

Data from more than 56 million users of the US federal health-insurance system Medicare suggested that long-term PM2.5 exposure was associated with a risk of hospitalization for disorders that involve α-synuclein. This link was stronger for the two dementia diagnoses than for Parkinson’s without dementia. The researchers also showed that, in mice, chronic exposure to air pollution caused brain shrinkage and cognitive decline. Mice engineered to lack α-synuclein were protected from these effects, suggesting that the protein has a causative role.

Finally, the authors showed that PM2.5 from sources in China, the United States and Europe all caused α-synuclein to misfold into a more aggressive strain that spread faster between cells and was more toxic to neurons than were ordinary clumps of the protein. Injecting this strain into mice caused mainly cognitive rather than motor deficits. The authors propose that pollution-induced α-synuclein is an environmentally triggered mechanism contributing to Lewy body dementia.

Scientists are increasingly viewing Parkinson’s disease as two subtypes on the basis of where the disease originates: the brain or the body, particularly the gut. People with these subtypes have different initial symptoms that eventually converge when misfolded aggregates of the protein α-synuclein spread. Although imaging data support the existence of these subtypes, scientists have struggled to collect pathological evidence of where the protein aggregates and how it spreads.

To investigate, researchers at Zhengzhou University in China turned to the skin for its high density of nerves and ease of sampling. They recruited 205 people with Parkinson’s and 60 healthy people. The researchers sorted the Parkinson’s group into the two subtypes according to whether the participants had experienced a disorder of rapid-eye-movement (REM) sleep — a typical marker of body-first Parkinson’s — before developing motor symptoms. They took skin biopsies from the lower leg and the back of the neck of each participant and analysed the samples for α-synuclein.

The results revealed differences between the groups. Those with a body-first pattern of disease had significantly more misfolded α-synuclein than did those in the brain-first group: 92% compared with 62%. In the body-first group, the abnormal protein was detected more often in the leg, whereas the brain-first group had slightly higher rates in the neck than in the leg — observations that are consistent with where each subtype begins. The body-first participants also had α-synuclein that clumped faster in laboratory tests.

The differences support the two-subtype model, the authors say, and suggest that skin biopsies could be a minimally invasive tool that helps to classify people according to their disease subtype.
