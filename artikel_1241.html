'We felt like medieval peasants coming up against a king': Insiders relive the battle to rid Blackpool of the Oystons, from tangerines stuffed in knickers to a bright orange GTI - and why 'insider dealing' claims mean club isn't out of the woods yet

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Published: 07:55 EST, 17 November 2026 | Updated: 18:12 EST, 17 November 2026

In the south London pub where they are readying themselves for a League One match with AFC Wimbledon, Blackpool fans who fought for their club’s heart and soul, 10 years ago, receive a message informing them that half the squad have gone down with a sickness bug so severe that the starting XI will be very unusual.

Three players are too ill to leave the team hotel, the goalkeeper selected is yet to feature in the league this season and the EFL don’t consider this predicament serious enough to cancel the game. The club are already on a run of five defeats in seven.

Moments like this reveal the Blackpool fanbase’s deep sense of perspective, born of their long and often wretched fight to kick out one of British football’s most detested owners, Owen Oyston.

‘We’re still here and we’re watching our club home and away,’ says Andy Higgins, chair of the Blackpool Supporters Trust. ‘We have a good relationship with the club,’ says his friend Dave Ragozzino, with Higgins in the Earlsfield pub. ‘The club are asking us to tell fans what’s going on, and they know we will help. Ten years ago, our club’s owners were suing us.’

The story of the campaign which brought these fans and many more together is told in a superb new documentary The Battle for Blackpool, which screens this week, capturing its spirit, imagination and intelligence.

Oyston and his son Karl have both cooperated with the documentary-makers. Oyston Snr, wearing dark shades and his trademark fedora, is interviewed at his stately home in the Forest of Bowland, while his son Karl, clearly still consumed with hate and contempt for fans, is filmed clay pigeon shooting across their estate.

The Tangerines are just two points above the relegation zone having lost five out of their last seven league fixtures

The film’s stars are the supporters who led the fight – Christine Seddon, Tim Fielding, Ragozzino, two fans in a Mk1 tangerine Golf GTI and twin sisters of advancing years who embraced the campaign’s guerilla tactics.

Oyston Jr, who chaired the club at his mother’s request after his father was jailed for rape in 1996, tells the documentary-makers: ‘My father likes football. I didn’t.’

It was after he’d stumbled on a managerial genius in Ian Holloway, who took the club into the Premier League for a season in 2010-11, that a full sense of the family’s greed and entitlement became clear.

The Mail on Sunday's Nick Harris revealed that Oyston Snr had paid himself £11million out of the Premier League jackpot, while critics say virtually all the club appeared to get out of promotion was a new sprinkler system. The fight against the Oystons took hold. The Blackpool Supporters Trust, then chaired by Fielding, was its strategic and political wing, flanked by the imaginative sabotage activities of the Tangerine Knights group.

A plot to smuggle tennis balls into the ground for a home match against Burnley, in April 2014, and collectively throw them on the pitch, provides one of the documentary’s many glorious sections. Fans stuffed the missiles into their pants, socks and bras.

‘I had them in my knickers,’ says Christine Atkinson, who is interviewed with her twin sister, Pauline O’Rourke. ‘I didn’t have tennis balls. I had tangerines. It was softer. Less uncomfortable!’

The family of Karl Oyston mocked the tennis ball protest on social media. But it was when his father began suing ordinary fans, some pensioners, for criticising him that the protest escalated.

‘My father created a whole department, scouring message boards full-time so he could sue them,’ Oyston Jr tells the film-makers.

‘We felt like medieval peasants, coming up against a king,’ says Tangerine Knight Kevin Quirke, interviewed with his fellow combatant Scott Koudellas inside the tangerine GTI, parked up on Blackpool’s South Shore – one of the film’s many wonderful touches.

Ragozzino lost his job and faced a £150,000 legal suit. Fielding, a solicitor, was told it would cost more than £250,000 to defend himself against Oyston’s legal action. The Oystons forced him to pay them £10,000 in cash and issue a public apology.

Karl Oyston reveals how much he revelled in the war with fans, delighting in the ‘Oyston Out’ personalised number plate his wife bought for Christmas to maintain the mockery. ‘She thought it was highly amusing. Which it was,’ he relates.

Suing elderly Blackpool fans was his father’s idea, he says. ‘But as soon as the board makes a decision, you fight to the death to support it.’

Oyston Snr maintains he was perfectly entitled to pay himself £11m of the Premier League money. ‘Why wouldn’t we? Why shouldn’t we?’ he asks. ‘We own the company. We’d had 25 years of pouring money down the drain without the least prospect of seeing any of it ever again.’

It was Fielding who came up with the game-changing strategy – persuading minority shareholder Valeri Belokon to mount a successful legal challenge against the Oystons, eventually clearing the way for Simon Sadler, a Hong Kong-based financier and Blackpudlian to buy the club.

It’s not been a bed of roses these past few years. An interim club board, formed after Oyston was ousted, included Ben Hatton, later chief operating officer at Aston Villa, former Inter Milan CEO Mike Bolingbroke and Fielding. But a succession of good leaders have left.

Brett Gerrity, a one-time close friend of Sadler who persuaded him to buy the club, resigned in 2023. The two no longer get on. Ben Mansford, respected ex-CEO at Leeds and Barnsley, also unexpectedly left. Former sporting director David Downes was recently made CEO. Amid these comings and goings, Sadler has not appointed a successful manager since Neil Critchley left to assist Steven Gerrard at Aston Villa in 2022.

‘We felt like medieval peasants, coming up against a king,’ says Tangerine Knight Kevin Quirke (right), interviewed with his fellow combatant Scott Coudellas inside the tangerine GTI

Christine Seddon was a leading figure in the fight to rid Blackpool of the Oystons

None of this has affected fans’ views of Sadler, a boyhood Blackpool fan whose investment is still giving the club a top eight budget in League One. He has spent money on upgrades to the stadium and training ground and the team’s playing surface is excellent.

But his own future at Bloomfield Road is in doubt. He is on trial in Hong Kong over accusations of insider dealing linked to his former hedge fund and could face a prison sentence if convicted.

That would mean him failing the EFL Owners’ and Directors’ Test and he would seemingly have to sell. The verdict in that case is expected next February. Sadler, who has pleaded not guilty to the charges, has revealed little of any contingencies he might have made. He is thought to be prepared to sell the club, though only if the buyer will be a true custodian.

‘He’s acutely aware of not putting Blackpool right back where it was with Oyston,’ says one source.

‘The club is so much better and professional than it used to be,' Seddon tells us. ‘But we are short of football business experience. We don’t have a proper board at the moment.

'Hedge fund management – our owner’s world – does not always translate well into football. It’s a steep learning curve.’

Oyston Snr revealed in a recent interview that he wants to buy the club back and gift a majority share to fans. ‘In 2015, we suggested to him that he walk away and leave the club’s assets to the fans,’ says Seddon.

‘He ignored us, yet now he’s suggesting this. These are deluded ramblings. Simon Sadler would not give Oyston the time of day.’

'When I hear some fans complaining about the club, I think: “Have you forgotten? Are your memories quite short?”'

Karl Oyston tells the documentary-makers that he has not spoken to his 92-year-old father since the family’s 32-year ownership of the club ended in 2019. Will he ever see him again, he is asked. ‘No. That ship’s sailed,’ he replies.

Blackpool, backed by a loud, strong away following, fell to a 1-0 defeat at Wimbledon and boss Ian Evatt’s job will be on the line when they face fellow-strugglers Wigan on Saturday, so the film is a particularly timely reminder of how far this club’s fans have come.

‘Yes, we want results to be better,’ Seddon says. ‘But when I hear some complaining about the club, I think: “Have you forgotten? Are your memories quite short?” Our club became an opportunity for people who were dismantling it for their own benefit. There were times when we thought we would lose it altogether.

'But we’re now in a place where we were beginning to believe we would never be. We’re thankful for that.’

The Battle for Blackpool will premiere in the UK on November 21 on Sky Documentaries and streaming service NOW
