Escape Winter With These 6 Underrated Desert Trail Runs

In his research for his upcoming book, “The 50 Classic Trail Runs of America” Andy Cochrane ran hundreds of trails across the U.S. These are his six favorites across the Southwest that come into season when most of the country is hibernating.

With all due respect to Presidential Traverse, Teton Crest, and Wonderland, the most sought-after one-day trail running route in the U.S. is the Grand Canyon Rim-to-Rim-to-Rim. Every spring and fall runners flock to Arizona to test their luck on this double lap of the big ditch, despite the National Park Service strongly advising against it. (Signs around the Grand Canyon warn of heat illness, elevation, and difficult evacs.)

Some have called R2R2R an obsession—while I’m not sure I would personally go that far, I do see the argument. I’ve run the route three times and each trip I find myself more enamored than the last. On my last visit in 2023, a peak autumn weekend with nearly perfect temps and all the water facets still running (the NPS shuts some of them down seasonally), I saw hundreds of runners attempting R2R2R. That’s a tiny fraction of the four and a half million people who visit the park each year, but very busy for a trail run.

However, that was before the closures. The North Rim of the Grand Canyon was shuttered for most of the 2025 season due to the Dragon Bravo Fire, which burned 149,000 acres and 118 buildings. Then, on August 29th, 2026, a flash flood struck Phantom Ranch at the bottom of the Grand Canyon, killing three and forcing 82 others to be evacuated by helicopter. Trail damage was widespread, taking out footbridges and much of the park’s water line. The North Kaibab Trail, parts of the Bright Angel and South Kaibab trails, and sections of the River Trail remain closed with no set date for reopening.

It’s getting colder and darker across most of the country. That means one thing: It’s desert season. In lieu of a double dip into the Grand Canyon—and to open your mind to the endless possibilities elsewhere—I’ve compiled a list of my six favorite trails across the American Southwest. All of them are in my upcoming book, “The 50 Classic Trail Runs of America” (which you can pre-order now!). They cover six different states and four unique desert ecosystems, so there is plenty of variety to choose from.

Unlike the rocky singletrack of the Northeast, the forested trails of the upper Midwest, or the alpine ridges of the West, running across the Southwest desert is a singular experience. Limited shade, even less water, and direct solar radiation create obvious hydration challenges. The temperature swings dramatically from near freezing overnight to scorching heat midday. The trails themselves are a mix of surfaces, from soft sand to loose scree to firm slickrock, with many—including the ones listed below—being very remote.

Beyond the physical experience, running in arid environments carries a different emotion, too. I often find myself in a quiet and contemplative mental space, passing by ancient saguaros, joshua trees, and prickly pears while trying to stay alert for subtle movements of rattlesnakes, lizards, and coyotes. Running in the desert is, simultaneously, the antidote to life’s complexity and a plague of endless possibility.

There are four distinct desert ecosystems in the U.S. The northernmost is the Great Basin, which covers most of Nevada and parts of neighboring states, making it the largest desert in the country. With frigid winters, it’s filled with sagebrush instead of cacti. Just south is the Mojave, the smallest of the four, which is home to Death Valley and Joshua Tree. Further south is the Sonoran, the “green desert,” with large saguaros and rainfall in both summer and winter. Last, the Chihuahuan, covers West Texas and southern New Mexico, and is known for succulents like Agave, Yucca, and Creosote.

Hidden in one of North America’s most remote national parks, Big Bend, the Outer Mountain Loop is a 32-mile circuit featuring a stark ecological contrast between the Chihuahuan Desert and the high-alpine Chisos Mountains. Beginning and ending in the Chisos Basin, a sky-island that’s home to rare species like the Colima Warbler, the trail plunges down stone steps and rolls across the sun-scorched flats of the Dodson Trail before making a grueling 3,000-foot final ascent up Blue Creek Canyon. Early Spanish explorers called this isolated region El Despoblado.

Why I love it: Getting to experience both sky islands and the solitude of the Chihuahuan desert.

Spanning 39 miles from the edge of the Navajo Nation to the Grand Canyon’s South Rim, the Little to Big Colorado route is the less conventional way to explore one of the country’s most popular national parks. Tracing the historic Hopi salt journeys, the route plunges down Bekihatso Wash, follows the Little Colorado to its confluence with the Colorado River, passes sacred sites, scrambles obstacles like the Papago Wall, and passes by billion-year-old rock. With a 4,700-foot grind up the New Hance Trail at the end, it’s just as challenging and rewarding as rim-to-rim-to-rim.

Why I love it: It’s so close to the most famous one day trail route in the country, but still unheard of.

Crossing the national park from east to west, this 37-mile rolling route passes two distinct deserts—the higher Mojave and the lower Sonora—as you wander by long shadows of Yucca brevifolia (Joshua trees) and massive, weathered granite batholiths. Championed by the activist Minerva Hamilton Hoyt after decades of cattle grazing and mining exploitation, this sandy sanctuary strips away the crowds, offering sheer solitude as runners cross ancient mountain passes, gaze down at the dark expanse of the Salton Sea, and experience a desert communion.

Why I love it: it’s the one place in America that feels straight out of a Dr. Seuss book.

One of the most remote routes we considered for the book, this 64-mile trek traces the ridgeline of the Toiyabe Range. Like Outer Mountain, this area is a sky island, a rare ecosystem in which the valley below is a scorching hot desert, but the trail itself is a lush forest of aspen and bristlecone pines, some 3,000 years old. Despite being Nevada’s longest named trail, Toiyabe is off-trail in nature, with significant routefinding and some sections of unrunnable terrain.

Why I love it: Remote and rugged, it’s a good test of navigation and alpine movement of all sorts.

Perched above Albuquerque, this 26-mile trail follows a limestone ridge along the backbone of the Sandia Mountains, with 360-degree views of the Rio Grande Valley below. Sandia is Spanish for watermelon, named after the ridge’s unique sunset alpenglow that turns the cliffs deep pink, with green conifers on top representing the rind. The trail features wildflowers, ponderosas, spruce, and white fir, and can be accessed via an aerial tramway, providing easy access without sacrificing its rugged, aesthetic appeal.

Why I love it: Quick and easy access paired with absolutely beautiful sunsets.

Winding through one of Southern Utah’s most distinctive landscapes, the 30-mile Bryce Canyon Traverse links the heavily trafficked northern amphitheater where thousands of hoodoos, rock fins, and natural arches create a natural maze with the primitive Under the Rim Trail to the south. Crossing a geological marvel shaped by erosion and freeze-thaw cycles over millions of years, the traverse is the best way to see it all. Heading southbound allows runners to catch sunrise over the spires before descending into remote backcountry, which is the direction we recommend.

Why I love it: There’s no place in the world with the same density or volume of hoodoos.
