Dad who discovered daughter's murder on Facebook feels ignored by South African police

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Okuhle's body was found dumped near a river in August. She had two children, aged three and nine, and was a civil engineering student

Tuku Nkosiyaphantsi found out his daughter had been killed from a post on Facebook.

It was August and he hadn't been able to reach 27-year-old Okuhle for days, although it wasn't unusual for her to drop out of contact from time to time.

Then one Saturday, two of Tuku's neighbours called him to say that they had seen that Okuhle had been reported missing by police on Facebook. They had also seen a photo of what appeared to be Okuhle injured.

"When I looked at this photo, I saw that the person in it was dead, and when I looked closer I realised it was Okuhle," says Tuku, 50.

After seeing the image, Tuku phoned a police officer he knew. The policeman confirmed the woman in the picture had been killed and her body dumped near a river in Kokstad, an agricultural hub in South Africa's KwaZulu-Natal province, in the east of the country.

But because the morgue was closed for the weekend, it wasn't until Monday morning that Tuku was able to identify Okuhle's body.

When he did, Tuku saw she had been mutilated and her face badly beaten.

"It was very painful, so much that I cannot accept it."

Okuhle's father, Tuku, says he feels "ignored and unseen" by the government after his daughter's death

Okuhle had two children, aged nine and three, and was a civil engineering student.

She is among 581 women who were killed in the country between July and September, according to the South African Police Service.

Overall, there were 5,590 murders - 204 fewer than in the same period last year.

It is unclear whether the number of women killed is an increase or decrease on previous years as police had stopped releasing this breakdown until now.

There has been a renewed national outcry this year about the scale of violence against women after the partially naked and bruised bodies of 12 women were found dumped in public areas in Ekurhuleni, a district to the east of Johannesburg, this summer.

South Africa has one of the highest rates of gender-based violence and femicide - the intentional killing of a woman or girl - in the world.

In response to this summer's killings, President Cyril Ramaphosa promised safety audits across every municipality to identify and address dangerous spaces for women, as well as an increased police presence in the worst-affected areas.

But in places like Kokstad, as well as Kempton Park, in Ekurhuleni, the BBC World Service saw people taking the protection of women into their own hands.

For Tuku, that means investigating his daughter's killing himself.

Months after she was killed, he says no-one has been arrested.

"It was just silent after we buried Okuhle - there was nothing at all. I so wish the police would stand up and investigate."

He believes police failed to review CCTV footage from a local government building in the town that may have shown who his daughter was with on the day she disappeared. The footage is wiped every seven days, he says.

The BBC approached the South African Police Service for comment but at the time of publication, no response had been received.

The police service has previously acknowledged shortcomings in its overall response to gender-based violence and femicide, adding that it was continuing to invest in addressing these challenges. Acting Police Minister Firoz Cachalia has said there needs to be "greater efforts" to prevent violence against women and children.

The killing of women this summer sparked widespread protests across South Africa

According to a 2024 report by the South African Medical Research Council, the number of unsolved cases of femicide was growing.

This year, Amnesty International said South Africa's police service was "under-resourced, poorly equipped, and insufficiently trained to respond effectively and sensitively to gender-based violence".

Tuku says he is now searching for CCTV footage and seeking out people who knew his daughter in the hope of finding leads.

Despite Ramaphosa's promises, he has little faith anything will change.

"I listened to Ramaphosa. The government acts only when something happened. We in Kokstad, we feel like we are ignored and unseen. The government does not care for us."

Asked earlier this month by the BBC what progress South Africa had made to keep women safe, Ramaphosa said the government would be allocating extra money in February's budget to deal with gender-based violence.

Speaking as he cast his vote in local elections, Ramaphosa added that the government had increased police numbers in the last three years "and we will continue to do so, so that there can be more visible policing".

Kokstad residents have started night-time patrols and are maintaining a curfew that advises children to be indoors by sunset and women by 21:00

Okuhle is not the only woman to be killed this year in Kokstad.

Between January and October this year, the bodies of five women and three very young children were found in the town, with arrests only taking place in two of the cases.

In response, this summer, the community set up street patrols and a curfew for women and children.

Since June, children have been told to be indoors by sunset and women by 21:00.

Kamvalethu Cetywayo, 34, from a local neighbourhood watch group, leads a group of around 15 men who patrol crime hotspots in the town at night, including areas where bodies have been found or trouble has been reported to them.

The patrols tend to take place between Thursday and Sunday because this is when people drink alcohol most heavily, says Kamvalethu.

Most of the patrollers carry sticks, whips and torches, but Kamvalethu has a licensed gun. He says the group will call police when they need back-up.

Asked how he feels about restricting the freedoms of women and children, Kamvalethu says the curfew helps keep them safe, is voluntary, and a temporary measure until police arrest the killers.

"With the resolution we made with the community, we get to assist them with this, but if people are refusing there's nothing we can do."

Even so, not all victims are attacked at night or on the streets.

Kgothatso says she was taking a taxi to work when the driver attempted to kidnap her

Kgothatso Mojela was kidnapped on her way to work in 2018 in Kempton Park.

She was in a taxi when the driver locked the doors and started driving in the wrong direction.

When Kgothatso told him he had missed the turn, she says he started punching and slapping her.

"Immediately, I just knew my life was in danger," says Kgothatso, who is now 35.

Kgothatso says she forced the passenger door open and threw herself out of the moving taxi, badly injuring her knee and causing nerve damage to her eyes. Two security guards, who were nearby, came to her aid.

She says her attacker was later jailed for a number of crimes, including what he did to her, but was released last year. Kgothatso says she now fears for her life.

Yet while Kgothatso was attacked by a stranger, according to the South African Medical Research Council, 60% of women murdered in the country between 2020-21 were killed by an intimate partner.

In the wake of her attack, Kgothatso has made changes to how she lives, including buying an imitation gun to scare off would-be attackers.

She has also stopped running alone, and insists on someone accompanying her if she goes to meet a client she hasn't met before at work.

Such precautions are all too familiar to many women in South Africa.

Zama says her running group now takes extra precautions during their runs, including carrying pepper spray and whistles

In September police urged women in the Kempton Park area to avoid walking or running alone, "particularly in isolated areas" - after the killing of 38-year-old mother Elizabeth Moselakgomo.

She vanished on 9 September while out running. Her badly bruised, partially clothed body was found dumped near a busy motorway in Kempton Park three days later.

It was a killing that resonated deeply within South Africa's running community, including sisters Zonke and Zama Mthethwa. They started The Pack, a women's running group in Johannesburg nine years ago. Elizabeth would sometimes attend The Pack events.

Runners across the city joined the search efforts following Elizabeth's disappearance, and took part in protests calling for violence against women to end.

Zonke and Zama's group now takes extra precautions during their runs, including carrying pepper spray and whistles. They also get cars to follow them and have started offering self-defence coaching to the women in their running group.

"We need to try and rally and create safe spaces for women to show up authentically and to do what they love doing," says Zama, 37.

Kgothatso told her story to her community for the first time at a prayer march to remember women who have been killed

Following her ordeal, Kgothatso moved from Kempton Park and now lives closer to her parents in another part of the country.

Her mother, a community activist, recently organised a prayer march to remember women who have been killed.

Kgothatso told her story to her community for the first time at the march.

Wiping away tears, she said: "To a survivor listening right now, I want to say you are not dirty, you are not broken beyond repair. You are not to blame and you are not alone."
