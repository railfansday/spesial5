When the Mountain Follows You Home: What Recovery Looks Like for Outdoor Accident Survivors

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

How a new wave of mental health professionals is helping people confront the trauma that can linger long after they make it back.

ON A LATE fall day in 2023, Laura Stilwell and her boyfriend, Marcus Wybrow, were descending the long mountain trail back to their vehicle after a day of ice-climbing among the rugged peaks west of Calgary, Alberta.

Wybrow, 29, was close to reaching full certification as an alpine guide. Stilwell, 27, had some backcountry experience, but this was her first time ice-climbing. They hadn’t attempted anything most climbers would consider difficult or risky. The point was simply to be in the mountains together, and for Wybrow to introduce Stilwell to a sport he loved.

Avalanche Canada, the main organization issuing the public avalanche forecasts that backcountry enthusiasts check before heading out, wasn’t yet forecasting for the season. (It was too early in the season to warrant forecasts.) Though there wasn’t much snow along the couple’s route, the pair carried avalanche gear to be safe.”

Then, as they hiked toward the trailhead, the snow suddenly cracked off the slope above and slid fast, overwhelming them in white chaos. The avalanche mostly buried Stilwell and fully engulfed Wybrow. It threw Stilwell onto her left side; her right hand was the only part of her body that remained unburied. With that hand, she was able to clear the snow from her face so she could breathe—then started digging herself out, screaming. She isn’t sure how long it took to free herself, but it felt like an eternity.

But this story isn’t about that horrific day. It’s about what happens after.

MANY SURVIVORS OF accidents—climbing falls, near drownings, mountain bike crashes, exposure, even near misses while hiking or trail running—suffer from post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Other mental and emotional injuries unique to outdoor accidents abound in these individuals too, including loss of identity and a severed connection to the natural world that once brought feelings of awe and peace. That’s all on top of anxiety, depression, and grief.

Yet we often don’t hear about this terrible aftermath, or how those suffering might find a way through it.

That’s because most narratives about adventure sport accidents tend to deal with the incident itself—the factors, both human and natural, that led up to it, and what it took to survive—while shying away from the psychological impact on those involved.

This is partly due to a culture at large that struggles to deal with mental health issues in general, combined with an outdoor one that has historically prized independence, stoicism, and strength. “Many of us learned how to disconnect from ourselves in order to respond to an emergency, but very few of us were ever taught how to reconnect once the emergency was over,” says Jenny Fiebig, a licensed clinical professional counselor and one of the first therapists to specialize in outdoor trauma.

At the same time, shockingly little access to mental health resources exists in the small mountain towns that are the hubs for these sports—despite the fact many of these areas have exploded in population since the pandemic, as has participation in the activities.

Climbing, ski touring, surfing, kayaking, and trail running are all among the fastest-growing sports in the country. But while many therapists are well trained in trauma, says Fiebig, they’re not always trained in outdoor culture. “If someone doesn’t understand why a person would willingly return to a landscape where they experienced trauma, or put themselves out there in the first place, it’s easy to misinterpret that decision as avoidance, denial, or unnecessary risk.”

The result is that those struggling with this complex trauma have had almost nowhere to turn, leaving a rapidly increasing subset of people isolated in silence. Until now.

Passionate recreationalists and mental health professionals are beginning to address that gap and offer alternative modalities to help outdoor accident survivors heal and to support victims’ families and communities as well. Some of these modalities are new, like peer-support groups and somatic nervous system exercises that help survivors reconnect with nature as part of the healing process. Others, like one-on-one talk therapy, are not revolutionary, but they’ve been dramatically underused for healing in a community that so desperately needs it.

Previously, the response to trauma in mountain towns was largely along the lines of “Let’s go drink a beer and forget about it,” says Fiebig, who works with Colorado-based Survivors of Outdoor Adventures and Recovery (SOAR), one of the organizations leading this movement that has nationwide reach and is growing. “Ignoring the signs or symptoms of PTSD affects relationships, jobs, marriages. It becomes systemic. There’s a high suicide rate in mountain towns, a high depression rate—mental health is a huge issue.”

In adventure culture, “we weren’t supposed to talk about our traumas,” says Darcy Chenoweth, DNP, a 49-year-old psychiatric nurse practitioner in Missoula, Montana, who’s also a mountaineer, climber, and skier. Chenoweth is on the board of directors for Canmore, Alberta–based Mountain Muskox, another leading organization in the field, for which she coauthored a paper on care approaches to trauma and loss in the mountains.

Once, when Chenoweth was talking through her own outdoor trauma with a friend with whom she ice-climbed, he told her she “was just being highly sensitive.”

This new movement is different from anything that came before because most of the professionals involved have been through outdoor trauma themselves, and they’ve seen firsthand how the care gap leaves survivors adrift in the hellish experiences that come after disaster. Also, given that adventure sports and the outdoor arena have historically been male-dominated—and traditionally masculine traits like speed, capability, and infallible expertise have been valued, while showing vulnerability was often seen as weakness—perhaps it’s no surprise that this movement is almost entirely women-led.

And for Laura Stilwell, it couldn’t have come soon enough.

AFTER THAT DAY in the mountains, Stilwell descended into a nightmare of grief tangled up with trauma, an emotional storm that many adventure accident survivors experience. She couldn’t sleep. When she woke in the predawn, she’d think, It’s too early, I can’t make it through a day this long. In the immediate aftermath, she was “a numb ghost, feeling nothing, not really living,” she says.

Then the pain roared in. Anything that sparked a memory of Wybrow, from books to clothing to photos, sent her into a sudden breakdown. Sometimes her grief wailed louder, the trauma manifesting in nightmares and hypervigilance. She’d lived in Squamish, British Columbia, for only a year at that point; her community was still fairly small and couldn’t identify with what she was going through.

Most of her friends were newer to adventure sports, “without a grasp of how bad things can be,” she says. “I was in that camp too, and then this happened to me, and it’s like that innocence just gets ripped away.”

Stilwell also grappled with thoughts of what she could have done differently that day, and battled with blame, shame, and guilt—another unique layer of trauma that outdoor accident survivors frequently suffer.

Many of these activities require an enormous amount of knowledge and skill, from snow science or river hydraulics to understanding weather, navigating remote terrain, operating technical equipment, performing companion rescue, and accepting inherent risk. An accident is often treated as a gap in that knowledge—a mistake of sometimes fatal proportions for which judgment, both from the self and others, is often lobbed liberally.

Consider, for example, the judgment-laden media coverage of the February 2026 avalanche near Lake Tahoe in Northern California that claimed nine lives, six of them mothers.

“Folks who choose to connect to outdoor spaces through recreation often experience a joy and connection that is hard to find in our manufactured way of life,” says Fiebig. “But when we put ourselves in the ‘risk’ of being outside, we also get shamed when things go wrong—a sort of ‘well, why did you choose to be there?’”

Chenoweth says that the rise of social media, in addition to glorifying adventure without acknowledging the social and emotional costs, only exacerbates the situation. These platforms allow “many people outside the community [to] live vicariously through the activities of mountain athletes. This often leads to outside judgment from those with no knowledge of the individuals involved in an incident or its context,” she writes in her coauthored paper.

The adventure community itself can also contribute to the shame survivors feel by scrutinizing the accident for mistakes. This is partly to learn from the incident, and partly a defense tactic to distance ourselves from the trauma.

“There’s a culture of analyzing all the data after an accident that takes the humanity out of the survivors,” says Fiebig. This scrutiny, she says, may send survivors into a “shame spiral.” “We need to know what happened, but we also need to make that space for the survivor to feel cared for, to feel validated that they did the best they could do, and recognize the pain that they just went through.”

Trying to navigate her own mental health, Stilwell did one-on-one therapy. It was a lifeline, but when a session would end, she’d panic knowing that she had to get through an entire week before the next session. She’d been a digital marketer, but she couldn’t work. She couldn’t focus, engage with other people, or regulate her emotions enough for a professional setting. She started watching television for up to 10 hours a day as a survival mechanism to distract herself from the pain, even while contemplating not being alive anymore at all.

SOME MAY STILL question why anyone would venture into the outdoors knowing that these pursuits might deliver such trauma and loss.

Science offers answers. Even just 20 minutes per day spent in nature can lower stress hormone levels, boost mental health, and increase life expectancy, and spending time outdoors provides an added sense of well-being specifically for women, per research. Time in nature serves as an escape from the everyday pressures unique to females (such as conforming to expectations about weight, appearance, and demeanor), according to an REI study on women in the outdoors.

But many recreationalists say it’s about more than what the research shows. The very presence of risk is part of what makes adventure so alluring and often so hard to describe to those outside of the community. Navigating situations with risk, where individual decisions, grit, and capability can determine personal fate, demands the kind of complete focus that some try to achieve through meditation practice—and is often impossible to find in our busy world.

Navigating those situations in the company of others can also be a profound experience. “I have built the strongest, deepest, most fulfilling relationships in my life while doing activities like climbing or ski touring,” Stilwell says. “You put yourself in a situation where there’s some risk involved, [along with] decision-making and fear, you have to manage your nervous system—it allows you to connect with somebody so much deeper, to get to know what motivates them, what scares them, what exhilarates them. And I feel so much more seen in those environments.”

Then there’s the awe factor. Awe, “our response to powerful things that are obscure, vast, and mysterious,” can have benefits from “calming inflammation to activating the vagus nerve, deactivating our brain’s stress center, to reducing pain perception,” according to Dacher Keltner, PhD, author of Awe: The New Science of Everyday Wonder and How It Can Transform Your Life. Awe often helps us transcend the self so that even our perception of time changes and we feel part of something bigger. Many people who love the outdoors will say, in one way or another, that connecting to this feeling through recreation might be the closest they come to grace.

But, says Chenoweth, “awe and tragedy are not separate. Both push us beyond ourselves, where the ideas we have about the world shift, and sometimes shatter, and human consciousness confronts the unknown. You can’t say, ‘I’ll choose awe, and avoid tragedy.’ The unknown holds both.”

STILWELL’S THERAPIST EVENTUALLY recommended that she check out SOAR.

Starr Jamison, an outdoor professional in Telluride, Colorado, founded SOAR after losing three friends to separate avalanches in a single year and wading through her own grief and traumatic experiences. She also watched people involved in or adjacent to accidents attempt or commit suicide after losing loved ones—including after a highly publicized avalanche in October 2017 on Montana’s Imp Peak that claimed the life of 23-year-old Inge Perkins, a rising star in the backcountry skiing and climbing worlds.

Perkins’s partner and famed climber Hayden Kennedy, 27, survived the slide but couldn’t locate Perkins, who was buried with her transceiver turned off. After searching in vain for hours, Kennedy drove home to their apartment in Bozeman, wrote a note describing where to search for her body, and then took his own life.

That tragedy spurred a reeling mountain community to reckon with unaddressed trauma. Organizations formed to support the professional community of guides, ski patrollers, wildland firefighters, and others who are outdoors for their jobs, including Responder Alliance, run by Laura McGladrey, and Climbing Grief Fund, started by Madaleine Sorkin.

But Jamison found that support for people who simply wanted to adventure for the love of it (rather than it being their job) remained nonexistent. Eventually she was connected to a couple of mental health professionals who were also considering how to bridge this gap, including Fiebig. SOAR piloted its first peer-support group for outdoor trauma survivors in 2021.

The organization now offers community connection workshops on topics such as mindfulness, survivor’s guilt, and neuroplastic healing. It also maintains a growing public list of counselors, therapists, body workers, psychiatric nurse practitioners, and resiliency coaches who speak the language of outdoor trauma and grief.

SOAR’s peer-support model is akin to Alcoholics Anonymous, but whereas AA is led by rotating members, these groups are led by professional facilitators. The sessions, which run monthly for five to six months, are free and often take place over Zoom, so they can be as accessible as possible. SOAR’s groups comprise people with common experiences: those who survived an outdoor incident in which another person perished; those who’ve lost a partner but weren’t on the scene; parents who’ve lost children to outdoor trauma; people who’ve sustained injuries in adventures; and others. Stilwell joined a group facilitated by Fiebig specifically for survivors of outdoor accidents in which their partners had died.

Like most of the women leading this movement, Fiebig, 46, follows the wounded healer arc (essentially, helping others as a means of healing yourself). She’s a whitewater boater who experienced several near misses with drowning in her 20s, and she once fell into a Class 5 stretch of river without a life jacket when the bank gave way while she was hiking. She chalked it up to not having enough skills and dismissed the accompanying emotional trauma as invalid, because, after all, she’d survived. It’s a common response, but insidiously dangerous. While not glaringly tragic, such experiences can have serious psychological impacts—and more so when they’re cumulative.

“I never sought help because of the shame of the experience—I felt like I could have prevented it,” Fiebig says. “Especially as a woman in the outdoors, it was easy for me to immediately go to blaming myself for not being good enough.”

Fiebig still grieves the two decades of her life during which she believed she was “not enough” because of what she perceived to be her mistakes, which made her pull back from whitewater even though she loved it. “I want to help marginalized communities work through their traumas so they’re not missing years of their lives, holding themselves back because of what happened.”

This movement, it must be said, is not exclusive to women. Far from it. While most facilitators and movement leaders are women, the people seeking help are split evenly between men and women, says Sarah Hueniken, 52, an alpine guide who cofounded Mountain Muskox, which runs in-person peer-support groups, after losing a good friend to an avalanche at one of her ice-climbing camps. Several men are also on her organization’s board of directors. Jamison, too, reports that SOAR’s workshops and support groups, while fairly balanced, are mostly composed of women.

WHEN STILWELL LOGGED on to her first group session with SOAR, she was apprehensive, wary, and unsure what to expect—which Fiebig says is normal. “I didn’t know whether people would have similar experiences to me or not,” Stilwell says. “I remember asking, ‘Is there space for grief here, or is it focused on trauma?’ ”

Fiebig told her that everyone in the group had lost someone in an accident and had a paired experience of both grief and trauma. Thank God. I found the right place, Stilwell thought.

Fiebig explains that a shared lived experience allows people to talk freely about what happened without feeling the need to hold back in order to protect others in the group. But before anyone is expected to share stories, Fiebig offers some psychoeducation on trauma and grief. She talks about the nervous system, fight-or-flight, dissociation, and other physical and emotional symptoms, which Stilwell called “epiphanies that validated how I was feeling.” Eventually, Fiebig says, members begin taking the group where it needs to go, turning it into “a kind of living space” with Fiebig there to ground and guide.

Peer support has been associated with reduced isolation and improved well-being, and it has helped survivors find an enhanced capacity for meaning after traumatic events, per research. It also provides perspective in introducing people to others at different points in their journeys, offering both validation and hope. And it allows for reciprocity. “Unlike traditional models of care that position individuals primarily as recipients, peer-based approaches enable a more fluid exchange. Individuals who are further along in their process can support those who are earlier, while continuing to engage with their own experience,” write Chenoweth and her fellow authors in their paper.

SOAR is currently training a new cohort of outdoor trauma–informed mental health professionals. And every peer-support group that SOAR and Mountain Muskox run creates new ambassadors who can help the next people in the community suffering from trauma and grief. Thus the movement—like all effective groundswells—is meant to be culturally transformative, changing the way we understand the motivations for connecting to nature through risky endeavors and the way we show care and support for people in the aftermath of the unthinkable.

ALONG WITH ATTENDING the support group, Stilwell is undergoing somatic experiencing therapy to address trauma and stress held in the body and nervous system.

“A lot of my trauma is on my left side, because that’s how I was lying in the snow, and my body remembered that,” Stilwell says. “I found it really hard to lie in bed on my left side. I was immediately anxious, my heart rate went up, my breathing pattern changed.”

Stilwell’s practitioner guided her in small movements and exercises that helped to desensitize her physical responses. Some practitioners, like Brooke Edwards, a resiliency coach who works with SOAR, do visualization and meditation and occasionally even take survivors back to the environments of their trauma to help them reconnect to nature through somatic exercises.

Stilwell might join the next support group that SOAR runs, because the path to healing is not linear. “There’s no end date to this,” she says. “I think Western culture sees grief as something that needs to be healed, not something that needs to be carried. You have to carry it with you your whole life. There’s no healing from it. Somebody who had a huge influence on my life is no longer there. I’ve had to relearn who I am on my own.”

She still has nightmares and wrestles with hypervigilance. She still grapples with whether she had enough experience to be out there that day. “It’s inevitable that you ask yourself, ‘If I had been the strongest, most fit, most practiced, well-trained person, could things have been different?’ I’ll never have the answer to that question. I don’t think the answer is always yes. Ultimately, Mother Nature is more powerful than us.”

But she went back to work in April. And while she’ll never ice-climb again, she says, she’s relearning her passion for playing in the outdoors, where she discovers so much capacity to see beauty and feels connected to Wybrow. He was the one who instilled that love in her. She feels she’s out there for both of them now.

This is also the first time Stilwell has shared her story publicly. It’s daunting, to say the least. “Connecting with people who have been through something similar has been one of the most useful things to me,” she says. “I feel like I’m ready to pay it forward. And if my story could help someone else, then it’s worth sharing. If I hadn’t found some of these resources…I don’t want to say I wouldn’t be here, but it could look very different than it does.”
