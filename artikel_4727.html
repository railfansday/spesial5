Do Your In-Laws Treat You Like Their Son’s Biggest Mistake?

Maybe you did marry the love of your life, but that still doesn't guarantee you'll win over the people who raised them. Some in-laws are loving and accept you with ease, while others seem to spend decades waiting for their son to finally come to his senses.

As a couples therapist, I've listened to stories of complicated in-law dynamics, ranging from mildly irritating to downright abusive, on a daily basis.

Maybe your career ambitions don't align with your in-laws' traditional expectations of what a wife and mother should be. You’re selfish for working or having a self-care routine, but if you stay home, you're accused of being lazy and mooching off their son.

Perhaps your mother-in-law was perfectly lovely for years, until your children reached adolescence, got their own phones, and your services in orchestrating family get-togethers were no longer needed. Suddenly, the woman who used to call you every week barely acknowledges your existence.

Maybe your sister-in-law crashed your family vacation, or your mother-in-law feels entitled to drop by your home whenever she pleases, and everyone expects you to go with the flow. Or your father-in-law has barely acknowledged you for a decade but couldn't contain his relief when your newborn baby looked exactly like his son and nothing like you.

You are bombarded with unsolicited advice about your finances, your diet, and your children's education.

Family relationships are complicated, and nobody should expect their in-laws to agree with or approve of every decision they make. However, repeatedly being made to feel unwelcome is not the same as a disagreement.

Secure-functioning relationships are built on the couple bubble, a protective partnership in which emotional safety and connection for the couple are prioritized. This includes managing thirds, which can be anything the couple navigates, including relationships, careers, hobbies, obligations, and even addictions that can interfere with the stability of the partnership (Tatkin, 2024).

Managing extended-family dynamics doesn't mean cutting ties, although in some circumstances, distance may be necessary. Nor does it mean demanding that everyone accommodate you at all costs. But it does mean recognizing that as you build your life together with another person, you become one another’s primary attachment figure. Protecting the stability and safety of that relationship is a shared responsibility.

Both partners should be able to trust that they won't be abandoned when things get uncomfortable, especially when the discomfort comes from each other's family of origin.

Consider a husband whose mother repeatedly criticizes his wife, dismisses her parenting decisions, or excludes her from important family conversations. The husband may feel that because his mother treats him warmly, the problem is between the two women. He may even insist that his wife is being too sensitive and expect her to let it go in order to keep the peace. This is exactly how the couple bubble begins to break down.

One of the more challenging dynamics in extended families is the relationship that seems perfectly pleasant until someone sets a boundary.

Your in-laws may be affectionate, attentive, and generous when you are agreeable, show up every Sunday for dinner, accept their unsolicited advice, and remain available whenever they need something. But the moment you decide to spend the holidays elsewhere or decline a financial request, the warmth disappears.

In healthy families, people are not loving only when they are being accommodated and unpleasant the minute someone exercises their autonomy. Boundaries around your home, time, money, and decisions that affect your immediate family are part of being an independent adult and building a life with your partner. Your in-laws might not agree with or love these boundaries, but they do have to respect them.

Children depend on their parents and caregivers to provide them with a safe container where they can play and grow. Their early attachment figures help them determine whether people are safe and predictable, whether conflict can be managed, and whether the people they depend on have the capacity to protect them.

Imagine a child at a family gathering where one parent is repeatedly interrupted, criticized, or excluded from the conversation. Perhaps their parenting decisions are openly questioned, or they are surrounded by relatives who make jokes at their expense while everyone else laughs. The child may not understand the content of the disagreement, but they can sense the tension.

When a parent is undermined, ignored, or humiliated in front of them and other family members, children may feel unsafe and receive damaging messages about loyalty and commitment.

This doesn't mean children need to see their parents as invulnerable or that witnessing an occasional disagreement will be irreparable. In fact, seeing adults navigate conflict respectfully and repair ruptures is an important part of development. However, repeatedly watching a parent be treated as though their feelings, authority, or presence don't matter disrupts the child’s sense of safety and trust.

Protecting children doesn't require pretending that everyone gets along. It does, however, require showing them that the adults responsible for their well-being can stand up for themselves and one another.

In couples therapy, one of the recurring challenges I see is the tension between loyalty to one's family of origin and loyalty to the partnership.

For some people, setting boundaries with parents can evoke a tremendous amount of shame and guilt. They may have grown up believing that keeping their parents happy was how they would be lovable, that disagreements were unacceptable, and that love meant accommodating everyone else's needs. As adults, they may continue to prioritize avoiding parental disappointment, even when doing so comes at the expense of their own family they're trying to build.

The partner who grew up in a family that is the source of conflict may have learned to tolerate behaviors that feel unacceptable, intrusive, or outright hostile to the other partner.

Damage to the couplehood is more preventable when the partner whose family is involved assumes responsibility for communicating boundaries.

The goal isn't to force everyone to like each other or to turn every insensitive comment into a family dispute. Healthy relationships don’t require everyone to be best friends, but they do require mutual respect.

Your in-laws don't have to think you're the greatest thing that ever happened to their family, but they do have to recognize that disrespecting you means disrespecting the family he's chosen to build.
