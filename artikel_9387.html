For How Long Do You Need to Meditate?

Picture this: 71 adults signed up for a study comparing brief to longer mindfulness practice. Many had never meditated, while others had dabbled years before.

Participants were divided at random into three groups: One group got 20 minutes of mindfulness meditation, a second group got five minutes, and the control group relaxed with audiobook excerpts from Bill Bryson’s A Short History of Nearly Everything, Bryson’s soothing recounting of the cosmos and our place in it.

In studies like these, “mindfulness meditation” typically means sitting quietly, eyes closed or softened, resting nonjudgmental attention on your breath or bodily sensations, gently noticing when your mind wanders (as it will), and just as gently bringing it back.

Now, you might expect that longer meditations would deliver greater benefits. After all, we’re conditioned to believe that more is better—especially when it comes to self-improvement. But this research and many other studies find that more minutes don’t necessarily mean more benefit: Shorter practices can work just as well—and sometimes better—while consistency may matter more than duration.

In this first study, each participant attended four 25-minute sessions over two weeks. In every session, the time not spent on audio-guided mindfulness-of-breath practice was filled by Bryson’s eloquence. To ensure the results reflected only the study activities, participants were asked to avoid any formal mindfulness practices outside these sessions.

After those two weeks, the five-minute meditators actually showed greater improvements in stress and mindful awareness (the tendency to stay present in daily life with less judgement) than the 20-minute group. Even depression and anxiety improved just as much—if not more.

Was the extra time with Bryson the magic ingredient for those five-minute meditators? The control group, who luxuriated in 25 uninterrupted minutes of his charm, showed no meaningful improvement. So while his voice may soothe, it wasn’t what moved the needle.

One five-minute meditator put it this way: “I always thought that to do mindfulness, I’d need a long time to dedicate to this, which seemed too challenging to even start. But I’ve learned that five minutes can help!” In contrast, a 20-minute participant admitted, “I think a shorter one would be better for me to learn mindfulness.”

There’s more. The facilitator observed that participants in the 20-minute meditation sessions more often fidgeted, shifted in their seats, and peeked through half-closed eyes, as if tranquility were just out of reach. Meanwhile, the five-minute meditators appeared more at ease and less prone to these signs of discomfort. Five of them even requested recordings to continue at home. The 20-minute group? Not a single request. For these beginners, less really did seem to be more.

Now, before you dash off to cancel that meditation retreat, remember that this study focused on people with little to no meditation experience. So if you’re a seasoned meditator, those longer sessions might still pack more punch for you, as we’ll explore later. And both the five-minute and 20-minute groups showed improvements in mindful awareness, depression, anxiety, and stress compared to the control group. The five-minute meditators just improved more when it came to mindful awareness and stress.

The takeaway? For beginners, a quick dip into mindfulness might be more refreshing than a long soak.

You might be wondering, Isn’t this just one study? Could it be a fluke? But multiple randomized controlled trials have explored how the length of meditation shapes its impact.

Microhabits: The Science of Habits So Small They Actually Stick

In the largest of these studies, researchers recruited 372 adults online—about half of them new to meditation—to see how the “dose” of mindfulness meditation affected mindful awareness and emotional well-being. Participants were randomly sorted into one of four groups: Some meditated for 10 minutes, others for 20, and the rest listened to a 10- or 20-minute National Geographic article on sequoia trees. (Because nothing soothes stress like a deep dive into bark thickness.)

What did the researchers find? The 10-minute meditators showed greater improvements in mindful awareness than those who listened to the 10-minute sequoia lecture. Surprisingly, the 20-minute meditation group didn’t outperform any group—not even the tree enthusiasts. In fact, the 10-minute group showed slightly greater improvements in mindful awareness than the 20-minute meditators, although the difference wasn’t statistically significant, meaning it could have occurred by chance. Still, it suggests that for boosting mindful awareness right after practice, 10 minutes might be at least as effective as 20.

But that was just one session. What happens with consistent practice? In another study, 77 college students were randomly assigned to meditate daily for 10 or 20 minutes. After two weeks, both groups showed similar improvements in stress and, again, mindful awareness. But those assigned to the 20-minute group experienced a slightly greater boost in self-compassion, the skill of meeting your own struggles with the kindness you’d offer a dear friend.

So if you’ve poured hours into longer meditations, you can rest easy—it wasn’t for nothing. The evidence suggests that whether your sessions are short or sustained, they can offer meaningful benefits.

Another notable finding is that the 10-minute group practiced more consistently, logging about 82 minutes total. The 20-minute group missed more days and didn’t always finish, ending up with just 103. Double the goal, barely a quarter more practice.

If you’ve ever tried meditating, you might be nodding along. Some sessions are bliss, while others are a tug-of-war with time. Maybe you’ve battled that nagging frustration, trying to keep your mind steady for more than a fleeting moment. Or felt the magnetic pull of distraction, your mind wandering just when you need it to stay put. As the minutes crawl, doubt can creep in: Am I even doing this right? Is it worth it? These tough moments can teach you a lot, but they can also make meditation feel intimidating—and make you less likely to stick with it, especially when you’re just starting out.   Another study looked at even longer sessions, comparing 10 minutes of meditation to 30 minutes. This experiment split 161 adults into four groups: 10 or 30 minutes of daily sitting meditation, or the same amount of mindful movement. The mindful movement was a stretching practice that brings mindful awareness into motion—less stillness, same presence.

After two weeks, everyone saw benefits: Mindful awareness increased, well-being improved, and distress levels dropped. But there were no significant differences among the groups. Whether they meditated for 10 minutes or 30, sitting or moving, all the groups seemed to benefit equally.

So, here’s the bottom line: One study found that longer sessions more strongly boosted self-compassion. Another showed that shorter ones more effectively reduced stress. But across studies, most of the key benefits—mindful awareness, mental health, well-being—weren’t tied to how long people practiced.

Why? Because as we’ll keep exploring, the nervous system learns through repetition, not duration. It’s consistency that reshapes patterns. Not heroic efforts once in a while, but showing up, again and again, however you can.

The benefits of consistency over length show up in observational studies, too, where people choose how often and how long to meditate. While observational studies can’t pin down cause and effect, they offer a richer view of how shorter practices play out in the real world.

Imagine it’s early morning. You reach for your phone and open Insight Timer, a free meditation app. Before your meditation, it asks, “How are you today?” You tap an icon—maybe “OK,” maybe “great”—capturing a snapshot of your mood in seconds.

Now imagine doing this regularly, for 14 months straight. That’s exactly what 10,409 people from 103 countries did in a groundbreaking study. Many were new to meditation, using the app to track their emotional ups and downs in real time. It’s like taking your emotional temperature—not with a thermometer, but with a single tap that sums up how you feel.

The findings? Consistency (four to seven days a week)—not duration—was the strongest predictor of better mood, resilience, and equanimity, the ability to stay cool under pressure. Think of it like watering a plant: A little each week keeps it happy and growing. Forget all month and drown it after four weeks? It wilts anyway.

For beginners in the Insight Timer study, short sessions—five to 10 minutes—outshone longer ones in improving mood over time. But after 20 sessions? Longer practices—21 to 30 minutes—began to take the lead in mood improvement. It’s like your brain needs to build its meditation muscles before longer sessions feel better than short ones—like a marathon that only gives you the high once you’ve earned it.

But do shorter sessions lose their impact as you gain experience? Not at all. They might just become your secret weapon. After 80 sessions, shorter practices make a late-game comeback, boosting equanimity and showing that even seasoned meditators can benefit from briefer sessions.

The idea that consistency beats duration is backed by a meta-analysis challenging the belief that longer equals better. Think of a meta-analysis as a “study of studies,” combining data from multiple research papers to uncover clearer, big-picture insights.

When researchers analyzed 203 randomized controlled trials totaling 15,971 participants, they found no association between meditation session length and better outcomes. In fact, a trend emerged in favor of shorter practices for reducing depression symptoms, although it wasn’t statistically significant. Still, shorter sessions were at least as effective as longer ones, calling into question the assumption that more minutes always mean more benefits.   So what’s the takeaway? When you’re starting out, don’t sweat the long stuff. Quick sessions might do more to lift your mood early on. As you settle in, longer practices might bring deeper emotional gains. But even if you’re a pro, short sessions don’t lose their value—they just shift their superpower, helping you stay steady when life throws you curveballs. Different strengths, different stages. And who doesn’t love a shortcut that actually works?
