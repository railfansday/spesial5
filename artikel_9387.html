Yayoi Kusama sets $15m record as Sotheby’s opens New York mega-sales

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Tom Eddison, Sotheby's co-head of contemporary art London, sells Yayoi Kusama's No. 3P.B. Courtesy Sotheby's

Just months after her death, a work by Yayoi Kusama set a new auction record at Sotheby’s in New York, as the auction house kicked off the marquee November sales on Monday night with back-to-back auctions that brought in $378.7m (with fees). The evening also saw records for Sam Francis, Jonas Wood, Firelei Báez and Tomma Abts. Not a bad start to a week in which auction houses expect to sell as much as $2.4bn in art.

Kusama’s No. 3P.B. (1960) fetched $12.5m ($15m with fees), against an estimate of $10m to $15m, setting a new auction record for the Japanese artist who died in August. One of Kusama's earliest landmark Infinity Nets series, the painting was included in a 1961 exhibition at the Whitney Museum of American Art, among the artist's earliest inclusions in an American institutional show. The work was the fifth most expensive lot in Sotheby’s The Now and Contemporary Evening auction, which brought in $224.78m ($268.7m with fees).

No. 3P.B. (1960) by Yayoi Kusuma. Courtesy Sotheby's

The 53-lot sale, which carried a pre-sale estimate of $226.2m to $323.1m—the highest since May 2024, according to Sotheby’s—saw two works withdrawn and five unsold, including works by Ellsworth Kelly, Kerry James Marshall, Roy Lichtenstein and Keith Haring, as well as Ayrton Senna's Formula 1 car inexplicably included in the sale. While around 45% of lots hammered within their estimates, bidding was uneven.

Roy Lichtenstein’s Reflections on “Painter and Model” (1990) led the sale in terms of value, hammering for $22m ($24.6m with fees) against an estimate of $20m to $30m. It was followed by Willem de Kooning’s Water...Soft Banks and a Window (Untitled VIII) (1975), which fell short of its $20m low estimate, hammering for $17.1m ($20.3m with fees). Andy Warhol’s Flowers (1964) fetched $15.2m ($18.2m with fees), just above its $15m high estimate.

Sam Francis’s Towards Disappearance I (1958) sold for $15m ($17.1m with fees), at the low end of its $15m to $20m estimate, setting a new auction record for the artist. It was a case of déjà vu: the same painting had previously set Francis’s auction record when it sold in 1999.

One of the most exciting moments of the evening came when five bidders battled for six minutes over Jeff Koons’s Bob-Tail (1991), a depiction of an Old English Sheepdog. The work ultimately hammered for $9m to a roar of applause.

Jeff Koons’s Bob-Tail (1991) Courtesy Sotheby's

Another standout result was Salman Toor’s Lavender Boy (2019), which went for $340,000 ($435,200 with fees), more than four times its $80,000 high estimate even without buyer's premium being factored in.

The evening kicked off with the single-owner sale of the collection of cable television magnate Theodore Baum and his wife Ruth. The 24-lot auction generated $90.8m ($109.9m with fees), comfortably within its $74.4m to $109m pre-sale estimate, with a 100% sell-through rate. However, one-third of the lots hammered below their low estimates, while just one-quarter exceeded their high estimates.

The top lot was Joan Mitchell’s Green Book of Barbara Guest (1965-68), which hammered for $16.2m ($19.3m with fees), among the highest results for the artist at auction, against an estimate of $12m to $18m. The Baums acquired the work in 1986 for just $65,000; including Sotheby’s fees, the winning bidder paid almost 300 times that amount.

Roy Lichtenstein's Two Paintings Sleeping Muse (Study) (1983) Courtesy Sotheby's

It was followed by an untitled 1998 Mark Rothko painting, which fetched $15.5m ($17.7m with fees), surpassing its $10m to $15m estimate. The Baums purchased the work for $115,000 in 1983, according to Sotheby’s, meaning Monday’s buyer paid more than 150 times that amount, including fees. The third most valuable lot was Andy Warhol’s The Scream (After Munch) (1984), sold for $6.8m ($7.9m with fees) against an estimate of $4m to $6m. Together, the three works accounted for around 42% of the sale’s $91.4m hammer total.

Not every work met expectations. Franz Kline’s Figure (1955) recorded the largest shortfall in dollar terms, hammering for $1.8m ($2.3m with fees), against an estimate of $4m to $6m—55% below its low estimate.

After the sale, Sotheby’s said works from single-owner collections achieved $239m (with fees), making up almost 60% of the evening’s total earnings.

“When your sale is the opener for a major season like this, there’s always a sense of heightened anticipation," Lucius Elliott, Sotheby's head of contemporary art marquee sales, said following the auction. "I think this evening’s sale did what everyone hoped it would—signaled that the market is in a great place and demand is strong. We saw real competition for coveted works, with a keen focus both on works from admired collections and for the works we specifically sought out for this sale to meet the demand we knew was there. The evening’s final tally—double that of the equivalent evening last season—says it all.”

On Tuesday night, the New York art world's eyes will turn toward rival auction house Christie's for the 20th century auction, led by paintings with eight-figure estimates by Wassily Kandinsky, Gustav Klimt and Pablo Picasso.
