Has Rockstar taken the "Grand Theft Auto" out of GTA 6? Or has it just gamified car-jacking properly for the first time?

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

It's impossible to have a big game launch these days without some section of the internet, somewhere, finding grievances with the final product because it does not align with the expectations they had pre-release.

The most recent upset comes in the form of Grand Theft Auto 6's revised take on, well, grand theft auto. In case you're one of the few still unfamiliar, grand theft auto is more than the titular moniker for Rockstar's premier crime simulation series; it's actually a US legal term for the act of stealing or attempting to steal a motor vehicle. In most previous GTA entries, this was a central part of the core gameplay loop: accept a mission, nick a car, complete the mission, nick another car, getaway. It's an endemic part of the loop, and has become so popular and identifiable as such that even other games - Watch Dogs, Saint's Row, Cyberpunk 2077, and so on - have emulated the mechanic.

So it makes sense that the internet is in a bit of a spin about GTA 6's more mature and restrained take on its eponymous crime. A quick, no-frills getaway in a vehicle you've hijacked on the road isn't always the most effective option. A mindless crime spree on the streets of Leonida is not a simple thing to pull off, nor an easy thing to live down. In this game, actions have consequences, and an ill-timed or ill-judged car jacking can quickly and efficiently put an end to your criminal ambitions.

That's not to say you cannot simply steal cars. Of course you can: the option to saunter up to an occupied vehicle and jack it from its registered owner is available to you from the very beginning of the game. But the citizens of Leonida aren't going to take your wanton act of foolishness lying down; many of the motorists in the county will fight back, pull a piece on you, or run you over mid-robbery attempt. Even if you are lucky to find one of the more pliant residents of the city, you might find something peculiar is happening in their car. I jacked a woman in the city over the weekend, and the cat she was ferrying from place to place decided to attack me, causing me to crash. I've heard other people say they've had muffled cries coming from the boot of the car where someone is transporting a hostage, or incidents where they've jacked the same person multiple times, leading to the poor car's owner to have a breakdown in the middle of the city.

That cat event, the team here at Eurogamer has worked out, is an early game trigger - it seems stealing random cars early in the game will increase the chance of this encounter occurring. Rockstar's guiding hand is more visible here, then, in trying to teach you a lesson: don't steal cars. You're given your own - Jason's two-door coupé utility, a Vapid Creado - that I actually formed quite an emotional bond with in the opening 10-or-so hours of the game. 'Look after this car,' Rockstar insists, tutorialising you how to repair it, customise it, make it all pretty so it's in good condition when you go pick up Lucia for the first time.

Shortly after the 10 hour marker, narrative events whisk you away elsewhere and you're given a 'slim jim', a long, thin tool that allows you to break into locked cars - something you could only do with a quick elbow jab before. The benefit here is two-fold; you can break into cars without setting off their alarms, and without breaking glass and lowering their resale value (something quite important in the earlier sections of the game). So, it takes you until Chapter 2 to start being able to more reliably and consistently break into vehicles. Good.

And it'll take even longer to break into keyless vehicles. We're in 2026 after all, and it's heavily implied this is when GTA 6 is also set - it stands to reason that many cars run keyless fobs, and your old-school tools won't cut it if you want to break into a Zorruso, rather than an old Vapid truck. But then, even when you're well-armed and ready to start an illustrious new career as Vice City's most nefarious car thief, there's more to consider. Most modern cars also come with trackers: standard trackers simply prevent you modding cars you don't own (not an issue you'll face very often), but advanced trackers are more problematic.

Boosting a car with an advanced tracker immediately alerts the cops to your location, and they close in on you fast. The only answer to this problem is flooring it, and outspeeding the signal ping the tracker is sending out: you need to drive fast and cleanly in order to stay mobile long enough for the tracker to deactivate, and this often incurs damage that makes selling these cars to fences a less viable option. You can't even sell cars with trackers installed at all to some fences until you're much, much further into the game.

So, long story short, stealing cars is a faff in GTA 6. But I like that. I like that Rockstar has evolved the gameplay formula to more accurately reflect the state of the world in 2026. Modern problems require modern answers, and given that Rockstar has gone above and beyond in its creation of a sandbox simulation of Florida, I feel the immersion would be broken by just being able to saunter up to any car you want and break into it. No, needing a 'slim jim' or a key cloner - or needing to case my victims properly before trying to jack them - makes for a better game as far as I'm concerned. It makes me roleplay Jason and Lucia better, it makes me engage with the game's systems more, it makes me appreciate the cars that I do have whilst I have them.

It also improves the flow of the game and how I interact with missions. I recently played GTA: Vice City, and after most missions were wrapped up, I would simply fling the nearest motorist onto the sidewalk, hop in their car, and drive away into the Malibu-styled sunset. I can't simply do that in GTA 6: I need to think about my getaway options, whether there's heat on my car, and where my next best (and cheapest) ride is going to be if a robbery or raid goes awry. 'Ah,' I think, 'I understand why there's a garage next to that safehouse on the map now.'

In making the act of grand theft auto harder and more awkward, Rockstar has nudged me into playing Grand Theft Auto the game like a more thoughtful criminal. I play it like someone on the run, who wouldn't just be indiscriminately committing wanton acts of felony theft on a whim, just because I didn't want to run four blocks down the road to meet my pals for a drink. It's a gorgeous example of functionality, design, and narrative dovetailing - one of the key reasons this is a five star game. The complaints about the system, to my ear, don't reflect what Rockstar has built here. Plus, if it bothers you that much, you could always try to play GTA 6 like a mindless thug if you really want to - just expect the world to treat you like one, too.
