The Boulies EP600 office chair gave me an existential crisis

The Boulies EP600 is a conundrum of an office chair that gets something wrong for everything it does right. The lumbar support is one of the best, but the backrest doesn't seem to be in the right place; the armrests are mind-bogglingly adjustable but also rock-hard. The result is a chair that fails to deliver on the comfort it promises.

Am I doing this wrong? As a chair reviewer, I’d like to think I’m better at sitting on chairs than most people. I put a lot of thought and effort into how I position myself on a chair. But the Boulies EP600 has made me question how good I am at sitting on things.

On paper, the Boulies EP600 can be adjusted to a ridiculous degree. It has an innovative floating lumbar support that slides up and down depending on your fore or aft position, which would make it one of the best office chairs for back pain. In reality, it’s just not that comfortable.

The real highlight of the Boulies Ep600 is the lumbar support, a really smart piece of counterbalanced engineering that tracks up and down with your lumbar as you move forwards and backwards on the chair. It achieves mechanically what the Hbada X7 failed to do electronically, with none of that chair’s irritating motor noises and intrusive micro-adjustments. It can be locked in place, too, but I found the floating mode suited me really well.

While the lumbar support is the best feature here, it’s coupled with the absolute worst: the back support. There’s nothing wrong with the support itself, but it’s positioned too far back in relation to the seat pad. If you want back support, then you have to lean right back in the seat, and you’re then an unfathomable distance away from your screen. The headrest is fine, but its bracket either goes higher or more forwards, but never both. As a taller person, I found that the chair was conspiring to position my head as far away from the monitor as is physically possible.

This distance is what got me questioning whether I’ve been sitting properly for all these years. Are you meant to sit like your computer screen is repulsive? It's something that I’ve been aware of in other chairs, but here it’s really noticeable: ideally you should be able to touch your screen with a fully stretched arm when your back is supported, but the Boulies EP600 puts a good few inches between my fingertips and Reddit.

When you want to relax, the chair does lean back further, and while the pull-out leg rest is appreciated, it’s made of rock-hard plastic. I’d sacrifice the leg rest for a chair that leans forwards to offer back support when you’re in those intensely focused sessions — it was a feature I really appreciated when I reviewed the Titan Evo NanoGen.

Elsewhere in the Boulies EP600 it’s business as usual. The armrests flex and pivot all over the place, and even tilt upwards or out of the way completely, but they’re coupled with very, very firm cushions that make you painfully aware of your elbow bones. The seatpad also moves backwards and forwards to accommodate different leg lengths and preferences.

From the front, the Boulies EP600 tends towards the uglier side of office chairs, with its curved back panels resembling an insect: thorax, abdomen, and. It looks better from behind; its curved central column gives it an aesthetically pleasing structure, and its chunky armrests make it feel like the captain’s chair on the U.S.S. Enterprise.

One thing I like about mesh chairs (and this is mesh throughout) is that their transparency helps negate some of the weightiness of the design, and this is the case here. The grey colour scheme Boulies supplied has a slightly Y2K feel, especially in the silver plastic under the armrests and the metallic accent on the rear column. It’s also available in black, which looks even more insectoid.

I almost forgot to mention the pull-out leg rest, because I’d blanked it from my mind, but it’s really hideous; a single piece of plastic that wouldn’t look out of place in a 1970s hospital, which was truly the worst time for healthcare implements.

The Boulies EP600 is never going to win a good-looking chair contest, but this bug-like design language has become fairly standard in mid-range ergonomic chairs, and I don’t think it’s better or worse than any other.

One thing I’ve come to loathe in chair construction is bases that need to be screwed together. I get that it saves packaging, but it’s such a chore! Thankfully, Boulies has overcome this with arms that lever themselves into position when you stand on the centre and, oh my gosh, it’s one of the most satisfying things I’ve ever experienced. More of this, please!

The manual is a little basic, and there are a few bolts that are fiddly to get in the right place, but overall it’s a 15-minute process with very little that can go wrong. The fully built chair feels solid and well-made; there are no creaking parts and nothing wobbles.

At 21kg, it’s reassuringly heavy, and its wide dimensions make it tricky to move through my Hobbity Victorian English home, so this is one that you won't want to move around too much.

The Boulies EP600 carries a retail price of $489.99 / £429.99, but at the time of writing it’s $409.99 / £339.99, placing it firmly in the mid-range of ergonomic chairs.

For around the same price, you can pick up the Sihoo Doro C300 Pro, which includes a similar range of features. It lacks Boulies’ dynamic lumbar support and built-in leg rest, but it’s still a supremely comfortable chair with those all-important adjustable armrests.

If you want even more adjustability in a similar form factor, look no further than Hinomi’s H2 Pro. While its price point is a bit higher (it’s currently $569 / £569), it has adjustable points just about everywhere, and I found it really comfortable and supportive once I got the fit dialled in.

Boulies primarily makes gaming chairs, and it feels as if the EP600 hasn’t quite shaken off its specialisation. It could work well if you’re primarily into gaming or watching movies and occasionally delve into more focused tasks. The flip-up armrests make it good for people who like to draw at their desks or noodle on a guitar to get those creative juices flowing. But designers, editors, and chair review writers who like a more upright position for focused work just won’t find enough support here.

It’s a shame because the lumbar support is smart, the armrests are versatile, and the mesh design is comfortable and cool. It’s also one of the easiest chairs to assemble. A few tweaks, such as better back support and plusher armrests, and the Boulies EP600 would be an instant classic. But as it stands, it’s hard to recommend, and I'm confident that the problem is with the design, not the person who's sitting on it.
