Young children are fascinated by sharks – so why are older kids more fearful?

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

A survey finds most children like sharks, but older kids express more fear, raising questions about how views of these animals develop.

Sharks have a scary reputation, but many young children like them. Some think they look cool, while others like the way they swim.

A survey of parents of 374 children aged two to 12 found that 69% of the children liked sharks. Older children were more likely to say sharks scared them.

Brianna Le Busque, an environmental psychologist at Adelaide University‘s School of Physics, Chemistry and Earth Sciences, led the study. Parents asked the questions and recorded their children’s answers.

“What’s really interesting is that most of the children in our study actually liked sharks, and many were fascinated by them,” Le Busque said.

People are often less willing to protect animals they fear or dislike. That makes children’s early views of sharks worth understanding.

The few earlier studies on this topic mostly found fear. In a 2020 study, five-year-olds chose a face to show how a shark photo made them feel, and most chose a fearful face.

But those studies left out toddlers. They also usually asked children about sharks alongside other animals, which may have made sharks seem less appealing next to dogs, horses and dolphins.

For the new study, 323 parents took an online survey in 2024. They asked their children what sharks do, what they look like and whether they liked them.

Parents wrote down the answers word for word and noted any hand gestures. They asked brothers and sisters separately so they couldn’t copy each other.

The team counted a child as expressing fear if they called sharks scary, said they were afraid or clearly showed fear another way.

Saying sharks bite or are dangerous didn’t count, because knowing an animal can hurt you doesn’t always mean you fear it.

The most common reason children gave for liking sharks was that they were cool. One four-year-old girl said, “sharks are very cool.”

When describing sharks, 44% mentioned teeth and 33% said they were big. Most talked about sharks swimming and eating, although about one in five said they hurt people.

Some children mixed facts with imagination. A six-year-old boy asked whether sharks were reptiles, then answered himself, “no they’re not, they’re dinosaurs.”

Children who disliked sharks most often said sharks hurt people. One 10-year-old boy explained, “because they could eat me.”

Overall, 13.9% of the children described sharks as scary or expressed fear of them.

Older children did this more often, and the team’s analysis suggested the pattern was unlikely to be due to chance.

“But as children got older, we saw an increase in associations between sharks and fear,” Le Busque said.

“That raises important questions about what children are learning about sharks as they grow up and the role that society, media and other influences might play.”

The team found no clear difference between boys and girls or between children in Australia, the U.S. and the U.K., where most participants lived. How often children went into the ocean made no clear difference either.

The survey compared different age groups. It did not follow the same children as they grew up, so it couldn’t show how any one child’s feelings changed.

Books were the most common way children had come across sharks. About 65% had read shark books or had someone read them aloud.

Another 59% had seen sharks at an aquarium, and 38% had watched TV shows featuring them. About a quarter had seen shark films, including Finding Nemo and Jaws.

Some 13% had sung “Baby Shark,” which became YouTube’s most-viewed video in 2020.

The researchers think these experiences may help explain why many children liked sharks.

Watching sharks swim among other fish at an aquarium, for example, may help children see sharks as more than dangerous animals.

Films may leave a different impression. Older children were more likely to mention Jaws, a film long linked to negative views of sharks and even government responses to shark bites.

Finding Nemo also mixes friendly and frightening images. Bruce appears as a friendly shark trying to stop eating fish, but becomes aggressive when he smells a single drop of blood.

“Sharks are often portrayed in very dramatic and sensational ways, particularly when there is a shark bite or in popular films,” Le Busque said.

Still, the study did not test whether films changed children’s feelings about sharks. The researchers say that needs more study.

Among parents, 44% reported low fear of sharks and 30% reported high fear. Many less fearful parents said they lived far from sharks or could avoid them.

One parent wrote, “The same reason I’m not afraid of earthquakes – I don’t live in an area where they reside.”

The team found no clear link between parents’ fear and their children’s fear.

Some parents said sharks weren’t an everyday threat, which may give children few chances to see their parents react fearfully.

A larger study might find a small link, the researchers noted.

Parents may have influenced their children’s answers or misunderstood them. The researchers accepted that risk because toddlers can’t complete a survey alone.

Views of sharks also differ across cultures, and shark populations vary around the world. Only 14 children in the study were 12, and none were teenagers.

Children’s fear might ease during their teens as they learn more. But strong fears that start in childhood can also last into adulthood.

A recent review found that people who know more about sharks tend to fear them less. Earlier research also linked direct experiences with sharks to greater tolerance of them.

In another study, people who swam with sharks were more likely to report helping sharks two years later.

These findings suggest that learning and positive experiences may help, though they don’t tell us how each child’s feelings will change.

The researchers suggest using lessons and media to show children the many kinds of sharks, helping balance stories built around fear.

“Children are clearly capable of seeing sharks as fascinating, interesting animals, rather than simply something to be afraid of,” Le Busque said.

“Education and positive experiences could be really important in helping children understand sharks as diverse marine animals and appreciate the important role they play in our oceans.”

To learn whether fear fades or lasts, researchers will need to follow the same children into their teens.

They also want future studies to use a standard set of questions about fear, making the results easier to compare.

The study is published in the journal Human Dimensions of Wildlife.
