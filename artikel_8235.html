Are Ultralight, Ultrastrong Dyneema Rain Mitts Worth Hikers’ Money? We Tested Them.

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

In Gimmick or Gamechanger, the Backpacker editors test hiking gadgets to see what’s worth your money—and what’s a waste of it.

I’ve backpacked in my fair share of nasty conditions. On Vancouver Island’s West Coast Trail, I trudged through a downpour so heavy that I had to wring out my pants by the time I got to camp. In Gates of the Arctic National Park, I wore neoprene socks over my Darn Toughs to trap some warmth in my feet as I slogged through boggy tundra. Wet conditions, even when temperatures are mild, can be the most difficult in which to stay warm and comfortable. Looking back, I wish I had packed some rain mitts on those trips.

Most backpackers aren’t carrying waterproof overmitts. They serve a niche purpose of keeping your hands dry in wet but warm(ish) weather. In truly cold conditions, you need heavy-duty gloves. For summer showers, you can usually go without gloves or mitts entirely. But for hikes in steady rain with temperatures in the 40s and 50s? Rain mitts can work wonders to maintain comfort while taking up minimal space and weight in your pack.

As a hiker based in the PNW, I was excited when I came across a pair of ultralight rain mitts from cottage brand High Tail Designs, which is known for colorful printed fabrics and artist collaborations. Made for use alone or with a liner glove for warmth, they employ a blend of Dyneema Composite Fabric (DCF) and Dyneema Hybrid Fabric and weigh roughly an ounce per pair. They also run a whopping $105. What sets them apart from most other rain mitts on the market is the material and the fun aesthetics. Few other brands use Dyneema for hiking apparel; it shows up mostly in products like backpacks and shelters where strength is paramount. It’s also unusual to see Dyneema in colors other than black at white, as the material is difficult to dye.

Because it doesn’t absorb moisture, Dyneema dries faster than other waterproof materials like treated nylon. This also means it doesn’t stretch or bag out when exposed to lots of water. The flipside is that it doesn’t breathe—a Dyneema rain jacket would dry faster than a Gore-Tex one, but you’d likely be soaked from the inside after a few minutes of hiking. I wondered how those competing properties would work in a pair of rain mitts.

Off the bat, I was a fan of how the mitts protected my hands on rainy hikes. The fully taped seams kept moisture at bay, and I appreciated the large pull loops and cinchable shock cord wrists. They’re oversized for use with or without liners, so dexterity is limited, but that’s expected with any kind of mitts.

Other rain mitts, like those made with Gore-Tex, boast some level of breathability. These, in comparison, are basically a vapor barrier for your hands. They’re not meant to be breathable, and I found myself surprisingly unbothered by this. Hiking in a downpour with temperatures in the mid-50s, they kept my hands comfortable and dry, and I didn’t work up enough of a sweat over an hour to feel clammy inside. And since I care more about keeping my hands warm than dry, I don’t mind that these aren’t designed to breathe.

But does the fact that they’re made of Dyneema make them better than other rain mitts? What makes Dyneema appealing for outdoor gear is its remarkable strength-to-weight ratio, which can make all the difference in heavy-duty gear like backpacks and shelters. But it is invariably more expensive than other materials. In a small product like mittens, the weight savings become almost negligible, and the added tensile strength seems frivolous for the cost.

I haven’t done enough long-term testing of these mitts to speak to their durability over time, but I’m skeptical that they truly outlast similar products made from treated nylon. Generally, DCF is more tear-resistant than nylon, but it can also be more susceptible to abrasions and pinholes. While my pair hasn’t shown any signs of deterioration yet, the properties of DCF seem like overkill for a mitt where treated nylon will do.

Which brings me to the price. High Tail Designs’ rain mitts are $105 per pair. At 30 grams per pair (size small/medium), they are among the lightest rain mitts out there—but not by much. The Helium UL Rain Mitt from Outdoor Research costs half as much at $55, though it weighs twice as much at 68 grams per pair. Black Diamond’s UL Overmitts weigh a few grams less (23 grams for a size small) and are still more affordable at $70. I haven’t personally tested either of those mitts, but they are comparable in weight and function at a much lower price point.

As someone who hikes in everything from a drizzle to a deluge, I’m a rain mitt convert. I’ll be wearing these all winter long for lowland romps where fully weatherproof, insulated gloves are overkill but liners alone would soak through. They’ll also have a place in my pack on wet overnights or long-distance summer hikes where I need some just-in-case protection and don’t want to pack heavy gloves.

In my opinion, rain mitts as a category are a gamechanger. But it’s extremely hard to justify $105 for a pair made from the highest-end ultralight materials when there are cheaper options on the market that function similarly. For that reason, Dyneema rain mitts feel more like a gimmick. However, if you’re someone who puts a high value on style, that added cost may be worth it. High Tail Designs sells rain mitts in 11 different patterns; their company ethos is all about self-expression on the trail, and their colorful designs support real artists.
