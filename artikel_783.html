'Officials can't use common sense' - was Rogers handball correct?

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Leeds dominate Chelsea for first Stamford Bridge win since 1999

It is rare when a key handball decision is given for the widespread response to be "that is correct".

Often the discussion centres on the desire for a seemingly more lenient interpretation taking into account perceived intent.

And that is again a talking point after the crucial penalty given against Chelsea in Saturday's 4-1 home defeat by Leeds which ended up putting the visitors ahead for the first time.

Interestingly, despite the perception, handball penalties are much rarer in the Premier League compared with the other top five leagues in Europe.

Last season, there were 22 penalties for handball in the English top flight's 380 games - easily the lowest tally.

Spain's La Liga (36) and Italy's Serie A (38) produced the most.

This season in the Premier League there have been three in 106 matches - less than in 2025-26.

So why was the penalty given against Chelsea's Morgan Rogers?

The feeling among Premier League clubs is that handball is in a good place now, where the arm must either be fully extended away from the body, raised, or be a deliberate act to be punishable.

And it is the last of these that applied to the penalty given against Rogers, which to some might seem a bit harsh, considering the Premier League's more relaxed interpretation of handball.

There are, however, a few key reasons why referee Michael Oliver pointed to the spot, why the video assistant referee (VAR) did not intervene, and why it will be judged as a correct decision.

Two of the factors which a referee looks for when judging a potential handball apply here.

As Jayden Bogle's shot was from distance, a defender is considered to have more time to adjust their body and react - and thus avoid a potential handball.

Most important in this case is the movement of the arm.

When Bogle shoots, Rogers' arm is not raised - but it comes up to the flight of the ball.

Crucially, had Rogers kept his arm in its original position - lower and level with his midriff - there would not have been a handball offence.

What does this mean? Put simply, if the ball would have hit the body if it had not come off the arm, it is considered to have no impact.

But this case was different. Rogers raised his arm and that is what led to the ball being deflected away. This is considered a deliberate act, and this trumps all other considerations.

Perhaps there was a question about whether the ball hit Rogers too high on his arm, and thus should not be a handball offence.

It was close, but it looked like it was in what the laws call the 'red zone', the part of the arm which is punishable for handball.

But this really would be the only reason the VAR, Michael Salisbury, would have any cause to consider an on-field review.

We saw a similar case last season, in terms of how this kind of handball offence is judged - coincidentally involving a penalty awarded to Leeds.

As Anton Stach's powerful strike arrowed towards goal, Everton defender James Tarkowski leaned to his left in an attempt to block the shot - and did so with his arm, which was tucked tightly to the side of his body.

Everton manager David Moyes described the decision as "wrong", but it was absolutely correct in law.

That Tarkowski's arm was close to his side was irrelevant because of the deliberate act of leaning into the path of the ball.

Likewise, Rogers raised his arm to stop the ball and a penalty was a justifiable call.

Despite the Premier League having a more lenient interpretation of the handball law already, it is clear that many observers would like it to go further.

For example, BBC Match of the Day pundit Alan Shearer echoed many when he said there should be more intent for a handball.

"I didn't agree with the penalty when Michael Oliver gave it," he said.

"I know once the on-field decision is made there is a high bar to review it, but I don't think officials are allowed to use common sense.

"Morgan Rogers is not deliberately trying to stop the ball. He just doesn't want it hitting him in the face.

"Although the penalty came at an important point in the match, Chelsea were deservedly beaten by a much stronger, far more physical and better-organised Leeds team."

Rogers may argue he raised his arm to protect his face.

This is one of the possible mitigating factors, but usually only applies when a player raises their hands to their face - not using the arm as a barrier.

Chelsea boss Xabi Alonso said that, while he did not "want to make a big thing about it", the penalty should not have been awarded.

"For me it was quite a harsh decision," he told Sky Sports. "I would not have given a penalty. I don't want to make a big thing about it but for me it's not a penalty."
