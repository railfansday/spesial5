Most early-stage founders keep their runway in a spreadsheet nobody trusts. One cell is hard-coded, another formula was dragged one row too far, and the "what if churn doubles?" question means copying the whole sheet. A small, pure JavaScript module is easier to test, easier to version, and easy to rerun with different assumptions.

In this tutorial we'll build a runway calculator in plain ES modules, with no dependencies. It runs in Node 18+ or in a browser. We'll model monthly cash, run slow, base and fast growth scenarios, test how sensitive the plan is to churn, and add the unit-economics check (LTV, payback) that tells you whether growth is worth buying. Every number in this article comes from running the code below, so you can reproduce it.

A note on scope: this is a planning model, not a forecast. It's only as good as the assumptions you feed it. Where I make a simplification, I'll say so.

Runway is simply how long cash stays above zero. The interesting part isn't the arithmetic; it's seeing how the answer moves when your assumptions are wrong.

Two design choices worth noting. First, the function is pure: same inputs, same output, no hidden state, which makes it trivial to unit test. Second, new customers are an array rather than a single growth rate. Real acquisition is lumpy (a launch month, a slow summer), and an array lets you encode that. If you prefer a growth rate, generate the array first and pass it in.

The Math.round on retained customers keeps us in whole people. It introduces small rounding effects; for planning purposes that's fine.

A table of twelve rows is hard to compare across scenarios. We want a handful of numbers:

lowestCash is the number I care about most. Founders tend to ask "when do I run out?", but the better question is "how close do I get?" A plan that bottoms out at $1,785 and one that bottoms out at $14,327 both survive, yet only one of them survives a bad month.

scaleGrowth lets us ask what happens if acquisition comes in at half, or 130%, of plan without rewriting the plan.

Our assumptions: $40,000 in the bank, $4,500 a month in fixed costs, 15% variable costs, a $49 monthly price, 4% monthly churn, and a ramp from 0 customers in month one up to 26 new customers a month by month twelve.

Note that "break-even month" and "lowest cash" are different events. Cash keeps shrinking until the month before net turns positive, because net is still negative in month nine.

This is the table that changes decisions. In the slow case the business doesn't run out of money in the first year, but it ends at $1,785 and still isn't profitable. Month thirteen is a problem. That's the point at which you'd want a trigger written down in advance, something like "if month-six customers are below 25, we cut fixed costs or start fundraising conversations." Deciding that on a calm day beats deciding it with eight weeks of cash left.

Growth gets the attention, but churn quietly sets the ceiling. We can sweep it:

Look closely and a useful, slightly counterintuitive result appears: in a twelve-month window, churn is a mild effect. Going from 2% to 10% costs about $2,750 of lowest-cash headroom and delays profitability by one month. Growth moved the outcome far more than churn did.

That isn't a reason to ignore churn. It's a reason to be careful about the time horizon. Churn compounds: the customer base you're building at month twelve is the base you'll be paying to replace in year two. Extend the newCustomers array to 36 months and rerun the sweep. The gap widens considerably. A short horizon flatters bad retention.

Two caveats. The LTV formula (monthly gross profit ÷ churn) assumes constant churn, which overstates lifetime value if early-life churn is higher than late-life churn, as it usually is. And a CAC of $150 is an assumption; a ratio of 6.9 would be unusually healthy, which should make you suspicious of the input before you celebrate. A common rule of thumb is that a ratio around 3 is solid, but treat any benchmark as a conversation starter, not a verdict.

Cheap-to-compute doesn't mean cheap-to-measure. Real CAC includes your time, tooling and any content costs, and most founders undercount it.

The takeaway is the habit more than the code: write your assumptions down as inputs, run the bad cases before reality does, and decide your response triggers while cash is still comfortable.
