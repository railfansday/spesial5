Adding limestone to farmland is helping to store more carbon dioxide than it releases

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Agricultural liming in the Mississippi Basin removed more carbon dioxide than it released, after accounting for acid from fertilizer and pollution.

Limestone is rock made partly of carbon, so spreading it on farm fields sounds like a way to put carbon dioxide into the air. Climate accounting rules treat it that way.

However, a new study finds that, over the past century in the Mississippi River Basin, the limestone that farmers spread has removed more carbon dioxide than it released.

The old math leaves out the acid. Fertilizer and air pollution add acid to soil and water. That acid releases carbon dioxide, whether or not anyone spreads lime.

Measured against a basin with the same acid and no limestone, the lime has worked as a carbon sink.

Geochemist Tim Jesper Suhrhoff of the Yale Center for Natural Carbon Capture led the work with colleagues at Georgia Tech and elsewhere.

The team paired a computer model of soil chemistry with a century of river records from one giant watershed.

In an interview with Earth.com, Suhrhoff said the result adds to what farmers already gain from lime.

“Liming may provide a public climate benefit on top of the benefits farmers already get from healthier soils and better yields,” he said.

Many farm soils slowly turn acidic, and crops grown in acidic soil tend to yield less. Farmers fix that by spreading finely ground limestone or a similar rock called dolomite, a practice known as agricultural liming.

Between 1935 and 1950, lime use in the U.S. rose from about 1.1 million tons (1 million metric tons) a year to about 22 million tons (20 million metric tons).

Most of it went to the Mississippi River Basin, the land drained by the Mississippi and its tributaries. It holds about 65 percent of the cropland in the contiguous U.S.

When limestone dissolves in acidic soil, it gives off carbon dioxide. So the default method from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) treats all the carbon in applied lime as emitted.

The U.S. national inventory assumes about half of it escapes.

When nitrogen fertilizer and manure break down in soil, they produce acid. Burning coal and oil sends sulfur and nitrogen pollution into the sky, and it comes back down as acid.

Rivers carry carbon dissolved in a form called bicarbonate. When acid reaches that water, it turns some of the bicarbonate back into carbon dioxide, which escapes into the air.

Across the basin, acid from farming and pollution far outpaced the lime. By 2015, about half of all the acid added since 1900 hadn’t been offset by lime, mostly on land that sees little liming.

In parts of the Corn Belt, though, decades of liming matched or exceeded the acid.

In 1980, the team’s model estimated, acid alone would have released about 22 million tons (20 million metric tons) of carbon dioxide.

Adding lime cut that to about 17 million tons (15 million metric tons). The IPCC and U.S. methods came in lower, at roughly 8 million and 4 million tons (7 million and 3.5 million metric tons) respectively.

So the standard methods assign the emissions to the lime and miss most of what the acid does.

“I was also struck by how much excess acidity has been added to the system over the last century, implying that agricultural CO₂ emissions from acidification may have been substantially larger than we usually account for,” Suhrhoff told Earth.com.

To check the model, researchers pulled more than seven decades of water chemistry from five U.S. Geological Survey stations between Vicksburg, Mississippi, and Belle Chasse, Louisiana. Samples from the early 1900s filled in the start.

The team compared the bicarbonate that the river carried once liming took off with what it carried before. They traced the extra to about 440 million tons (400 million metric tons) of carbon dioxide. This was removed between 1900 and 2015.

That’s roughly 90 percent of what the lime could have removed if all of it had reacted in the ideal way.

The soil model, built up from conditions across the basin, estimated about 240 million to 410 million tons (220 million to 370 million metric tons). Two different methods reached nearly the same answer.

“I was surprised by how well the bottom-up modeling and top-down river data converged, and that we could actually see these trends at the scale of the entire Mississippi River Basin,” Suhrhoff said.

When liming first ramped up in the late 1930s, the model shows a brief burst of carbon dioxide before removal began. Calcium from dissolved lime attaches to soil particles and releases acid stored there.

That stored acid has to be neutralized before much of the lime’s effect reaches the river.

In the river records, carbon left the basin decades after the limestone went down. The authors warn that carbon removal programs that credit lime as soon as it’s spread could overstate the near-term benefit.

Liming is a close relative of enhanced weathering, which means spreading crushed rock on fields to draw carbon dioxide out of the air.

“This is a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity to trace more than 120 years of historical records that can tell us whether enhanced weathering works,” said Noah Planavsky, a corresponding author at Yale University.

“I would caution against assuming that the Mississippi result translates directly everywhere,” Suhrhoff said.

The early emissions, the delay and the amount of carbon removed all vary, he added. They depend on how much lime is used, the local soils and how water moves.

Where acidic soils hold back harvests and farmers can’t afford lime, he said, subsidies or pay-for-practice programs could widen access. Three of the authors advise CREW Carbon, a company that adds lime in wastewater treatment plants rather than on farm fields.

For most of the Mississippi’s own tributaries, the records aren’t long or continuous enough to separate lime’s effect from everything else.

The authors call for more regional studies wherever long, detailed records exist. Spreading more lime than the basin’s farmers did would need its own look at local soils, water and that early burst of emissions.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.
