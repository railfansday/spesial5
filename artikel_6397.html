What happened when scientists kept birds and bats away from cocoa trees?

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

An experiment on cocoa farms in the Peruvian Amazon reveals how nearby trees, birds, and bats can help protect the harvest.

In the Peruvian Amazon, the trees beyond a cocoa farm can help determine how much cocoa it produces.

Farms surrounded by forests, orchards, and other tree crops had some of the highest yields in a new study, and an experiment pointed to an unlikely pair of allies: birds and bats.

To find out how much those animals mattered, researchers put nets around some cocoa trees and left others open.

Where forests covered 30–35% of the surrounding landscape, the cocoa trees that birds and bats could reach produced nearly twice as much as the netted trees.

An international team led by the University of Vienna mapped the connections between shade trees, wildlife, and cocoa productivity across 28 organic agroforests in the northern Peruvian Amazon.

The researchers estimated that cocoa trees accessible to birds and bats would yield about 265 kilograms per hectare per year where trees covered roughly 20% of the surrounding landscape.

Where surrounding tree cover reached 70–80%, the estimated yield was nearly four times as high, at about 1,000 kilograms per hectare per year.

At 2023 cocoa prices, the researchers valued the extra cocoa associated with bird and bat activity at about US$1,640 per hectare per year.

For smallholder farmers in a region where average incomes are modest, that’s not a rounding error. It’s the difference between scraping by and building something.

“Birds and bats can be powerful allies for cacao farmers, but only if the surrounding landscape provides enough habitat. If these landscapes become large monocultures, much of this natural pest-control benefit is lost,” said senior author Bea Maas from the University of Vienna.

The intuitive assumption – more shade trees, more biodiversity, better farm – doesn’t hold up cleanly.

Shade-tree cover benefitted wildlife, improved soils, and buffered temperature extremes. But very dense shade starved the cocoa of light and substantially cut yields.

On farms in tree-rich landscapes, a mix of tall and short shade trees helped support biodiversity.

Dense shade, meanwhile, reduced cocoa yields by limiting sunlight.

“Our data show that cacao farms do not need uniform shade to support biodiversity. A mosaic of tall and short trees, tree clusters and open patches can create habitat for wildlife while maintaining production,” said lead author Aycart-Lazo from the University of Vienna.

The researchers surveyed birds, bats, and arthropods. They measured cocoa yield, natural pest control, pollination rates, pest and disease incidence, soil fertility, and microclimate.

Then they used drone imagery and remote-sensing data to connect what was happening inside each farm to what the surrounding landscape looked like.

The finding that probably matters most for policy is this: what’s outside the farm fence may be more important than what’s inside it.

A farmer can do everything right on their own plot and still lose most of the pest-control benefit if the surrounding land has been stripped bare.

This is an uncomfortable truth for programs that focus exclusively on farm-level certification.

A certified organic plot in a deforested landscape doesn’t get the same ecosystem services as one embedded in a matrix of forests and orchards.

“Our results show that these benefits depend strongly on how trees are managed both within farms and across the surrounding landscape,” Aycart-Lazo said.

The team proposes a landscape-level formula for the region: roughly 30–35% forest cover, another 40–45% in tree-based habitats like agroforests or orchards, and 20–30% in open areas for annual crops.

That mix, they argue, could create multifunctional landscapes that sustain both high biodiversity and strong cocoa production.

This is a recommendation though, not a decree. Different regions have different land pressures, different communities, and different histories.

“This is not a one-size-fits-all blueprint. Landscape strategies need to be adapted to local land availability and developed together with local communities,” Ocampo-Ariza said.

The study was conducted in one of the world’s most biodiverse regions. The specifics belong to the northern Peruvian Amazon.

But the underlying logic is portable. Wherever crops depend on natural pest control, the surrounding habitat either supports or undermines it.

The study cuts against a stubborn narrative in tropical agriculture – that farmers must choose between productivity and conservation. The data from these 28 Peruvian farms suggest the opposite.

Biodiversity didn’t just coexist with cocoa production. It actively drove it.

The ecosystem services provided by wildlife – pest control above all – were the mechanism converting habitat into yield.

That doesn’t mean conservation is free. Managing a structurally varied canopy takes skill.

Maintaining landscape-level forest cover requires coordination across farms, communities, and governments. But the economic argument is now harder to wave away.

For cocoa growers, keeping forests nearby can help protect the harvest.

The study is published in the journal Communications Earth & Environment.
