AI Will Make the Next 12 Months 'a Slaughterhouse' for Crypto Security: Anchorage Exec

Crypto companies face a wave of AI-powered attacks over the coming year, on top of an already relentless threat from North Korea, security leaders warned at Solana's Breakpoint conference in London.

"The next 12 months are going to be absolutely brutal," said Diogo Mónica, co-founder of crypto bank Anchorage Digital. "They're going to be a slaughterhouse."

Over the past three to six months, AI systems have gone from finding vulnerabilities to building full exploits and carrying out every step of an attack on their own, Mónica said on a panel chaired by Gordon Corera, co-host of The Rest Is Classified podcast. Aggressive hacking tools now let anyone with no security knowledge simply tell a model to "please hack this website," he said.

Michael Coates, chief information security officer at the Solana Foundation, said AI has "dramatically lowered the cost and the barrier to hacking." Projects that once escaped attention will now be targeted opportunistically, he said.

North Korea remains the biggest threat to crypto firms, Coates said in a separate presentation. More than $13 billion in crypto has been stolen over the past decade, over $7.5 billion of it this year alone, with some $6 billion drained in a single breach: last month's hack of Binance, which the FBI has blamed on North Korea. "This is how they fund their programs in North Korea," he said.

Coates described the Binance attack as a chain of familiar tactics. A developer downloaded poisoned software from a sponsored search result, and an engineer later took a job interview on a work device and downloaded software as part of it. That gave the attackers a foothold to move through the network and reach signing services, which they used to authorize malicious transactions rather than steal the keys themselves, he said.

Fake job interviews are a "telltale tactic of North Korea," he said, along with poisoned tools and deepfake video calls. North Korean hackers have stolen $400 million using deepfake calls alone. When North Korean operatives can't get hired directly, they use people in the U.S. to take jobs on their behalf, then connect remote keyboards and mice so attackers can access the company's network, Mónica said.

The U.S. Treasury recently sanctioned 11 people over just such a North Korean IT worker scheme. Anchorage now onboards new staff in person at its U.S. office, Mónica added, and every employee works on a locked-down Chromebook, logging into remote machines for development, which he said removes 90% to 95% of its corporate attack surface.

Coates pointed teams to STRIDE, a free security framework the Solana Foundation launched in the spring. It sets out 18 core controls tailored for crypto, and protocols covering more than 74% of DeFi's total value locked on Solana, including 10 of the top 11, have gone through it, he said. The foundation is also using AI to reverse-engineer newly launched programs and find flaws before attackers do, which he said has prevented more than $5 million in potential losses in the past month.

Both executives warned against basic mistakes. Connecting email inboxes to AI agents invites prompt injection attacks, Mónica said, while Coates urged teams to "protect the keys," using hardware security keys for logins and properly configured multisig wallets, with plans for key rotation and loss. "That's where we see the attackers going," Coates said. "They're getting the entry points through the basics. They're pivoting around to get the keys, and that's where they're unleashing their fury."
