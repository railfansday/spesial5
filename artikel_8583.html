Your gut tells you to sell after a drop and buy after a run-up, the reverse of buying low and selling high. While you’re calm, write a one-page plan that sets your mix of stocks and bonds, monthly buys, rebalancing date, and reasons to sell. Then automate what you can and follow the plan, not your gut.

The S&P 500 index fell about a third from its Feb. 19, 2020, peak to its March 23 low, and investors who sold that day locked in the loss. By Aug. 18, the index had rallied more than 50% to a record close.

Selling felt safe that day because instinct is built to run from danger and chase what’s working. Four biases explain how it shows up in your trades.

Loss aversion makes a loss hurt more than an equal gain feels good, so a falling balance pushes you to sell just to stop the pain. Recency bias makes you expect the last few months to continue, so a slump feels endless and a hot streak feels safe. Herd behavior has you buy what everyone else is buying, usually after it has already run up. And after one good call, overconfidence makes you trust the next hunch more than it deserves.
