This Emerging Artist Is a Hit Restaurateur

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Borgo sits on one of those blocks in the high 20s that you swear you’ve never been down before. The restaurant opened in 2024 but feels like it’s been there forever, a place with a natural hum and not an ounce of perspiration. Its clientele is specific but unpredictable; when I went there this summer for lunch with a movie producer, we spotted a powerful name-brand media executive, an architectural bigwig who designs the homes of billionaires, and a two-time Academy Award–winning actor. A few nights later, I sat near Walter Isaacson, who’s a regular. And don’t just take it from Elon Musk’s biographer. Matthew Schneier, the chief restaurant critic at New York magazine, recently named Borgo the city’s best all-around restaurant.

On one occasion, my server was a painter who used to bartend at The Odeon, as painters have done for decades. He met Ross Bleckner there, and David Salle. He used to have a massive loft studio in Williamsburg, but as rents went up, he moved deeper into Brooklyn, to Fort Greene, where he built a studio in his basement.

The twist is that my server’s art was also hanging on the walls of Borgo, as was his daughter’s art. That’s because he was Andrew Tarlow—the painter and restaurateur who kick-started the Brooklyn aesthetic with his restaurant Diner and, yes, also owns Borgo.

After launching that instant hit in an old, reclaimed diner, Tarlow opened five more era-defining Brooklyn spots before finally venturing into Manhattan with Borgo. All along, he’s still been the artist who got a job at The Odeon so that he could meet his heroes. He’s still painting away in his brownstone, doodling on notepads during concept meetings. He’s had shows recently at galleries such as Tiwa Select; he’s loosely associated with the Galerie Sardine extended universe; and he’s someone whose work I’ve been told about for years by those in the know.

As they put it, Tarlow is an emerging artist who happens to own the best all-around restaurant in Manhattan, as well as a bunch of other successful spots in Brooklyn.

“I realized, opening Borgo, that I really loved working in restaurants—and not that I had forgotten, but I hadn’t actually worked services in a while until I opened,” Tarlow told me during a studio visit. “And then, in a similar way, I think—I love making paintings. I actually will paint forever, no matter what happens.”

The studio had dozens of unstretched canvases heaped on the ground, some still rolled up, and others hanging via a pin on the studio wall. They showed figures grappling with pain in fierce color-blocked fields, automatic-painting abstractions, faces fading into other faces. One painting was a portrait of the artist Joe Bradley, a friend.

The studio is in the basement of the Fort Greene brownstone where Tarlow has lived for two decades with his wife, Kate Huling, raising four kids in a kind of neo-boho paradise. There were no studio assistants, no art handlers, no frames, no boxes, no packaging—just the artist and the work.

“It was kind of just as chaotic as everything else there, but in the most perfect warm embrace of a way. I saw the pure energy that he put into his paintings and the stories that they told,” says Alex Tieghi-Walker, the founder of Tiwa Select, calling from London. “So many artists could be quite—rightfully so, but quite precious with their works. And I love that Andrew was just like, ‘Oh, pull out the ones you’re taking and just take them in an Uber home.’”

Tarlow grabbed at a canvas bare-handed, one with a bit of a conceptual bent, and put it up on an easel.

“I’ve had this up on the wall for a while now, and I just continued to look at it because I’m happy with it. I’m also a little unsettled—I mean, I’m showing you the inside of my drawers. I’m also a little unsettled by how far it’s gone abstract-wise.”

He told me he couldn’t really do his other job, the restaurant job, without painting at odd hours. They’re totally separate, but complementary in a cosmic way.

“What are the processes? Like you said, for some people, it’s nine to five. Then you listen to the news, smoke a cigarette, drink coffee. It’s either working or it’s not. The door either opens or it’s not,” Tarlow said, gesturing around to his studio. “But I’m going to go here every day, and I’m going to push some paint on canvas.”

Diner opened in 1999, a few years after Tarlow moved to the desolate southern tip of Broadway in Williamsburg—where the crumbling street dead-ends into the East River. While looking for a cheap studio space, he found a 6,000-square-foot loft across the street from an abandoned diner from the 1920s. The loft’s landlord bought the diner, and in 1999, Tarlow opened Diner with then partner Mark Firth—and watched as it was patronized by only the artists across the street.

“You could stand outside the restaurant in those days and not see a single person walk by,” he told Bon Appetit in 2010.

Tarlow kept expanding. He opened Marlow & Sons, and then the butcher shop and specialty food store Marlow & Daughters. He founded a bakery, She Wolf, and a literary magazine, Diner Journal. He operated the restaurant at the Wythe Hotel, but only for a few years. Instead of doing the easy thing, Vegas-ifying Diner in some new development on the Williamsburg waterfront, he got even weirder, opening a nautical bar called Achilles Heel in a far-out corner of Greenpoint.

A dealer with a gallery in Tribeca was the first, but far from the last, to tell me I had to check out Tarlow’s paintings. After an introduction was brokered, Tarlow invited me to lunch at Borgo this past summer. The restaurant has many fantastic pork-laden dishes, but we ate almost entirely vegetables, as well as one very elegant, simple piece of fish. A summer lunch. We fell into an easy patter, talking about exhibitions, other artists—but mostly his painting practice. He’d been working on Long Island, not on the East End but in the middle. He’d had a few shows. There was discussion of representation. He spoke about all this with an unpracticed earnestness and complete confidence. He sounded like a very prominent emerging artist, which, at 56, he is.

“I don’t know if it’s just age or time, or I was too much of a young person, or a young mind, but I’ve been more secure with my ability to hold all these things in the same time,” he told me back in the studio. “I can go work tonight in the restaurant and feel perfectly confident in that work, and I can show you this work and feel perfectly confident in this work. Whereas, I think, as a young person, I felt like, Am I fake?”

Sometimes it’s not the enfant terrible who breaks out—it’s the working parent, the person with a desk job, the successful entrepreneur who back-ends into revolutionary art making. Think William Carlos Williams writing “The Red Wheelbarrow” while working as a doctor in Rutherford, New Jersey. Or Ruth Asawa making art in her kitchen while raising six children in San Francisco. Or Jeff Koons working on Wall Street in order to earn money to make his sculptures. Having a demanding job actually granted them the freedom to be artists. Tarlow started drawing during restaurant concept meetings.

Sometimes Tarlow hangs his own paintings upstairs in his brownstone’s salon room, or at his house on Long Island, to see what they look like in the wild. The paintings have a confident thrust to them, with the colors bold-blocked in a way that reminds me of Albert Oehlen, drawing from Roman and Greek mythology in Twombly-esque riffs: visages multiplying and fading like a Kippenberger-ian pomo ride into the ether. Sure, any painter who’s spent time in Europe since the 1990s has wanted to be Martin Kippenberger. But Tarlow has an actual connection to him: Like Tarlow, Kippenberger was a lover of Italian food and good wine, and he made art to hang in restaurants. And like Tarlow, Kippenberger was a restaurant owner, balancing his studio practice with running Capri, a Venice, California, spot in which he invested after moving to Los Angeles in 1989. According to those present at the time, Kippenberger became a part-owner of the place because, as the artist put it, “You can’t get a decent Bolognese in Los Angeles.”

As a bartender at The Odeon, Tarlow maintained a working painting studio but found it hard to produce art. After opening Diner, he skipped painting sessions in favor of opening businesses and starting a family. Once that duality settled, he started drawing on the move, carrying sketchbooks and working through potential paintings. Eventually, once the kids got old enough, he built out a studio in a nook of his 19th-century basement.

“I really started painting back downstairs. Let’s just say, after dinner, it was 8 p.m. and they were watching Nemo. No one actually needs me, they’re totally happy. My wife’s up there, everyone’s laughing, giggling with little fish,” he said. “And I can just walk down here and do my thing. Having it in my house for sure is the only way I could do this amount of work, and for sure even get to this place. Because I do think you have to do a certain amount of work to get to this place unless you’re just a born prodigy. But when you think about the genius of Picasso or whomever, he made a million fucking paintings.”

Tarlow didn’t really show anyone his work for a long time.

“I met Andrew socially. I knew his restaurants way before I knew Andrew. And one day he was like, ‘Come and see my studio,’” Tieghi-Walker tells me. “It did not surprise me at all that he had an art studio in his basement and that these were the types of works he was creating.”

The show at Tiwa Select was on view right as Tarlow was getting ready to open Borgo. There was a certain amount of unconscious transference, Tarlow told me. For the Tiwa dinner, instead of creating name placards, the artist hand-sketched an artwork of each guest’s name on the paper tablecloth. Now, when a regular makes a reservation at Borgo, Tarlow will elegantly sketch out their name on the paper tablecloth.

The showcasing of his art at Borgo marks the first time Tarlow has put his own work on the premises of his own restaurant. Tarlow doesn’t exactly promote this. When I asked about it, he told me that only one of his own works was up at the moment, then changed the subject. Alex Levy, the media strategist and theater producer, has been a regular at Borgo since it opened. “Borgo is the grown-up Platonic ideal of what a restaurant can be,” he said. After several visits, Levy, who also collects a bit of contemporary art, noticed what was on the walls.

“I complimented one of the paintings, and Andrew said, ‘It’s by my daughter.’ And then he pointed to another one and said, ‘That’s by me,’” Levy says. “I had no idea that there’s this whole other part of him.”

Like Tarlow, Levy balances two seemingly divergent but strangely complementary careers—as a speechwriter on one hand, a Tony Award winner on the other—and Tarlow invited him to see his studio on Long Island. Levy bought a painting immediately. Seeing the studio, Levy says, helped him realize, to some extent, what the special sauce is behind a place like Borgo. It’s for incredibly confident, hyperproductive creative types, run by an artist who has complete faith in his ability to work.

And for Tarlow, the focus and passion in one arena lead right into the passion for the other.

“I’m also being very present for your needs in the same way as I’m trying to be very present with these paintings,” he said. “They’re not separate boats. When the restaurant’s working, I feel creative inside of it.”
