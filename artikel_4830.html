Why Better Recovery Might Be the Missing Link in Your Workout Routine

You work hard in the gym, nail your nutrition, and consistently try to challenge yourself with heavier weights or more reps. But no matter what you do lately, it seems as if your progress just won’t budge. So what’s going on?

A plateau can be incredibly frustrating, but there is some good news: It may not be your meticulously planned workouts that need tweaking. The solution for your stall in progress may lie in something a little simpler: recovery.

Many people think results happen during our sweat sessions, but in actuality, the gains we’re working toward happen afterward. Adapting to stress, building muscle, and getting stronger all happen when we take rest. So, before you fire your personal trainer or scour the internet for a new workout plan, try making a few simple tweaks to how you spend your downtime, if you’re even giving yourself any.

Read on for what the science and experts say about recovery—and how you can use yours to escape your fitness plateau.

Meet the experts: Donna Duffy, PhD, is an associate professor in the department of kinesiology at the University of North Carolina, Greensboro, and head of research at Collective X Health, a women's health intelligence and research platform co-founded by WH advisory board member Stacy Sims. Lisa Woodroffe, MD, is a sports medicine physician at the University of Iowa Health Care and team doctor for several of the Iowa Hawkeye women’s athletic teams. Asad Siddiqi, DO, is the chief of rehabilitation medicine at NewYork-Presbyterian Brooklyn Methodist Hospital.

Working out is only one piece of the equation when it comes to seeing results. In order to get all the benefits of training, your body needs time to rest and recover after a workout. “During rest, your body recovers using sleep, food, and downtime to repair the tissues you’ve stressed during training and prepare them to handle the next challenge,” says Donna Duffy, PhD, an associate professor in the department of kinesiology at the University of North Carolina, Greensboro.

There’s a lot that goes on in your body during rest: In addition to your muscles repairing and building back stronger, you experience a nervous system reset—as your heart rate decreases after a training session, your body shifts out of a stressed state and begins to calm down, Duffy explains. Then there is increased blood flow during and after exercise, which helps flush out things like lactic acid and delivers fresh oxygen and nutrients to stressed parts of the body.

That is, assuming you’re giving your body enough time to do all that. “If you continually pile more workouts on top of fatigue without giving your body enough time and resources to recover, that fatigue can start to interfere with your overall performance in and out of the gym,” Duffy says.

When a person fails to recover adequately, a weight that seemed doable on Monday feels heavier on Wednesday, and an easy run becomes a hard one. All of this can be incredibly frustrating, and may make people think they just need to work harder, but experts say sometimes the opposite is true. “Your body may not need more training,” says Duffy. “Instead, it may need more opportunity to recover from the training you're already doing.”

When deciding if your body needs a rest, consider factors like workout duration, intensity, and fatigue, says Asad Siddiqi, DO, chief of rehabilitation medicine at NewYork-Presbyterian Brooklyn Methodist Hospital. Soreness or fatigue that stays below a 3 to 4 out of 10 during or immediately after training and does not last beyond 24 to 36 hours is the upper limit of “acceptable” exercise-induced discomfort, and a day in between loading is probably fine. Meanwhile, if you go above a 5, Dr. Siddiqi recommends taking an extra day beyond that to monitor how you’re recovering before doing the same workout again. Typically, a two-day break will allow people enough time to recover, but the most important barometer is how you feel, he says. If you’re still super sore and think your form will be compromised, you need more time off.

This doesn’t mean you need to be completely inactive during those days off, particularly if you’re doing circuit training. If you hit arms really hard on Monday and need to give them some extra time to recover, you can still do legs the next day. But, if you are doing intense and fatiguing full-body workouts, try alternating resistance training with low-impact aerobic or mobility exercises like yoga.

Even if you’re doing circuit training, at a minimum, most people should have at least one full rest day per week, says Lisa Woodroffe, MD, a sports medicine physician at the University of Iowa Health Care and team doctor for several of the Iowa Hawkeye women’s athletic teams. And, if you’re someone who has been feeling consistently sore, unusually fatigued, or seeing a dip in performance, it’s time to up your number of rest days.

For a variety of reasons, many active people are just simply not eating enough, says Dr. Woodroffe. “They are either underestimating how much energy they are expending in exercise, or overestimating what they are eating, or with busy schedules running out of time in their day to get everything they need.” It’s also worth mentioning that some people may be exercising with the explicit goal of losing weight—which can result in underfueling for their workouts, she says.

Eating enough is critical for recovery. “We know that insufficient fueling patterns have cascading and multiplying effects on the body’s ability to respond to training load and recovery,” Dr. Woodroffe says. “All cells of the body need adequate energy to maintain optimal function. When there is not enough energy to go around, there will be effects not only at the cellular level but eventually also at the organ system level.” We’re talking effects on muscle recovery, bone density, cardiovascular function, and cognitive performance.

That’s why experts recommend having a plan in place for getting all the fuel you need. “Emphasizing a routine of three to five meals a day that includes carbs for energy, protein for muscle building, and fresh vegetables and fruits for minerals and gut health is the baseline,” says Dr. Siddiqi. You don’t need to count calories if that’s not your thing—but you should have a goal of at least three meals supplemented by additional snacks every day that hit all of the macros (carbs, protein, and fats).

Protein is particularly important for active people, but no macro should be ignored. Dr. Woodroffe always notes that no food groups should be restricted unless otherwise directed by a medical professional. If you need any help with your fueling strategy, it’s a good idea to check in with a doctor or dietitian who can give you personalized recommendations.

One overall, simple way to make sure you’re eating enough? Feeling full. You should eat to be satiated and not feel constantly hungry. When it comes to snacking, it’s recommended that you eat easily digestible carbs (Think: fruit, bagels, toast) 30 to 60 minutes before a workout, and then a combination of carbs, protein, and fluids within 60 minutes following. It’s one reason dietitians love chocolate milk as a post-workout drink.

Your training program is a balancing act. “A plateau can happen either due to not enough training, or not enough recovery,” Dr. Woodroffe says. But how can we actually tell which one is happening? What are the clues that someone needs to pull back versus increase the challenge?

“Overall, training should be a positive experience that challenges the body and mind but doesn’t put you out of commission,” Dr. Siddiqi says. You want to be sore after a workout, but not so much that you can’t move. If you’re experiencing the symptoms above that are signs you’re not recovering properly, you’re probably overloading and should pull back. Start by taking two full rest days to give your body a real chance to recover, and then consider implementing one additional rest day per week compared to your old schedule or adjusting your training plan to allow for less intensity.

On the flip side, if you’re plateauing and not feeling drained at all, then it may be because you need to challenge yourself more. If that’s the case, Dr. Siddiqi recommends a steady increase in load by roughly 10 percent week over week. “The definition of ‘load’ in this situation is variable, and so this could mean 10 percent more intense based on your perceived exertion, 10 percent greater distance, 10 percent greater duration, or however else you think could be meaningful,” he says. The key, though, is to have a steady increase on your way to your goal, avoiding any major spikes.

So how do you know when symptoms are more than just poor recovery after a challenging workout versus a health issue that needs a professional follow-up? “Sometimes fatigue, soreness, or a dip in performance really can be a sign that you need more recovery, but it's also important not to assume that every change is caused by your training,” says Duffy. “If symptoms are persistent, getting worse, or affecting your ability to function normally, there may be something else going on.”
