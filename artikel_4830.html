The movement is the engine of the watch—it's what keeps it ticking. Below, we've broken down the main three types and their benefits.

Powered by a battery and known for being super accurate, quartz movement is low maintenance, lightweight, and affordable. It's a great option if you just want a reliable watch without the fuss. Quartz watches will only require the replacement of the battery every few years, which is a simple task and costs fairly little.

Backed by the motion of your wrist—no battery required. The gears that make up the movement wind themselves when the watch moves. Automatic watches feel a little more special, sometimes like high-end brands Rolex or Omega, but they'll need to be worn regularly or placed in a watch winder to stay powered. They'll also require consistent maintenance and servicing to keep them optimally running. Automatic watches typically cost more than most quartz watches, but there are many great options that are affordable these days.

You wind it yourself, usually every couple days. Instead of the movement rotating automatically with motion, the gears need hand-winding to keep the movement running. These watches require attention and typically harken back to the era of vintage, throwback timepieces like pocket watches. Manual watches usually cost more than quartz watches and are on par in price with some automatic watches. They tend to be a little less affordable though.

When shopping for the best men's watch, features and complications can make all the difference in terms of functionality and style. Ultimately, these details should match your lifestyle. We've broken down some important specs to keep an eye out for, but these are by no means an exhaustive list of everything you could find in some complex watches.

This is the ring around the watch face, and when it rotates, it actually serves a purpose (beyond looking cool). On dive watches, for example, the bezel is used to track elapsed time—turn it to line up with the minute hand before you dive, and you'll know how long you're underwater. Some bezels work for other things, like GMT watches, where they help track a second time zone. But in everyday life, most people just fidget with them because it's oddly satisfying with a ratcheting sound.

Water resistance tells you how well your watch can handle moisture. There are two equivalent measurements you will typically find when it comes to this rating. Meters (as in depth) is the first and atmosphere (designated as ATM and measures the pressure of every 10 meters. So the first rating is 30m, which is rated at 3 ATM. This is splash-proof, which means your watch can handle occasional hand-washing or light rain, but don't submerge it. 50m (5 ATM) is good for shallow water activities like light swimming, but no diving. 100m (10 ATM) can handle swimming, snorkeling, and most water sports. 200m+ (20 ATM) is built for diving and serious water activities. Watches like the Omega Seamaster are built specifically for this sort of pressure.

A chronograph is a type of complication added to the watch movement that essentially gives the watch a stopwatch functionality. It's great for timing events, workouts, or even laps around a track. You'll typically see sub dials for seconds, minutes, and sometimes hours. While it adds functionality, it also gives the watch a sporty, complex look. The most famous example of a chronograph is the Rolex Daytona, made famous by watch icon Paul Newman.

A date window adds convenience—you won't have to check your phone or calendar to know the date. It's a small but handy detail, especially for busy schedules. Watches such as the Seiko 5 or Tudor Black Bay makes this feature feel seamless by blending it into the design.

Luminous markers or hands are a must if you plan on wearing the watch in low-light situations, like at night or in darker environments. Watches with Super-LumiNova or tritium tubes glow brightly and for extended periods and make the dial easier to read when overhead light is sparse.

Pay attention to case diameter and thickness. Case diameter is the main number you'll see alongside watches. Generally speaking, around 38-42mm diameter is ideal for most wrists, but larger or smaller watches can suit personal taste. The diameter of cases tends to ebb and flow with watch trends. For the first part of this century, case sizes gravitated over 40mm for lots of watch makers. But in 2026, smaller watches (even down to 32 and 34mm) are en vogue. We like to find a happy medium and suggest trying on different sizes to see what's most comfortable for your wrist.

If you’re rough on your gear or are looking for an everyday wear, opt for a scratch-resistant sapphire crystal (the transparent covering that protects the dial), shock resistance, or robust designs for long-lasting wear.
