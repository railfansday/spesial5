Pronouncing Law Latin

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

A conversation with a student reminded me of one of my favorite articles, by my UCLA colleague Henry A. Kelly (who is emeritus in the English Department). It's called "Lawyers' Latin: Loquenda ut Vulgus?," and here's the introduction (though all 14 pages of the article are much worth reading):

In "Rex v. Venables and Others: The Dead Pronunciation" (one of A. P. Herbert's "Misleading Cases"), Mr. Wick, an advocate making his first appearance in the High Court, tries out his textbook Latin before the Lord Chief Justice. The young advocate, attempting to apply for a writ of certiorari, asks for "a rule of neesee of kairtiorahree." The Lord Chief Justice pretends not to understand this or any other of Mr. Wick's Latin phrases. When ultra vires is rendered ooltrah weerayze, the Court interrupts: "Stop! Listen, Mr. Wick. The two groups of sounds last formed by you have no meaning for me, and I order you not to make use of them again."

Although the unfortunate Mr. Wick is genuinely baffled by the Court's pronunciation of Latin terms, he is offered scant sympathy:

It is not for me at my time of life to learn a new language; it is not for the King's judges to remodel their diction according to the whims of pedagogues or the habits of the Junior Bar. The bitter conclusion is, Mr. Wick, that you must go away and learn to pronounce the Latin tongue correctly, according to the immemorial practice of your profession.

According to the juristic apophthegm, Loquendum ut vulgus, sentiendum ut docti, one should "speak like the common people but understand like the learned." When it comes to legal Latin, lawyers have observed the first part of this advice in various ways. Traditionally, Latin in England and other European countries has not been spoken uniformly but has been pronounced according to the sounds of the vernacular or lingua vulgaris of each region. Nowadays, however, most lawyers must be classed with the vulgus in never having learned Latin, and so they tend to avoid it whenever possible. But when an expression is unavoidable, they give it what they consider to be the "most common" pronunciation, or they imitate the commons in acting according to whim rather than according to rule. The result is what we might charitably call an eclectic or ad hoc approach—we hear, for example, ADD HOCK, ODD HAWK, ADD HOKE, ODD OAK, and so on.

Four different but internally consistent systems of Latin pronunciation have been used in England and America, each entirely "correct" and defensible in theory and in practice. The following analysis is concerned more with showing how the four methods have been adapted to English tongues than with reflecting how each would be spoken by a "native" or purist. My aim, after tracing the vagaries of Latin pronunciation, is to offer some practical recommendations to English-speaking lawyers using Latin phrases….

For the lawyer who occasionally uses Latin words or phrases, I recommend that all  expressions that have survived comfortably from an era when all Latin was pronounced in the English way—for example, et cetera—should continue to be pronounced as English, that is, in the English system of pronouncing Latin outlined above. But when Latin is cited as a foreign language, it should either be pronounced according to the system used by its author (if it is a literary citation) or according to any of the four systems outlined here that seems apt or convenient. Most often, no doubt, the system used will be the one learned in school.

But lawyers and legal educators should keep in mind the certainty of Herbert's Lord Chief Justice that such "hateful sounds" as "ooltrah weerayze" (for ultra vires) "will never proceed from the lips of an English judge, however many innocent boys are instructed to make them at school." Those who learned the Classical system need to be cautioned against trying to impose it upon all Latin expressions as if it were the only correct, or the most correct, way of pronouncing Latin. To do so would be to mistake the purpose of the scholars who resurrected it. It was intended only for the classics of the Golden Age of Latin, not for later Latin or for technical Latin terms or phrases brought into the various vernaculars in modern times

He labels the four methods "Classical (Ciceronian)," "Italian (Dantean)," "Continental (Chaucerian)," and "English (Shakespearean)," and discuss them further. He also cites a medieval source which I quite liked, at least with regard to pronunciation:

[T]he desire to appear wise among the foolish … is itself a kind of madness.
