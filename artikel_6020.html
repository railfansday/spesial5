Huang’s Surprise Return Doesn’t End Nvidia Questions

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

President Donald Trump stands with Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang after presenting him with the National Medal of Science in Washington, D.C., October 8, 2026.

Well, that was quick. Less than two weeks after unanimously accepting CEO Jensen Huang’s resignation, Nvidia’s board has now, again unanimously, agreed to reinstate him. This followed “a series of discussions and meetings . . . after which the board was satisfied with respect to Huang’s candor and ability to lead Nvidia.” The first hurdle must have been trickier than the second. Presumably even more importantly, based on “input” from “senior levels” of the U.S. government, the board was “confident” that Huang’s return posed “no national security risk.”

President Trump, no respecter of the division between boardroom and state, was heavily involved in Huang’s reinstallation. In a post on Truth Social, he argued that it was needed “in order to ensure the success and prosperity of not only the company, but the American economy.”

On Monday, Nvidia’s stock jumped by over 30 percent, increasing its market cap by not far short of a trillion dollars. The tech-heavy NASDAQ rose nearly 6.5 percent, the S&P 500 by 4.75 percent. Trump views the stock market as a critical indicator of the nation’s economic health, not without reason, including the “wealth effect” created by rising stock prices. Booms can go bust, but, although calculating the effect of the AI build-out on the economy is not straightforward, by some estimates it may account for a quarter or more of current GDP growth.

Trump also wrote that the U.S. government has no reason to believe that Huang’s “return to Nvidia poses any risk to National Security. The Attorney General of the United States has confirmed that the U.S. Government does not possess evidence that he has engaged in any unlawful conduct.” Trump added that Nvidia “should continue its thorough internal investigation of [its] compliance with U.S. export controls in order to ensure that it is fulfilling its legal obligations and patriotic duty to protect U.S. national security.” And so it should.

Various government agencies are investigating, as are a number of congressional committees. It was also announced on Monday that the U.S. is prohibiting the sale of AI chips to Megaspeed, the Singaporean data center firm previously connected to Alice Huang, the alleged Chinese agent with whom Nvidia’s CEO was in a relationship. The concern is that Megaspeed may have been diverting contraband chips to China. More generally, the administration has already tightened the rules governing exports of such chips to other countries where there is a diversion risk, a welcome, if overdue, step, as is the closing of the loophole that allowed enterprises in China and other hostile states to have remote access to the power of advanced American AI chips from businesses located in third countries.

This is a saga with a lot to unpack, much of which remains, at best, guesswork. Nevertheless, we do know (or can be pretty sure that) Nvidia’s board gave Jensen Huang considerable leeway when it came to the way he ran Nvidia, not least with regard to the way that so much of the company’s decision-making was concentrated in him. That may well have underpinned Nvidia’s extraordinary success — a win for the U.S., as well as for Nvidia — but it also clearly played an important part in the current imbroglio and raises troubling questions about the consequences were Huang to step down again or, even if he weathers this crisis, at some later stage. It is said that no man is irreplaceable, but that is not always so.

We also know that Nvidia, sometimes referred to as AI’s central bank, has played a key role in supporting the growth of earlier-stage companies. This has been criticized as a form of circular financing by Nvidia (the company is helping fund some of its client base), but it has played a vital part in the creation of an American AI ecosystem. Through Nvidia, Huang was a critical element in this ecosystem’s growth.

And we know that the success of the American AI sector has geopolitical as well as commercial implications. There is an AI race between China and the U.S. The U.S. must hang on to the lead it has built up — here’s the catch — in no small part with Nvidia’s assistance. Huang has argued against overly broad U.S. restrictions on the export of U.S. chips, arguing that they will only encourage China to make their own, an unconvincing argument given the importance Beijing puts on autarky. Of course, Nvidia would benefit financially (for a while) by selling AI chips to China, but it is not in our strategic interest to allow it.

Huang is entitled to his views, but they do not entitle his company to evade U.S, export controls or even to turn a blind eye should Nvidia’s customers “divert” chips to forbidden destinations. The question of whether this occurred will almost certainly be the main focus of the investigations. It is a question that dwarfs (however much corporate governance purists may object) Huang’s reckless choice of companionship and, in this respect, his lack of candor with Nvidia’s board. The investigations will lead where they will lead. They will also be highly politicized. Regardless of the investigations’ conclusions, they will be a major distraction and, as such, unhelpful to Nvidia and the U.S. AI sector more broadly. That will be a win that should never have been handed to Beijing.
