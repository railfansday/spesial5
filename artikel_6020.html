Former Navy SEAL says New Jersey’s Islamophobia bill puts protected speech in the crosshairs

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Bill Brown, a Parlatore Law Group partner and former Navy SEAL, tells Fox News Digital why he believes New Jersey’s Islamophobia bill could make people think twice before criticizing religious and political ideas.

A New Jersey proposal aimed at combating anti-Muslim discrimination could make Americans think twice before criticizing religious and political ideas, a lawyer and former Navy SEAL warned.

Bill Brown, a partner at Parlatore Law Group, told Fox News Digital that Assembly Bill 1146 contains language broad enough to discourage public debate, even though the legislation expressly preserves First Amendment rights.

"The mere threat of state scrutiny is a direct assault on public debate," Brown said.

The proposal would establish a state definition of Islamophobia and require public officials and law enforcement officers to consider it when determining whether alleged discriminatory acts were motivated by anti-Muslim bias.

Brown argued that requiring officials to use a definition that includes harsh criticism and other speech could make people afraid to speak openly.

"The First Amendment guarantees that no religion or theology is immune to criticism," he said.

Democratic Assemblyman Joe Danielsen sponsors the bill, which is co-sponsored by fellow Democrats Assemblywomen Verlina Reynolds-Jackson and Mitchelle Drulis and Assemblyman Roy Freiman. The legislation describes a uniform definition as a tool to help officials identify and investigate anti-Muslim crime, harassment and discrimination."

Fox News Digital made multiple attempts to contact Danielsen and all three co-sponsors for comment but did not receive a response.

Its definition includes "hatred or severe disparagement towards Muslim persons or their culture," as well as "rhetorical, violent, or physical anti-Muslim manifestations."

Other examples include pulling hijabs from Muslim women, discriminatory treatment and blaming Muslims collectively for wrongdoing committed by an individual or group.

The bill would create no standalone criminal offense for criticizing Islam. It directs officials to consider the definition when reviewing an alleged violation of an existing policy, law or regulation prohibiting discriminatory acts.

It also explicitly says nothing in the legislation should diminish or infringe on rights protected by the First Amendment or New Jersey’s Constitution.

Asked about that safeguard, Brown maintained that the prospect of scrutiny itself could discourage people from speaking, regardless of whether a case ultimately succeeds.

He also singled out a provision in the bill’s legislative findings that lists "anti-Islamism" among terms that may describe the same characteristics as Islamophobia. Brown argued that the wording risks blurring the distinction between prejudice against Muslim people and criticism of a political movement.

"Islamism is a distinct geopolitical movement," he said. "It’s not just a religious faith."

Brown described the proposal as a "backdoor blasphemy law," arguing that government should protect people’s rights while allowing their religious beliefs to face public scrutiny.

The legislation excludes criticism of Muslim-majority countries comparable to criticism directed at other countries. Brown argued that criticism of religious and political ideology also needs protection.

Brown said his concerns are informed in part by his military service. He recalled responding to two suicide bombings in Iraq and said he remains committed to speaking out against jihadist ideology.

He also recalled studying Arabic at Rutgers after leaving the military, where Muslim American classmates helped him when he struggled with the language.

For Brown, the broader concern is whether Americans will feel free to challenge ideas they oppose.

"True diversity is diversity of thought," he said.
