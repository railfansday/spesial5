Have you ever been told that there was something about you that was ‘too much’?

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Inflection points spark reflection. And currently, I’m at two inflection points: approaching 10 years of being in business and the end of another calendar year. One of the things I am recalling is that as a woman, some of my characteristics that I once thought of as deficits have conspired to be my greatest assets. Unbeknownst to me, they are actually the factors that contribute to my success.

For example, I am indecisive. I constantly weigh the pros and cons of a situation. I blame that in part on being a Libra — but that trait is also why I loved studying cultural anthropology and gravitate towards multidisciplinary work. In my job history I was sometimes told that I had to “pick a lane.. And it’s been brought to my attention multiple times, especially in corporate environments, that decisiveness is a winning attribute. But as I have evolved, my indecisiveness is the very quality that helps me consider multiple perspectives and identify alternative future scenarios of a situation. Ironically, my indecisiveness helps me navigate ambiguous terrain. 

How wonderful that years later that ability to straddle lanes and work in multidisciplinary environments has become my asset as I’ve built a company to help people toggle between wonder and rigor. 

What about others?

I was curious to learn from other women if an inherent quality that they were told was “too much” turned out to be a creative asset in their work. Bear with me — I know that such a question could be posed to men too. But in my experience, it’s women who tend to contort themselves in order to fit into the workplace. This phenomenon is called “covering.” According to a 2024 Deloitte report in collaboration with the NYU Meltzer Center, covering is when people feel the need to downplay certain identities in order to fit in at work. The report found that cisgender women are more likely to cover than are cisgender men. So for the purposes of this exploration I am focusing on women.  

Sheila Lirio Marcelo, the founder and CEO of Ohai and founder of Care.com shared a time when two mentors took her to lunch to tell her that she was “too intense.. She was able to take the feedback and reflected in the following way:

“They were right. I cared enormously about the work, but I hadn’t yet learned to separate my intensity about the outcome from how I showed up with people. That conversation changed how I lead. I developed a mantra: Be intense about outcomes, but chill with people.”

First, what maturity in Sheila’s ability to take that feedback! Second, what a great reframe on her part. She never disavowed herself of her innate intensity. Instead she converted that intensity into a creative asset. As Sheila put it, that intensity was “the drive to question what others accept, imagine a different answer, and keep pushing until we build it- while giving people the space and trust to get there.”

Daisy Auger Dominguez, Chief People Officer at Digital Asset shared a similar story:

“I’ve been told that I bring too much passion and joy to work, as though enthusiasm somehow diminishes seriousness or credibility. 

“Early in my career, I sometimes felt pressure to temper both to be taken seriously. But I’ve learned that joy and rigor aren’t mutually exclusive. My passion fuels my curiosity and creativity, and joy creates the conditions for others to imagine, contribute, and take risks alongside me. Some of my most meaningful work has come from bringing people together around possibilities they couldn’t yet see. What was once considered “too much” became how I bring others along.”

The lesson

There’s a lesson here for all of us who manage women. I call it inside-out leadership: the ability to be more transparent, humble and curious about the inner lives of those we lead and of ourselves. Consider the following questions:

  • Are there qualities in some of the women on your team that are innate personality traits that could be assets, as in the case of Daisy Auger Dominguez?
  • How might you draw that out in them?
  • Is it worth having a constructive conversation, as in the case of Sheila Lirio, so that you help the woman reframe it as an asset?
  • Are you, as a manager, covering? Could that be to your and your team’s detriment?
  • Or, are you transparent about your unique qualities and encourage your teammates to heighten theirs for the betterment of the work? 

These questions are a starting point to ensure that women thrive, that work flourishes, and that in a time when AI can do basic tasks, we add texture to our work by leaning into what makes us uniquely human.
