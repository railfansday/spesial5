Why Are We Still So Easy to Fool in the Age of Unlimited Information?

We gave the world access to knowledge. We also gave every bad explanation a publishing platform, an audience, and somewhere to belong.

Some time ago, while working on my research into why people believe in pseudoscience and anti-scientific claims, I kept coming back to a question: why do people hold on to explanations supported by so little evidence? I had been looking at astrology, numerology, homeopathy, vaccine myths, and conspiracy theories—not because they are identical, but because each reveals something about what we want an explanation to do for us.

After finishing that piece, another question bothered me more.

We now carry what would once have looked like an absurdly powerful research library in our pockets. A claim about medicine can be checked against clinical evidence. A claim about the stars can be compared with astronomy. An elaborate conspiracy can, at least in principle, be examined against documents, dates, and independent accounts. So why haven't these beliefs simply faded away?

Part of the answer is that we handed the library keys to everyone. Including the people selling the nonsense.

The internet did not merely give us access to reliable knowledge. It gave almost anyone the ability to publish a competing version of reality, discover a receptive audience, and build a community around it. An unsupported claim no longer has to survive in isolation. It can acquire a website, a podcast, a thousand videos, an app, and a group of people ready to explain why the critics are wrong.

My argument is not that technology made humanity uniquely irrational. It is that we expected a problem of human judgment to disappear when we solved a problem of information delivery. Those were never the same problem.

There was a reasonable optimism behind the early promise of the web. Knowledge would no longer be trapped behind a university wall, an expensive encyclopedia, or the accident of where someone was born. A teenager could learn astrophysics. A worried parent could read medical guidance. Someone suspicious of a grand historical claim could compare competing accounts without waiting for an expert to visit the town library.

Those gains are real. Yet the optimistic version quietly assumed that making the good explanation available would make the bad explanation less attractive. Information was treated rather like clean water: improve the supply, and people will naturally drink it.

But information is not water. A person does not always prefer the most carefully supported explanation. Sometimes we prefer the explanation that removes a frightening uncertainty. Sometimes we want a villain, a cosmic plan, or the satisfaction of being among the few who have "figured it out." And sometimes we simply trust the person sharing the story more than we trust the institution disputing it.

These motives matter in different ways. A tarot reading can be a harmless ritual of reflection. A claim that a homeopathic remedy can replace proven treatment is a medical claim with consequences. A conspiracy theory can offer an enemy and a ready-made community. A vaccine rumor may grow from a genuine fear, then become an identity marker. They are not interchangeable, and the consequences certainly are not. What they share is that the factual strength of an explanation is only one of the things people may value about it.

The psychologist Karen Douglas and colleagues have described the appeal of conspiracy theories through several kinds of motives: the wish to understand events, the wish for security and control, and the wish to protect a social identity. That is not a universal diagnosis for every believer, but it helps explain why another fact sheet may miss the point. A fact sheet answers a question about evidence. The person may also be asking whether the world is safe, whether their community can be trusted, or whether they still matter in it.

The internet is exceptionally good at serving the second set of questions. It may offer not just an answer, but other people who find that answer comforting.

An unusual belief once depended heavily on local surroundings: family conversations, a bookshop shelf, a television show, perhaps a small organization that printed newsletters. People still encounter ideas that way. But a believer in a far-fetched explanation can now find a community across the planet in minutes. That can be socially meaningful long before it becomes intellectually convincing.

Picture a hypothetical person unsettled by a medical decision. They type a worried question into a search box. One result leads to a personal testimony. The testimony links to a forum. The forum offers a video. The video introduces a creator who appears knowledgeable, patient, and sympathetic. The person returns the next day because the community has noticed their concerns. A week later, the issue is not just whether an assertion is true. It is also whether the people who welcomed them are wrong.

This is an illustration, not a claim that every worried patient follows this path or that every platform sends users down it. But it captures an important distinction: the network can turn a claim into a relationship.

Websites supply an archive. Search makes it discoverable. Social feeds give it repeated exposure. Private chats let people circulate it without public scrutiny. Applications make the practice routine; a daily horoscope or a numerology notification can become part of a morning ritual. Online groups provide shared language, jokes, heroes, enemies, and a sense of belonging. No single technology does all of this. Together, they can give a belief a surprisingly durable home.

It is tempting to imagine that each new follower has been convinced by an argument. Often the process can be more ordinary. An idea appears frequently, then begins to look familiar; people around us repeat it, then it begins to look respectable. A 2026 meta-analysis covering 182 studies found that repetition can increase perceived truth on average, although effects vary substantially by context and type of statement. Familiarity is not proof, but it has a way of borrowing proof's clothing.

One of the internet's peculiar achievements is that a claim can arrive at our screen already surrounded by social evidence: comments, reactions, creators, testimonials, and apparent consensus. None of those features establishes whether the claim is correct. Yet they change how the encounter feels. The old village rumor has learned how to count its followers.

The phrase filter bubble suggests an invisible machine that surrounds us with acceptable ideas. The phrase echo chamber suggests a room where our own opinions return to us louder. Both are useful metaphors. Both are also capable of misleading us if we mistake the metaphor for a complete explanation.

There are at least three processes worth separating.

First, we choose. We search with particular words, follow particular people, leave annoying groups, and return to places where we feel understood. Someone who searches for "why this treatment is dangerous" is starting from a different question than someone who searches for "what do clinical trials say about this treatment?" The results may reflect that difference even before a recommendation algorithm gets involved.

Second, our communities choose with us. Friends recommend sources. Group administrators set norms. People who challenge the shared story may lose standing or simply stop participating. A community does not need to ban disagreement formally to make disagreement expensive. Social belonging can do much of the work.

Third, platforms select and rank. Search engines and feeds cannot show everything at once. They decide what is visible, what appears early, what is recommended next, and what never gets encountered at all. Ranking is unavoidable at scale; the meaningful questions are which signals it uses, what it optimizes, and how it interacts with human preferences.

Research has documented echo-chamber patterns. A 2021 PNAS study compared more than 100 million pieces of content across several social platforms and found different patterns of like-minded connections and information sharing. That is evidence of selective clustering, not proof that a single algorithm mechanically creates every extreme belief.

Here is where the evidence becomes more interesting than the standard internet horror story. In a 2023 field experiment involving 23,377 Facebook users during the 2020 US election, researchers reduced exposure to posts from like-minded sources by about one-third. They did not find corresponding measurable reductions in political polarization over the study period. Related experiments changing chronological versus algorithmic feeds also changed what people encountered without significantly changing several measured political attitudes over three months.

These were studies of particular political settings, not direct tests of astrology, homeopathy, or every conspiracy community. They do not absolve platforms of influence. They do tell us not to present the bubble as a switch that an engineer could simply turn off. Beliefs have histories, relationships, habits, and identities attached to them. Changing a feed is not the same as changing a mind.

I find that both more reassuring and more disturbing. Reassuring, because people are not programmable puppets. Disturbing, because our own choices can maintain the room even when the door is open.

The most unsettling part of this story is that the person inside a misinformation community may genuinely believe they are doing research. We often picture the problem as a refusal to look things up. Sometimes the person is looking things up constantly.

Consider a false claim that has acquired its own vocabulary. Search for it in those exact terms and you may find websites built to repeat it, articles quoting one another, and videos citing the same weak source through several layers. Ten links can look like ten independent confirmations when they are actually ten roads back to a single assertion.

There is a name for the problem researchers have observed here: data voids—search areas where limited trustworthy content makes low-quality material unusually visible. It is not true of every search. But the general design risk is clear: search results reflect available pages and ranking decisions, not an automatic audit of reality.

A particularly relevant study published in Nature reported five experiments in which searching online to evaluate misleading news could increase belief in it. The researchers found evidence consistent with a role for lower-quality results. This is a narrow, experimentally studied effect, not a claim that Google or online search always makes people more credulous. Its philosophical implication is larger than its scope: the act of checking is not enough if the checking process cannot distinguish independent evidence from recycled assertion.

It is a painful irony. We may mistake effort for rigor. We spent two hours reading, so surely we know more than someone who skimmed an official summary. But hours of exposure are not the same as hours of verification. An elaborate conspiracy can demand extraordinary amounts of reading while arranging that every failure of proof becomes evidence of a deeper cover-up.

That is why conspiracy theories can appear complex yet offer a remarkably simple emotional structure: someone planned this, someone is hiding it, and someone has discovered the secret. The details multiply. The underlying story stays comfortingly stable.

Science often offers the opposite bargain. Its methods are complicated, but its conclusions may be unsatisfying: the evidence is incomplete; the causes are multiple; the result is probabilistic; the answer may change. It asks us to accept uncertainty without inventing a person responsible for it. Human beings are not always enthusiastic customers for that offer.

It would be convenient to blame the technology industry for all of this. It would also repeat the very habit this essay is criticizing: replacing a complicated reality with one villain.

Platforms have responsibilities. Product teams make choices about recommendations, moderation, sharing friction, source visibility, and how engagement is rewarded. Researchers have examined how false news diffuses online; a major 2018 Science study of fact-checked stories on Twitter between 2006 and 2017 found that false stories tended to spread farther and faster than true ones in that dataset. But those results are not a universal law of every social network in 2026. Nor do they demonstrate that all users believe everything they share.

The human side does not disappear when we demand better systems. Someone who doubts a medical institution because of a personal experience may not become trusting because the platform places a verification label on a post. Someone whose friends all belong to a conspiracy group may face a genuine social cost for leaving it. Someone who reads tarot for comfort may not even be making the factual claim a skeptic assumes they are making.

There are also limits to the idea that everyone is abandoning science. A 2025 study drawing on 71,922 respondents across 68 countries found that most people in most surveyed countries trusted scientists. There is no need to invent a universal collapse of reason to recognize the serious consequences of particular unsupported beliefs.

More information can help. Clearer explanations can help. Better platform design can help. Teaching people to recognize manipulation techniques has shown promise in controlled studies. None is a magic patch, and they operate on different parts of the problem.

I would rather ask a more uncomfortable question of both engineers and readers: what would count as evidence that our own favored explanation is wrong?

If the answer is "nothing," then the size of the library is irrelevant. We are not using it to learn. We are using it to decorate an answer we already chose.

The internet is one of humanity's great achievements. It has made obscure knowledge accessible, connected isolated people, supported independent investigation, and given ordinary individuals a voice. Those are reasons to value it, not to retreat from it.

But the same openness that lets an excellent explanation cross borders lets an unfounded one do the same. The same tools that help a scientific community collaborate can help an anti-scientific community coordinate. The same ability to find people who understand you can become a powerful reason not to listen to anyone else. This is not an accidental contradiction. It is part of what it means to build a network for humans rather than a machine for distributing certified truths.

We imagined an encyclopedia and built a public square with an encyclopedia attached. In the square, knowledge competes not only with error, but with identity, fear, entertainment, belonging, and the irresistible appeal of a good story.

I do not think the answer is to make the public square silent. I think it is to stop treating availability as understanding and popularity as evidence. The uncomfortable work is learning to ask where a claim came from, whether its sources are genuinely independent, what would disprove it, and what part of our identity we have attached to believing it.

A century from now, people may have tools that make today's search engines look primitive. They may still enjoy horoscopes. They may still find conspiracies that explain every disappointment. The technology will have changed. The desire for certainty, belonging, and a world that makes sense may not have changed nearly as much.

The question is no longer whether we can reach the truth. It is whether we are willing to recognize it when it asks us to give up a story we love.
