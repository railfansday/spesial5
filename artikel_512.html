Inside Google’s Failure to Make Google Maps Work for Palestinians

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

On the road that connects the villages to the north of Ramallah, in the occupied West Bank, there is always a watchtower and a gate, often flanked by Israeli soldiers with machine guns around their necks. None of these things are visible on Google Maps. Where drivers see a checkpoint, a barrier, and soldiers searching cars, the world’s most popular navigation app renders a clear blue line indicating the coast is clear.

This story was produced in partnership with the Palestine Reporting Lab, Arab Reporters for Investigative Journalism (ARIJ), Lighthouse Reports, and Evident.

Routes and maps are not just a matter of journey times or congestion for the nearly 3.5 million Palestinians in the West Bank. Drivers there are forced to navigate a growing and arbitrary system of more than 900 movement obstacles, like checkpoints, which can be policed with lethal force. For Palestinians, these roads can be a matter of life or death. Google Maps could help them make safer choices. It has failed for years to do so.

Google has the ability to alert checkpoints, a feature that it added to Maps in some locations around the world during the Covid crisis. It is aware of the problems experienced by Palestinian drivers in the West Bank. In fact, a former Google product manager spent six years trying to get Maps to serve Palestinians in the same way it serves the rest of the world.

Interviews with more than a dozen former and current Google employees reveal for the first time the inner tensions at the heart of the Google Maps project. Multiple former employees described how the company abandoned the effort and settled on a map that fails, and even harms, Palestinians. As we revealed last month, Palestinians in the West Bank have bootstrapped makeshift navigation solutions such as the popular Road Conditions Telegram channel to help them go about their lives more safely—achieving what Google will not.

Rubén Lozano joined Google Maps as a product manager in 2019. He devoted his days to keeping track of hyperlocal driving restrictions, making sure that what appeared on the map matched what appeared on the ground. He fielded emails from Barcelona city officials about small shifts in their emission zones and flew to Australia to document one city’s toll prices. Whatever he missed, drivers were sure to alert in feedback forums.

One day in 2019, Lozano was sifting through user complaints when he came across “Mapping Segregation,” a report published by 7amleh, a Palestinian digital rights organization. It alleged a failure of Google Maps unlike any he had ever seen: its exclusion of the web of road restrictions choking Palestinian life in the West Bank. The report notes the way in which Maps privileges illegal Israeli settlements and makes Palestinian communities invisible. The report described that the app’s functions amounted to discrimination and that this was “not a technological issue, but rather a systematic omission.” Lozano saw it as a challenge—one he was uniquely suited to take on.

“How can we help this?” Lozano remembers thinking after reading the report. Google was the best-equipped company in the world at dealing with mapping infrastructure, he thought; surely he could use these resources to make Google Maps relevant and safe for Palestinian drivers. Lozano worked for over six years to create a more equitable map of the West Bank. His effort cratered in close to six weeks.

“Google has more than enough computing capacity and engineering capacity,” one former Google Maps engineer told us. “They have solved much larger problems.”

A driver who maps a route from a point inside Israel to a Palestinian town, such as Rawabi, is alerted to a border crossing. If a driver takes that same route to Ateret, the settlement next door, the map shows one seamless drive, with no mention of a border. Israel and an illegal West Bank settlement are shown as part of one contiguous territory on Google Maps.

Google spokesperson Caroline Bourdeau tells WIRED that the company has experienced “challenges acquiring comprehensive and reliable data in this highly complex area.” Since the launch of Maps in 2005, Google has specialized in innovating methods to secure the best data from the hardest-to-access places. Over the past two decades it has carried out crowdsourcing efforts in almost every country in the world. Jennifer Mazzon, who helped lead the company's first community mapping tool, says the company had “armies of people” manually adding and updating map information for everywhere from Costa Rica to the Democratic Republic of Congo. In addition to relying on user inputs, Google built out its maps using satellite, aerial, and street view imagery, as well as government data, and licensed third-party sources when necessary. This commitment to completeness has not extended to Palestinians.

When Lozano first decided he wanted to map the West Bank, his direct managers agreed to back and fund the project. But his colleagues warned him against any optimism. He discovered he was not the first person at Google to try this. “It's a very difficult problem,” he recalls being told. People at the company remembered the backlash when, six years earlier, the company began referring to “Palestine” rather than the “Palestinian Territories” on a regional site: The Knesset questioned a company executive about this, and Google reportedly promised to keep the Israeli government updated about any related policy decisions in the future.

Lozano remained undeterred. Mapping the world as it is was his job, he thought. He picked up the pieces of previous efforts and set to work.

Lozano learned that since the 1995 Oslo Accords, the West Bank has been divided into three zones. Two of them, known as A and B, should be administered to a greater or lesser extent by the Palestinian Authority and are where most of the Palestinian population lives. But Areas A and B are small islands inside Area C, which constitutes the vast majority of the landmass, mostly comprising farmland and smaller Palestinian communities. Although Area C forms part of what's internationally recognized as the Palestinian West Bank, the land is under complete Israeli control, crowded with outposts and settlements, military firing zones, and segregated corridors from which West Bank Palestinians are banned.

Google has increasingly preferred to rely on outside authorities to provide and maintain various types of data used in Maps, according to a current employee. In search of reliable information on West Bank roads, Lozano discovered that a former colleague had identified what seemed like the best source for data on Palestinian geography: an Egyptian company that he says held detailed information on coordinates of Palestinian- and Israeli-controlled areas in the territory, as well as descriptions and locations of permanent checkpoints, which the Israeli government has never published. Using Google's internal app for map building and testing, a process known as “fish fooding,” Lozano created a prototype. It was getting close, he thought, but was missing crucial input. He wanted to talk to Palestinian drivers, the actual people the map was supposed to help. “We don't want to launch this just based on the team here in Seattle,” he recalls thinking.

Lozano had previously conducted user research around the world for other projects at Google. He thought this process would be no different. As usual, he reached out to Google’s user research (UX) team with his request for interview subjects to discuss how to improve his product, asking them to connect him with Palestinians who had driven in the West Bank. Typically, the UX team would then coordinate with their external contractors to plan the research. He had sometimes experienced delays as the researchers connected him with people. But he had never been denied, until now.

The UX team told him that Google’s research vendors did not have access to users with experience driving in the West Bank and were not going to look for them. “We couldn't get Palestinians as part of the users to take our study because most of the companies that Google was contracting with would not be able to source Palestinians,” Lozano says. Another Google source familiar with UX projects and the region says that these vendors would not interview people in Palestine because of concerns for the safety of their workers. Google tells WIRED that it has solicited input on its West Bank map before, from employees who know the region. But Lozano wanted to talk to Palestinian drivers on the ground. Cut off from Google's sprawling network, Lozano drew on distant personal connections to conduct user research. “We were asking, for the Palestinians, ‘What do you want to know?’”he said, “‘What is the information that you want?’”

The Palestinians he spoke with told him that even if the map could not route around Israeli-controlled areas or checkpoints, they still saw value in having a map that gave them a bit more visibility before getting on the road, mitigating the unpredictability they faced in their journeys.

Just as Lozano was ready to have Palestinian drivers test the product on actual West Bank roads, Covid-19 hit. His scheduled trip there in April 2020 was canceled, and he was pulled away from his West Bank work to prioritize mapping pandemic restrictions around the world. This had an upside for his waylaid project. “The concept of a checkpoint,” Lozano says, was introduced into Google's infrastructure. This included a new checkpoint icon and a navigation algorithm to help drivers route through it.

A road closure update on the map sourced from the “Binyamin Council,” which is responsible for governing nearly 50 Israeli settlements and outposts occupying the West Bank in violation of international law.

As the pandemic subsided, Lozano's attention returned slowly to Palestine, continuing to work on the problem when other duties permitted. By the time he chose to leave Google, in March 2025, he felt that his West Bank map was ready to go live. “As long as we ingest it in the system,” he says he told the team, “it will work.”

Multiple former Google colleagues agreed that the work Lozano did was robust. After a user plugged in directions for a West Bank drive on Google Maps, the display would shade the Israeli- and Palestinian-controlled areas that the person would pass through on their route. A note in the step-by-step directions would notify drivers about which areas the route crossed too. He had developed a way to mark and route through checkpoints, a list of permanent checkpoints with their locations for the West Bank, and a plan to display them on drivers' routes using the system built for Covid checkpoints. He was satisfied with the handover: “I’m leaving happy that the person that is taking over also cares about this,” he says he thought at the time.

A few months after he left, Lozano's confidence was rewarded. Google updated its West Bank map. The administrative zones were highlighted and route alerts activated.

Drivers traveling between Israeli cities, on Israeli roads that cut through the West Bank, would be alerted that the route “may cross country/region borders” and would be told in the directions that they were going through “Area C.” It was clear, in other words, that the drivers were exiting Israeli territory.

A former Google manager, who was familiar with the project but asked for anonymity for fear of reprisal, described the new map as a qualified success: The checkpoints were not shown, but nonetheless it was “the maximum that could be done based on the resources that we knew would be available.” Google retired its checkpoint icon for Covid in March 2025, the same month Lozano left, and did not repurpose the system for West Bank checkpoints, as Lozano had set his colleagues up to be able to do.

The success was short-lived, however. Days after Google updated the West Bank mobile map with Lozano's work, Israeli newspaper Haaretz published an article criticizing a “glitch” impacting Israeli drivers. It reported that Google Maps was now avoiding routes on Israeli-built roads that cross into the West Bank, designed to let Israelis travel through the territory quickly. Google responded to the article saying that it was aware of these problems and was “working to fix them.”

Weeks later, by December 2025, the company had retracted important parts of the West Bank map update. Google confirmed this to WIRED. A current employee close to the company's work in the region said that he was not aware of any communication, even internally, about Google's decision to do this.

Now, drivers routing from Tel Aviv to a major settlement in the West Bank could travel without once being informed that they were leaving Israel and entering the West Bank. Although Google's map includes a static dashed line marking a 1949 “armistice agreement line,” drivers using the mobile app are given no notification that they are crossing it. From a user experience perspective, Google Maps made much of the West Bank seem to Israeli drivers like it was Israel.

What did remain of Lozano's efforts were notifications when users were entering the parts of the West Bank that contain the towns and cities where most Palestinians live. The updates effectively amount to warnings for Israeli drivers before entering Palestinian population centers.

The former Google manager with knowledge of the project was unsurprised that Google backtracked part of Lozano's work: A complaint from Israel is all it would take for Google to alter the West Bank map, he believes. Google did not deny this. The company has become increasingly entangled with Israel in recent years through its acquisition of two Israeli companies, Wiz and Waze, and the signing of a joint $1.2 billion cloud infrastructure contract with Amazon Web Services and the Israeli government, known as Project Nimbus, that rights groups claim has enabled war crimes in Gaza. Today, as Israeli military and settler violence reaches unprecedented levels in the West Bank, the stakes of a wrong turn have never been higher for Palestinians there. Yet, Google's West Bank map continues to cater exclusively to Israelis. “Any claims of Google Maps showing bias in this region are false,” Google’s Bourdeau tells WIRED. “We are constantly working to improve the map for all users.” In response to WIRED’s questions about military and settler actions, the Israeli military says that “the security forces operate” in the West Bank “to provide security to all residents.”

Amazon did not respond to a request for comment.

Not only are there indications that the world's leading tech company has in effect ceded the West Bank to Israel; in at least one instance, Google populated its map with information drawn from an illegal settlement authority. Google tells WIRED that it does not have a policy against using or purchasing data from settlers. As recently as this November, a road closure update appeared on the map that had been sourced from the “Binyamin Council,” which is responsible for governing nearly 50 Israeli settlements and outposts occupying the West Bank in violation of international law.

“Israel's changing the facts on the ground, and the map is then adapting to represent that,” says Zena Agha, a scholar of colonial cartography. “They’ll put that on their maps because that's what the Israelis are saying, and then it de facto creates a reality in itself.”
