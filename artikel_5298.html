Investors in Anthropic's IPO could lose money if AI tokens keep getting cheaper

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Businesses are moving more of their AI work to cheaper models. Anthropic can’t raise Claude’s prices without losing more customers

Until this summer, Lindy.ai paid more to Anthropic, the company that makes the Claude AI models, than to anyone else. Lindy, a San Francisco startup with more than two dozen employees, builds AI assistants that manage people's email and calendars, and its Anthropic bill was bigger than its rent. "By far, our No. 1 expense was Anthropic. Like, more than payroll," Flo Crivello, who runs Lindy, told NPR. In June, Lindy moved all of its work off Anthropic's top models and onto DeepSeek-V4, a model from a Chinese lab that anyone can download for free. "It was just 10x cheaper," Crivello said. "So it was a very, very simple business decision."

Lower prices for AI haven't cut what businesses spend on it. Customers of Vercel's AI Gateway, a service that connects developers to models from many labs, paid about half as much in September as in December for each token, the small chunk of text that AI companies charge by, according to Vercel's estimate. The gateway's customers used so much more AI over the nine months, about 24 times as much, that their total monthly spending grew about 13-fold. Among paying customers active in both December and September, the median one more than doubled its bill.

Anthropic's IPO is a bet that customers will keep paying more for Claude even as every unit of AI gets cheaper. The company's shares opened at $111.50 when it went public last week, about a third above the $83 offering price, before falling below that price this week. Because buyers keep paying less for each piece of AI work, Anthropic can earn more only by selling far more of it, keeping its own costs low, or charging extra for a better model. Amazon $AMZN Web Services (AWS), which rents computing power to businesses, keeps its costs down and earned $17.8 billion in operating profit in the third quarter, more than every other part of Amazon combined. Anthropic's new shareholders need Claude to stay far enough ahead of cheaper models that customers keep paying extra for it, because the company has already agreed to pay for computing power for years.

On Vercel's gateway, the cheapest models now do most of the work, while the most expensive ones collect most of the money. Open-weight models, which anyone can download and run instead of renting from the lab that built them, handled more than seven of every 10 tokens in September but took in less than a quarter of the spending. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, a Chinese model, processed nearly half of all the gateway's tokens on its own, yet it charged about a fifth of the average open-weight price and collected only about $2.60 of every $100 businesses spent there.

Cheap models can take over most of the work because businesses are starting to buy AI the way internet companies have long bought space on one another's networks, mostly on price. TeleGeography, a telecom research firm, describes wholesale internet access as a service sold in huge amounts with little profit on each sale, where "a packet is a packet" and buyers have no reason to pay one seller more than another. Across major cities, the typical monthly price of a 100-gigabit connection fell about 17% a year from mid-2023 to mid-2026, so a connection that cost $100 at the start cost about $57 three years later. The price of getting an AI model to perform at a set level has dropped far faster than bandwidth prices, about 13-fold each year since 2023, Epoch AI, a research group, found. At Epoch's rate, AI work that costs $100 one year costs less than $8 the next.

Teams on Vercel's gateway change suppliers often, and in September nearly half of them switched the model they use most, while almost a third moved to a different lab, a record for the gateway. So each lab has to persuade its own customers to stay, month after month.

When customers pick whoever charges the least, the companies with the lowest costs are the ones left making money. AWS holds down its expenses by designing its own chips and closely managing its fixed costs, and it turned nearly 39 cents of every dollar of sales into operating profit in the third quarter. "The profitability you've been seeing recently is really not a random output," Amazon Chief Financial Officer Brian Olsavsky told analysts on the company's earnings call. Most of Bedrock, AWS's service for hosting other companies' AI models, now runs on Trainium, a chip Amazon designs, and CEO Andy Jassy said the chip lets AWS keep prices low for customers. "We like the margins associated with it," he added. Even AWS earns a smaller profit on AI than on its other services, Olsavsky said, so as AI becomes a bigger part of AWS's sales, AWS can keep a smaller share of each dollar it brings in.

Companies still pay top prices for the most capable AI models. In the third quarter, each lab's best and most expensive releases collected nearly two of every three dollars companies spent on AI models, up from about one in three a year earlier, according to Menlo Ventures, a venture capital firm that has invested in Anthropic. Menlo found that buyers keep choosing the most capable model over the cheapest one. Anthropic gained the most, collecting about $55 of every $100 companies spent through the labs' APIs, up from $40 a year before. When teams on Vercel's gateway put more money into open-weight models in September, about two of every three also put more into the big labs' closed models. Gateway customers now pay the top labs 10 times what they did in December, yet the labs' share of the gateway's money has fallen 22%, because open-weight models' revenue there grew more than seventyfold. A company can spend more with a top lab while relying on it for fewer jobs, Vercel found, so the lab gets more money but a smaller part of the customer's business.

For some young companies, a cheaper model costs more than it saves. Jon Gordner is CEO of Comment.io, a weeks-old startup whose product is a shared document editor like Google $GOOGL Docs for programmers and their AI agents, and he sticks with Anthropic's and OpenAI's models. "We need to make as good software as we can as fast as possible. And for us, saving a few dollars on a cheaper model isn't worth it if we have to spend two or three more weeks fixing its mistakes," he told NPR. Gordner said Anthropic and OpenAI are selling monthly subscriptions at a steep discount to sign up customers, and once the cheap prices end, his company will start looking at Chinese and open-source models. He expected the two labs to start charging more as they faced pressure to show a profit before going public, which Anthropic did on Thursday. "At some point," he said, "the music's going to stop."

Epoch AI suggests that when an AI model first reaches a new level of skill, its maker may be able to charge extra for a short time, until rivals catch up and the price plunges. In January 2025, OpenAI's o3 model scored 75% on a PhD-level science exam at a cost of about 30 cents a question. Just under 18 months later, OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Luna matched o3's score for four-hundredths of a cent a question, a drop Epoch compares to a new car's sticker price falling from $50,000 to $69.

Menlo's count covers companies' API spending across the market over a full year. Vercel's monthly figures for its own gateway show how fast companies can move their spending from one lab to another. Anthropic's share of spending on the gateway fell from about 64% in August to 44% in September, its first month under half, and a drop in spending on its top Opus models accounted for most of the decline. "No one's position is guaranteed. Every major model release is an opportunity to shift market share," Deedy Das, a partner at Menlo Ventures, said.

Anthropic has cut its prices too, and it now charges $5 to feed a million tokens into Claude Opus 5, down from $15 for Claude Opus 4.1, a two-thirds reduction. In recent weeks, Anthropic and OpenAI have begun discounting even their flagship models, Menlo found. Ara Kharazian, lead economist at Ramp, a corporate-card and expense-software company, predicted in July that the American labs would answer cheap Chinese competitors by holding down prices or releasing strong open models of their own. "The only reason why I'm bearish about the Chinese models is because I assume that the American model companies will respond competitively," he told NPR.

Telecom companies in the late 1990s went broke because they laid cable faster than customers signed up to use it. Forecasts that internet use would keep growing without limit led companies to borrow heavily and lay huge amounts of fiber, but sales never matched the predictions, John Thompson, an associate economist at the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas, wrote in early 2002. The downturn left many firms with huge debts and weakening revenue, and lenders stopped lending freely to any company with "telecom" in its name. Credit-rating firms lowered their grades on the industry's debt, and WorldCom, Global Crossing, and many smaller carriers went bankrupt.

Anthropic's revenue can shrink faster than its bills. Four of every five dollars Anthropic brought in during the first half came from charges for each token customers used, according to Alpha Agentic, a site that analyzes company filings, so customers can cut their spending in any month. Anthropic's own prospectus warns that customers might drift toward "smaller, cheaper, or open-source alternatives." Anthropic's computing commitments are much harder to cut. By the end of June, Anthropic had committed to about $587 billion in computing purchases it can't cancel and data center leases that haven't started yet, about 30 times its revenue over the previous 12 months, Moat Explorer, a research site, calculated from the prospectus.

Anthropic now earns far more on each customer request than it did in 2024, but its new computing contracts could lower what it earns on each one. In 2024, Anthropic spent about $2 serving customers for every $1 they paid. By the first half of this year, the company kept about 85 cents of every revenue dollar after paying for the computing behind customers' requests, Moat Explorer calculated, though Anthropic doesn't report the figure. Anthropic says its profit on each request may shrink as the data centers it has signed for start running and as it pays more for computing power it rents on short notice. The computing power from its SpaceXAI deals, which Anthropic expects to arrive in full in December, will cost about $3.1 billion a month, roughly $37 billion a year, though Anthropic can leave the contracts on 90 days' notice after early 2027.

AI customers will keep paying less for each answer, however Anthropic's shares trade. For Anthropic's new shareholders to come out ahead, companies will have to keep paying top prices for Claude even as rivals match its models within months.
