All modern hiking apparel is made from natural fibers, synthetic fibers, or a blend of the two. Merino and alpaca wool are my two favorite natural options, as both offer excellent thermal regulation, breathability, and odor-resistance. They’re fantastic for longer trips, allowing you to pack less and travel farther while staying dry, comfortable, and stink-free. In most cases, however, they tend to be more expensive than their synthetic counterparts.

Synthetic fibers like polyester, nylon, and polypropylene all dry faster than Merino, are typically lighter weight, and more budget-friendly. However, they don’t offer the same odor resistance, and they’re all petroleum-based products. So, they’re hardly eco-friendly.

The key is regulating your body temperature with proper layering. Start with a thin base layer that feels good against the skin and fits closely without being too restrictive. Add an insulating midlayer like a half-zip or full-zip fleece. In especially cold conditions, adding a down shirt or full down jacket will help provide extra warmth. Top it off with a final layer that’s designed for weather protection (to fend off wind and precipitation) in the form of a hard or soft shell. Gore-Tex is the gold standard for this last layer as it offers the best protection, however, it’s also the most expensive. To maintain a comfortable body temperature and ensure you don’t overheat (and sweat), shed or add layers as necessary throughout your cold weather hike. This is where having a little extra room in your pack allows for stashing those extra layers.

There are a lot of individual factors that determine which jacket is right for you, including the quality of down used and how cold or hot you tend to run personally. But fill power is often the most straightforward spec to consider when determining the warmth of a puffy or down jacket. In general, 700 to 800 fill power will keep you warm for most cold-weather hikes. Upgrade to 850-plus fill power for extreme, subzero winter conditions.

It’s also important to consider the face fabric construction. Denier measures both weight and durability. For example, a 7D to 10D down jacket will be lighter and more packable, but also more prone to damage on the trail. Upgrading to a 15D-plus puffer adds weight but also durability. There’s no right answer as it depends on your budget and style of hiking.

Boot insulation is often measured in grams. A winter hiking boot with 200 grams of insulation should suffice in most cold weather conditions, while a 400g+ alternative is better for subzero temps, hiking in snow, or if your feet run cold. A waterproof membrane (again, Gore-Tex is best) is essential for winter boots, especially if you know you’ll be hiking in wet or snowy conditions. Finally, look for an outsole with a winter-centric tread compound with multi-directional lugs (I love Vibram Arctic Grip) for maximum traction in snow and ice.
