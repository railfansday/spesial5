Muscle Size vs. Strength: Which Matters More for Living Longer? Experts Explain

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Plus, the difference between the two resistance training methods.

When it comes to aging well, you've probably heard that building muscle is one of the best things you can do for your body. And it's true: Maintaining muscle mass is essential for staying healthy, mobile, and independent as you get older. But here's something you might not realize: Having more muscle doesn't necessarily mean you're stronger. And when it comes to living as long (and as well!) as possible, that distinction matters.

Resistance training, which is often used as an umbrella term, can have different goals, including building muscle size and increasing maximal strength. Training for muscle growth, known as hypertrophy training, focuses on increasing the size of your muscles, says Vonda Wright, MD, an orthopedic surgeon who researches longevity and muscle health.

On the other hand, training for maximal strength focuses on improving how much force your muscles can produce. It’s often measured by how much weight you can lift in a particular exercise, Dr. Wright explains. Although larger muscles have the potential to produce more force, muscle size alone doesn’t determine strength. “That’s why someone who is smaller can sometimes be stronger than someone with visibly larger muscles,” she adds.

Meet the experts: Vonda Wright, MD, is an orthopedic surgeon and author of Unbreakable. Mark Kovacs, PhD, CSCS, is an exercise physiologist, longevity and human performance specialist, and founder of the Kovacs Institute in Atlanta. Stuart Phillips, PhD, is a muscle researcher and professor of kinesiology at McMaster University. Ben Walker, CPT, is a personal trainer and founder of Anywhere Fitness in Dublin, Ireland.

Still, both adaptations play an important role in healthy aging—especially since muscle mass and strength naturally decline with age. Building muscle supports your joints and bones, helps regulate metabolic health, and gives you the strength and physical capacity to keep moving and stay active, says Mark Kovacs, PhD, CSCS, an exercise physiologist, longevity and human performance specialist, and founder of the Kovacs Institute. Strength, meanwhile, allows you to put that muscle to work, whether you’re carrying groceries, climbing stairs, or getting up from the floor, he explains.

It’s imperative to build both strength and muscle for a long, healthy life. But if your goal is to stay active and independent well into your later years, one may be worth prioritizing over the other.

Resistance training can help you build muscle and become stronger, but these are distinct goals that emphasize different outcomes. Training for muscle growth focuses on increasing muscle size, while training for maximal strength focuses on improving how much force you can produce, says Kovacs. The two goals overlap, and people typically gain both muscle and strength through resistance training. But you can formulate your workouts to emphasize one outcome over the other, he adds.

For instance, if your goal is maximal strength, the weight you lift matters more. So, training for strength means practicing movements with loads that feel heavy to you, says Stuart Phillips, PhD, a muscle researcher and professor of kinesiology at McMaster University. Strength gains depend not only on muscle size, but also on how effectively your nervous system activates your muscles and how efficiently you perform the movement. “Lighter weights can still make you stronger, but taking them to failure doesn’t automatically make them equivalent to heavier training for maximizing strength,” Phillips explains. For this goal, he recommends 2 to 3 sets of roughly 4 to 8 reps with a challenging weight, stopping before your technique breaks down.

In terms of muscle growth, both lighter and heavier weights can build muscle—what matters is that your body is sufficiently challenged. Of course, lighter weights will require more repetitions to grow muscle, Phillips says. (In fact, his research shows that performing roughly 20 to 25 repetitions with light weights produced similar muscle growth to sets of 8 to 12 repetitions with heavier weights when both were taken to failure.)

As a rule of thumb, Phillips recommends choosing a weight that makes the last few reps feel challenging while leaving about two good reps in reserve. (If you finish 25 reps and feel like you could easily do five more, the weight is probably too light to provide enough of a stimulus to spark growth.) One way to gauge this is with the resistance-training rating of perceived exertion (RPE) scale, which measures how hard a set feels based on how many more reps you think you could complete with good form, he explains. The scale runs from 1 to 10: An RPE of eight means you could squeeze out about two more reps, a 9 means you have one left, and a 10 means you’ve reached failure and can’t complete another rep.

The number of sets you perform matters for building muscle, too. Doing one challenging set per exercise consistently over time can grow muscle, Phillips says, but research suggests that performing multiple sets generally leads to greater muscle growth.

Strength, which is the maximum force you can produce in a particular task, directly influences how well you can move through everyday life. “Strong muscles support our skeleton, protect our joints, improve metabolic health, and allow us to continue doing the things we love at 50, 70, and 90,” says Dr. Wright. Strength helps us with getting up off the floor, carrying our own suitcase, climbing a mountain, playing with grandchildren, and living independently long-term.

Consider something as simple as getting out of a chair. When you’re younger and stronger, Kovacs says that movement may require only a small percentage of your maximum strength. As you lose strength with age, though, that same movement requires more of what you have available. If you aren’t regularly training it, everyday activities can become increasingly difficult, he explains. But when you continue to build strength as you age, you have more to pull from, making those activities easier.

Additionally, research draws a direct connection between strength and a longer life: A 2026 study in JAMA Network Open looked at the association between muscular strength and mortality. Over 5,000 women ages 63 to 99 underwent testing for grip strength, chair stand performance, and a timed walk test. Participants also mailed health update questionnaires annually between 2012 and 2014 until 2023. They found that higher muscle strength was associated with lower mortality.

Strength training is also associated with cardiometabolic benefits, endurance performance, and bone density—which are all important for living long and well. In fact, a small 2022 study in Sensors had 29 postmenopausal women with osteopenia or osteoporosis (when you have lower bone mineral density than normal) either do strength training exercises with heavy and moderately heavy weights, or no exercise at all, for six months. The group that strength trained had higher bone mineral density at the end of the trial period.

Muscle is one of your body’s best forms of insurance for getting older. Everyday activities depend on having enough muscle (as well as strength) to do them independently. “Muscle supports our joints and loads our bones, helping stimulate the bone-building process, but it’s also essential to mobility and functional strength," Dr. Wright explains. “If we want to continue getting ourselves off the ground, climbing stairs, carrying things, traveling, playing sports, and living independently, we need muscle to do it.”

Research also concludes that less muscle mass is associated with a higher risk of mortality. A 2023 review in PLOS One observed 16 studies with over 81,000 participants and looked at the connection between their body composition and when they died. The review concluded that a low skeletal muscle mass index was significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality.

Additionally, having muscle mass can improve your metabolic health. A 2025 study in Nutrition & Metabolism had older participants with type 2 diabetes either build muscle through resistance training alone, resistance training and eating a whey protein supplement, or doing neither of the two, for 12 weeks. Those who completed the muscle-building interventions significantly improved their blood glucose control and insulin resistance.

Plus, lower muscle mass is linked to poor balance and a higher risk of falls. So, maintaining muscle helps protect your mobility and independence as you age, Dr. Wright adds. A 2020 study in Menopause Review had 122 women over 60 do physical performance tests (gait speed test, grip strength, and a timed up and go). Their body composition was measured, and they filled out a survey of how often they fell in the past year. Less muscle was associated with a higher risk of falls.

A well-rounded workout plan should naturally progress both muscular size and strength. After all, they address different pieces of the same puzzle and one form of training can help you make progress in the other. As you build muscle, you generally increase the amount of tissue available to produce force, says Ben Walker, CPT, a certified personal trainer and founder of Anywhere Fitness in Dublin, Ireland. And as you train for strength, your brain and nervous system become better at coordinating and activating those muscles, he explains.

Remember, longevity isn’t about simply adding years to your life. It’s about extending your healthspan, or the number of years you remain healthy, mobile, and strong. “The goal is to bring our healthspan as close to our lifespan as possible, and for me, that means building a body that can carry you through the life you want to live,” says Dr. Wright. That way, you’ll be ready to throw grandkids around at 60, deadlift hundreds of pounds at 70, and lift your own suitcase on vacation with your family in your 80s and beyond.
