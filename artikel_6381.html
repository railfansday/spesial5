When AI does the work, what happens to our skills?

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Relying on AI may quietly erode professional skills, a new paper argues, calling for shared safeguards to preserve human expertise.

Letting an AI tool draft a report or read a scan feels like getting help.

But handing over those tasks may gradually weaken the skills needed to do them independently, and people may struggle to notice the change.

Kaisa Kärki of the University of Helsinki makes the case in a new paper on what researchers call deskilling.

The word covers both losing a human skill and never developing it, because the task has been handed to artificial intelligence.

Her conclusion is that professions, not professionals, should decide which skills stay in human hands.

The paper is an argument built on published studies, not a new experiment, and it draws most of its evidence from medicine.

Most human skills need regular use to survive, Kärki wrote, citing what psychologists call the “use it or lose it” principle.

In one study of surgeons she cites, every skill measured decayed after a long break, and complex tasks done by novices suffered most, while heavily practiced skills held up best.

AI changes what counts as a break. A professional who does a task with an AI assistant doesn’t think of herself as having stopped doing it, Kärki noted.

So the skill can decay while the job appears to go on as before.

The same goes for skills never learned. In a programming study she cites, people learned far more without AI help because they had to find and fix their own errors.

In medicine, trainees may lose chances to learn when the tool does the hard part, and people can struggle when it is taken away.

People are poor judges of their own abilities, Kärki wrote.

Across many studies, self-ratings line up only moderately with measured ability, and most people rate themselves generously.

So professionals may not reliably recognize when their skills are fading.

The studies reviewed by Kärki suggest that experienced professionals are also vulnerable.

Very experienced radiologists reading mammograms showed deskilling, though far less than inexperienced ones.

After endoscopists began using AI, their colonoscopies performed without it detected adenomas – potentially precancerous polyps – in 22.4% of patients, down from 28.4% before AI was introduced.

Accountants who had worked for more than a decade without AI tools showed heavy skill loss once they had them.

“One of the problems with deskilling is that professionals have difficulty noticing it in their everyday lives, partly due to biases about AI reliance,” Kärki said.

“When we converse with large language models and they respond convincingly, it easily leads to overreliance. However, these models also make mistakes.”

Doctors are not oblivious. In a survey Kärki cites, 74% of physicians said they worried about their own deskilling.

Worry is not measurement, though, and the loss usually shows up only when the tool is switched off.

Kärki’s central point is about responsibility.

Philosophers have generally held that people answer for what they do knowingly. A bias you can’t see in yourself doesn’t fit that picture.

“The inconspicuous biases associated with AI use pose something of a challenge to these viewpoints: after all, we don’t notice our own biases or, say, tendency to rely too much on AI,” she said.

“It’s therefore unclear whether we can be held responsible for the insidious deterioration of our skills and the mistakes that will result.”

The mistakes can be serious. In one test she cites, the best large language models answered 85% of cancer-care questions correctly.

More than 80% of the wrong answers carried a high chance of moderate to severe harm.

In Kärki’s view, holding individual doctors responsible may overlook the wider changes in training and practice that contribute to skill loss.

If a whole field relies on AI, Kärki wrote, the field can lose the ability to teach its own basics, a problem earlier authors called the fragility of the system.

No single surgeon knows how many other surgeons are relying on a robot for the same step. A professional body can find out.

So the first job, in her view, belongs to professional communities. They should decide, from inside the field, which skills are core and which tasks should stay at least partly in human hands.

After that come the tools, such as regular skill checks, stretches of training with no AI, and making the human decision before looking at the AI’s answer.

Pilots already train for autopilot failure and log manual flying time, she wrote.

The European Union’s AI Act recognizes that people over-trust automated systems, but Kärki pointed out that it says nothing about the skill loss that follows.

“In medical education, it has already been argued that training in basic skills should include a stage where AI is not used,” she said.

“For instance, diagnostic reasoning is a skill central to medical practice, as is general understanding of the field and the associated tasks.”

Deskilling is hard to measure, Kärki noted. It builds gradually and may become apparent when the AI is removed, and its effects on judgment and critical thinking are hard to count.

Most of the evidence comes from medicine, and Kärki wrote that other fields need the same scrutiny, along with studies that follow the same professionals for years.

Nor is it settled which skills are safe to hand over and which are not, or whether a professional who lets a skill lapse is ever to blame.

According to Kärki, those are the next questions for philosophers, but regulators should not wait for the answers.

“Deskilling involves major risks that humanity and professions should pre-emptively consider before skills decay at the community level,” she said.

Until professions draw those lines, each worker is left to notice a loss that the research says is hard to notice alone.

The full study was published in the journal Human Affairs.
