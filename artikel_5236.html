Enjin Matrixchain Votes on OpenGov Today: What Changes for ENJ Holders on November 16

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

AI-assisted summary, reviewed by NullTX editors.

Enjin holders vote through November 13 on moving Matrixchain to OpenGov on November 16. Here is how the nine tracks, the 1,000 ENJ deposit and conviction voting work, and what the Relaychain vote shows.

Enjin's new proposal deposit sounds steep at 1,000 ENJ. It's actually the cheap part.

Enjin holders have until today, November 13, to vote on moving the Matrixchain to the Fort Canning release. The referendum opened November 10. If it passes, the upgrade is targeted for November 16 at block #12,319,600, with the network moving from runtime 1.3.1 to 1.4.3. Enjin's Fort Canning announcement lays out the plan, and the official changelog carries the technical detail.

The Relaychain has already made the jump. Referendum #16 approved its upgrade on October 28, and it went live on November 9. The Matrixchain vote is the second half of one upgrade, and it is the half that changes how Matrixchain makes decisions from then on.

Early coverage reported the schedule and the headline numbers: nine tracks, 1,000 ENJ. The changelog and the governance portal show the mechanics behind them, and those mechanics change more than the headlines suggest.

Today, the Matrixchain runs its governance through the older Democracy, Council and Technical Committee pallets, the modules that handle proposals and votes. Fort Canning replaces all three with a new set: Referenda, Conviction Voting, Origins, Whitelist and the Enjin Fellowship. Under OpenGov, any account can submit a referendum on one of nine tracks, and from the moment the upgrade lands, Matrixchain's own upgrades go through that process.

A track is a lane for a particular kind of decision. Enjin's changelog describes the families: Root and the admin tracks for the highest-stakes changes, Whitelisted Caller for proposals the Fellowship fast-tracks, and two special tracks, Referendum Killer and Referendum Canceller, for stopping a proposal that shouldn't proceed. Matrixchain gets no treasury tracks: those, sized by how much a proposal would spend, stay on the Relaychain, where treasury requests now go. Each track has its own deposit, approval curve and enactment period, and the Enjin Fellowship can fast-track time-sensitive proposals.

Splitting decisions this way means a small treasury request doesn't face the same bar as a change to the chain's core. Riskier tracks demand larger deposits and more support, while smaller ones move faster and cost less.

The submission deposit rises from 0.025 ENJ to 1,000 ENJ on every track, a 40,000-fold increase. The deposit is refunded when a referendum passes or is canceled, and it exists to deter spam. As an illustration, using the roughly $66 million market cap reported in early October, ENJ traded near three cents, which would put 1,000 ENJ around $33.

That is only the entry ticket. The cost that matters comes next. A referendum also needs a decision deposit before it moves into voting, and the changelog lists steep figures: 2,500,000 ENJ on Root and Fellowship Admin, 1,000,000 ENJ on the other admin tracks, and 2,500 to 250,000 ENJ on the Relaychain's treasury tracks. At the same illustrative price, the 2.5 million ENJ Root deposit comes to roughly $80,000, about 0.12% of the 2.02 billion ENJ total issuance.

So proposing is cheap, and pushing a proposal through is not. Anyone can raise an idea, but anyone who wants the network to vote on something big needs real stake in the outcome. The changelog says the same values apply on both chains, and Matrixchain adopts them for its new tracks.

The best evidence of how these votes work comes from the Relaychain referendum that has already finished. Enjin's governance portal shows referendum #16 as finalised, approved on October 28 and enacted on November 9.

The final tally on the page is small. Eleven voters cast 381,303.02 ENJ of conviction-weighted voting power, and all of it went to Aye: 100% in favor, 0 ENJ against. The page also shows raw, unweighted support of 271,303.2 ENJ, which it describes as the total balance backing the proposal and the figure the support curve uses. The conviction multiplier on the page runs from under 1x up to 6x.

Two numbers stand out. First, an entire chain upgrade passed with 271,303 ENJ of raw support, about 0.013% of the 2.02 billion ENJ total issuance, a figure worked out from the portal's number. Second, conviction added about 110,000 ENJ of weight on top of the raw balance, an average multiplier of roughly 1.4x. Voters picked up some extra weight, but most didn't lock their tokens for the long periods that earn the top multipliers.

A 100% Aye result says little by itself. A scheduled upgrade with no organized opposition passes easily, and the tally shows how thin engagement was.

Fort Canning adds the safeguard that thin turnout calls for. A referendum now needs a minimum share of total ENJ voting in support. The changelog sets 0.5% on Root and the admin tracks, 0.005% to 0.125% on the Relaychain's treasury tracks, 0.1% for Referendum Killer and 0.05% for Referendum Canceller. Measured against 2.02 billion ENJ, that means about 10.1 million ENJ on Root, roughly 101,000 to 2.5 million on the treasury tracks, about 2.02 million to kill a referendum and about 1.01 million to cancel one.

Referendum #16's 271,303 ENJ of raw support would clear the lowest treasury floor of about 101,000 ENJ, but it would miss the Root floor by a factor of about 37. The page doesn't show which track the referendum used, and a runtime upgrade typically sits on a high-stakes track, so the Root comparison works as an illustration and not as a finding. The floors also didn't exist when #16 passed, since Fort Canning introduced them.

That is the real change. Under the new rules, 11 wallets can't carry a chain-level decision on their own. Low turnout means failure, and proposals that matter will need far more ENJ behind them. Enjin's changelog says Matrixchain upgrades go through OpenGov starting with this one, which suggests today's vote still runs on the current system, so the floors will first bite on the first OpenGov referendum.

Holders who haven't voted can still do so through Enjin's governance portal before the window closes. Voting locks tokens, so the lock length is worth checking first. Enjin's support article on voting explains the trade-off: the longer the lock, the more a vote counts, and tokens stay locked until the period ends.

Node operators on the Matrixchain can wait for the result before upgrading, as Enjin has indicated, and then follow the changelog for the version to install. Relaychain operators already needed client version 1.8.1.

This vote is more about control than speed. OpenGov spreads decisions across tracks with deposits and support floors built for the weight of each choice, and it moves ENJ holders from voting on a handful of items to a standing governance system. The Relaychain's first vote showed how little participation it took under the old rules, and the first referenda after November 16 will show whether holders turn out for the new ones. If most proposals stall at the decision deposit, the system costs too much. If they clear with a handful of voters, the floors aren't doing their job. Either result will tell holders what their ENJ can do.
