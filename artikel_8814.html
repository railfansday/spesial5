Jess McClain’s Coach—Record-Setting Ultramarathoner David Roche—Shares a Key Training Tactic for Fighting Late-Race Fatigue

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

This simple long-run tweak can help your legs stay strong when the late miles hit.

Many runners think of the climbs as the toughest part of a hilly race. Uphills send your heart rate soaring and slow even the strongest runners to a grind, while descents offer a chance to catch your breath and let gravity do some of the work. But David Roche, ultramarathoner and coach to New York City Marathon women’s runner-up Jess McClain, says those easier-feeling downhills can come at a cost later in the race.

And Roche knows the importance of late-race performance, which is why he integrates strategies into his training to fight fatigue through long distances.

Take McClain’s finish in New York City as an example: She outkicked reigning champ Hellen Obiri just feet from the finish line to take second place by mere seconds, clocking 2:24:42 and becoming the first American woman to reach the podium in NYC since 2018.

Roche’s fatigue-fighting tactics take him even farther in his own races. The two-time Life Time Leadville 100 winner—and course record holder—covered the famously demanding trails in 15:12:30, averaging roughly 9:07 per mile. Yet he rarely relies on extremely long, arduous training runs to prepare for conquering late miles.

Instead, Roche uses downhill workouts to reproduce some of the muscular stress that can cause runners’ legs to fail in an endurance event. “Downhills are the gateway to fatigue resistance in these really long events,” Roche tells Runner’s World.

The best part is, you don’t have to be an elite like McClain or Roche to benefit from his advice. He shares how purposeful downhill training can help runners preserve their strength, form, and pace deep into a race. Plus, he offers his go-to workout to add to your buildup.

Running downhill may provide some relief to your cardiovascular system—by lowering your heart rate a bit as gravity naturally carries you downward—but it puts much more strain on your musculoskeletal system. To keep you from tumbling down the hill, the quads must repeatedly brake the body’s momentum.

Braking action involves eccentric contractions, meaning the muscles produce force while lengthening under load, Roche explains. Eccentric contractions occur during many activities—including simply walking down stairs—but running a lengthy descent can deliver thousands in succession, significantly increasing the load on your muscles.

When the body isn’t accustomed to the stress caused by downhill running, the aftermath can wreck you. Roche explains that while a runner may feel relatively normal after their first downhill running workout, they end up developing significant soreness within a day or two. “That feeling of, ‘Oh man, I have no quads right now,’ a few days later is also what happens to your quads in an ultra,” Roche says—and even a marathon.

The initial downhill running effort, however, also triggers an adaptation known as the repeated bout effect. A review published in 2024 on the subject describes the repeated bout effect as a form of skeletal-muscle memory in which the first eccentric challenge activates protective responses that make the muscle more resistant to damage than subsequent ones.

Scientists haven’t identified one physiological mechanism that fully explains the effect. Instead, the review suggests several adaptations may work together. The nervous system may distribute the load more effectively across the muscles, while changes to muscle fibers, tendons, and the extracellular matrix—the structural network surrounding the cells—may help the tissue tolerate force. The inflammatory response may also become better regulated after the body has encountered that stress before.

Basically, the more downhill running you do in your training, the less sore your legs will become after each workout, Roche explains. Using the downhill bout effect to your benefit in this way helps keep your legs stronger later in races as they handle similar impact.

Research shows this phenomenon in action: In a study published in 2022, 10 recreational male runners completed the same 30-minute treadmill run at a 20 percent decline twice, with three weeks between sessions. Following the second run, the athletes experienced a smaller rise in creatine kinase—a blood marker commonly used to assess muscle damage following exercise—along with less soreness and a smaller reduction in maximal force. Their running mechanics were also less disrupted after the second session.

The practical takeaway: With the appropriate exposure, the legs can learn to handle downhills with less fallout. That makes downhill training particularly valuable for ultrarunners who spend multiple hours in a race descending hills, but Roche believes the same principle applies to road runners, too.

At mile 20 of your marathon, your limiting factor isn’t always your heart or your lungs. You could simply lack the muscular strength to keep the same mechanics or pace. Downhill running is Roche’s solution to prepare for that problem without requiring immense volume, and his go-to workout is simpler than you might expect.

For most runners, Roche’s downhill workout isn’t a complicated interval session. It’s an easy long run with a deliberate change in pace when you reach a decline. Roche uses variations of this downhill workout twice per week in his own training, recommending most athletes start with one session and progress from there based on how they recover.

“Because the aerobic demand [of the run] is low, it’s just about pushing the steeper terrain in a way that causes eccentric muscle contractions,” Roche says.

Instead of trying to stack as many miles as possible to mimic race-day conditions, Roche recommends runners focus race-day muscular demand into a 16-mile effort. The faster descents repeatedly load the quads, while the surrounding easy running provides the aerobic work that’s crucial for long-distance racing, says Roche.

According to Roche, in an ideal scenario, you should aim for downhills as steep as—or steeper than—those in your goal race. Running these downhills faster than your race pace ensures your muscles can take on the challenges your goal race will present.

While Roche’s recommended 16-mile run is generally reserved for experienced athletes preparing for marathons or longer, you can scale the same principle to nearly any experience level or race distance by shortening the overall run.

The purpose of downhill training isn’t to leave the quads wrecked every week. Initial sessions may cause soreness, but over time, the same type of descent should become less disruptive, allowing you to recover more easily and withstand greater late-race fatigue.

The workout succeeds not because of how much punishment the quads absorb during one run, but because they’re still capable of working when the race delivers the same punishment as you approach the finish line.
