Your Cloud Went Post-Quantum Last Week and Nobody Told You

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

On November 6, Microsoft turned on hybrid post-quantum TLS on Azure Front Door. By default. No toggle, no migration guide, no email you had to act on. Three days later, Azure App Service and Azure Functions started the same rollout. The next day, AWS shipped hybrid post-quantum key exchange in DocumentDB 8.0.3.

I only noticed because I was checking something else entirely. That is the story here, really. The biggest migration in the history of cryptography is happening the way most infrastructure changes happen: quietly, in a platform update, while nobody is watching.

Front Door is the interesting one, because it sits at the edge. When a user opens your app, their browser talks to the nearest Microsoft point of presence first. As of this month, that handshake can negotiate X25519MLKEM768, a hybrid key exchange group that runs a classical X25519 exchange and an ML-KEM-768 encapsulation side by side, then mixes both shared secrets into the session keys.

The default key exchange policy, called Standard, now offers SecP256r1MLKEM768 and X25519MLKEM768 first, then falls back to the classical groups prime256v1, secp384r1 and secp521r1 if the client does not support either hybrid. If your browser is recent, you are probably already doing this and have no idea.

App Service is the same idea one layer down. Inbound connections to Web Apps and Functions apps on App Service infrastructure negotiate X25519MLKEM768 when both sides support TLS 1.3 and the group. Microsoft says you do not need to change your code, add a setting, touch your minimum TLS version, or get a new certificate. It is a platform-managed rollout, region by region, and during the rollout some front ends will have it and some will not.

AWS did the smaller, quieter version of the same thing. DocumentDB 8.0.3 negotiates a hybrid ML-KEM exchange for TLS 1.3 when the client supports it, and AWS added two CloudWatch metrics so you can actually see it happening: TLSHandshakesClassical and TLSHandshakesPostQuantum. That pair of metrics is the part I liked most. Somebody at AWS understood that a migration nobody can measure is a migration nobody will finish.

The pure post-quantum argument goes like this: ML-KEM is a NIST standard, it has survived years of scrutiny, just switch. The hybrid argument goes like this: ML-KEM is a NIST standard, it has survived years of scrutiny, and it is still young enough that a surprise would not shock anyone who has watched cryptography for a decade. Running both at once means the session stays safe if either one holds. You are not betting the house on a single algorithm that is younger than most of the engineers deploying it.

That is also why the rollout is opportunistic rather than enforced. There is no setting in App Service to require the hybrid group. If the client cannot do it, the handshake falls back to classical X25519 and life goes on. Microsoft and AWS both chose reach over strictness, and I think that is the right call for a first wave. A hard cutover in November would have broken old clients and turned the whole thing into a support incident. The quiet version gets the coverage up first and the arguments can come later.

The timing is not an accident either. The harvest-now-decrypt-later clock is the thing that actually forces the schedule. Traffic captured today can be stored and decrypted the day a capable quantum machine exists, so the deadline is not the day the machine arrives, it is years before it. The UK's Bank of England, PRA and FCA published joint effective practices on quantum risk on November 19, and IOSCO published its final report on quantum computing and financial markets the same day. The regulators are now writing down what the engineers have been saying for a while: start now, document the plan, do not wait for the machine.

Here is the honest version of where this leaves us. The edge is moving. The core is not.

Front Door protects the hop from the user to the edge. App Service protects the hop from the client to the front end. Neither one covers what happens after that. The connection from the App Service front end to your worker is still classical, and Microsoft says so plainly in its own docs. Functions apps on Container Apps are not in this rollout at all, because they use Container Apps ingress instead of the App Service front end. The back half of the request path is a separate project with no date attached.

That is not a criticism. It is how you roll out a change this size without breaking the internet. But it matters if you are the kind of person who reads an announcement headline and assumes the whole path is covered. It is not. The segment that changed is the one an attacker sitting on the wire can see, which is the segment that matters most for harvest-now-decrypt-later, but the job is half done and the second half has no deadline.

The same is true of the clients. Chrome switched its hybrid key share to ML-KEM in Chrome 131. Go 1.24 enables X25519MLKEM768 by default in crypto/tls. If your users are on modern browsers, they are covered. If your users are on old phones, old embedded clients, or a corporate fleet that updates once a year, they are still negotiating classically, and the platform will not tell you unless you go looking. AWS at least gave you the metrics. On Azure, the rollout is invisible by design.

This takes about thirty seconds if you have OpenSSL 3.5 on your machine:

Look for the negotiated group in the output. If it says X25519MLKEM768, your app is already doing hybrid post-quantum key exchange with clients that support it. If it falls back, either your region has not rolled out yet or the front end serving you does not have it. Try again in a week.

In Chrome or Edge, open DevTools, go to the Security tab, and look at the connection details. It will name the key exchange group in plain language.

For DocumentDB, the check is easier: open CloudWatch and look at TLSHandshakesPostQuantum versus TLSHandshakesClassical. That ratio is, as far as I know, the first time a cloud provider has let you watch your own post-quantum migration happen in real time. More providers should copy it.

I built my own messenger this year, and the key exchange in it is a hybrid of ML-KEM-768 and a classical curve, with ML-DSA-65 signatures on top. Doing it by hand taught me something the cloud announcements hide: the cryptography is the easy part. The hard part is everything around it. Key storage, algorithm negotiation, fallback behavior, what happens when one side is older than the other, and how you explain to a user why their connection is safe without lying about what "safe" means.

Microsoft and AWS just solved the negotiation and fallback parts for millions of apps at once, and they did it without asking any of those apps to change a line of code. That is a genuinely impressive piece of engineering, and the performance numbers back it up: Microsoft's synthetic testing showed under 1% CPU overhead for the ML-KEM groups, and production A/B testing found no measurable regression against classical X25519. The old argument that post-quantum is too slow for the edge is, at this point, mostly dead.

But the hand-built lesson applies to the cloud too. A hybrid handshake at the edge does not make your app post-quantum. It makes one hop post-quantum. Your database driver, your internal service-to-service calls, your long-lived backups, your signed artifacts: those are all still classical until you move them, and nobody is going to move them for you. The platform did the part it could do without your permission. The rest is yours.

So check your endpoint. Look at the metrics if you have them. And if the number comes back classical, do not panic: the rollout is still spreading, and the fallback is by design. Just do not mistake a quiet platform update for a finished migration. It is the start of one, and the start is the part that looks like nothing happened.
