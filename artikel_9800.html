Enterprise AI has become quite good at prediction. Given a food-delivery order, a machine-learning model can estimate the delivery time. A retailer can forecast demand for the next week. Large Language Models (LLMs) added another capability. They can understand unstructured information, generate content, summarize documents, interact with users and perform reasoning tasks.

But many enterprise problems do not end with prediction or reasoning. Eventually, the system has to decide:

This is where decision systems become important. Recently, System One models have been proposed for fast, structured decision-making. TypeSafe AI, for example, positions Jev as a System One model that accepts state and typed questions and returns structured probabilistic answers.

Another approach worth examining is the Energy-Based Model (EBM). EBMs are not new. The concept has been studied for several decades. What makes them relevant to enterprise architecture today is their ability to score possible outcomes or actions instead of simply producing one prediction.

This article looks at where Energy-Based Models can fit alongside traditional machine learning, LLMs and emerging System One models.

From this distribution: Mode=45 minutes; the median (P50) is also 45 minutes because cumulative probability crosses 50% at 45 minutes. Similarly: P90=55 minutes. We now have more information than a single ETA. But there is an important point here.

Traditional machine learning already provides strong techniques for uncertainty estimation. Quantile regression can directly estimate P50, P80 and P90. Probabilistic regression can estimate a complete probability distribution. LightGBM, XGBoost and neural-network-based approaches can all be used for this purpose. Hence, generating a probability distribution around an ETA is not sufficient justification for introducing an EBM.

An EBM becomes more interesting when the candidate being evaluated is richer than a single number. Before getting there, however, let us look at how prediction becomes a decision.

Every other candidate costs more (45 minutes: 18.5; 60 minutes: 13.25). Among these discrete candidates, the optimal customer promise is therefore 55 minutes. This agrees with the classic critical-ratio result: when being late costs four times as much as being early, the best promise is the 4 / (4 + 1) = 80th percentile, and 55 minutes is the first candidate whose cumulative probability (91%) reaches 80%.

Step 5: Train Good Actions to Have Lower Energy

The model still has to learn what constitutes a good action. Suppose historical data, an operational simulator, or another trusted decision process tells us that Action B produced the best outcome for a particular marketplace state.

We want: E_theta(x,A_B) < E_theta(x,A_A) and E_theta(x,A_B) < E_theta(x,A_C)

One convenient implementation is to treat negative energy as the candidate logit and apply a cross-entropy loss across the candidates of each decision, the same contrastive objective used to train energy-based policies in Implicit Behavioral Cloning. In practice the model learns from many logged decisions, each with its own context, its own set of feasible candidates and the index of the best one:

System One Models and Energy-Based Models should not automatically be treated as competing technologies. TypeSafe describes Jev, its first System One Model, as taking state and typed questions and returning typed probabilistic decisions. Unlike an autoregressive LLM, which generates strings token by token, Jev is designed around structured outputs and parallel sampling.

TypeSafe reports approximately 70–500 ms end-to-end response times and 40×–200× speed improvements for comparable System-One-shaped queries in some of its demonstrations. In our delivery example, a System One model might rapidly classify the marketplace state:

This judgment can influence which actions should even be considered. The Energy-Based Model then compares possible responses within that context. This can be interpreted as “System One narrows the interpretation of the world. Energy-based scoring compares possible responses to that world”.

LLMs remain extremely useful because enterprise information is often messy and unstructured. Suppose a restaurant manager writes:

"Heavy rain around the store. Two riders cancelled, kitchen is running about 15 minutes behind and we suddenly have 23 open orders."

An LLM can transform this into structured state:

That state can feed predictive and decision models. The LLM can also explain the final decision:

"The delivery promise was extended because the restaurant is currently delayed, driver availability is low and heavy rain is increasing expected travel time."

This leads to an architecture in which each model does what it is good at.

You can also build an EBM on top of a small LLM such as gemma-3-270m or Qwen3-0.6B. The idea is simple: use the LLM only to read, not to write. Describe the context and one candidate as a short piece of text, pass it through the model, and add a small layer on top (the “energy head”) that turns the model’s internal representation into a single energy score. The model’s text generation ability is not used at all.

This approach is useful when the candidates or the context contain free text: dispatcher notes, restaurant messages, or constraints written in plain language. A small LLM already understands such text, so you do not have to convert it into numeric features by hand. Gemma 3 270M is the lighter and faster option; Qwen3 0.6B is about twice the size and gives somewhat richer representations at a higher cost.

For purely numeric inputs, like the food delivery example in this article, a plain MLP (multilayer perceptron) is the better choice. It is faster and usually more accurate, because LLMs of any family handle numbers poorly when they are written as text. Switching from Gemma to Qwen does not change this.

A practical middle path is a hybrid: let the LLM read only the text part of the context, and feed the numeric features straight into the energy head.

None of these components has to replace all the others. The interesting architecture is often a composition of them.

An EBM can be a small neural network like the PyTorch example above. It does not require GPT, Claude, Llama or another LLM. For a structured enterprise problem such as pricing, scheduling, delivery planning or inventory allocation, I would often prefer a separate, domain-specific energy model. However, energy functions can also be built around large language models (References #7).

Unlike LLMs provided via closed APIs (OpenAI's GPT or Anthropic's Claude), Energy-Based Models are rarely offered as turnkey cloud APIs. This is due to the inherent computational difficulty of inference-time optimization (finding low-energy states typically requires iterative sampling or gradient descent loops at runtime). Instead, they are actively researched, custom-built, and integrated into specialized domain software such as autonomous vehicle trajectory planners, robotics policy optimizers, and advanced self-supervised world model architectures.
