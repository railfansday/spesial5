The Art of Releasing Resentment in Your ADHD Relationship

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

ADHD can complicate communication, expectations, and daily routines between partners. Here’s how to spot resentment and take steps toward healing and reconnection.

Resentment – the anger, bitterness, and disappointment that build from the feeling we’ve been wronged and treated unfairly over time – is a slowly simmering emotion. Eventually, its toxic burn clouds the way we view our relationship and may even suffocate it.

In relationships touched by ADHD, traits like forgetfulness, disorganization, and inconsistent follow-through can hasten these feelings and chip away at intimacy. ADHD partners, in turn, commonly resent their neurotypical partners for misunderstanding the condition and setting unrealistic expectations.

Resentment is a protective, multilayered emotion that deserves reflection. Follow these steps to gradually untangle the complex feelings you may be harboring about your partner so you can let go of resentment and start healing.

Resentment is a defense mechanism. Landing a sarcastic comment, giving the silent treatment, or thinking, “Because you didn’t do X, I won’t do Y,” helps us feel in control. These behaviors allow us to avoid confronting difficult feelings and situations, though they eventually backfire and sow deeper resentment. Become clear-eyed about resentment’s function in your relationship so you can opt for more productive responses instead.

Do you resent your partner for any specific behaviors (or lack thereof)? Peel back the layers on this. Resentment often sits on top of hurt, fear, shame, helplessness, and other uncomfortable feelings. It means that something important to you feels threatened, ignored, or unmet.

For each pain point, think about what lies underneath. What do your partner’s actions trigger in you? What stories are you telling yourself about their behavior? Some examples include:

“I feel like a child in this relationship.”

“I feel more like a parent than a partner.”

“My partner doesn’t respect how I do things or who I am.”

“I resent feeling like the family project.”

“My partner did something long ago and hasn’t apologized adequately.”

Resolving resentment won’t happen overnight. Acceptance and forgiveness are distinct processes that can help dissolve resentment, but they work differently. If forgiveness feels too “big,” go for the route of acceptance. If one partner keeps forgetting things, for example, asking for forgiveness every time can feel exhausting for both people. Acceptance says: “This is a feature of our relationship that I need to plan around” rather than “I need to forgive you 50 times.”

Miscommunication in neurodiverse relationships often comes from assumptions we make about our partners. We assume they process the world as we do and can read our minds. Review the following steps with your partner to engage in a brief and insightful “learning conversation” about resentments:

Speaker: When you seem to be upset and I ask you how you’re doing, you often say you’re feeling “fine.” That makes me feel anxious because I’m not sure it’s true.

Listener: So you feel anxious when I say I’m fine when you don’t perceive that to be the case… because you think I’m lying to you?

Speaker: You’re right that I do feel anxious, but I don’t worry so much that you’re lying to me. What I’m concerned about is that you’re not sharing your true self with me.

Listener: You feel that when I say “I’m fine,” I’m choosing not to open up to you… which makes you feel like I’m not comfortable with you and not invested in our relationship?

What are you willing to relinquish? To decide, get clear about your boundaries. Terri Cole, LCSW, a psychotherapist who specializes in boundaries, asks us to define our values by the following:

Things that matter to you but are highly flexible and negotiable. Rule of thumb: You’d be okay if you didn’t have them.

Our most important wishes and goals. They relate to how we envision our life. Compromising on deep desires often leads to resentment.

Behaviors, values, or situations that are fundamentally unacceptable to you. Crossing them usually means the relationship ends.

There are no right or wrong answers. A dealbreaker to one person may be a preference to another.

Consider where your resentments about your partner fit within your system. If you decide that something is a non-issue, create boundaries – rules that you set for yourself, not your partner.

Example: You’ve decided to let go of resentment about your partner’s untidiness. Your boundary: “What’s on their side of the room is their responsibility to clean.”
