Was Heineken’s ‘Pub Succession’ the most successful ad campaign ever to fail?

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

On Achill Island off Ireland’s west coast, there’s a pub that’s been in the McLoughlin family since 1870. Its owner, the fourth-generation publican Josie McLoughlin, was ready to retire but had no one in the family willing to take over. All he wanted was another McLoughlin to keep the pub running and the name above the door.

So Heineken stepped in and launched a global search for a successor. Ads from Times Square to Sydney. Press coverage in 128 countries. More than 2,000 people responded. Heineken flew two McLoughlin descendants to Ireland to meet Josie.

The campaign, “Pub Succession,” won five Cannes Lions in 2025 and made the shortlist for the festival’s top prize. In 2026, it added three D&AD Pencils and a Clio Grand. By every ad industry measure, it was a hit.

The problem? The campaign didn’t work.

McLoughlin’s closed on September 27. Nobody took over. It’s still for sale for 550,000 euros ($605,000), the same price at which it was listed for nearly two years before Heineken stepped in. Whoever succeeded Josie was always going to have to buy the pub.

Heineken says brokering a deal was never its job. Its role, a spokesperson says, was to “help McLoughlin’s identify potential successors, not to manage the sale.” Besides, the company adds, Irish law states that only licensed real estate agents can broker a sale. Fair enough. But Heineken wrote the pitch, and the pitch wasn’t just about introductions. It was about finding the next McLoughlin.

Why 2,000 sign-ups weren’t enough

The pubs you’re probably picturing are your local American iterations of Irish pubs or packed Dublin bars charging Dublin prices. That’s not McLoughlin’s. The pub sits in Achill Sound, a village of fewer than 300 souls in County Mayo. Many owners of rural pubs like this make a living and little more. Taking one over is less a Hallmark movie and more The Bear meets It’s Always Sunny in Philadelphia. Either you inherit a family business that can’t pay its own bills, or you need a deep-pocketed Danny DeVito character to bankroll it while you figure out how to make it viable.

So it’s easy to see why Josie McLoughlin’s children, according to Heineken’s PR agency, chose other careers. They’re not alone: 84% of publicans say family members don’t want to take on the family pub, according to research Heineken cited with the campaign.

“Mom and Pop can live on air,” says Pat Crotty, CEO of the Vintners’ Federation of Ireland, the trade association for Irish pubs outside of Dublin. “They have no mortgage. They have no loans. If something needs fixing, they’re probably fixing it themselves.” Meanwhile, he adds, their children have mortgages, car payments, and kids of their own. A pub that kept the parents comfortable can’t cover all that. “When you hand it on within a family, there’s not much to hand on.”

Banks aren’t lining up to lend 550,000 euros against a pub like this, either. “There isn’t much capacity to invest, or obvious or visible capacity to repay, if someone is even prepared to go borrow,” Crotty says. So whoever answered Heineken’s call, McLoughlin or not, needed to bring more than a surname. They needed cash in hand, and lots of it.

The pub that refused to die

“Pub Succession” is one campaign under For the Love of Pubs, Heineken Ireland’s platform for supporting the country’s pubs. More than 2,200 Irish pubs, roughly one-quarter of them, have closed in the past 20 years.

Kilteely is a village of about 200 people in southern Ireland’s County Limerick, the county hit hardest by closures. It once had seven pubs. By 2025, its last one, Ahern’s, was up for sale, and there was little interest.

“There wasn’t anybody that we could see who was going to purchase the pub and run it as a going concern,” says Liam Carroll, a local lawyer. Without it, he says, the village would have had “very few places for people to come together, or indeed none.” So Carroll and 25 neighbors—farmers, tradesmen, a teacher, an accountant—bought it themselves. “None of us were publicans,” he says. “We really didn’t know much about the economics of running a pub.”

They raised 300,000 euros, with 250,000 for the building and 50,000 to keep it running, and reopened it as The Street Bar. Heineken, which had long supplied the pub, took it under its wing after the group approached its local sales rep for advice. It advised the group on what income to expect and how to bring in more customers, and it installed a new keg room, lines, and taps that Carroll says could have cost the group as much as 20,000 euros. It also made a short film about the rescue, starring the local people, which premiered at the Dublin International Film Festival.

The pub now turns a profit, Carroll says, helped by the short film and by its owners’ reach; each of the 26 owners brings their own friends, family, and coworkers. The film brought visitors, too. “We’ll sometimes encounter somebody sitting at the bar with an American accent who had seen us online,” he says.

The community ownership model that saved the pub in Kilteely isn’t the answer to Ireland’s pub problem, says Crotty at the Vintner’s Federation. “It’s not gonna be a solution to a nationwide issue. People have to run it as a business. Everything can’t be a community affair,” he says. Still, local people in Herbertstown, also in County Limerick, have bought their pub the same way, and communities in Ballylooby and Cullen are exploring the model.

Heineken’s film, The Pub That Refused to Die, won the 2026 Cannes Grand Prix for Creative Strategy. This time, the trophy came after the outcome.

Where the succession stalled

Whoever agreed to take over McLoughlin’s would get a succession package from Heineken that included mentorship, staff training, marketing support, and help with relocation costs. Thinkhouse, one of Heineken’s PR agencies, says in its case study that the campaign found a successor: Alan McLoughlin, an Irishman based in Canada. Fast Company reported this in the spring when we included Publicis Groupe, whose agencies created the campaign, on our 2026 list of the most innovative companies in advertising and marketing.

Months later, McLoughlin’s closed without changing hands. Thinkhouse’s case study still lists Alan as the successor and says the campaign “didn’t just save a pub; it preserved a legacy” and that it helped make sure McLoughlin’s remains “a beating heart of Achill Island.” Yet the pub remains closed and for sale.

Heineken says it supported Alan’s travel so he could take part in the campaign, but that its support “did not extend to the underlying property transaction,” which his participation agreement made clear. Choosing a buyer and completing the sale, the company says, “remained with the pub owners and their representatives.”

Heineken did not address why Alan never bought the pub. Josie McLoughlin declined to be interviewed.

One pub that did change hands this year is The Crow’s Nest in the port town of Cobh, about 12 miles from Cork, Ireland’s second-largest city. A couple, Stewart and Beth Hackney, who work in healthcare in Charlotte, North Carolina, closed on the pub in February. Buying it meant an “onslaught of admin work and documentation,” Beth says. It took 10 months, nine trips across the Atlantic, and all cash. “You’d have to have 10 hours for me to explain all these weird nuances,” she says.

When asked whether a package like Heineken’s would have made a difference, Beth says: “It looks good for marketing, but actually, I don’t think it would help.”

What’s next for McLoughlin’s?

Heineken says it has no confirmed plans to rescue McLoughlin’s, though it is “exploring appropriate ways to help.” That echoes early launch coverage, which reported that if the search failed, Heineken might look for other ways to help Josie find a replacement.

Under a law dating back to 1902, Ireland caps the number of pub licenses; a new one can be issued only if an existing one is extinguished. Today, many licenses from closing pubs are sold to supermarkets that want to sell alcohol.

As long as McLoughlin’s keeps its license, reopening it is a matter of finding a buyer. Once the license is sold, whoever comes next has to find another pub willing to give up its own license, buy it for tens of thousands of euros, and get a court to grant a new one to replace it, all on top of buying the building.

Crotty doesn’t fault Heineken for its ambition. “You set the bar high,” he says. “But if you can’t reach it, well then, surely there has to be a second bar.” “Pub Succession” cleared the first bar: global headlines, thousands of sign-ups, a shelf of trophies. By marketing standards, a resounding success. But all that attention was supposed to be a means to an end, that end being a new owner for McLoughlin’s. And Achill is still waiting on its reward.
