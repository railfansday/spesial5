French Bulldogs and English Bulldogs are brachycephalic breeds. Their shortened skull and squishy faces are a big part of their appeal. But there is a very uncomfortable truth behind that cute face.

That same anatomy that many people find irresistible can make it harder for a dog to breathe, exercise, sleep, regulate their body temperature, and, in some cases, give birth.

We have to stop pretending these things are simply âpart of being a Bulldog.â

A dog struggling to breathe is not just a snorer. A dog that overheats easily is not just âsensitive to the heat.â These are medical problems.

Brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome (BOAS) refers to the conditions that affect breeds with a shortened skull, or âbrachycephaly.â These conditions include hypoplastic trachea (narrow  windpipe), stenotic nares (narrow nostrils), and a tongue and soft palate that are too large and long for the mouth, resulting in obstruction of the airways and difficulty swallowing.

These issues have led many veterinary professionals and animal welfare advocates to question the ethics of breeding these dogs. When a physical characteristic can affect basic functions like breathing and temperature regulation, choosing which dogs reproduce needs to consider more than appearance.

That little flat face. Those big eyes. The stout little body. The funny little snorts.

Most people see cute, even adorable. But as veterinarians, we see a whole list of problems that need to be monitored.

A Bulldog can be an absolutely wonderful companion and still have anatomy that puts them at risk for serious health problems. Breeding of these dogs should require more responsibility, not less, with breeders selecting for less extreme conformations.

There is something very sad about how much importance we place on how dogs look. A certain face. A certain size. A certain coat. A certain breed.

The more desirable that look becomes, the more money there is in producing it. That creates a market, and unfortunately, where there is money, there will always be those willing to cut corners.

This does not mean that every breeder is bad. There are responsible breeders who health-test their dogs, carefully select breeding pairs, provide appropriate veterinary care, and take responsibility for the puppies they bring into the world. Those who are actively trying to breed healthier versions of dogs whose health and function have been crippled through selective breeding for exaggerated features.

Puppy mills are something very different.

The problem is when the animals producing those puppies become a means to an end.

And veterinary care becomes an expense to minimize or avoid, not an essential part of responsible animal care.

The problem is loving an appearance so much that we stop asking what that appearance means for the animal.

These dogs have been through a terrible situation, but now they have a chance.

These dogs were not born to produce puppies. They were not born to be quiet. They were not born to look a certain way for someone else's profit.

For that reason, adoption should always be considered before buying a puppy.

There are so many wonderful dogs already waiting in shelters, foster homes, and recovering from neglect. Dogs that may not have the perfect face or pedigree but have an enormous capacity to love.
