3 Reasons to Claim Social Security at 70

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

It's one of the best ways to maximize lifetime benefits.

By Stefon Walters – Nov 22, 2026 at 6:13AM EST

For most American retirees, Social Security plays a large role in their retirement income. In some cases, it's most of their income; in many, it's all of their retirement income. That's why the decision of when to claim shouldn't be glossed over.

You can begin claiming benefits at age 62, but your benefit will be reduced, making it a trade-off some aren't willing to take. You can also delay benefits until age 70, which has the opposite effect, but is it worth it?

Your full retirement age is when you're qualified to receive your full monthly benefit, called your primary insurance amount (PIA). For anyone born in 1960 or later, full retirement age is currently 67, but you can delay benefits until you turn 70 and receive a bump for each month you delay past 67.

For each month you delay benefits past your full retirement age, you'll receive Delayed Retirement Credits (DRCs) equal to two-thirds of 1% monthly. This works out to 8% annually, and a 24% total boost if your full retirement age is 67 and you delay until 70.

In many cases, waiting for the increased monthly benefits results in a higher lifetime payout.

Social Security's annual cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) is added as a percentage to someone's current benefits. By delaying benefits until 70 and securing a higher monthly benefit, you also lock in larger dollar raises with each COLA.

For example, if your PIA is $2,000, next year's 3.6% COLA would raise it to $2,072. However, if you delayed benefits until 70, boosting your benefit to $2,480, a 3.6% COLA would raise it to $2,569.

That's an extra $204 for the year just from the one COLA, but over time, the larger dollar increases compound.

Social Security survivor benefits provide benefit support for a surviving spouse after the primary earner passes away.

Unlike spousal benefits -- which allow the claiming spouse to receive up to 50% of the primary spouse's PIA -- the person receiving survivor benefits receives 100% of the dead spouse's benefit, including any DRCs received.

As an example, let's return to someone whose PIA is $2,000, meaning they'd receive $2,480 if they delayed benefits until age 70. If their spouse claims spousal benefits, they could receive up to $1,000 in benefits, even though the primary spouse is receiving more than their PIA. However, if the primary spouse passes away, the surviving spouse could receive the full $2,480.
