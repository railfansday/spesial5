10 world leaders ranked by salary, from Australia's PM to Spain's head of government

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

See how 10 world leaders' salaries compare in U.S. dollars and against the minimum wage paid to workers in their own countries.

Every national leader cashes a paycheck that the public funds, and the sum draws scrutiny from the people footing the bill. Citizens who argue about wages, taxes, and the cost of living want to know whether the person in charge shares their economic reality or occupies a different one. The question carries weight beyond curiosity. A leader's compensation can signal how a country values public office. It also offers a rough gauge of the distance between those who govern and those who earn the legal wage floor. Lining up those paychecks across borders creates a puzzle of its own. Each country sets the amount in a separate currency and under separate laws.

Converting every salary into U.S. dollars yields a neat table, yet the result says more about exchange rates and the size of each economy than about the job itself. A different yardstick cuts closer to the issue readers raise. Dividing a leader's annual salary by the local full-time minimum wage shows how many of the country's lowest-paid workers it would take to match the person at the top. The measure keeps every comparison inside a single economy, with one currency and one set of prices. It can reshuffle the order a dollar ranking produces, lifting some countries and pulling others down. Salary and wealth differ, too. A generous salary does not equal a large net worth. The numbers that follow track what each office pays. They say nothing about what any officeholder owns.

Neto gathered pay figures for 10 leaders, limiting the list to those whose 2026 earnings it could confirm through an official source. Germany's chancellor fell outside the list for lack of such a confirmed amount. The publication orders the 10 by approximate U.S. dollar value and expresses each total as a multiple of the local full-time minimum wage. Several entries depart from the 2026 baseline. France's total comes from a presidential payslip dated January 2024, which the publication expects has not changed. Spain's reflects the sum disbursed in 2025. Mexico's total folds in the year-end bonus and benefits. The bundled total and the base salaries elsewhere measure different things. Italy's entry rests on parliamentary pay.

Australia's prime minister commands A$622,102 a year, about US$433,900 and the largest dollar sum of any leader on this list. The figure begins with the A$239,270 salary every federal parliamentarian receives. The office layers on another 160%, or A$382,832, which turns a lawmaker's standard pay into the richest package in the group.

The premium by itself outstrips the parliamentary salary by A$143,562, a margin equal to 60% of that salary. Holding the office supplies 61.5% of the total, and the seat in parliament provides the other 38.5%. Put another way, the top job pays 2.6 times what an ordinary member earns. The loading alone comes to roughly US$267,000, more than the entire annual pay of Britain's prime minister or any leader below that entry. The standard parliamentary salary, valued near US$166,900, would by itself exceed the full packages of the leaders of Mexico, Italy, Brazil, and Spain.

Australia's Remuneration Tribunal, an independent body, sets the base salary on which the prime minister's pay rests. The tribunal said that federal politicians' pay had to be fair and comparable with other earnings. It added that the level had to draw in and retain capable people. Any shift in the base passes through the 160% loading. Each extra dollar there adds A$2.60 to the prime minister's pay.

The prime minister's pay equals 12 times what a full-time worker on Australia's minimum wage earns, a smaller multiple than the presidents of the U.S., Mexico, and Brazil post despite their lower dollar totals. Working back from the multiple puts the full-time wage floor near A$51,800 a year. The parliamentary salary on its own runs to about 4.6 times that floor. A wage floor of that size keeps the gap between Australia's top office and its lowest-paid full-time workers near the middle of the group, even as the dollar figure exceeds every other leader's.

The U.S. president receives $400,000 a year, 27 times the pay of a full-time worker on the federal minimum wage. The salary appears in federal statute as a fixed dollar amount. Raising it would mean rewriting the law itself.

A $50,000 expense allowance arrives on top of the base pay, lifting the full package to $450,000. Counting the allowance, the total reaches about 30 times a full-time federal minimum-wage income. The salary alone falls about $33,900 short of the Australian prime minister's converted pay, the single entry on the list worth more in dollars. The gap between the two top earners amounts to less than a tenth of the U.S. figure.

Former presidents keep collecting public money after they leave office. Their pension matches a Cabinet secretary's pay, which stands at $253,100 in 2026. Departing the job trims the yearly payment by $146,900 from the presidential salary. The benefit equals about 63% of a sitting president's pay and about 17 times what a full-time federal minimum-wage worker takes in over a year. Even in retirement, a former president outpaces the lowest-paid full-time worker by a wide margin.

The federal minimum wage accounts for much of the 27-times gap. It has stayed at $7.25 an hour since 2009, which works out to about $15,080 a year for full-time work. The floor has gone 17 years without an increase as of 2026. A wage stuck that long will inflate any ratio that uses it as the divisor.

Most states set a higher minimum of their own. A full-time worker in one of those states makes more than $15,080 a year, which pulls that worker's multiple below 27. Neto attributes the extreme U.S. showing on this measure to the federal rate. The 27-times multiple tracks a stalled federal wage floor as much as it tracks the size of the presidential paycheck.

Canada's prime minister earns C$435,400 a year, about US$305,300, under pay rates that took effect April 1, 2026. The sum splits into two equal halves. A seat in the House of Commons brings C$217,700, and leading the government adds an identical C$217,700. In euros, the package is worth about €273,000. Canada prices the premiership at one more legislator's paycheck, no more and no less.

Britain's and Ireland's leaders pair a legislative salary with pay for the top office, though the two pieces differ in size in each country. Canada's matching halves leave no ambiguity about the premium. Taking charge of the government doubles what a member of Parliament earns.

Canada's federal minimum wage climbed to C$18.15 an hour in April 2026, the same month the new parliamentary pay began. A worker earning that wage across a full-time year collects about C$37,750. The prime minister's total equals 11.5 times that income. Measured by the hour over the same full-time year, the leader's pay works out to about C$209, set against the C$18.15 floor.

Each half of the package would amount to nearly six times a full-time minimum wage income on its own. The parliamentary salary by itself clears the floor by a wide margin. The supplement for running the government repeats that distance a second time.

Canada's total sits third among the 10 leaders in dollar terms, behind Australia and the U.S. Canada's leader trails the U.S. president by about US$94,700, yet Canada's multiple comes to less than half the American one. The two leaders stand closer together in currency than in their separation from the lowest-paid worker at home.

Australia's prime minister collects about US$128,600 more than Canada's, but the minimum wage multiples of the two countries differ by half a point. A full-time Canadian minimum wage income equals about 8.7% of the prime minister's annual pay.

Ireland's Taoiseach earns €256,311 a year, about US$286,600, and the total stacks two separate lines of pay. A TD salary of €119,467 forms the base. The office of Taoiseach adds €136,844 on top of the seat in parliament. Both rates took effect June 1, 2026.

The leadership portion outstrips the parliamentary share by €17,377. Running the government accounts for about 53.4% of the package. Canada divides its prime minister's earnings into two identical halves. Ireland tilts the balance toward the office, attaching a sum worth about 115% of the TD rate. The design attaches more money to leading the country than to holding a seat.

The Taoiseach's income equals nine times what a full-time minimum-wage worker in Ireland makes in a year. Five of the nine leaders whose countries set a statutory minimum wage show a wider gap: Australia, Canada, the U.S., Mexico, and Brazil. Ireland's drop from fourth on dollar value to sixth on this yardstick suggests its lowest-paid full-time workers stand closer to their leader than counterparts in those five countries stand to theirs. Canada offers the sharpest contrast. The Canadian prime minister outearns the Taoiseach in dollars and also sits farther from the country's wage floor.

Ireland's leader also outpaces every other European entry on the list in dollar terms. France, the U.K., and Spain each post a smaller multiple of the minimum wage, and Italy sets no statutory floor at all. Among the European entries, Ireland pairs the biggest dollar paycheck with the widest gap between the head of government and the lowest-paid worker.

A few Irish office holders hand back part of their pay. As a result, the €256,311 figure reflects an entitlement, and the sum reaching any given holder may come in lower.

The Taoiseach supplement by itself exceeds the entire sum Spain paid its head of government in 2025.

Britain's prime minister earns £174,039 for 2026-27, about US$230,300, even after turning down part of the pay attached to the top job. The ministerial entitlement stands at £83,837 a year. The prime minister claims £75,440 of it and leaves £8,397 unclaimed. As a result, the office holder accepts about 90% of the available sum.

Claiming the full amount would lift the annual package to £182,436. The unclaimed portion looks small against a six-figure income. Even so, the figures list the entitlement and the claim as two separate numbers, which marks the lower amount as a choice the office holder makes.

A seat in Parliament supplies the larger slice of the money. The £98,599 MP salary accounts for about 57% of the package. In practice, the parliamentary role pays more than the prime ministerial one. The imbalance would persist even with the full entitlement in hand, because £98,599 still exceeds £83,837 by £14,762.

Britain splits the money in the opposite direction from Canada and Australia. Canada pays its prime minister the same amount for leading the government as for serving as an MP. Australia's top-up exceeds the base parliamentary salary. Britain's claimed ministerial pay equals about 77% of the MP salary, and even the full entitlement reaches only about 85%.

The British premier's pay equals about seven times the local full-time minimum wage, a narrow spread by the standards of this list. Neto pairs the U.K. with Spain as the two countries with the smallest gaps, both sitting around six to seven times. France's president, one spot lower in dollar terms, shows a wider multiple than Britain.

The decision to claim less feeds into the narrow spread. A smaller claim shrinks the package, and a smaller package pulls the multiple down. The distance between the office and the lowest-paid full-time worker owes part of its modesty to the prime minister's restraint.

France's president grossed €16,039.71 a month in January 2024, and 12 such payments add up to €192,477 a year. The salary equals about US$215,200, which slots France below Britain and above Mexico among the 10 leaders. The annual total matches 12 identical monthly installments, with none of the extra 13th payment Brazil's president receives.

The president collects 8.6 times the annual earnings of a full-time worker on France's minimum wage. Working backward from that ratio puts the French wage floor near €22,400 a year. Ireland's Taoiseach stands at nine times while drawing about US$71,400 more in dollars. Dividing the Irish total by nine yields a floor close to €28,500. The eurozone neighbor guarantees its lowest earners about €6,100 more each year than France does.

Britain's prime minister earns about US$15,100 more than the French president yet sits at seven times the local minimum wage, a tighter distance from the bottom of the scale. France's smaller salary and wider multiple mean Britain's floor runs higher in dollar terms. The same arithmetic puts the British minimum near US$32,900 a year, against some US$25,000 for France.

France, Ireland, and Britain form a European cluster of single-digit multiples, far from the U.S. at 27 and Brazil at 29. The French gap amounts to less than a third of the American one. France's salary also exceeds Mexico's full presidential package, bonus and benefits included, by about US$58,100. Yet Mexico's president sits at 25 times the local minimum wage, close to triple the French ratio. France pays its president more in dollars than Mexico does, yet the French salary stays far closer to its country's lowest legal wage.

France's civil service pay scales have stayed frozen since July 2023. Any increase in the French minimum wage would pull the 8.6 multiple down as long as the president's monthly gross holds at €16,039.71.

Mexico's president takes home MX$134,290 a month after deductions, and the full yearly package reaches MX$2,882,131, equal to some US$157,100 or €140,500, once the year-end bonus and benefits join base pay. The monthly net figure has held at that level since 2025.

Twelve months of net pay add up to MX$1,611,480, or 56% of the annual total. The remaining 44% stays outside the monthly deposit. A president reading a pay stub sees a little more than half of what the office delivers over a year.

Mexico's total pay equals 25 times the local full-time minimum wage, which puts a full year at that wage near MX$115,000. A single month of the president's net pay outstrips what a minimum-wage worker earns across 12 months.

The Mexican gap comes close to the American one on a dollar amount less than half the size. The U.S. president's US$400,000 salary is more than 2.5 times Mexico's compensation, yet the American multiple of 27 sits two above Mexico's. Canada offers a sharper contrast. Its prime minister earns 1.9 times as much as Mexico's president in dollar terms, while the Canadian multiple of 11.5 comes to less than half of Mexico's.

Mexico's earnings come in below the dollar pay of six other leaders on the list, yet the president's distance from the lowest-paid full-time worker trails only Brazil and the U.S. The total edges past Italy's US$140,000, the next figure down, by US$17,100. Dollar totals, in Mexico's case, understate how far the president stands from the country's minimum-wage earners.

A federal law governing public servants' pay in Mexico bars anyone in public service from earning more than the president. As a result, the president's salary sets a legal ceiling for the whole state payroll. A freeze at the top holds that ceiling in place for every other official on the government payroll.

Giorgia Meloni draws €0 as Italy's prime minister, and all €125,220 of her official annual pay, worth about US$140,000, flows from her seat in parliament. She stands alone among the 10 leaders as the one whose head-of-government post pays nothing.

Meloni has declared that she accepts no salary for running the government. Italian law bars a prime minister from adding the government salary to an MP's wage. The rule keeps the two incomes from merging into one larger package, and she retains only her parliamentary earnings.

Canada's prime minister, Ireland's Taoiseach, and Britain's prime minister each collect a government salary on top of parliamentary pay, the doubling Italian law rules out. Each of them brings home two paychecks for the same dual role Meloni holds. Italy's arrangement leaves her with one.

Her parliamentary salary amounts to €10,435 gross each month, plus allowances. Twelve months at that rate produce €125,220, which shows the annual figure counts base salary alone. The allowances lift her income from public office above that total, by an amount the list leaves undisclosed.

Meloni's legislative wage by itself outstrips the full packages of two other leaders on the list, Brazil's president and Spain's head of government. Italy and Spain both pay in euros, which strips exchange rates out of the second comparison. Her MP salary runs €29,276 above what the Spanish leader received in 2025.

Italy has no statutory minimum wage, which leaves Meloni as the only leader on the list without a pay multiple. The other nine entries each show how many times their leader out-earns a full-time worker on the legal floor. Italy supplies no such benchmark, and the distance between her pay and the bottom of its labor market goes unmeasured.

Italian taxpayers fund Meloni at a lawmaker's rate, and the premiership adds nothing to the bill. Her eighth-place spot in the dollar ordering reflects a legislative wage.

Brazil's president takes in R$602,760 a year, about US$120,700, a sum 29 times the country's minimum wage and the widest gap among the 10 leaders. A worker on that minimum would need about 29 years of full-time labor to earn what the president collects in one.

The money arrives in 13 installments of R$46,366.19, one more payment than the calendar has months. A single installment equals about 28.6 times the R$1,621 minimum wage set for 2026. The gap stays about as wide paycheck by paycheck as it does across the full year.

Brazil's dollar total sits ninth of 10, ahead of only Spain's head of government at US$107,300. A margin of US$13,400 separates the two sums, yet Spain's leader receives 5.6 times the local minimum wage, a ratio less than one-fifth the size of Brazil's. Two salaries close in dollars can rest on wage floors far apart.

Australia's prime minister and the U.S. president each collect more than three times Brazil's dollar sum, at US$433,900 and US$400,000. Canada's prime minister brings in about US$305,300, some two and a half times the Brazilian amount. All three trail Brazil on the ratio, with multiples of 12, 27, and 11.5. Europe sharpens the contrast. Ireland's Taoiseach posts the highest European multiple at nine, and Brazil's figure exceeds triple that level.

The Americas hold the widest gaps. Brazil, the U.S., and Mexico account for the three largest multiples, at 29, 27, and 25. Mexico's president outearns Brazil's in dollars, at about US$157,100 with benefits included, yet falls behind on the ratio. Mexico's figure also counts benefits, so Brazil's edge on the ratio survives against a broader measure of pay. Neto notes that some Latin American countries beyond these 10 show a wider gap. Brazil's top office looks modest to anyone tallying dollars and lavish to anyone earning the legal minimum, a split wider than any other leader's on the list.

Spain's president of the government received €95,944 in 2025, about US$107,300, the smallest dollar sum attached to any of the 10 leaders on the list.

Spain's salary trails the leaders above it by a wide margin. In dollars, it comes to about one-quarter of the Australian prime minister's pay and less than one-third of the U.S. president's federal salary. Brazil's president, the nearest leader in dollar terms, collects about US$13,400 more.

Four leaders on the list draw pay in euros, which strips exchange rates out of the comparison. Spain's €95,944 sits about €29,300 below the €125,220 Italy's prime minister receives from her parliamentary seat. France's president grosses €192,477 a year, about double the Spanish figure. Ireland's Taoiseach collects €256,311, more than two and a half times as much. Spain's leader is the only one of the four paid less than €100,000.

Spain's leader also sits closer to the country's lowest-paid full-time workers than any other leader with a minimum wage comparison, at 5.6 times the local minimum wage. Neto pairs Spain with the U.K. as the two countries showing the narrowest gaps, putting both at around 6 to 7 times. Britain's prime minister lands at 7 times, France's president at 8.6, and Ireland's Taoiseach at 9.

Brazil's president stands more than five times as far from that country's wage floor as Spain's leader does. Mexico's president sits nearly four and a half times as far. The U.S. president, at 27 times, stands nearly five times as far.

No other leader lands at the same end of both scales. Australia's prime minister collects the most in dollars, yet three other leaders show a wider gap to their own minimum wage. Brazil's president sits ninth in dollar terms despite the widest gap. Spain's head of government stays at the bottom on either yardstick, which makes the post the most modest on the list measured against foreign peers and against Spanish workers alike.
