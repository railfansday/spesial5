On October 1, 2026, OpenAI told developers that tts-1, tts-1-hd and gpt-4o-mini-tts are deprecated and will be removed from the API on January 6, 2027. The recommended replacement, gpt-realtime-2.1-mini, isn't a supported model for the /v1/audio/speech endpoint at all. If your phone agent calls that endpoint, you're doing a migration this quarter whether you planned one or not.

The rest of the field moved too: Cartesia shipped Sonic 3.6, Deepgram put Flux TTS on a new /v2/speak endpoint, ElevenLabs has Eleven v4 and v4 Turbo, and on October 27 AssemblyAI launched AssemblyAI-TTS-1. It gets the same treatment as every other entry.

Here's the thing. Nearly every TTS comparison judges voices on full-bandwidth audio through good headphones. Your caller hears 8 kHz G.711 through a phone earpiece, often in a car. Some differences that sound huge in a demo vanish on that line, and other failures get much worse.

So this piece judges TTS APIs on the phone line.

Twilio's Media Streams docs are blunt about it: the audio you send back "must be encoded audio/x-mulaw with a sample rate of 8000." Carriers in North America and Japan use G.711 μ-law, and most of the rest of the world uses A-law. At 8,000 samples per second nothing above 4 kHz survives, and companding squeezes each sample into 8 bits.

Look at the first column. Seven of the eight return G.711 natively, so "does it speak telephony" is no longer much of a filter. The concurrency column is where the real differences hide, and it's the one most comparisons leave out.

Good at: concurrency, and being built for this exact deployment. AssemblyAI-TTS-1 was trained on millions of hours of audio, including real conversational data. It returns pcm_mulaw or pcm_alaw at whatever rate you ask for, with the server resampling before it encodes. The WebSocket takes streamed LLM text, returns per-word timings, and has a cancel() for barge-in. There's also an HTTP API, an OpenAI-compatible route, US and EU data zones, LiveKit and Pipecat plugins, and instant cloning in the dashboard. Concurrency is unlimited: the rate limit counts new sessions per minute, not sessions open at once. We'd describe it as frontier-level quality, faster and cheaper than other production voice agent TTS models. It costs $15 per 1M input characters (pricing).

Where it breaks on the phone: it launched October 27, so there's no long track record. Six languages (English, Spanish, French, German, Italian, Portuguese) is the shortest list here, and there's no language detection. There's no SSML or custom lexicon either. Sessions last at most an hour and close after two minutes idle without a keep-alive. Pipecat's AssemblyAI TTS service shipped in pipecat-ai 1.15.0 (install pipecat-ai[assemblyai]).

Pick it when your languages are covered, call volume is spiky or large enough that concurrency caps would hurt, or you want STT and TTS on one API key.

Good at: voice range and cloning. The v4 family covers 90+ languages. Flash v2.5 is the fast, cheap option, and the docs quote a model latency of about 75 ms, excluding application and network latency. Instant and Professional cloning are both mature, and both G.711 formats are first-class outputs.

Where it breaks on the phone: concurrency is tied to your plan. The published table runs from 4 concurrent Flash requests on Free to 30 on Scale and Business, and requests past the limit queue. On the standard TTS WebSocket, only time spent generating counts against the limit, which helps. But Eleven v4 Turbo is served on the Text to Dialogue WebSocket, where each open connection reserves a session for as long as it stays open. On a phone agent that means one session per live call.

Pick it when you need many languages, a cloned brand voice, or the most expressive delivery, and you can size your plan to your peak call volume.

Good at: latency-focused engineering with the telephony details documented properly. The output-format docs pair pcm_mulaw and pcm_alaw at 8000 Hz with the regions that use them. The WebSocket supports contexts and continuations, so you can stream LLM tokens into one utterance. Sonic 3.6 covers 44 languages, and the docs say it handles confirmation codes and heteronyms without preprocessing.

Where it breaks on the phone: self-serve concurrency is low. The pricing page lists 2 to 15 concurrent TTS requests across self-serve plans: fine for a pilot, a hard ceiling for a contact center. Also, the sonic-3.6 ID moves to the newest snapshot, so pin sonic-3.6-2026-08-27 if you run evals.

Pick it when latency is your top constraint and your call volume fits a plan, or you're ready to negotiate Enterprise.

Good at: barge-in. Flux TTS treats synthesis as a turn-based conversation. When you send Interrupt, the server reports text_spoken and text_remaining, so your LLM context matches what the caller actually heard. That's the most explicit barge-in contract in this list. The streaming socket returns mulaw or alaw at 8 or 16 kHz.

Where it breaks on the phone: two things to read carefully. First, the docs say Early Access languages "do not have text normalization yet: numbers, dates and abbreviations are read as written." Ten languages have normalization; for the rest, write entities out in words yourself. Second, the rate-limit page caps Flux TTS streaming at 5 concurrent requests on the EU, Australia and India endpoints, versus 45 in North America, on pay-as-you-go. If you need EU data residency, plan around that.

Pick it when you already run Deepgram STT, your languages have normalization, and accurate barge-in bookkeeping matters more to you than voice cloning.

Good at: pronunciation control for entity-heavy calls. Coda takes a custom lexicon of X-SAMPA pronunciations in six languages, and both models support a spell() function for codes, IDs and acronyms. Rime also documents on-prem engines, which matters when your network hop is your latency budget. Pricing is per minute of generated audio, which maps neatly onto per-call economics.

Where it breaks on the phone: the Coda WebSocket's format table lists G.711 μ-law (audio/pcmu) but not A-law, so outside μ-law regions you'll transcode yourself. Language coverage is narrow: 10 languages for Coda and 4 for Mist v3, with most voices serving a single language. And the docs warn that a request without modelId, or with one the API doesn't recognize, gets Mist v3. "Coda is never served by default." Set it explicitly.

Pick it when your calls are full of brand names, SKUs and confirmation codes, you're in a μ-law region, or you need on-prem.

Good at: breadth and enterprise plumbing: 100+ languages and locales, native μ-law and A-law, SSML, and quotas that go from 30 to 1,000 transactions per second on request.

Where it breaks on the phone: limits are counted in transactions, not calls. A chatty agent that sends one request per sentence burns through TPS faster than you'd expect. Custom and personal voice are limited access, so budget time to apply.

Pick it when you need a language nobody else covers or you're already standardized on Azure.

Good at: quality and locale coverage on a cloud you may already use. Chirp 3: HD covers 53 locales, returns MULAW or ALAW on both streaming and batch, and supports custom pronunciations in most locales. Default quota is 100 concurrent streaming sessions per project.

Where it breaks on the phone: streaming only works with Chirp 3: HD voices, and it runs over gRPC bidirectional streaming. Your carrier hands you a WebSocket, so you'll build a bridge. Instant Custom Voice is allow-listed and costs twice the Chirp 3: HD rate.

Pick it when you're on GCP, you need its locale list, and gRPC doesn't scare your team.

The replacement is a Realtime API session, which is a different architecture from a TTS endpoint you call after your own LLM.

Pick it when you're prototyping something that won't be running in January.
