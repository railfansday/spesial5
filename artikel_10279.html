Ask one of the newest language models whether a backpack comes in green, and it'll usually admit it doesn't know. Ask it how to return that backpack, and it may send the customer to a returns page, an account screen, and a return label that the store has never had. That's still a hallucination: an answer that sounds right, comes with total confidence, and isn't true.

Whatever you're building, the fix is the same: stop trusting the model with the parts it can get wrong, and check its work before anyone sees it.

In this tutorial we'll build a small support bot for a made-up outdoor gear store, three times over. The first version is the prompt plenty of bots start with. Then we'll apply five fixes, run six tricky customer emails through every version, and finish with a bot where the model can't slip a made-up price, date, or product past the code.

An LLM hallucination is output that's fluent and confident but not backed by the facts: a product that doesn't exist, a policy nobody wrote, a date that's a week out, or a citation to a paper that was never published. The word covers two different failures:

Each one needs a different fix, so it helps to keep them apart.

A language model writes by predicting likely text, one token at a time. It has no separate store of facts it checks against, and nothing in that process says "stop, you don't know this". Training adds to the problem. OpenAI's research on why language models hallucinate points out that when models are graded only on how many answers they get right, a guess scores better than "I don't know", so they learn to guess.

Newer models are much better at the first one. They'll often say they don't know a price rather than invent one. But they still fill gaps with what's typical, and what's typical isn't always what's true for your business. You can't train that out of a model you call through an API. What you can change is what you ask it to do, what you give it, and what you check.

The last line of that prompt matters as much as the data: "If the answer isn't in the data, say you don't know and that a team member will reply by email." Without it, a model that can't find the answer has to choose between letting the customer down and guessing, and training has taught it to guess. With it, "I don't know" is a right answer, not a failure.

If version 2 got everything right, why build a version 3? Because "right on this run" isn't the same as "right every time":

The next three fixes take the facts and the rules away from the model completely.

Version 3 splits the work into three steps. First, the model reads the email, but instead of writing a reply, it fills in a form. Every field is a choice from a list, a yes or no, or text copied from the email:

Zod describes the form, and z.toJSONSchema() turns it into a JSON schema for the API's structured outputs. The API holds the model to that schema, so product can only be one of our four product IDs, not_sold, or none. There's no way for it to answer ridgeline-60. Zod then checks the answer again, so the rest of the code gets a typed object. SitePoint's guide to LLM structured output with JSON Schema goes deeper on this pattern.

The model is still reading natural language, which it's very good at. It just isn't trusted to know what the store sells anymore.

A form also makes the model's mistakes easy to see. The first time I ran this, the form had no asks_something_else field, and the model filed the price match email under product. The reply didn't make anything up, but the email never reached a person. In version 2, a mistake like that would be hidden inside a paragraph. Here it was one wrong value in a JSON object, and the fix was one more field.

Second step: plain code takes the form, looks up the product and the order, applies the rules, and does the maths. What comes out is a list of facts in plain sentences:

Every number in there was worked out by code that does the same thing every time, and that you can unit test. The 34 days, the December 5 deadline, and the $151.20 in store credit come out the same on every run, whatever the model does.

The last rule in decide() matters most. If an email asks something the rules don't cover, the facts say so and the email goes to a person. Code decides when to say "I don't know", not the model.

Third step: the model writes the reply from the facts and nothing else. Writing is the one job here it does better than code.

Even with the right facts, a model writing a reply can still add something: a helpful "ships in 1–2 business days", or a price it remembers from somewhere. So the last step checks the reply. Anything that looks like a number, an order number, or a product name has to appear in the email or the facts:

It's simple on purpose. It doesn't understand the reply. It just refuses to let through a value that came from nowhere, and that covers the hallucinations that cost money: prices, dates, quantities, and products. Nothing in the reply can talk it out of a result.

The writer runs the check on every draft:

If a draft fails, the model gets one retry and is told exactly which values weren't in the facts. If the retry fails too, the customer gets the facts as a plain list. That's less charming, but it's true.

A check like this will raise false alarms, and that's fine. On my first run, it read a line about the tent's 2.1 kg weight as a product called "Weighs 2" and sent the draft back for a retry. The weight was right, and it was the only thing flagged. A false alarm costs a few seconds. A missed one costs a customer. I tightened the patterns (the version above has the fix), but I'd still rather it flag too much than too little.

It's the same thing that separates the better agentic AI coding tools from autocomplete: they run the tests and read the errors instead of trusting their first draft. Model output isn't finished until something other than the model has checked it.

No model is free of hallucinations, so you can't stop one from ever making something up. What you can do is build so a made-up detail can't reach a customer: let code own the facts, and check the model's output against them before it's used.
