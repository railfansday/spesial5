The 8 Best Headphones for Giving Your Ears the Treatment They Deserve

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Our handpicked, tested sets start at around $100.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. Why Trust Us?

The fact of the matter is that it doesn’t take a lot of work to find a good pair of headphones these days. After decades of competition and high-tech design, the market is saturated with dozens of high-quality options at every shape, size, and price point. Whether you’re looking for a basic set of earbuds for less than $100, or would prefer to drop $300 to $500 on a pair of wireless ANC headphones with high-tech bells and whistles like support for lossless audio files, or even want to spend top-dollar on a pair of wired, audiophile-grade cans to plug into an immaculately constructed hi-fi system, you won’t be short on choices.

Over the past five years or so, there haven’t been many game-changing innovations in the audio space, but that has led to a flattening of the field that’s great if you’re looking for a bargain. Features that would have once been considered high-end, like active noise cancellation, have trickled down into budget products, and audio quality has been steadily improving among budget- and mid-tier headphones. There are benefits to buying a high-end pair, for sure, but there are more bargains than ever before.

Obviously there are hundreds, if not thousands, of headphones out there—more than even I can test. I’ve put in a lot of time, though, going over the best, the most interesting, and the most talked about headphones, to find the ones that stand out from the pack. Because even when there are a lot of good options out there, we all would rather bring home the best.

I’ve been professionally testing tech products and audio gear, especially headphones and earbuds, since 2011. When I evaluate headphones, I spend weeks and, in some cases, months using each product as my daily driver, assessing its sound, battery life, and ergonomics. I pair the headsets with phones and laptops and frequently switch between devices.

My test routine included streaming an eclectic selection of tracks from Apple Music and Qobuz to evaluate each headset's ability to produce audible bass, treble, and mid-range. I also listened to podcasts, streamed videos, and played video games while evaluating each pair of headphones.

To put the headphones’ noise cancellation through its paces, I wear them on the New York City subway to get to work, in the large open office I share with my colleagues, and on long-distance flights. I make calls, too, carefully observing if their microphones help participants hear me clearly.

I monitor the battery performance of all wireless headphones by tracking how often I have to charge them. I want to ensure that every pick’s battery life at least matches its manufacturer’s claims.

Because you may wear your headphones for long stretches each day, I carefully scrutinize the size, fit, and ergonomics of every pair by using them for hours between breaks. I also evaluate the layout and reliability of their controls, whether they are buttons, touch-sensitive surfaces, or a combination of both.

With that guidance in mind, find your next favorite pair of headphones among the thoroughly tested and reviewed sets below.

If I had to part with all my headphones and keep just one pair, I’d choose the Technics A1000. These wireless headphones are my new benchmark for all-around excellence, blending an elegant yet understated design, supreme comfort, and outstanding audio performance. That includes high-fidelity sound and efficient noise cancellation on par with the industry’s best offerings.

Featuring sophisticated magnetic-fluid audio drivers, the Technics A1000 headphones impressed me with balanced, detail-rich, distortion-free sound right out of the box, faithfully reproducing everything I listened to during testing — from music and podcasts to video content. Instrumentals in my eclectic jazz selection sounded crisp and vibrant. On my favorite downtempo albums, the bass was deep, while the vocals were superbly defined and perfectly separated.

The Technics companion app gave me a rich set of tools to customize the headphones’ sound and noise cancellation. Depending on the music I was listening to, I could adjust the bass and treble levels and tweak the mid-range output to my liking, using a graphic equalizer or pre-loaded presets.

In a mandatory comparison with identically-priced rivals like the Sony WH-1000XM6 and the Bose QuietComfort Ultra (2nd Gen) — a duo of products with superior button controls, longer battery life, more futureproof Bluetooth 6.0 connectivity with the ability to simultaneously link to three devices, and full Dolby Atmos audio capabilities with optional head tracking. The last point is a step above Sony’s proprietary, multidimensional 360 Reality Audio, which is more cumbersome to access.

We’ve reached a point where a pair of wireless headphones from a reputable brand with elegant design, high-quality sound (including spatial audio), stellar noise cancellation, and long battery life costs over $400. By offering some of these features for $200, the Nothing Headphone (a) over-ear cans are the biggest bang for the buck and worth the attention of value-obsessed gearheads.

Design- and durability-wise, the Nothing Headphone (a) makes similarly priced rivals look generic by comparison. The headphones’ rectangular ear cups with rounded corners give them the same distinctive look as pricier offerings from the same maker. These include transparent and solid-color bits that give the headset unique character. Their IP52 water- and dust-resistance rating is also remarkable, as it’s rare for over-ear headphones at any price point.

Amazing hardware button controls that include roller and paddle keys, as well as a customizable one, are another reason I became a fan of the Nothing Headphone (a) and all other headsets from the brand. Their responsiveness and tactility are better than products that cost twice as much.

On the audio front, the headphones delivered enjoyable, slightly bass-forward sound and immersive spatial audio. The Nothing X app offered intuitive tools to customize my listening experience, and I liked the option to link the cans directly to my devices via a USB-C or a standard audio cable. The cans’ noise cancellation was also solid, if not on par with the serenity offered by more expensive competitors.

The 75-hour maximum battery life with noise cancellation alone is reason enough to put the Nothing Headphone (a) on your radar. I didn’t have to worry about recharging the headset for weeks. Turning noise cancellation off almost doubles the battery life, bringing it to a staggering 135 hours between charges.

Bose headphones and earbuds have been synonymous with noise-canceling excellence for decades, so it’s no surprise the brand’s QuietComfort Ultra (2nd Gen) over-ear cans are the first product that comes to mind if this feature is a priority. Of course, the headphones are also stylish, supremely comfortable, and deliver top-notch sound, including immersive spatial audio.

Regarding noise cancellation, I wouldn’t recommend the headphones solely for their ability to eliminate ambient noise distractions quickly, since that’s a given in this price range. What gives the Bose product an edge over any rival in this department is the option to customize the noise cancellation by choosing from eleven levels of intensity, then saving it into a custom mode for specific activities (e.g., work, travel, commuting, etc.) that’s easy to access by long-pressing a button.

Having a quick-access mode with maxed-out noise cancellation for work and travel, another where the feature’s intensity was set below 50% for my daily subway rides, and one with full ambient awareness at the tip of my right thumb was super convenient with the Bose headphones. Plus, I could include spatial audio in my custom modes.

During months of use, the QuietComfort Ultra headphones delivered powerful, entertaining sound, customizable via their companion app. Their spatial audio capabilities are just as impressive, and I tip my hat to Bose for making proprietary Immersive Audio technology, with or without dynamic head tracking, available on any content, not just Dolby Atmos-enhanced music and videos. That said, if audio fidelity is your top priority, you’ll likely prefer the identically priced Technics EAH-A1000 or the Sony WH-1000XM6.

Calls sound superb on the QC Ultra 2, as usual. I like the headphones’ control layout, with clicky buttons and a volume touch slider that doubles as a customizable key. The QuietComfort Ultra’s 30-hour battery life with noise cancellation is far from best-in-class, but ahead of the previous version and more than enough to keep you tranquil on any flight.

We live in times when most consumers think of headphones as bulky over-ear headsets, and that’s what makes the Marshall Milton ANC special. These headphones offer features and performance you’d expect from a larger set—top-notch sound customizable via an app, reliable noise cancellation, and long battery life—in a more compact, lightweight package with an on-ear fit and scratch-resistant ear cup covers better suited to on-the-go use.

Sound-wise, the Marshall Milton ANC impressed me with crisp, energetic sound that you can customize via a companion app. Its noise cancellation is also stellar, though the headset doesn't deliver the same level of tranquility as a top-shelf over-ear pair. The extra layer of passive noise isolation such headsets provide is instrumental. Still, the Milton ANC makes up for it with a lighter and more comfortable fit.

The headphones’ knob and customizable button are excellent and miles ahead of many competitors’ control layouts. So is their 80-hour maximum battery life without noise cancellation. If you like the Milton ANC’s design and controls, but want an over-ear set, I have good news. The Marshall Monitor III ANC headphones offer similar design and controls in an over-ear form factor with a slightly higher price tag.

The Soundcore Q31i wireless headphones by Anker are your best bet if you want to stay in two-digit price territory. Believe it or not, for under $100, you can get a reliable set with a comfy fit, solid audio performance, good noise cancellation for the price, futureproof connectivity, and price-defying battery life in a choice of three colors (black, white, and pink).

Acoustically, the Soundcore Q31i headphones performed better than their price tag suggests. The sound was surprisingly harmonious out of the box and customizable via the feature-packed app. While I wouldn’t ditch my expensive headphones for the Anker offering, I found its sound super enjoyable. Their support for Hi-Res audio in wired and wireless mode was a particularly nice touch that felt unthinkable at this price point.

The headphones’ noise cancellation is adequate, if far from the best in the business. I didn’t get the same silence as with more expensive products, but I had no trouble focusing at our large open office when I put the headphones on. The key feature, like the sound, is adjustable in the companion app.

Overall, the Soundcore Q31i exceeded my expectations when I put it through its paces. The lack of spatial audio is my only gripe with the headset, but at $60 on sale, it’s nowhere near a dealbreaker.

Audio-Technica’s ATH-M50x is the first option that comes to mind when someone asks me to recommend a pair of wired over-ear headphones that offer top-notch sound, commendable ergonomics, and impeccable brand cachet, all at an agreeable price. The legendary maker’s product ticks all these boxes, so whether you are looking to take a break from Bluetooth or need a reliable tool for work, or to enjoy, say, your vinyl collection, it is an excellent buy.

A consistently balanced, high-fidelity sound is among the many reasons why many audio engineers and audiophiles swear by these Audio-Technica headphones. My favorite aspect of their audio performance is its accurate and enjoyable bass depth. Countless wired and wireless headphones, especially in this price range, are heavy on bass but nowhere near as proficient in producing mid-range and highs, which is why I always keep the ATH-M50x in my rotation.

The headphones’ retail package is as rich as their sound. It includes three cables of various lengths and a soft pouch. Due to their immense (and well-deserved) popularity among consumers, Audio-Technica ATH-M50x headphones have an excellent ecosystem of third-party cables, cases, and ear cup covers.

If what I told you about the ATH-M50x piqued your interest, but you prefer wireless headphones, I have good news. Audio-Technica offers a Bluetooth version of the cans. The Audio-Technica ATH-M20x headphones, on the other hand, are a great option for upgraders on a very tight budget.

The Technics EAH-AZ100 wireless earbuds offer the same blend of acoustic excellence and onboard features as the brand’s over-ear headphones I mentioned, but in a sleeker, lighter package with an in-ear fit and a lower price tag. These qualities make the buds my top pick in their crowded product category. I’d take the Technics offering as my sole set of earbuds over any big-name rival.

Equipped with magnetic-fluid audio drivers, the EAH-AZ100 delivered pristine, distortion-free sound across countless listening sessions. The harmony among bass, mid-range, and treble fully met my high expectations. The buds’ spatial audio output, with full Dolby Atmos support and optional head tracking, was equally impressive. Their call performance was also stellar.

The earphones’ ability to tune out ambient noise matches their audio fidelity and is rightfully among the industry’s best. Thanks to passive noise isolation from their comfortable ear tips and sophisticated hardware, I had no trouble blocking out distractions when I needed to. The companion app let me choose between fully adaptive noise cancellation based on my surroundings or a desired level of noise cancellation.

Like the Technics over-ear headphones, I compared the EAH-AZ100 buds with their direct competitors: the Bose QuietComfort Ultra (2nd Gen) and the Sony WF-1000XM6. In this group of elite options, the Technics set earned my nod for offering (arguably) better Dolby Atmos-certified sound, longer battery life, and simultaneous connection to three devices. Noise cancellation is equally great across all products, though I’d give Bose an edge for offering custom settings for specific scenarios. Lastly, this might sound like nitpicking, but the EAH-AZ100’s charging case is sleeker and fits more easily in the small pocket of my jeans than its rivals’.

Bowers & Wilkins has sterling credibility among discerning audiophiles — the folks who obsess over high-fidelity stereo sound with precise separation between left and right channels and are willing to spend big on gear that delivers it. The British audio maker’s Px8 S2 wireless headphones should top the wish list for this group of gearheads, offering a listening experience similar to its luxury speakers in a pair of over-ear headphones.

Listening to the Bowers & Wilkins Px8 S2 headphones felt like being in a private space with a hi-fi system. Their out-of-the-box stereo sound captivated me with its accurate presentation, as intended by the content’s audio engineers. Depending on the song and genre, I could hear lively vocals, crisp instrumentals, and deep bass without opening the companion app to tweak settings. By contrast, their strong noise cancellation put me in a cocoon of silence, letting me focus solely on the music.

Of course, the Bowers & Wilkins app gave me plenty of options to customize the cans’ sound and even activate an immersive spatial audio mode. However, I liked the original sound so much that I kept it as-is most of the time, whether wired or Bluetooth.

In a manner befitting their price category and brand heritage, the Bowers & Wilkins Px8 S2 deliver style and craftsmanship in spades. The headphones are made from a mix of leather, metal, and fabric, so they look and feel luxurious. Their comfort and button controls are equally impressive, fully meeting my lofty expectations after months of testing.

Before we dig into hardware specs and features to keep on your radar, know that spending more on your next Bluetooth or wired headset will get you better audio performance across the board and more onboard features. By audio performance, I mean noise cancellation, support for immersive spatial audio, and superior call quality, among other essentials. These features require sophisticated onboard processors and multiple integrated microphones, which inevitably reflects the retail price.

Many budget cans support lossless audio formats, but you shouldn’t prioritize this unless you have a top-shelf Android phone that supports audiophile-grade LDAC and Qualcomm AptX codecs. Lossless audio also isn't available on Spotify, so make sure your streaming service supports it. Apple Music, Amazon Music, Tidal, and Qobuz are a few that do.

If you’re shopping on a tight budget, I suggest spending $80 to $100 for a good pair. The most feature-packed wireless noise-canceling headphones cost north of $400, so budget accordingly if you want one. You’ll find your investment well worth it when you turn on noise canceling and discover it’s like a mute button for your surroundings.

Due to their lack of Bluetooth chips, batteries, and additional components to control noise cancellation, spatial audio, and other features, wired headphones can treat you to better stereo sound quality than equivalently priced Bluetooth models. Their makers can use more capable, better-tuned audio drivers while keeping the price reasonable without those extra bits, so you can score a high-quality pair from a big-name brand for less than $200.

On the other hand, if ultimate high-fidelity and craftsmanship are priorities for you, prepare to spend a hefty four-digit sum to get what you need. Paired with an amplifier and lossless streaming, high-end wired headphones can help you (literally) discover new tones and layers in songs and albums you’ve listened to hundreds of times before. To be clear, though, only the most dedicated audiophiles should even consider something like that.

The headphones’ audio drivers deliver sound to your ears. The materials used in their making and their tuning are more important than their size. Translation? More expensive headphones feature more sophisticated drivers, resulting in higher-quality sound with a more palpable balance between bass, treble, and mid-range.

After more than a decade of headphone testing, I can confirm that cheaper models often hide their sonic deficiencies behind heavier bass. A booming low end can affect treble and mid-range production, reducing the quality of the sonic experience even if it still sounds entertaining.

Multi-dimensional spatial audio has become a standard feature on most high-end wireless headphones. By making the sound effects feel as if they come from distinct directions instead of just your left, right, and center, headphones with this lifelike functionality will make you feel like you are in the studio with the artist, part of the audience when streaming a live album, or in the onscreen action.

Better yet, the best wireless headphones with spatial audio have sophisticated onboard tech that can process standard audio into immersive multi-channel sound, including the option to add dynamic head tracking for a truly lifelike experience. As its name suggests, this feature shifts the sound source as you move your head for an even more natural listening experience.

This feature is a must for a new pair of wireless headphones in 2025—and you no longer have to pay top dollar for it. Once reserved for premium headsets, the functionality is available across the price spectrum, including under-$100 territory. It utilizes microphones and a processor integrated into the headphones to detect ambient sounds and then deliver audio that cancels those sound waves out.Noise cancellation will reduce auditory distractions during your commutes, travels, and workday. Don’t buy Bluetooth headphones that lack it, even if your budget is below $100. Naturally, spending $200 or more will give you superior noise cancellation, often resulting in near-total silence on top-shelf sets.

I typically suggest wireless headphones if you’re looking for an upgrade, not only for offering cable-free convenience but also for desirable features like noise cancellation, spatial audio, and app controls for customizing those. Look for wireless headphones with Bluetooth 5.2 (or higher) on board. These are the most futureproof wireless connectivity standards. Don’t worry if your device doesn’t support them. They are backward-compatible with older Bluetooth standards.

Wired headphones deserve your attention for their rock-solid connection to hardware, which provides instantaneous sound transmission with zero latency. Measured in milliseconds, latency is the time it takes for audio to reach wireless headphones from the source—your phone, tablet, or TV, for example. It’s imperceptible in most cases.That said, a zero-latency wired link can come in handy for video-editing projects, where the audio and on-screen content have to stay in perfect sync. The same goes for competitive gaming, where every millisecond counts.

Opting for a cable-toting set of headphones also frees you from charging, companion apps, and firmware updates. By lacking electronic components and degradable batteries, wired headsets also last longer.

Don’t settle for a wireless noise-canceling headset that can deliver less than 20 hours of battery life, as it might not be enough to get you through a super-long flight. The over- and on-ear sets I recommend here will last at least 30 hours between charges, and that’s my minimum requirement in 2026.

Still, since superior noise cancellation and fancy spatial audio tax the headphones’ battery, I didn’t mind charging the Sony WH-1000XM6 and Bose QuietComfort Ultra (Gen 2) to get a better experience than what their lower-priced rivals offered. The same goes for the Apple AirPods Max 2.

If you are shopping for wireless earbuds, a double-digit maximum battery life of at least 10 hours between charges from the bundled charging case is preferable, but not mandatory. Top-tier AirPods and Bose earphones can’t reach this milestone, but they are still easy to recommend for their excellence in other areas.

Except for AirPods and Beats by Dre products, which benefit from Apple’s superb hardware integration, any new pair of wireless headphones must come with a companion app. This includes Beats products, which can play nice with Android phones just as well as they do with an iPhone. It lets you control them and keep them up to date with firmware updates.

And look for a hard-shell storage case when shopping for Bluetooth headphones. It will keep them intact when you toss them in a bag or backpack on your way to work, or stuff them into a carry-on suitcase when traveling. No need to fret if your headphones come with a soft pouch for storage, though; just keep them at the top of your bag while packing to avoid potential (if highly unlikely) damage.
