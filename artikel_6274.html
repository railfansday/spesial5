Electrons usually flow freely through metals, but Columbia scientists just found a way to trap them in place

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Physicists trapped electrons at a missing atom inside a metal crystal, where quantum interference left them no route out.

A metal conducts electricity because its electrons spread through the whole crystal, the way a ripple spreads across a pond rather than staying where it started. There’s nowhere in a metal for one to hold still.

However, in a palladium compound made at Columbia University, two of them hold still anyway, pinned to a single missing atom.

Morgan Thinel, Simon Turkel and colleagues in Columbia’s Department of Physics and Department of Chemistry found them with a microscope that feels a surface atom by atom.

Two of the authors also work at Brookhaven National Laboratory, and the group’s theorists reproduced both states on paper.

The trap is not a wall around the electron. It is the metal crystal’s own geometry cancelling every route out.

The material is Pd5AlI2, a palladium-aluminum-iodine compound that Xavier Roy’s chemistry group at Columbia built a few years ago. It is a van der Waals crystal, which means it comes apart into sheets the way graphite does, and the sheets stay metallic.

The compound’s electrons moved more slowly than anyone expected, and the group published the reason last year. Call it gridlock: an electron has competing routes to the next atom, and the routes cancel.

The cancelling is the whole mechanism here. A quantum particle does not take one path; it takes every available path at once.

The paths add up like waves, reinforcing in some directions and wiping each other out in others. When the wiping-out is complete, there is no route out at all.

Physicists call the result a bound state in the continuum. The particle is stuck in place even though it is surrounded by an unbroken range of energies into which it could escape.

Light does this. Over the past decade, engineers have etched patterns into materials to catch photons that way, and the trick now drives lasers and sensors. In a metal, it had stayed out of reach.

The team split a crystal open in vacuum and cooled it to about 7 degrees above absolute zero. Then a scanning tunneling microscope measured how easily electrons flow into the surface, point by point and energy by energy.

Across a clean stretch, the answer was much the same everywhere, which is what a metal crystal looks like. Over one kind of flaw in the lattice, two sharp peaks appeared. One sat 475 millivolts above the metal’s own energy floor and the other 620.

Comparing the images with simulations identified the flaw: a single palladium atom missing from the layer buried inside each sheet. Vacancies like it turned up across the crystal at roughly two trillion per square centimeter.

Both states hug the vacancy inside a single nanometer, a span a few atoms wide. A normal defect in a metal only nudges the passing electron waves. These two were not passing.

“Bound electrons shouldn’t occur in a metal, but they do in Pd5AlI2,” said Raquel Queiroz, a theoretical physicist at Columbia who built the models behind the result.

“What we have observed is the first clear evidence of quantum interference trapping a state in a metal.”

Mapping each peak across the surface produced two pictures. The lower one has four lobes, like a clover. The upper one is a plain circle.

Those are the shapes of orbitals around a single atom, which is an odd thing to find around an absence.

The team’s model is a stripped-down version of the layer with one atom removed. It produced both shapes, and put them a few hundred millivolts above the energy floor, where the microscope found them.

The circular one only appears once the model includes the iodine atoms sitting above and below the gap.

The team also answered the obvious objection. The gridlock in an idealized version of this lattice is perfect, and perfect conditions are not what a real crystal offers.

Loosening the model so the electrons are not quite frozen keeps the two states tightly bound across a wide range of settings, including the ones that match the real material.

When two palladium atoms were missing from neighboring sites, the pair of states became four. Each of the original two had split, into a slightly lower energy and a slightly higher one.

That is what happens when two atoms bond into a molecule. The resemblance runs even further. For the four-lobed state, the lower version piles electron density between the two gaps at 440 millivolts. The higher one, at 510, leaves the middle empty.

Move the vacancies two lattice spacings apart and the splitting disappears.

So the trapped states behave like the orbitals of atoms that are not there, and two of them nearby make something like a molecule out of two holes.

No one is placing these on purpose yet. Every vacancy here arrived by chance while the crystal was growing.

That is the sharpest difference from the light version of the effect, where the pattern goes exactly where the designer wants it. Roy’s and Abhay Pasupathy’s groups are working on putting flaws where they choose.

What a trapped electron is good for is also open. The authors note that photonic bound states have essentially unlimited lifetimes, and suggest these might carry charge without loss at their own energies, but nothing here measured current through one.

“And if you trap a particle, you can manipulate it,” Roy said. The uses he and his colleagues point to, from quantum sensors to quantum computers, are a long way past what was measured.

The prediction worth testing is that the lattice did this, not the material. If gridlocked hopping is the ingredient, other crystals with the same frustrated geometry should trap electrons the same way.

The authors name two common lattice patterns to look at next. That is a recipe, and the next paper either finds one or does not.

The full study was published in the journal Science.
