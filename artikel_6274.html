A tube made of tiny speakers is called ‘the closest thing to magic’ this scientist has ever seen

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Finnish physicists turned ultrasound into a sound tube with no walls that steers water, powder, gas and even sparks along bendable paths.

Making water flow horizontally through the air without spilling sounds like magic. But scientists in Finland just made it happen by inventing a brand new kind of pipe.

Instead of using plastic or metal, they built an invisible tube out of sound waves that holds a stream of liquid perfectly in mid-air.

Ari Salmi and Dmitry Nikolaev of the University of Helsinki built the device with colleagues at the Public University of Navarre in Spain and the University of Michigan.

A ring of 256 small ultrasonic speakers, each about the size of a shirt button, surrounds the water from about 2 inches (5 centimeters) away. They push on it from every side, but a person standing next to the ring doesn’t hear a thing.

“Of all the experimental setups I have seen, I would say this is the closest thing to magic,” said Salmi, a professor of electronics who led the development of the device.

The same ring, driven differently, guided powder, mist, two heavy gases and even an electric spark along paths the team could bend on command. So far it works over a stretch about 3 inches (8 centimeters) long.

Sound is pressure moving through air. A loud enough sound pushes on whatever it meets. Labs have used that push for decades to float a drop of water or a small bead in mid-air.

Levitation of that kind moves one object, or a few, at a time. Alternatively, it needs a solid vibrating surface underneath. A wall-free channel that carries a flowing stream and bends on command is the new part.

The Helsinki team worked out how to time the 256 speakers so that their waves add up into a long, thin field. Its strongest pressure wraps around a quiet core, like the walls of a hose. Anything inside gets pushed back toward the center whenever it drifts.

Change the timing and the field bends. The team sent the water straight through the sound tube, then tilted it left and right. They shifted where the water landed by about six-tenths of an inch (16 millimeters) without losing a drop.

Salmi, answering questions from Earth.com together with Nikolaev, said the thing that surprised them most was simple. “We were surprised that the ultrasonic pipe does not leak,” he said.

The sound tube has settings, and different forms of matter need different settings. A field shaped like a long donut, with high pressure around a hollow center, held a stream of water in place.

It also held single drops about a sixteenth of an inch (1 to 2 millimeters) across.

The donut field failed on fine powder, though. Grains of silica about the width of a human hair started circling inside it. They were caught in a slow swirl of air that the sound itself stirs up. The swirl flung them out.

A field with petal-shaped lobes around the center kept the powder on track instead. Only a few stray grains fell along the way.

Gases needed a third setting, with the pressure focused down the middle.

Carbon dioxide and nitrous oxide, both heavier than air, followed the field in a hollow sheath around that core. So did the mist from a household humidifier. Helium, which is lighter than air, couldn’t be steered at all in these tests.

“By changing how the speakers are controlled, we can reshape and turn the tube as needed,” said Nikolaev, a postdoctoral researcher who led the team on the project.

“Transporting a solid substance, for example, requires a different field configuration than transporting a liquid or a gas.”

The strangest passenger in the sound tube was a spark. The team put the tip of a small Tesla coil inside it. With the sound off, its discharge jumped in random directions and wandered as much as an inch (25 millimeters) from side to side.

With the sound on, the spark locked onto the path that the field traced. It strayed by only about a millimeter, roughly the width of the glowing channel itself.

A spark heats a thin thread of air, and the authors argue that the same sound pressure that steers a gas steers that thread.

The team used the steered spark to weld the edges of two plastic pieces without touching them.

Other demonstrations in the paper spelled the letter E in water drops on a plate and filled rows of tiny wells with liquid or powder. One merged two liquid streams in mid-air.

The tricks point at 3D printing. Powder came through the ring at about a seventh of an ounce (4.2 grams) a minute, near the low end of what metal printers feed. Liquid came through at up to 1 fluid ounce (30 milliliters) a minute, more than most printers that lay down living cells push.

The ring runs at about 40,000 vibrations a second, too high-pitched for a human ear to register. The pressure inside isn’t gentle.

“At the core of the device, the intensity of the ultrasound is comparable to a continuous roar of gunfire right next to your ear,” Nikolaev said. “But it is too high-pitched for the human ear.”

Salmi said the idea came to him on an evening dog walk four years ago. His lab had built a device that cleaned the insides of pipes with ultrasound. At lunch, a colleague had remarked that it would be handy if a pipe had no surface for dirt to stick to.

The University of Helsinki has filed a patent application on the method, naming Salmi and Nikolaev among the inventors.

The prototype is a tabletop device. In their answers to Earth.com, Salmi and Nikolaev said it’s meant for small, delicate flows rather than anything resembling plumbing.

They expect the stream’s width can grow to about an eighth to a fifth of an inch (3 to 5 millimeters).

The field is also easily disturbed. Any turbulence in a gas stream broke the guidance. The researchers had to drape fabric around the device to block drafts in the room.

Sound bouncing around inside the cylinder weakened the field more than their calculations predicted, which is one reason they built the steering section as a widening cone.

The first real jobs, the two researchers told Earth.com, will likely be moving gases, “since there are very few current methods that can do that.”

They added, “We find the universality of the method very intriguing – it works with all states of matter (solids, liquids, gases and plasma).”

Whether chained modules can carry a stream across a bench, or a room, is the question a 3-inch prototype can’t answer. The team’s code for shaping the fields is public, so other labs with speaker arrays can now try.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
