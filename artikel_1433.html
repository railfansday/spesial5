But I reached out to Susannah to learn more, and I found that she had a nuanced view. She explained to me that, sure, there are hard choices ahead of us, but opportunities too.

Susannah Fisher: We have got two choices really in the future that's coming up and that agency lies within us. So it's a scenario where we keep going as we are. We don't adapt significantly into climate change and we don't reduce greenhouse gases fast enough. And I call that a sink scenario. That's where things continue and unfortunately vulnerable countries and communities bear the brunt of those risks. Or there's a scenario where we look at those risks right now, we take them seriously, we rethink how we're managing water, droughts, relationships between countries, and we take it as an opportunity to imagine new futures. And for me that would be a swim scenario.

Nick Petrić Howe: Having agency to make a better future certainly sounds more optimistic than I was expecting, because the thing is… adaptation a bit of a tricky topic. This podcast series has focused a lot on optimism, about what we can do and to help people who, like me, might be close to losing hope. So, talking about how we adapt to a warmer climate almost feels like an admission of failure, that we’ve been too slow and haven’t done enough.

But, frankly, I need to go over myself. Adaptation is necessary. I’m not the only one who needs to get over myself though.

These days adaptation is a big area of research and a key part of the negotiations around climate. But historically, it’s been pushed down the agenda. Understandably, researchers and politicians have wanted to focus more on trying to stop climate change in the first place, so discussions of how we adapt to a warmer world were seen as a bit of a distraction in early climate discussions. That has changed in recent years and it’s not like society isn’t adapting. We have walls to defend against rising seas, people have been relocated to avoid floods, or funding for farmers who are facing droughts. But as Susannah explained to me, the changes we’ve seen haven’t really been enough.

Susannah Fisher: Whilst we've got lots of adaptation going on so far, the problem is it's mainly incremental. So, it's mainly addressing the current risks and helping people to live as they are in the places where they currently are. So, whilst we've made important starts, one of the key arguments I wanted to make in the book is actually the impacts that we're going to see are so significant that we're going to need to make this step change in the activity and the way that we do adaptation.

Nick Petrić Howe: Part of the issue, Susannah told me is that people have focused on short-term fixes that may not really pay off in the long-term. Like with the discussion of GDP, politicians tend to get rewarded for short-term changes, rather than longer term investments that may pay dividends as they help us live in a warmer world. That world is a long way off for a politician looking at the next election cycle.

You can think of this way. Air conditioning is an adaptation to a warmer climate; it helps you stay cool as summers get hotter. In a particularly hot spell, a savvy politician may even offer subsidies to fit air conditioning into homes and businesses. But long-term, those air conditioning units may actually drive carbon emissions, making the problem worse. Not to mention all the hot air they spurt out into the outside world. The harder, longer-term, adaptation would be to make city environment cooler. Creating canals that can cool the surrounding area, planting trees along streets to provide shade, and changing building design to cool the area around and in them. These kind of changes require a lot more political will and time, for something that people may not see the benefit of immediately.

Now, air conditioning probably will be part of adaptation, especially in large public areas, to allow people to escape the heat, but they help illustrate the idea that short-term changes are attractive, even if they risk causing more harm in the long-term. And that’s one of the key ideas behind Susannah’s book – there are hard choices ahead.

Susannah Fisher: There are some things that aren't really politically simple. There isn't one clear scientific answer. They actually really depend on what people value, what they want for their future, and involves many different perspectives. And they might also involve trade-offs, short-term political and economic costs for long-term benefit.

Nick Petrić Howe: Politically, as well you will often have the environmental department of a nation proposing adaptation, but they have to compete with the treasury. It’s not hard to see who often wins that fight.

Susannah Fisher: You know, what government would want to take a short-term hit for managing something that could have an upfront economic cost but longer-term economic benefit? It's a real political trade-off between managing those risks.

Nick Petrić Howe: According to Susannah, that means that often governments will do adaptations that just make sense anyway, like changing all the bulbs to be more energy efficient. Sure, that saves energy and therefore helps stop climate warming emissions, but it’s also just cheaper. A nice synergy perhaps, but not really enough to help us adapt to climate change.

As the climate warms, we’ll see much more significant choices ahead of us than lightbulbs. As disasters strike and seas rise, people are going to be forced to move.

Susannah Fisher: So I would want to make sure that we don't go down a kind of a narrative of fear around huge flows of climate-related migration, because climate-related movement is actually much more complex than that. It's really hard to put numbers on the scale of the challenge because it involves a whole load of assumptions. So you'll see numbers between, say, 200 million and over a billion. And it's really down to how you assume that climate change plays out, but also the policies in place.

Nick Petrić Howe: Most climate migration has been, and likely will be, within countries, rather than between them. In the US, for example, millions of people have already moved to escape flooding in the past 20 years, not all of that will be down to climate change, but as the planet warms flooding is projected to get worse. And some cities in the US, like Buffalo, New York, have start advertising themselves as climate refuges — places where people can come and be free from drought, heat and other disasters.

Even when people do cross borders, those movements tend to be to close countries. But as the climate gets warmer, Susannah thinks we need to think hard about how we manage this.

Susannah Fisher: And so what's really important is how do we facilitate that as an adaptation strategy? I think not just about the people who might need to go for a variety of reasons, of which climate is one, but also the places that those people go. Like what does it mean to host people well, to find space for people, to provide services for people so that the whole trajectory of any potential climate related migration is supported.

Nick Petrić Howe: And I don’t have to go far to see how this might play out. In the UK, millions of people may be at risk of coastal flooding, and so may have to move. Most of that will likely be to places in the UK, but the question is, where should they go? And what will happen to their communities.

I had hoped that Susannah had some answers to these questions. I ask everyone I talk to ‘how to save world?’, I desperately want to know what the path forward is.

But her point is that there are hard choices ahead of us, and we all need to think carefully about the world we want to make. People moving is only one part of this too, we need to think about how to adapt crops, deal with the effects on wild animals and plants, and manage conflict in a warmer world.

This may all sound a bit overwhelming, I know it did to me as I was reading Susannah’s book. There’s a lot of difficult choices ahead of us, and, so far, limited progress. But Susannah focuses on the opportunities that are there too.

Susannah Fisher: So one can often feel downhearted with the climate crisis, unsure where to find hope and optimism. And I think the important thing is that we look at the future that we want to build for ourselves, for our families, for our communities, and for global relationships. And we think, what is our role in creating that? And I think it's important to hold on to the hope and hold on to the next step, that we've all got the chance to make change where we are, and that is how we're going to adapt to climate change and build the world that we want to live in.

Nick Petrić Howe: Climate change is a big, scary problem. So, I’m glad that there are people out there who also have big ideas on how to fix it. We can find fixes to multiple problems at once, maybe we can finally end humanity’s addiction to meat, and we can perhaps nudge our economies in a way that makes more long-term sense for the climate. At the same time, we’ll need to adapt to the warmer world we’ve committed to. That’ll mean making some hard choices, but the longer it is until we make them, the harder they’ll get.

The ideas we’ve discussed here are by no means an exhaustive list. There are so many people working on climate change, and so many ideas on what to do about it. We haven’t talked about carbon taxes, for instance, where climate is costed in — something which a lot of evidence supports, even if politicians are a bit more wary. Or heck, if you want a really big idea, there’s geoengineering, but that is a difficult thing to test and there’s a lot of unintended consequences that could come with it. And there’s methane too, efforts to stop this particularly greenhouse gas leaking out could make a sizeable dent in reining in climate change. Maybe you have a big idea of your own. As Susannah alluded to, we’ll need all of us to come together to try and solve this big problem.

But as I bring this episode to a close, I still have Susannah’s last words ringing in my head. That we’ve all got a role to play in making the world we want to live in. So far, I’ve been pleasantly surprised by where the data say we’re at in terms of climate change, and I’ve optimistic that some of these ideas out that can maybe help, but I still feel a bit lost myself. The scale of the problem seems overwhelming, and I’m just one person. And I’m not sure what to do about that. What am I supposed to do with that distress?
