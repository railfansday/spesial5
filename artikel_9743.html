How Southeastern University Got a Handle on Its Data

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

News, news analysis, and commentary on the latest trends in cybersecurity technology.

Data security posture management (DSPM) allowed the university to uncover sensitive information, tighten access controls, and lay the groundwork for safer AI adoption.

Southeastern University had a data security problem hiding in plain sight. Like many universities, it operated a sprawling IT environment in which individual departments maintained their own workflows and data repositories. Student records, financial information, and other personally identifiable information (PII) were scattered across file servers, Google Drive, Microsoft OneDrive, SharePoint, databases, and other systems. The university had no reliable way to see what sensitive data it held, where it was stored, whose information it contained, or who could access it.

The challenge is not unique to Southeastern. A 2024 Kiteworks survey of higher education institutions found that nearly half of respondents (49%) used five or more tools to send and share sensitive information. Just 19% said they could track and control sensitive data shared internally, and only 14% could do so when information was exchanged externally.

While Kiteworks' study found the education sector to be at considerably higher risk than other sectors, its more recent 2026 survey suggests the problem extends well beyond higher education. Among 450 security, compliance, and technology professionals surveyed across industries, 54% said their organizations used four or more separate platforms to exchange sensitive information, 73% had no enforcement restricting which of those channels employees could use, and just 39% validated their sensitive data on a quarterly or more frequent basis. The findings highlight the challenges organizations face in maintaining visibility and control of sensitive information across increasingly complex IT environments.

"We knew there was a lot of data out there, but we didn't know exactly what it was or where it lived," Rhoden says. "Our data was kind of spread across diverse areas, and it basically created a challenge for us to have visibility across all of that."

The university initially relied on discovery and security features built into platforms such as Google Workspace and Microsoft 365. But looking at each environment separately made it difficult to develop a comprehensive picture of the university's data and the protections around it. It was harder to tell who had access to sensitive information or when sensitive data was exported and shared without necessary protections. Meanwhile, growing AI adoption across campus was creating new concerns about how employees and other users accessed, shared, and handled sensitive information.

As a small private university in Lakeland, Fla., Southeastern's IT team didn't have the resources to manually discover, classify, and govern data across all these environments. So a little over a year ago, Rhoden began looking for a data security posture management (DSPM) platform that could automate much of that work and give his team a clearer picture of the university's exposure.

After evaluating at least three DSPM products, Southeastern selected Lightbeam's Data Identity Graph because the data engine could consider the broader context when identifying sensitive information, rather than relying primarily on predefined keywords and patterns. Other evaluated products generated too many false positives when they flagged information based on superficial similarities to sensitive data. Lightbeam's approach resulted in fewer false positives, he says.

Southeastern connected Lightbeam to its Microsoft and Google environments, file servers, databases, email systems, and other repositories. The platform uses APIs and purpose-built connectors to link these disparate sources, scan information where it resides, and identify and classify sensitive data. This meant the university could gain a unified view of its data across its environment without moving all of it into a centralized repository.

One particularly difficult part of the process was determining when records across different systems referred to the same person.

"How do we link the user in a database for our student information system to that same user in Google Workspace and Microsoft 365, so that we get the full picture that the entity is the same person across all those different sources?" Rhoden says, noting that was one of the biggest deployment challenges the security team faced.

Lightbeam also helped the university develop more granular categories for information, such as academic records, student financial aid, loans, tuition, and FAFSA records. This allowed Rhoden's team to distinguish among different types of sensitive information and apply more appropriate access, retention, and compliance policies.

"If files are being shared internally within a department, they should be shared in a designated location," Rhoden says, by way of an example. "If they're being shared internally within SEU, they should be shared in another designated location. And if they're being shared externally, there's a specific process to follow."

The deployment has given Southeastern visibility into more than 180TB of data, encompassing 7.6 million files across 13 data sources. Lightbeam identified 642,000 files containing sensitive information and helped the university associate that information with approximately 1.7 million individual and organizational identities, Rhoden says.

The improved visibility has already helped the university identify files and cloud links that were accessible to more people than necessary. That has given the IT team a basis for tightening permissions and moving toward least-privilege access. Southeastern has also established baseline policies for identifying externally exposed information and data that no longer needs to be retained. The next step is to implement more automated access and retention controls, including safeguards for the sensitive information that AI applications can access.

That last area, in particular, is becoming increasingly important as the university encourages students, faculty, and staff to use AI tools to improve productivity and the broader campus experience. Rhoden wants to support that adoption without allowing sensitive university information to fall into the wrong hands. One concern is employees signing up for free AI accounts and entering student records, personal information, or other confidential material into services that may use conversations to train or improve their models. Because these tools are readily available, employees without an IT or security background may inadvertently expose information without realizing the implications.

"What scares me most is just how embedded AI has become in everyone's lives," he says. "It's how accessible these tools are, how easy they are to use, and how tempting it is to turn to them and say, 'I could spend time figuring this out, but I could also just get it done in a minute while I'm working on something else.'"

Rhoden sees the work with Lightbeam as an important foundation for managing that risk. With a better understanding of what information the university holds and how sensitive it is, his team can begin drawing clearer boundaries around AI use, distinguishing data that can safely be shared with AI applications from information that should remain off-limits.

Southeastern's work with Lightbeam reflects a broader shift in data security from simply finding sensitive information to understanding it in context, says Himanshu Shukla, co-founder and CEO of Lightbeam. Traditional discovery tools often focus on identifying data such as Social Security numbers or other personally identifiable information, but that alone offers an incomplete picture of risk, he says. For example, identifying a Social Security number in isolation, without understanding whose it is, where it resides, or how it is being used, provides incomplete context for assessing risk.

Building an entity-centric view of data allows organizations like Southeastern to map information scattered across different systems to the associated individuals and organizations, Shukla says. This can enable a more complete picture of what data they hold, where it resides, who can access it, and how it is being shared or used. That context can help security teams identify unnecessary exposure, tighten permissions, and apply more targeted retention, data-handling and automated workflow policies, he notes.

Meanwhile, Rhoden's advice to other universities considering a DSPM investment is to assess both the complexity of their data environments and the resources available to manage them. Universities need to consider whether they have the personnel to manually examine their many data sources and configure the necessary security controls or whether a platform can help automate discovery and provide a more comprehensive view.

"You can't solve problems you don't know exist," Rhoden says. "Once you know what's out there and what problems do exist, you can start planning to solve them."

Improving data security takes time, organizational buy-in, and sustained effort, but establishing visibility, he says, is the essential first step.
