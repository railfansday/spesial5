It’s Not Just Timbaland: Why Are So Many Artists Suing Their Managers Right Now?

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Disputes between artists and managers are nothing new, but the last year has seen a flood of lawsuits from Timbaland, Muni Long, Ellie Goulding and more.

In August 2025, when Timbaland’s attorneys sent notice that the star was firing his longtime manager Gary Marella, they didn’t mince words. “You were in the rare position of representing one of the most accomplished, influential, and respected talents in music history,” they wrote in the letter. “You exploited that position and betrayed that trust.”

The dispute exploded into public view last month when Timbaland sued Marella in Los Angeles court, claiming he “stole millions in unauthorized commissions, fees, and reimbursements” and “falsified financial records” to hide his alleged misconduct. The manager later denied the accusations and fired back that the hitmaker was “attempting to shift responsibility for his own overspending and financial decisions onto someone else.”

The case is the latest in a rash of recent lawsuits between artists and managers, including new ones filed by Ellie Goulding, Sara Landry, Muni Long and Andra Day and a high-profile trial in another involving Chance The Rapper. Such litigation offers a raw look into a relationship that’s at the heart of the music industry — and one that seems particularly prone to ugly disputes.

A manager is supposed to be an artist’s personal CEO, handling the many complex problems that arise in the modern music business so that their client can focus on their art. The best managers can leverage experience and connections to help a young artist break out, or to push an established star to the next level. They take a healthy cut — often north of 15% of earnings — but it’s a great deal for both sides when things go smoothly.

When they don’t, it can quickly turn nasty. Managers are intimately involved in so many aspects of an artist’s life, and often spend so much personal time with their clients, that the legal battles that erupt between them can sometimes feel more like divorces than ordinary business disputes.

“Artists and managers work so closely together. Sometimes they are really close friends,” says Precious S. Jacobs, an attorney at the law firm Jenner & Block who represented Chance the Rapper in his recent trial. “It’s one of the closest relationships that these artists have, because this is the person that’s supposed to be out there representing you and your interests.”

Any partnership can devolve into litigation, but the artist-manager relationship is especially ripe for conflict. That’s partly due to the very nature of the job, in which managers quietly handle all the complicated or boring stuff so an artist can focus on music. That arrangement works great until a dispute arises, when artists often feel blindsided by the number of decisions that have been made by somebody else and how little they actually know about their own affairs.

In Timbaland’s case, he claims he began investigating his management team only after he suddenly received a foreclosure notice on his home out of the blue: “Plaintiffs took control away from defendants, retained independent counsel and financial professionals, and reconstructed their affairs through third-party records, uncovering unauthorized payments, concealed credit-card arrangements, falsified communications, undisclosed liabilities, and purported loans [he] had never knowingly agreed to,” the lawsuit reads.

The problem is further exacerbated by an unusual level of informality, which leaves things murky for both sides. An artist and a record label would never release music based on a handshake, but that’s often all that exists between a musician and a manager. An oral contract was at the center of Chance’s dispute with manager Pat Corcoran, and Muni Long has claimed in her fight with ex-managers Chaka Zulu and Jeff Dixon that she never signed a written contract with them.

“It used to be that artists would stay with one manager and there was this close-knit relationship,” says Jacobs. “They trusted each other, and they had these long-standing relationships. That’s changing. They have shorter relationships, but they’re still operating on a handshake.”

Another growing area of dispute is conflicts of interest, a problem that’s become more common as managers evolve from trusted consiglieres to business entities with multiple clients and complex ownership. In August, Goulding sued her former managers over claims they failed to disclose a conflict of interest after concert giant Live Nation became the owner of their parent company.

Oftentimes, managers are just as aggrieved as artists. Last year, Day’s former manager accused her of refusing to honor contract provisions that entitled him to a 40% royalty on her publishing and a 20% commission on other profits. She quickly sued him back, claiming he had illegally “siphoned” more than $1.6 million from her.

Long and Chance’s cases are both those same kinds of two-way battles, as is the ongoing court fight involving Landry. The EDM star’s former managers sued her in London this summer, claiming she wrongfully terminated them; she later fired back with counterclaims and a U.S. lawsuit alleging they tried to “seize” control of her music rights and her label.

While many such disputes are exacerbated by quirks of the relationship, they still usually boil down to the same factor that’s behind most lawsuits: “In my experience, almost every artist-manager dispute is driven by one thing: money,” says David B. Jonelis, an attorney at Hollywood firm Singer Weinsten Wolf & Jonelis LLP who repped the singer Jena Rose in a case against her managers.

Some of those fights over pay can’t be avoided. If an artist is simply refusing to hand over valid commissions or a manager secretly took millions in royalties, no contract is going to fix that. But proper planning can go a long way in clearing up how exactly compensation will be handled, including what income is commissionable, how the “sunset” phase works after a partnership ends or whether a manager will retain any ownership stakes in the music.

“While it can be awkward to negotiate a written agreement with someone who is supposed to be your friendly creative advisor and counselor, having the terms of the relationship commemorated in writing will avoid any future ambiguity as to the manager’s compensation,” Jonelis says.

Fights between stars and managers are hardly a new phenomenon. In 1989, Billy Joel filed a $90 million fraud and breach of contract lawsuit after he allegedly discovered vast wrongdoing by his ex-manager Frank Weber. The Rolling Stones famously spent years litigating with their former manager Allen Klein after they split with him in 1970. Coldplay just wrapped up a lengthy court battle with Dave Holmes, who managed the band for more than two decades.

But the sheer volume of new cases involving big artists over the past year is unusual — perhaps driven by an increased awareness of legal rights among musicians in the modern era: “With AI and social media,” Jonelis says, “artists now have easier access to information about how an artist-manager relationship should work.”

Jacobs, the Jenner attorney, echoes that sentiment: “In the modern world with social media, people are seeing that they’re not alone, that there are other artists struggling with their managers,” she says. “Litigation is expensive, but artists and managers are going to the courthouse to make sure that their rights are vindicated.”
