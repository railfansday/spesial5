Want Studio-Style Pilates at Home? Start With These Editor-Tested Reformers

If you’re a pilates princess, you already know what a reformer is, and you’re well acquainted with the many ways it delivers a low-impact, strength-building workout. However, if you’re a newbie, here’s a quick crash course: A reformer is a specially designed machine, originally developed by Joseph Pilates, that consists of springs for resistance, a moving carriage, a foot bar that you push or pull, and pulleys to hold onto or hook to your feet when performing pilates exercises.

“I’d describe the reformer as a machine designed to help create an understanding of your body with resistance,” says Sam Miles, owner and co-founder of Good Time Pilates in New York City. “It gives real time feedback so you can find sensation through the shortening and lengthening of your muscles. In short and easy terms: an assist to finding your muscle engagement!”

There are unique benefits to using a reformer that are hard to replicate in a pilates mat workout, or really any other type of workout. But that doesn’t mean you have to be advanced to start using one. “The modifications to increase or decrease challenge make it a truly universal machine for any body and any fitness level, which is very unique and just awesome,” Miles says. “You can rehabilitate with a reformer, strengthen muscles, stretch and find release work, it’s the best!”

Pilates reformers are available in different types: classical and contemporary. The classical reformer stays true to Joseph Pilates’ original design; the machines are made of wood, and have springs that all have the exact same resistance level. Purists are going to want to consider these machines.

Contemporary reformers are an evolutionary riff on the original and often incorporate springs of varying resistance that can be adjusted. The dimensions also usually vary a bit from the OG classical reformer design.

While it used to be that reformers were only found in Pilates studios, more and more women are investing in a reformer as an addition to their home gym setup. There are also less intense reformer models on the market that are specifically designed for at-home use, and these generally come in at a fraction of the cost of a classical or studio-grade contemporary reformer.

After testing a bunch of options, we narrowed down our picks to a variety of machines, including commercial-grade reformers and pared-down, reimagined reformers that are made specifically to take up less space and be more affordable.

After testing a handful of Pilates reformers, the contemporary-style Balanced Body Allegro 2 came out on top for its studio-quality, strong springs, durable build, and versatile foot bar. WH fitness contributor Talene Appleton has been using it two to three times a week to practice at home for almost two years, and it’s still in mint condition. Appleton says it’s built like a tank and is commercial-grade, so it’s the best you can get for your at-home gym setup.

Five springs are featured for varying tension to accommodate different levels—three high resistance (red), one medium resistance (blue), and one light resistance (yellow), while the steel, grippy foot bar glides effortlessly for 10 vertical and horizontal positions. “I was blown away by how much the additional positions of its foot bar allowed me to change up my usual Pilates sequences,” says Appleton. “This adds incredible versatility in terms of movement options that most competitors can’t beat.” While the foot bar is its best feature, Appleton notes it’s the most difficult to set up and requires two people to line it up evenly on each side.Appleton says this machine is best for advanced users, given there’s a bit of a learning curve at first. “Using a traditional reformer at home can be a bit overwhelming at first, especially if you’re used to having an instructor call the shots in terms of spring resistance, foot bar positions, moves, and all the rest,” she says. “I found myself second-guessing my setup and wondering if I was getting the most out of each exercise (or sometimes if the move I was doing was even a real exercise at all).” She credits YouTube and TikTok tutorials for guiding her along, specifically from Balanced Body and East River Pilates.While this is the best Pilates machine money can buy (with a steep price tag of $4,345 *sigh*) it’s heavy at 157 pounds. It’s also large, at eight feet long and three feet wide, so make sure you have adequate space before splurging. It’s also not the easiest to set up. “Setting this thing up was no joke—I knew I was in for a challenge when I saw the multiple massive, heavy boxes it arrived in,” Appleton says. She notes it took her and a friend two hours to assemble.

The classical Merrithew At Home SPX Reformer is next-level if you want to up your Pilates game. It features a foldable vertical tower, which can improve flexibility and target different muscle groups with more controlled movements, adding more challenge. It has five springs—four full-resistance and one with half-resistance. Given the springs mostly have the same resistance, this machine is designed for more advanced users.

The foot bar is adjustable with four positions for different heights, while the carriage is padded for comfort. Another call-out is the included reformer box, which adds height and range of motion to moves in the seated and lying position. It’s especially helpful for those with muscle tightness or who need to modify moves involving the knees.

While this is a great reformer to elevate your practice, it’s quite large at 96 inches long and 26 inches wide. It’s also heavy at 115 pounds. Like the AeroPilates Pro XP 557, this isn’t ideal for apartments. The tower structure also requires more vertical space, so if you don’t have high ceilings, our best overall is most likely your best bet.

Pretty much all pilates reformers in this guide are relatively large, but the contemporary AeroPilates Reformer 4420 is foldable. If you’re an apartment dweller, this machine is your compact answer to your Pilates reformer dreams. It can be stashed under a couch, bed, or behind a door. At 69 pounds, it’s not the lightest, so having two people for setup is probably your best bet.

Like the AeroPilates Reformer 287, it uses cords, not springs, yet delivers impressive effectiveness. This model has four adjustable cords with varying resistances and color codes, unlike the 287: two standard resistances (black), one extra intensity (red), and one light resistance (yellow). So, this reformer is good for both beginners and those who are more advanced.

It also has a built-in cardio rebounder, which is essentially a mini trampoline at the foot bar, making it an especially good choice for someone looking for low-impact cardio options and variety.

The contemporary Your Reformer is studio-grade like our best overall reformer pick, and is super durable despite being a little over half the price. During a stay in Los Angeles, Appleton rented one for at-home workouts and was extremely impressed with its easily changeable five thick springs, and a carriage that glides smoothly and quietly. The springs are also color-coded: two heavy resistance (red), two medium resistance (blue), and one light resistance (yellow).

In terms of quality, it’s a direct competitor to the Allegro 2. “The straps are thick and definitely won’t fray on me, and the strap handles are comfortable to grip, even when my palms get sweaty,” Appleton says. It’s not compact enough for most apartments, but it’s slimmer to accommodate home spaces better.

Appleton is 5’5” and found the carriage to be wide and spacious, with plenty of room to move around from supine to standing to kneeling positions. It also accommodates her much taller boyfriend, who’s “20 times more passionate about a good reformer workout,” so it’s the real deal.

The AeroPilates Reformer 287 is the most affordable option we’ve come across. It’s dramatically less expensive than our best overall—it costs under $400! But that doesn’t mean it’s less effective. Its quality is still impressive, just not studio-grade. WH entertainment editor Amy Wilkinson, an avid Pilates practitioner, was surprised by how sturdy this machine is because of its bargain price, and since it comes pre-assembled (another plus).

But, she stresses it’s best for beginners since it only features three resistance cords. “I could see someone eventually progressing beyond the capabilities of this model, especially when it comes to exercises like footwork and running, which are classically done on four springs,” says Wilkinson. “It also lacks certain accessories you’d typically find with a studio machine—a box, which is typically used for exercises in the short-box series and in a long position for pulling straps and T-press, among others.”

Speaking of resistance, unlike our best overall, this reformer uses cords instead of springs. While they can be trickier to hook on and off, their weight is identical to springs Wilkinson uses on studio-grade models.The carriage glides smoothly, although Wilkinson notes the attached handles would fall and get in the way. She recommends tossing them aside instead of hanging on the built-in shoulder blocks. As for the foot bar, it’s stable but can’t be adjusted for specific exercises.Aside from mechanics, this reformer is extremely compact, weighing just 56 pounds, and measuring 42 inches when folded and 85 inches fully extended. Wilkinson lives in a one-bedroom Brooklyn apartment and has no issues stashing it away, although she finds it tricky to fold and wide when the foot bar is attached.AeroPilates says this product can fit people up to 6'4", but Wilkinson, who is 5’11”, says even her height was pushing the machine’s limits. “I imagine anyone much taller than I am would feel a bit crunched,” she says. She also says it’s narrow, and didn’t have additional breathing room.

While most full-sized pilates reformers don’t have a built-in trampoline at the foot bar, the contemporary AeroPilates Pro XP 557 is the first full-sized model we’ve seen with a rebounder, so cardio moves like single-leg pushups and slow squats can be added to the routine. This is especially useful for beginners getting into the groove. WH associate visual editor Berkeley Brooks found it stayed sturdy when executing explosive movements at fast paces, while the material of the main carriage prevented her from slipping around.

Brooks notes that the four strong springs are easily interchangeable and have varying resistances labeled by color: two low resistance (blue) and two high resistance (yellow). The foot bar is adjustable, thick, and quiet for added comfort, and can be placed in three positions to accommodate different heights without disrupting your range of motion.

The platform is also wide at 23 inches and long at 100 inches, so this model isn’t ideal for small apartments, especially since you need additional space to execute exercises.

On the upside, Brooks says it’s easy to build and aesthetically pleasing. “I love the addition of wood to the machine because it looks great in a home gym,” she adds.

The springs in a pilates reformer are an important consideration when you’re choosing your machine. “Springs provide resistance and feedback, so the type and responsiveness of springs deeply influence your experience,” says Mo Wolfe, owner and Pilates instructor of Evolution Pilates in Port Washington, New York.

The weight of springs can also depend on whether a machine is classical or contemporary. Classical style reformers have a heavier spring tension that is the same throughout, while modern models generally tend to offer springs with a variety of resistance levels. A reformer with springs is the type you’ll find in a Pilates studio.

Alternatively, resistance cords are sometimes used in the place of springs in reformer machines that are designed for personal and at-home use. They offer similar effectiveness and are more affordable.

Taking the size of your home into consideration is important before purchasing a Pilates machine. Not only do you want to make sure you have enough room for the machine itself, you also want to make sure there will be enough space to perform the full range of Pilates reformer exercises, like leg circles, that require room to move.

Some reformers are very big and bulky, such as our pro-level, best overall selection. They take up considerable real estate and they’re best for a home gym where they can become a more permanent fixture that won’t need to be moved once it’s installed.

If your workout space is multi-functional (i.e., it’s your living room) and your equipment needs to have a smaller footprint and be more portable so you can move it around or stash it away, look for a lighter, foldable option. Just make sure that you’ll have enough space to do the moves when it’s all set up.

Since reformer Pilates is a low-impact activity, and the resistance can be so fine-tuned for your fitness level, there are no hard and fast rules about how often you do a reformer session each week. Miles says it really comes down to how your body feels. “With the variety you can get from a reformer, daily use is manageable. At my studio we have challenging classes, stretch and release classes, technique-focused classes; variety is important!” She notes that most of her clients do three reformer sessions per week.

We picked the brains of Pilates instructors to understand what to look for when shopping for a reformer, then researched dozens of the top-rated models. From there, Women’s Health writers and editors (including fitness contributors Nicolette Accardi and Talene Appleton, and entertainment editor Amy Wilkinson) tested some of the best themselves to narrow down the options.

During testing, they paid close attention to spring and cord resistance level as a measure of whether or not the machines were ideal for beginners or more advanced users. The testers also considered size, durability, and ease of assembly to determine what sorts of spaces were needed for these machiness. Moves like planks, triceps presses, running (pushing off the foot bar), chest expansions, and leg circles were all completed during our testing Pilates workouts to really put these machine to the test.
