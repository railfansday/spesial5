‘Price exposure, not the share itself’ — What do tokenized stock holders actually get?

Crypto exchanges spent the past week rolling out tokenized U.S stocks to buyers around the world. On an unrelated note, Nvidia slid by 30% from its ATH in a month following Jensen Huang’s departure. All while AMD rose — an early sign of decoupling.

One story is about tokenization, while the other is about AI stocks. However, both skirt around the same topic: a growing class of investors being handed exposure they don’t fully understand by markets that do not care to explain it.

For most people outside the U.S, buying an Apple or Nvidia stock is hard. It is either expensive, tedious, or simply unavailable. Pritam Dutta, Founder and CEO of Zoth, explained the same when he spoke to AMBCrypto, stating,

There’s a lot of excitement around tokenized stocks right now. Most people outside the U.S. still can’t easily buy shares in Apple, Nvidia and the rest of the Magnificent Seven. Tokenization changes that.

Undoubtedly, for a buyer in Nigeria or the Philippines, a tokenized stock could be their first realistic route into U.S markets. But Dutta’s next point is the one that should make an investor sit up and take notice.

A lot of what’s launching gives you price exposure, not the share itself. You don’t get voting rights, and dividends depend on the platform’s terms… Too few platforms are being upfront about it.

In a nutshell, investors are not buying Apple. They are buying a token that tracks Apple’s price, issued by a platform whose terms determine what else you get, subject to its discretion.

Dutta, then, reduced the entire conversation to three questions investors must ask themselves before running towards such stocks.

Who holds the real share? What happens to my dividends? What happens if the platform fails?

These are not clever questions. These are the questions you should ask before buying anything that is marketed as “tokenized.” Unfortunately, the fact that they need to be asked in the first place is a sure-shot sign of how little the platforms are disclosing.

In today’s age, the access is real, but the understanding might not be. And, that gap is the hardest on buyers with the least room to make an error.

Nvidia fell this week while AMD rose. The easy explanation is Jensen Huang’s exit. However, Ian Weisberger, CEO of CoinRoutes, believes that there is a larger cause. He told AMBCrypto,

The real problem is that Nvidia’s chips are becoming a commodity.

For years, Nvidia and the AI boom were not seen as two separate entities, simply because of the former’s chips. That is also how the company grew to roughly 8% of the S&P 500.

This week, though the market began pulling the two apart — and within the AI sector — the separation was underway long before anyone realized.

Anyone who has built on both stacks knows the gap is closing. As the chips become interchangeable, buyers will shop on price, and the profits will move to the companies building products on top of them.

That is the concentration problem in one paragraph. When a single stock is 8% of the AI sector, millions of retail investors own a large position in it through their index funds. That is exposure they never signed up to be a part of.

The AI boom is not ending, but the era of one stock carrying the index might be.

Both stories have the same problem — the people most likely to be hurt are unfortunately the least equipped to protect themselves. Here’s why.

Firstly, tokenized stocks are best for an overseas buyer for whom a U.S brokerage account is out of reach. Unfortunately, that buyer is also the first to suffer losses if their platform of choice fails.

Secondly, when it comes to artificial intelligence, the cracks are less noticeable. Most overseas retail investors own Nvidia through an index fund that was marketed to them as “diversified.” This causes the investor with the least information to hold one of the most important market bets.

As Nicolai Sondergaard, research analyst at Nansen, told AMBCrypto:

AI and crypto draw on the same pool of risk capital, so when Nvidia sells off, the money goes to cash, not into Bitcoin.

At the time of Sondergaard’s analysis, Nvidia was down about 32% from its 6th October high, Bitcoin [BTC] was 26% below its September peak, and gold was 17% off its August high. Meanwhile, the dollar was at a six-month high, and real yields were up more than 0.2 percentage points in two months.

So, the investor who thought they were spread across AI and crypto was actually holding double the risk. And in the worst case, they must have not figured it out before it was too late.

The same story can be crafted across different markets — the sophisticated investor can take on the risk, while the overseas investor cannot. Regardless, the product holds no difference when marketing to them.

The demand is real. The question is whether people are being sold what they think they’re buying.

The question at the end of it all is — were buyers sold a dream that was impossible to achieve? Looking further, was the promised portfolio diversification ever there at all?

In the end, the retail investor’s blind spot is not ignorance. It is the industry that keeps expanding faster than investors understand what’s going on, causing them to buy the narrative and allure. Not the reality.
