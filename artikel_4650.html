Benevolence, Not Beneficence, Will Drive Trust in AI

Years ago, I was visiting Notre-Dame in Paris and was moved to light a votive candle, thinking of my young and growing family. It also reconnected me to the teaching from my Catholic school, back in Hyderabad, India, and the idea of giving without counting the cost, a phrase from the prayer credited to St. Ignatius.

Coincidentally, the visit came while my coauthors and I were developing our research on consumer trust, and that research showed us that benevolence is key to trust. A key dimension in our model, across industries, is operational benevolence, or behavior by a company that reflects a motivation to put the customer’s interest ahead of the provider’s own (Sirdeshmukh, Singh, & Sabol, 2002). When I hear firms building AI describe their purpose in terms of beneficence, a word that sounds much like benevolence but asks far less of a firm, I fear that they are aiming too low and, frankly, not investing in building trust.

Beneficence means doing good and not doing harm. In research ethics, the Belmont Report treats beneficence as an obligation with two rules: do not harm, and maximize possible benefits while minimizing possible harms.

But in business, that obligation is assumed; you’d not expect to be offering something to the market that does harm, or is even merely neutral in its value. A hospital is expected to act for the good of its patients, and an airline to get its passengers where they are going safely; a firm that failed at this would not stay in business long.

Medicine draws the line clearly. The American Medical Association’s Code of Medical Ethics lists a commitment to act for the good of patients among the duties of a trustworthy physician, alongside professional competence. When the Code says what fosters a patient’s trust, however, it points to the physician’s responsibility to place the patient’s welfare above the physician’s own self-interest. Beneficence is necessary; benevolence builds trust.

AI ethics has largely focused on beneficence and neglected benevolence. A review of 84 sets of AI ethics guidelines issued by companies, governments, and research institutions found non-maleficence, the principle of avoiding harm, in 60 of them and beneficence in about half (Jobin, Ienca, & Vayena, 2019). Benevolence did not appear among the 11 principles the reviewers identified.

When an AI firm presents beneficence as its purpose, it is in effect describing the work of training a system that could go wrong to do good instead. While that improvement may feel substantial to the people doing that work, correcting something that could go bad is not an achievement a firm should want to tout, let alone make the great purpose of the company. It is the floor that customers, regulators, and the public already expect.

Geoffrey Hinton’s suggestion that AI be built with maternal instincts, to me, reads as him saying that AI should have benevolence coded into it. Maternal instinct is more than caring. A mother will do what it takes for her child, at a cost to herself, and that is a different thing from doing good. The promise of going to any extent to protect the welfare of the child is why the maternal posture is held up as a driver of trust, and even faith.

If AI firms mean trustworthiness and trust, then what they are taking on is a much heavier burden, and that is benevolence. When we were developing our model of trust, our interviews, focus groups, and then survey all showed something similar. Customers saw actions that showed that the firm put their interests ahead of (or at least in line with) the firm’s interests as central to trustworthiness. Benevolence, more than competence, drove trust and loyalty in a majority of our studies.

The burden lies in the cost. Benevolence almost always means that a provider has taken on a cost, is taking on a cost, or is willing to take one on. For a firm, this is what giving without counting the cost means. When it bears a cost it was not required to bear, customers can infer something about its intentions. Economists make a related point about signaling: a signal is credible when it would be too costly to send for someone who lacks the quality it claims (Spence, 1973).

In AI, the costs are easy to name: delaying a release until a model is shown to be safe, declining a business model built on capturing users’ attention, holding back a capability until those who defend against its misuse have it, and disclosing a model’s weaknesses along with its strengths. Even a large language model that, in its chat, risks user unhappiness and disengagement by offering advice that, on its face, may be seen as negative, is taking a benevolent action.

When customers or patients come to recognize that a provider is benevolent, that is where trust really occurs. It is also what Hinton’s image of the mother conveys: protective, concerned, vigilant, and willing to sacrifice, and trusted for that reason. AI firms that want to be trusted in the same way will have to show, over time and at some cost to themselves, that they will do right by their users when the two sets of interests diverge. Ironically, just showing the intent to do good is a low threshold for trust.
