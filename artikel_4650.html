Why We Look Away: Examining Our Response to Homelessness

“We do not see things as they are, we see things as we are." —Anaïs Nin

We see homeless individuals on street corners begging, with signs asking for help outside grocery stores, in bank kiosks trying to keep warm, and pushing carts with their belongings, to name just a few places. We see people in line at soup kitchens or awaiting entrance to shelters. Those of us in the mental health field see more and hear more about homelessness than the usual person.

Homelessness is often discussed in terms of economics, housing shortages, mental illness, and addiction. But there is an alternative dimensional way that we might examine this issue: the psychology behind our response to homelessness. What happens inside us when we encounter another person in need and in distress?

There are five different dimensions of our response to homelessness that I would like to discuss.

First, there is the discomfort of witnessing another person suffering or in need of help. There might be sadness, discomfort, helplessness, perhaps guilt for what we might have, and some revulsion. Witnessing another person’s suffering so openly tells us something about what we all experience at some point in our lives, which is human vulnerability. We depend on a sense of stability, security, and predictability in our lives. We have familiar routines, homes we return to, tasks we need to accomplish, friends who support us. Perhaps viewing another in such dire circumstances disrupts our own sense of permanence and predictability. We may look away, move on more quickly, or stare surreptitiously.

Secondly, we may manage discomfort by creating a sort of psychological boundary between the homeless person and us. We might note that these individuals are different from us. Their circumstances, such as severe illness, lengthy unemployment, and exposure to trauma or domestic violence, are difficult to comprehend. This apparent distance might falsely reinforce the idea that our lives are secure. The greater the difference we create between ourselves and homeless people, the less humanity and humility we experience. On the other hand, erecting this boundary provides us with a sense of distance and difference.

Thirdly, there are social cues that we all respond to. If one sees others passing a homeless person sitting or standing on the street, we may believe that no response is necessary or that others will help. We look to others around us to determine how to behave. For example, if we arrive for a party and note that all people entering are kissing the host or hostess, we may be more likely to behave the same way. Follow the leader is a lesson we might have been taught that perhaps needs to be unlearned.

Fourthly, perhaps one of the greatest barriers to understanding homelessness is our tendency to be judgmental and make assumptions about people who are homeless. We might make the mistake of assuming mental illness, laziness, or unwillingness to get a job, even if it is menial work. We may blame poor judgment or irresponsible decisions. It is a fundamental attribution error, which is the tendency to explain the behavior of another through personal characteristics rather than circumstances that might have befallen a person.

Stigma creates a social barrier. Once we label someone, we stop seeing them as an individual and more as their label. Their history, personality, relationships, hopes, and dreams become inconsequential to the label we assign someone. For instance, I have often heard practitioners call someone schizophrenic rather than a person with schizophrenia.

The reality is that homelessness is a circumstance, not an identity.

Finally, when we are constantly exposed to the homelessness, war, poverty, and violence that are a routine part of our social media today, we may experience what can be best called compassion fatigue. When people feel overwhelmed by the number of problems they are exposed to daily and their inability to do anything about it, they simply turn away. It produces emotional numbing and avoidance. As we travel to and from work each day by walking or driving, or go to the same supermarket and pass the same person on the sidewalk, they, in essence, become part of the landscape of the route.

This same response occurs for caregivers of very ill persons, who might experience an emotional numbing or habituation.

Understanding these five psychological reactions—discomfort, distancing, social conformity, judgment, and compassion fatigue—gives us a way to think about homeless people and their impact on our behaviors.

Awareness is the first step toward choosing a different response. When someone becomes part of the landscape, we stop noticing them altogether. If instead we stop for a moment and examine our own discomfort rather than rushing past, perhaps we can change our responses, if we so choose. Our own unease likely comes from our own sense of vulnerability and lack of predictability in this world.

This small shift in framing can neutralize the fundamental attribution error and restore personal history to the person. Each person sitting on a sidewalk was once a child, has a birthday, had a family of origin, probably went to school somewhere, and had people in their lives who cared for them.

Breaking the spell of social cues requires a conscious choice. Making brief eye contact, offering a warm greeting, or simply nodding in recognition of another person’s presence counters the invisibility of living on the street or having to beg. It reaffirms their dignity. In some way or another, we all want to be noticed.

Addressing compassion fatigue demands a different balance. We cannot carry the weight of every global crisis or every unhoused person on our shoulders. There is a marked distinction between empathy—feeling someone else’s distress as our own—and compassion, which combines care with a desire to help in some way. When we shift from overwhelmed empathy to compassion, we move away from paralysis to practicality.

Returning to Anaïs Nin’s insight, the way we react to homelessness is ultimately a mirror. When we look away, we are often protecting our own illusion of control and permanence. When we choose to look closer—with humility, curiosity, and basic human kindness—we acknowledge another person’s struggle and reclaim their and our humanity. There is an inner person inside all of us.
