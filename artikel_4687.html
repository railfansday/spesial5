Can a Mental Health Crisis Ever Be a Gift?

Posted November 11, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

Two years ago, I found myself locked in a county jail for four months because of threats I made while in a manic psychosis. I had spent almost a decade eluding treatment for my bipolar I disorder, and the consequences of that circumvention had caught up with me. Behind bars for the first time in my life, and surrounded by beige concrete, I took cold comfort in Nietzsche’s concept of amor fati, or love of one’s fate. Instead of accepting my fate, I thought of counterfactuals: What if I had accepted my diagnosis earlier? What if I had gone to the hospital instead of insisting I was fine?

Two years later, I find peace in a routine of stability and sobriety and writing and advocacy. I have a treatment plan that works for me, have rekindled a loving relationship with my mother after 10 years of estrangement, and have embarked on a career of mental health advocacy that grew out of the wreckage of a catastrophe.

Now, the impulse to tell a redemptive story is tempting. It would be easy, compelling, and even flattering to say that the illness was the price I had to pay for everything good that followed. A reckoning with the past invites the tantalizing questions: Can a mental health crisis be a gift? Who gets to decide?

In an essay from earlier this year, psychiatrist Awais Aftab narrates two recent accounts: although Tyler Alterman could not get out of bed for over a year, he now says that he is grateful for the depression that saved his life; Jazmine Russell considers her psychotic episode an inspiring lesson in how her life was once breaking her down. These narratives have real therapeutic value, since they translate the senseless suffering of psychic collapse into a language that one can speak confidently into the future.

I know their appeal. The remorse I feel, the memoir I’m writing, the advocacy talks I give—none of it could have come without the calamity that came before. Psychologists call this kind of change post-traumatic growth, and research suggests it is common.

It feels seductive to see mental health disorders as a type of evolutionary adaptation that confers advantages on a suffering soul. But as evolutionary psychiatrist Randolph Nesse points out, this is a fallacy. Low mood and anxiety may be useful defenses, but “diseases are not adaptations shaped by natural selection.” As Aftab explains, a symptom (such as a loss of sleep) can lead to positive changes in one’s life (such as improved sleep hygiene) without having been designed for it. A death of a loved one may alter the course of one’s life for the better, encouraging one to cherish remaining relationships, but no one with a straight face would call that death a gift.

In my case, I would be hard-pressed to find the good in my mania. While it felt like clarity, like the best version of me I had ever embodied, it made me cruel and selfish, and it cleared my life of family and friends.

While in jail and during my 10 months at a state psychiatric hospital, I tried to find meaning in my chaos. Whereas I once thought, I am a failure, I learned to reframe the global indictment into the more contained expression of I had failed. The failure supplied the raw material to craft something new. I took solace in Viktor Frankl’s expression of “the last of the human freedoms—to choose one’s attitude.” Edith Eger inspired me with her idea that “the opposite of depression is expression.”

Still, meaning-making was hardly a solitary act: my mother kept showing up to visit me, and read books on bipolar disorder and neuroscience alongside me; at the hospital, I met weekly with a caring psychologist who asked me about how I processed my shame. To call my illness and the decade of destruction it wrought a gift would be to credit it with my mother’s persistence and my psychologist’s curiosity, along with the arduous work I did myself.

But as I learned from my stay at that state hospital, not everyone has cognitive access to Frankl’s freedoms. My neighbor, whom I will call Johnnie, had spent decades at the institution, where he battled voices for what seemed like every hour of every day. He often interjected during our therapeutic groups with quirky one-liners. Rain tastes like Southern Comfort and moonshine. And that’s dangerous. Or I’m Johnnie, and today is a miracle, even with the rain.

Psychologist Steven Heine notes that intense trauma “can completely upend people’s meaning frameworks,” leaving some to “struggle, sometimes for decades.” The allure of a redemptive narrative is that it is written by a survivor, and hidden behind that appeal is a kind of survivorship bias. The very process of looking back on a past crisis, as Aftab says, distorts our perception of the crisis. While a crisis may be one route to change, it is rarely the best one. Telling someone in the middle of a psychotic episode, or a father waiting in an emergency room lobby, that their pain may turn out to be a gift risks romanticizing an episode, forcing a search on demand to find meaning in catastrophe.

Aftab provides a similar lesson for clinicians when he suggests that psychiatrists “should honor the patient’s meaning-making while being mindful of its fallibility.” For every person who can trumpet how a crisis saved their life, far too many, like Johnnie, are prisoners to illnesses that have given them nothing.

While I was initially drawn to the bravado of amor fati, I remained attached to it over time because it taught me the discipline of gratitude. I had the drive, determination, and education to make sense of a crippling mental illness whose riddle the mental health system seemed uninterested in helping me solve. I recovered because I read deeply about the illness that almost took my life, but I also recovered because I never felt alone: I had a mother who kept my memory alive for a decade, and who kept showing up for me because she believed in the promise of her son.

The redemptive arc—the notion that a mental health breakdown can be a breakthrough—is appealing to our quick-fix culture. But it is better to be honest: I can accept all the ugly that has happened, and even love the life it led to, without needing to claim that the illness taught me a necessary lesson in life.
