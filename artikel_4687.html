Last week, I had an argument with a friend that went like this: "UFOs are real." "No, they're not." "Yes, they are." "No, they're not."

You get the idea. There's a lot about philosophy of language, as a subdiscipline of philosophy, that is beyond me. But my one important takeaway from struggling through that class in graduate school was this: People will forever argue at cross-purposes if they don't first define their terms.

When considering the question of whether UFOs are real or not, we should be careful not to fall into the trap—a long-standing American tradition—of conflating UFOs with aliens. People still, in 2026, cringe when someone brings up the subject of UFOs because the conversation inevitably devolves into clichés about little green men and alien abductions. But "UFO" of course doesn't stand for "alien"; it stands for "unidentified flying object," which could turn out to be anything from a bird to a plane to Superman.

When did this conflation originate? Likely in the mid-20th century when UFO sightings spiked and pop culture was flooded with sci-fi tropes of flying saucers and aliens. But the misunderstanding lives on: Just the other day I overheard a professor ask a college student if she believed in UFOs. The student, an astrophysics major, laughed and said "No." I'd argue both the question and the answer are confused.

Ask yourself: Does it make sense to say that one doesn't believe in unidentified flying objects? This claim means you don't think it's possible that someone could look up in the sky and see something they can't identify. But this claim seems absurd. What UFOs are, and whether aliens from outer space visit us here on Earth, are two distinct questions and should be treated as such.

Putting aside the alien question, if our claim is simply that people do indeed see things the sky that they're unable to identify, then our astrophysics major would have to agree. The field of astrophysics wouldn't have come into being without people who studied the night sky and discovered things they couldn't identify and didn't understand.

Two 21st-century trends have brought the reality of UFOs into the light: 1) the internet has made it easier for both expert (e.g., airplane pilots) and non-expert UFO experiencers to share their stories, which helped legitimize UFO sightings as a shared and more common human experience than previously thought ; and 2) the Congressional hearings (begun in 2022) wherein trained military pilots shared their stories publicly lent further legitimacy to the phenomena as real, fascinating, and worthy of scientific study.

People see weird stuff in our skies all the time and in the vast majority of sightings, experts agree, these objects are ultimately identified as natural phenomena—lightning, satellites, lenticular clouds, airplanes, etc. But a small percentage of these sightings remain unidentified because they don't fit into our familiar classifications of reality with regard to how they look or behave, or both.

Jacques Vallee, a mathematician and astronomer who's authored dozens of books on UFOs, is one scientist who's always taken the UFO question seriously. He points out in his 1965 book, Anatomy of a Phenomenon, this ironic observation: Scientists are rational, analytical, and data-driven, until it comes to UFOs. Then they're emotional, gut-reactive, and dismissive of the data, which in ufology (so far) comes mainly in the form of first-person accounts. He notes that scientists have traditionally been content to wait for radio signals from outer space as evidence of extraterrestial intelligence (which assumes other forms of life traffic in radio signals) rather than collecting data on the innummerable acounts of UFO encounters from credible earthlings.

We don't yet know what UFOs are or where they're from though there are some hypotheses (in order from more familiar to least familiar): 1) they're (human-made) secret military vehicles either domestic or foreign; 2) they're humanoids from other dimensions of spacetime; 3) they're objects from lesser known regions on earth like the deep ocean depths; 4) they're visitors from other parts of the galaxy; and 5) they're mirages, holograms, or ephemeral elements of the universe's consciousness.

Science usually begins with wanting to understand something that is unknown. But in the case of UFOs, it might require a paradigm shift in thought from a deep-seated denial about intelligence in the universe to an acceptance that this indeed seems to be true. In Vallee's words, "They cannot replace in their imaginations the huge cosmic cemetery for celestial bodies which they describe in their books with a world of life and thought" (Vallee, 1965).

Vallée, J. (1965). Anatomy of a phenomenon: Unidentified objects in space—A scientific appraisal. Henry Regnery Company.
