Not every muscle gets immortalized in a rap song, but the glutes do more than look good in pants. Beyond the vanity benefits, the glutes help you do fun things in and out of the gym.

Although the gluteus maximus gets all the attention, the gluteus medius and minimus provide the stability that lets the glute max do its job better. That’s why it’s important to focus on all three, not just one.

Compound lifts hit them hard and heavy, but isolation exercises are necessary to train all glute functions and muscles. You can also load them in different ways: from a lengthened position (think RDL) and from a shortened position (think Hip Thrust).

Understanding those differences, rather than choosing sides in the great squat-versus-hip-thrust debate, is key to better glute training.

Each glute muscle has a different role, and understanding them will help you train them more effectively.

It’s the largest and most superficial of the three muscles, and it’s responsible for much of your backside’s size and shape. Although it’s best known for hip extension and external rotation, some lower fibers can assist with hip adduction depending on hip position, while the upper fibers assist with hip abduction.

The gluteus medius sits on the lateral side of your pelvis, partly beneath the gluteus maximus. Its primary job is hip abduction, but it also helps stabilize the pelvis. It also has different muscle fibers that perform different jobs. The anterior fibers assist with hip internal rotation, while the posterior fibers can contribute to external rotation.

The gluteus minimus is the smallest and deepest of the three, sitting underneath the glute medius. It assists with hip abduction and internal rotation while helping control the femur’s position within the hip joint. It also works with the glute medius to stabilize the pelvis when one foot leaves the ground.

The hip is a ball-and-socket joint that allows your hips to flex, extend, abduct, adduct, and rotate. But not everyone’s ball-and-socket joint is built the same way.

The depth and orientation of your hip socket, the angle of your femoral neck, and how much your femur rotates forward or backward vary from person to person. These structural differences influence exercise selection and positions.

Some squat with a narrow stance and toes pointed forward, while others go wider with toes pointed out. Your hip anatomy, mobility, strength, and limb length all influence what position works best for you. Adjusting your stance, foot position, and range of motion can help you find positions that load the glutes effectively and work with your anatomy.

Your glutes are among the body’s most powerful muscles, and paying attention to your backside pays off in more ways than one.

Glutes give you the horsepower to do the things you enjoy, from squatting to sprinting to other daily activities. The stronger they are, the more force they can produce, especially when you pair that strength with power training.

In many unilateral movements like walking, running, walking upstairs, and single-leg RDLs, smaller glute muscles control your lower-body position. Weakness can show up in a loss of balance, excessive wobbling in a single-leg stance, or your knee caving inward during squats and deadlifts. You don’t want any of that.

Your hips, knees, and lower back are teammates, and when one struggles, another may compensate. Weak glute med and glute min can cause knee valgus, which your knee ligaments don’t like. Weak hip extension strength or limited hip mobility forces the lower back to do a job it’s not designed for. Training the glutes for strength and mobility prevents this.

Almost every powerful lower-body movement involves the glutes.

During sprinting, the gluteus maximus drives powerful hip extension. When you cut, land, or change direction, all three glute muscles help control the hip and pelvis as your body absorbs and redirects force. The glutes also contribute to rotational movements, such as swinging a bat, throwing a punch, and a golf swing. Their ability to produce force and rotate helps link lower-body movement to upper-body power.

Here’s what your glutes need to grow bigger and stronger.

Compound exercises such as barbell squats, Romanian deadlifts, and barbell hip thrusts let the glutes produce force alongside the quads, hamstrings, and adductors, making them an excellent foundation for building strength and muscle.

Deep squats, front-foot elevated split squats, and deficit reverse lunges put a loaded stretch where you need it most. Because the hips are flexed and the glutes are lengthened, they can build greater muscle. Bonus points for using a controlled lowering phase.

Barbell hip thrusts, glute bridges, and cable kickback variations emphasize hip extension as the glutes approach their shortened position, given their smaller range of motion.

Neither approach is better, but both can help develop the glutes.

Hip abduction machines, lateral band walks, and side-lying hip raises train the gluteus medius, minimus, and the upper fibers of the gluteus maximus. These improve hip stability, allowing the glute max to work more effectively.

Seated band hip internal rotation, cable hip external rotation, and seated 90/90 hip rotations rotate the thigh within the hip joint while keeping the pelvis and lower back locked in. Rotational training supports hip stability, health, and more complete lower-body development.

The dreaded Bulgarian split squats, single-leg RDL’s, and step-ups train one leg at a time, improving hip stability. Unilateral exercises challenge the glutes to produce force and stabilize the pelvis, improving balance while strengthening imbalances between sides.

You don’t need 15 different glute exercises, but the right combination of movements that cover their various functions without turning every leg day into a marathon.

Start With Compound Bilateral And Unilateral Lifts

Compound lower-body lifts are the foundation of glute training because they let you go hard and heavy while other muscles join the party. But here’s an important distinction: Many compound lifts don’t isolate the glutes.

Compound lifts build strength and muscle, but moving more total weight doesn’t always mean the glutes get all the love. Squat depth, torso angle, hip position, and exercise technique influence how much work the glutes perform.

You need more than one type of hip-extension exercise because different movements challenge the glutes at different muscle lengths. Romanian deadlifts, full-range-of-motion squats, and front-foot elevated split squats load the glutes when they’re lengthened, while hip thrusts, glute bridges, and cable kickbacks emphasize tension closer to full hip extension, when the glutes are shorter.

Both can build size and strength, and each offers something the other doesn’t.

Don’t get caught up in the Team Squat versus Team Hip Thrust debate. Include exercises that challenge the glutes at both longer and shorter lengths, use a full range of motion you can control, and overload when you can.

Debates don’t build muscle. Training does.

Compound exercises train the glute med and mini, but direct hip-abduction exercises give them extra attention. When they’re stronger, your glute max can go to work unencumbered.

Broad jumps, sled pushes, hill sprints, and lateral bounds powerfully train the glutes and fire up your fast-twitch muscle fibers, which drive many of your gains. These exercises are great if you want your strength to carry over to athletic activities.

Your glutes can only work within the ranges your hips can access and control. Include half-kneeling hip flexor stretches with a posterior pelvic tilt, adductor rockbacks, 90/90 hip rotations, and prying squats in your warm-up to prepare for the work ahead.

The trick to training your glutes well is balancing heavy lifting, muscle-building work, and stability exercises while giving your backside enough time to recover. Here’s how to make it work.

Perform lower-body compound exercises, both bilateral and unilateral, with challenging weights. Maintain your technique and rest two to three minutes, or longer, between sets. Focus on progressive overload without sacrificing range of motion.

Again, use compound lower-body exercises that hit both lengthened and shortened positions, with a controlled eccentric contraction and full range of motion, training close to technical failure. Although muscle growth can happen across a wider rep range, this range balances load, effort, and fatigue.

Perform power movements early in your workout when you’re fresh, focus on maximum movement speed and quality, and rest 2 minutes or longer when needed to maintain explosiveness.

Hip abduction machines, cable abductions, and side-lying variations allow you to focus on the smaller glute muscles. Use controlled reps, avoid momentum, and progress the load.

Single-leg RDLs, step-ups, and Bulgarian split squats build strength while challenging stability. Only progress as your strength and balance improve.

Some exercises train several qualities at once. A Bulgarian split squat challenges glute strength, muscle growth, and stability, but you don’t count the same set three times. Assign each set to its primary programming purpose (muscle/stability) when using the table.

For most lifters, two to three sessions per week is a good starting point.

Beginners can split their weekly work into two sessions, while intermediate lifters can use two or three. Experienced lifters focused on glute development will benefit from spreading the work over three sessions to maintain training quality.

Training your glutes isn’t rocket science, but you can make it harder than it needs to be. Here’s what to watch for and how to fix it.

Hip thrusts and bridge variations are excellent for building the glutes from the shortened position, but making them your only option misses opportunities to challenge the muscles at longer lengths and train hip abduction and stability.

The fix: Combine hip thrusts with RDLs, squats, split squats, and direct hip abduction exercises to cover more of the glutes’ functions.

It’s tempting to focus on exercises where you can really feel the squeeze, but muscle growth isn’t determined by how hard you can contract your glutes at lockout. RDLs, deep squats, and front-foot-elevated split squats challenge the glutes at longer muscle lengths, providing a different training stimulus.

The fix: Include at least one exercise that loads the glutes during greater hip flexion.

When chasing the squeeze or extra ROM in a hip thrust, deadlift, or RDL variation, some lifters extend their lower back instead of their glutes. Overarching your back at the top creates movement that doesn’t originate in the hips and shifts the focus away from where it belongs.

The fix: Keep your ribs over your hips, extend through your hips, and stop when you reach lockout.

The gluteus maximus gets the glory, but the smaller glute muscles help stabilize your pelvis, support the ball-and-socket movement, and produce hip abduction. Relying on compound lower-body exercises may leave these muscles undertrained.

The fix: Include band, cable, or machine hip abduction variations to give these smaller muscles the attention they deserve.

There’s one camp that says all you need for glutes is squats. Another camp says hip thrusts are all you need. Opinions run wild, so let’s separate fact from fiction.

Squats are fantastic for building glutes from a lengthened position, but the glutes need more than that, as they don’t challenge every glute function.

The truth: No one doubts squats are great for glutes, but training from a shortened position and direct hip-abduction exercises provide a different stimulus that can enhance muscle development.

Doing a set of hip thrusts tells you you’re engaging your glutes, but just because you “feel” it more doesn’t mean hip thrusts are better than squats for building muscle. Greater muscle activation during an exercise doesn’t necessarily translate into greater long-term muscle growth.

The truth: Research comparing hip thrusts and squats shows both build the glutes effectively, despite their different mechanics. For some, it comes down to personal preference.

Influencers with great glutes will scream from the rooftops that you can isolate and build an upper-glute shelf and a better lower-glute hamstring tie-in. But here’s some fact behind that fiction: The glute maximus has different fiber orientations that contribute to hip extension, abduction, adduction, and rotation.

The truth: Certain exercises and hip positions can shift the emphasis toward different glute regions, but you cannot turn off one area while training another. This is similar to what I explored in the upper- and lower-ab training myth.

If you walk up the stairs or sit down in slow motion after glute day, you might assume you’ve had the greatest glute workout ever. Hold your jeans.

The truth: Extreme muscle soreness usually signals a new training stimulus or heavy eccentric loading, and it isn’t a reliable measure of muscle gain.

Adjust the loads, sets, reps, and exercise variations to match your experience and recovery. Perform them as standalone sessions or fit them into your existing program.

Rest periods: Rest two to three minutes after each superset and 90 seconds between exercises.

Progressive overload: When you reach the upper end of a rep range with clean technique, increase the load slightly.

People rap about and admire the glutes, but they’re the engine that makes the body roar in and out of the gym.

Building stronger, more muscular glutes takes more than hip thrusts and band booty burners. Train the glute max through all its functions, give the med and mini some love, and combine exercises that challenge your backside in lengthened and shortened positions. Include unilateral training, progressive overload, and adequate rest and recovery, and your jeans will thank you.
