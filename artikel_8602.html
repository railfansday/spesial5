actually thats not always true and is usually false, the money you save by not converting will grow but you most likely will pay the same % if not more in taxes down the road.
do the math
for example...$200k in TIRA - roth convert $100k - pay the roth taxes from a brokerage account- calculate 5 years of 8% returns on both TIRA and RIRA and see what the totals are and what you will owe on tira in 5 years, then recalculate but don't Roth convert, calculate 5 years of growth on 200k and see what you will owe on your tira in 5 years.

the net after taxes will be the same assuming tax rates will be the same, however its more likely taxes will be higher which is even more reason to do some roth conversions now.

so the roth conversion is better than no conversion unless you know your tax rates will be lower (nobody knows that but they most likely will be higher) also keep in mind the niit 3.8% penalty, and irmaa tiers as you calculate your estimated AGI when potentially doing roth conversions.

I will use my case as an example, worked out in Pralana. My actual plan is to convert to the top of the 24% bracket for several more years and then we will be in the 22-24% brackets after that. My planned Roth Conversions win vs. no conversions because of avoided higher brackets and IRMAA tiers late in life, avoided single tax rate near end of life and reduced tax drag. Comparing the No Conversion case to my actual plan, my plan does indeed have lower lifetime taxes.

However, that does not prove that lower lifetime taxes is useful as a metric. Consider a case where I do a single multi-million $ conversion immediately and pay the top marginal rate on the conversion. Doing the single giant conversion would reduce our tax-adjusted estate value fairly dramatically and we would agree that it would pretty obviously be a terrible plan as we would never have been in the top bracket.

If I were to judge the merits of the single giant conversion plan by looking at the sum of the taxes paid, it only takes 18 years for the giant conversion case to pull ahead of my actual plan and after that the giant conversion case seemingly would get better and better.

Using the sum of taxes paid can mislead because prepaying taxes will eventually produce a lower lifetime tax bill, even if it also reduces your tax adjusted wealth, because just looking at taxes is ignoring the growth that the early tax payment would have had. Fortunately, just using the tax adjusted estate value overcomes the problem.
