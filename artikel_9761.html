Your Pay Matcher Confused Annual Units With Earnings

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

The day-rate contract wins your job comparison because salaryFromPerAnnum is larger. Your assistant sounds decisive. You get to explain the contract risk it quietly skipped.

Two pay figures can share an annual unit and still answer different questions. Annualized advertised pay is a comparison basis, not a promise of yearly earnings.

Lab note 102: can we give an assistant enough compensation context to explain that distinction? The catch comes first: the available pay fields can't establish paid working time, contract duration, or guaranteed base pay.

Suppose you're building the part of a job assistant that answers, “Which advertised package looks better for this person?”

That question needs a preference: higher advertised potential, more predictable income, or something else. Sorting a normalized number answers a narrower question while wearing the broader question's coat.

The Actor uses Reed's own annualized values for hourly, daily, weekly, and monthly pay. That gives your application a common unit without requiring your own conversion formula.

For a contract, actual receipts depend on paid working time and duration. For OTE, advertised potential and guaranteed base pay are different concepts. Pro-rata wording makes the working schedule relevant to interpreting the amount.

A common unit helps with arithmetic. It doesn't settle those questions.

We'd map the compensation data by the question each group can answer.

The useful consequence is that a “salary card” needs more than salary numbers.

We'd keep the advertised text next to the annualized bounds, rather than making the model hunt through a description for the unit it just lost. For this compact card, that text carries the original rate; the serializer doesn't rebuild it from the original numeric bounds.

Keep salaryType when supplied, but don't invent a lookup from its values to period labels. Readable salary wording is more useful than a confidently guessed enum.

For this narrow comparison, company logos, promotional flags, HTML descriptions, and application mechanics don't help interpret compensation. Leave them out of the initial prompt.

That's a task boundary, not a recommendation to delete them from storage.

Keep full descriptions available for follow-up retrieval. They may contain working schedules or compensation conditions that the compact card can't resolve. Feeding every description into the first prompt also gives the model more opportunities to mistake recruiting language for evidence.

We'd also resist filtering away every advert without displayed pay. An undisclosed amount is a limitation worth showing, not evidence that the role has worse compensation.

And don't turn a missing qualifier into False. The Actor omits fields that aren't published. A default would make your application sound more certain than its source.

Likewise, jobType doesn't supply a contract end date or guaranteed billable days. Converting the rate again with your favorite calendar would add an assumption, not recover a missing fact.

Your comparison also needs more evidence if it expands into take-home income, benefits, or employment costs. This card handles advertised compensation. It can't establish a complete financial preference.

There's an ingestion catch too: detailed pay requires opening the job page. With fetchDetails: false, the Actor returns listing fields without the full description or detailed salary breakdown. An unavailable job page can also leave a listing record with detail-only fields omitted.

So requesting details is useful, but checking the returned evidence is still necessary. A missing salary field doesn't identify its own cause.
