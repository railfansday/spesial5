Medical AI Has a Trust Problem That Better Technology Alone Can't Fix

Approval is not proof: AI changed how fast claims arrive, not what it takes to know they are true.

Three months ago, I argued that artificial intelligence had changed the volume, speed, and polish of information moving through the world without changing the standard that makes any individual claim reliable. The responsibility for separating one from the other, I argued, has never belonged to a piece of technology. It belongs to whoever decides what to believe, and to whoever approves what other people may use. Nothing in the three months since has required a correction. If anything, confirmation arrived from an unexpected direction: a government audit of the tools doctors use to write down what their patients tell them.

The scoring method helps explain why Ontario approved inaccurate vendors. Of 530 points in the bidding criteria, a vendor’s domestic presence in Ontario carried a maximum of 159, about thirty percent. The accuracy of a vendor’s medical notes carried a maximum of 20, about four percent (Office of the Auditor General of Ontario, Use of Artificial Intelligence, app. 2, pp. 46–47). Bidders could have scored zero on system security, bias controls, and note accuracy and still met the minimum score for approval (Hauen). A process weighting criteria this way risks more than approving an inaccurate vendor: it can let an inaccurate vendor outscore an accurate one on criteria unrelated to whether a transcript is true.

None of this is a story about careless technology. AI scribes exist to solve a real problem: physicians spending unpaid hours each week typing notes instead of treating patients. Spence called the tools problematic but said “AI is a tool that will improve efficiencies and delivering services [sic]” (D’Mello and Callan). Ontario’s Minister of Public and Business Service Delivery and Procurement, Stephen Crawford, made a fair point in response: physicians oversee every aspect of the product. The errors, he said, surfaced during a testing phase meant to catch them before deployment, not during an actual patient visit (“AI Transcriber for Use by Ontario Doctors ‘Hallucinated’”). The point is incomplete, though: finding errors during testing is not itself evidence of failure, because testing exists to expose weaknesses before they reach patients. Still, a test protects patients only when its findings meaningfully affect approval, remediation, or deployment. According to the Auditor General’s office, all twenty approved systems showed at least one inaccuracy at the procurement testing phase, and there was no evidence of additional testing or evaluation at the time of the audit (D’Mello and Callan). Ontario approved them anyway. Physician oversight is also thinner than the minister’s framing suggests: none of the approved systems required doctors to sign off on notes (Office of the Auditor General of Ontario, Use of Artificial Intelligence, sec. 4.3). The test worked. The approval process did not adequately act on what the test revealed.

A related weakness appeared elsewhere. The Investigative Journalism Foundation reported that ADGScribe, an approved vendor, had published testimonials from two physicians who appear to be nonexistent (Vescera and Giancola; Raza and Spithoff). That would be a fabricated source, one that reached physicians who had no reason to doubt it and could shape a purchasing decision through endorsements nobody made. In both cases, approval tells physicians that someone has already tested the source and found it truthful. The dangerous output was not only the inaccurate note or the invented endorsement. It was the institutional signal someone had already checked the system adequately. The physicians who choose one of these vendors trust a process strangers ran, on a product whose failure mode looks like a correctly written note, or a genuine endorsement, until someone checks what was actually said, or who actually said it.

In my earlier piece, I proposed a three-part test for claims like this: Source, Method, and Motive. Each asks a different question when I apply it to a system instead of a sentence. Source asks where a claim originated, not merely that something which sounds official vouches for it: an approved vendor is not the same claim as an accurate transcript, and a doctor’s testimonial is not the same claim as a doctor. Method asks whether the output rests on the underlying evidence: a system capable of producing fluent clinical prose can generate a note that reads coherently even when the recorded conversation does not support parts of it. Motive asks who benefits if readers accept the claim: a vendor benefits from passing procurement and from an unchecked testimonial, and a government has reason to want a burnout-easing program to succeed. Neither interest has anything to do with whether a specific note or endorsement is accurate.

Not everyone who has studied this audit agrees it describes the tools doctors use today. Will Falk, a Toronto-based consulting executive who tracks AI adoption in Canadian health care, argued that the audit evaluated an earlier generation of the vendors’ products, and that today’s scribes have since improved (“Ontario AG Finds Flaws in AI Scribes”). Falk cited figures indicating 28 percent of Canadian physicians use AI scribes and three-quarters of those physicians use them daily, which raises the stakes of judging whether the audited products still match what doctors use now (“Ontario AG Finds Flaws in AI Scribes”). Dr. Paul Forman, a family physician in Markham, Ontario, added a related point: these tools reduce burnout because they work well most of the time, and the honest standard was never perfection. “No matter how advanced a system is, no system can ever claim 100% accuracy or efficiency,” he said (“Ontario AG Finds Flaws in AI Scribes”). The ministry has likewise said about 5,000 physicians use AI scribes, with no known reports of patient harm (“AI Transcriber for Use by Ontario Doctors ‘Hallucinated’”). These objections limit what the audit can tell us about the present accuracy of individual products. They do not answer the audit’s central procurement finding: Ontario approved vendors through a process placing little weight on accuracy and accepting critical assurances without consistent independent verification.

None of this is new, a point worth making to anyone tempted to treat AI Scribe as a uniquely artificial-intelligence failure. Procurement processes have always worked as vouching mechanisms officials run on behalf of people who were not in the room for the testing. A pharmaceutical label, a safety certification, and a credit rating all perform a similar institutional function: they allow people who cannot independently reproduce an evaluation to rely on someone else’s verification. Two family physicians who reviewed the audit for Healthy Debate argued the AI Scribe rollout amounted to a test run for a larger project: a province-wide electronic medical record system that will carry far more patient data than any single transcript (Raza and Spithoff). What AI adds to this older procurement problem is not a new category of risk. It adds scale and fluency to an old one. A fabricated blood-test referral reads exactly like a real one, with none of the tells, misspellings, or implausible claims that once flagged a human transcription error for a second look. The claim sounds no different whether it is true or not.

The Ontario audit demonstrated an old rule of institutional trust: approval is not proof, fluency is not accuracy, and a process protects the public only when someone acts on what the process discovers.
