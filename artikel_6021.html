Overworked? Leaked ATC Schedule Reveals Mandatory Overtime & 10-Hour Shifts

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

Air traffic controller (ATC) work schedules have been scrutinized and show the reality behind the shortage of air traffic controllers in the United States. The schedule, obtained by The Washington Examiner's Anna Giaritelli, noted that controllers are scheduled to report to their facilities every single day for two weeks straight.

These rosters surfaced after the standoff between controllers and the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), where a 2.8% pay rise was approved but stalled by Administrator Bryan Bedford. The roster shows that the controller worked from September 20 to October 3, with the only nominal day off on a Friday, but began on a midnight shift.

With 14 consecutive days on duty, the schedules indicate that the break immediately followed a Saturday night shift, beginning the evening prior at 9:00 PM. This resulted in controllers reporting every calendar day for two weeks.

Within a single week, there is a Rattler Shift, which has controllers starting at 1:00 PM and working mandatory overtime throughout the week. Across other rosters, mandatory overtime turns a five-day standard rotation into a six-day workweek, with backwards rotating start times dropping from afternoon shifts to early mornings within a matter of days.

The shortfall of more than 3,000 controllers needed across the country highlights the deficit, with the staffing levels falling below the 85% minimum threshold at 20 of the 26 busiest airports and facilities.

Chronic understaffing results in long shifts across consecutive days. Pushing controllers to their limit. This has led fatigue rules to mandate a minimum of ten hours off between standard shifts, and 12 hours off before midnight shifts. Despite this, controllers and union leaders have warned that relentless overtime will leave too little time for individuals to rest.

Tensions have continued to strain since the 2.8% pay rise was withheld, with controllers being told to spend at least five hours of an eight-hour shift actively working traffic positions. It has been argued that controllers should spend more time on each shift on radar or tower scopes.

National Air Traffic Controllers Association (NATCA) and controllers have pushed back, noting that the non-scope time is required for mandatory weather briefings, training, and federally mandated recuperative breaks following high-stress radar sessions. Withholding the raise after years of mandatory six-day workweeks has accelerated retirements and attrition in the agency as it struggles to rebuild the workforce.

FAA ties delayed pay to efficiency metrics that demand more on position time amid a 3,000 controller shortfall.

Controller staffing and fatigue can directly shape daily airline reliability and the system's resilience if a disruption hits. At understaffed facilities, an absence of just one or two workers could force the FAA to limit arrival or departure rates at major airports, like New York, Newark, Los Angeles, or Atlanta. These limitations can force ground stops or ground delay programs even on clear-weather days.

The compounding strain during these outages sees the FAA already operating to its limit of staffing capacity and facing legacy systems and aging telecommunications infrastructure that is vulnerable, as highlighted by the Friday nationwide router outage that left airlines grounded across the country, displacing millions, canceling more than 10,000 flights, and leaving airline crew timed out and aircraft stranded. With a 3,000-controller shortfall and a stalled 2.8% raise, airports continue to operate at the limit of required staffing thresholds. The intense work schedules put into question how these professionals continue to work to a high standard in high-pressure environments.

Aging infrastructure, a long-standing deficit, federal government shutdowns, and a rigorous multi-year training pipeline show the vulnerability of the air traffic control system. The massive communications and radar failure on Friday led to the historic nationwide ground stop, which affected millions of passengers. Less than 10% of US airport control towers remain fully staffed.
