Our Editors Ride-Tested Virginia Tech’s Top-Rated Road and Gravel Helmets—Including a $60 Pick That Punches Way Above Its Price

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Lab safety scores matter, but so do fit, ventilation, and price. Here’s how the top lids compare.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

As a cyclist for over three decades, I’ve crashed many times. Unfortunately, I’ve also had a few concussions. Because of this, safety is important to me when I choose a new helmet. Over the last few seasons, I’ve tested dozens of great helmets, from budget picks under $50 to go-fast aero lids and lightweight models for over $500. Plus, I also test trail helmets, commuter lids, and everything in between.

When shopping for new road and gravel helmets, cyclists often base their choices almost entirely on looks, price, and overall comfort. Depending on our riding goals and budget, we also balance weight, aerodynamics, and style. We want safety features, but many riders assume most helmets do a decent job of protecting our noggin—they’re all certified, right?

Helmet safety isn’t as straightforward as it may seem. Current Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) standards—as well as more rigorous, voluntary requirements, such as those from ASTM—account for straight-line impacts and G-forces from a collision. These standards don’t address the rotational forces from a crash that contribute significantly to concussions and traumatic brain injuries. Plus, the CPSC’s standards took effect on March 10, 1999—advancements in technology, design, materials, and manufacturing over the last 27 years have changed bike helmets.

So, what standards and certifications cover rotational forces? Currently, none. To better protect against rotational forces and reduce concussions, many brands incorporate Mips liners (and similar anti-rotational designs) into their helmets. Brands also test their helmets internally to their own, generally unpublished, criteria. The Virginia Tech helmet lab’s STAR score provides the best independent testing and ranking for helmets across most cycling disciplines.

Virginia Tech tests helmets for safety; it doesn’t evaluate fit, comfort, weight, price, or overall practicality. Bicycling’s test team collectively rides tens of thousands of miles each year on tarmac, gravel roads, singletrack, and commuting. That means we have a lot of time to ride-test lids—including more than half of Virginia Tech’s 5-STAR-rated road helmets.

For 15 years, Virginia Tech has tested helmets through a battery of 24 impact tests that analyse safety performance and assign an overall STAR (Summation of Tests for the Analysis of Risk) score.

“The overall score estimates the number of concussions the average person would see if they experienced identical impacts at rates matching their weightings while cycling,” says Virginia Tech’s lab. The lower the STAR score, the better a helmet performed in testing.

Then, to each helmet it tests, Virginia Tech awards an overall STAR rating (one through five); helmets with four- and five-STARs are “recommended.” The lab does not recommend helmets scoring three STARs or fewer.

Virginia Tech has tested and published results on more than 300 bicycle helmets. To date, the lab has awarded 56 helmets its coveted 5-STAR rating, and another 104 received 4 STARs. To make researching and choosing a new helmet easier, it also sorts the helmets it tests into mountain, road, urban, multi-sport, full-face, and E-bike categories. Of the 5-STAR helmets, 21 of them are road lids.

“Who is Pikio?” and “What is a Si Oblik?” Those were the questions our editors all asked when we saw this helmet rocket to the top of Virginia Tech’s STAR ratings. The Pikio isn’t only the top-rated road helmet; it’s also Virginia Tech’s best-performing helmet, period. It beats the others by a considerable margin, logging an unbelievably low 4.61 STAR score. For context, the difference between the Pikio and the second-place overall Canyon Deflectr RLS mountain bike helmet is 2.21 points; that’s the same gap between the 2nd and 28th positions in current rankings.

All of that safety comes at a relatively steep $379 cost. While the Si Oblik’s price aligns with other range-topping helmets from Giro, POC, Specialized, and MET, Pikio’s looks and finish feel comparable to mid-tier models from more well-known brands. For example, Pikio’s color options are somewhat conservative choices, and the rear strap anchor point is through-shell.

The Si Oblik fits well and has a broad range of adjustment for my medium-sized head. The rear adjusts up and down (to account for head shape and hairstyles) and features a dial to microadjust fit. The straps, however, are not as cleanly executed as expected for the price, especially compared to POC or Specialized, and I found them slightly tedious to adjust. On road and gravel rides, the Si Oblik’s airflow doesn’t feel bad, but I didn’t test this lid in peak summer heat. And while the Pikio weighs about the same as Giro and MET helmets in this test, it feels physically bigger, though not as mushroomy as the POC Cytal.

For a new brand, Pikio Labs made a solid first effort. Unlike its competitors, Pikio doesn’t have decades of trial and error or a flashy WorldTour team. Hopefully, as the company grows, it can dial in details, sharpen pricing, and spice up the aesthetic, because the Si Oblik already sets a very high bar for safety.

The POC Cytal’s unique shape and bold look make it easy to spot in a peloton. Another reason the Cytal stands out is due to its overall size—it’s a physically big lid. But despite its dimensions and slightly heavy weight, it doesn’t feel that large on your head while riding and ventilates surprisingly well.

The Cytal’s great ventilation comes from its massive vents and internal air channels that funnel air directly over your forehead while riding. I found out how well it vents while riding over the summer in France during “la canicule” (the heatwave). I could still feel the Cytal pushing air in 100-degree temps, even if it was roasting hot.

In our recent review of P2R’s $100 aero-helmet, ride tester Tom Messina said it “has no business being this good.” And with its lower price and STAR score, we say the same thing about P2R’s $60 A20 Mips. With its excellent safety rating, great adjustability, and budget-friendly price, the A20 Mips outperforms many helmets that cost three, four, or even five times its price.

P2R somehow hits the A20’s low price without cutting corners on features. It has nice straps with adjustable quick clamps that offer plenty of adjustment. P2R also includes a USB-rechargeable light that magnetically clips into the rear vent for commuting or extra visibility on the road.

The one downside we found with the A20 Mips is that it feels optimized for rounder head shapes. While it fits me well, a couple of others who tried it on said it was a little snug near their temples.

Given the Trenta 3K Carbon’s pedigree, it’s hard not to name it “Best for Racing.” UAE Team Emirates’ official sponsor is MET, and the Trenta is one of the helmets it supplies to the squad—including Tour de France champion Tadej Pogačar and World Champion Brandon McNulty. While UAE riders often race the Italian brand’s Manta aero helmet, they sometimes pick the lightweight, well-ventilated Trenta 3K Carbon for climbing stages and training.

Unlike some modern helmets with similarly strong protection, the Trenta feels light and racey on your head, and almost disappears when you’re riding. The straps offer good adjustability, and the Mips Air Node liner protects without feeling bulky. However, the Trenta’s biggest drawback is its price tag. At over $500, this is an expensive piece of kit.

Ranked 14th overall and 5th in road helmets, Giro’s Aries Spherical stands out in our testing for its overall comfort, adjustability, low weight, and great looks. While its STAR score is much higher than the first-place Pikio, the Aries is a very safe helmet and received Virginia Tech’s 5-STAR rating. Having crashed on the road while wearing an Aries Spherical, I also know this helmet works.

Of the approximately one dozen road and gravel helmets in my rotation at any given time, I reach for the Aries Spherical most often. The Aries fits my head perfectly, and the Roc Loc dial system offers plenty of adjustability for hair length or if I’m wearing a cap underneath in rain or cold weather. Giro makes the Aries in a wide range of colors and graphics to fit almost any rider’s personality or fashion.
