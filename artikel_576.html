Einstein’s relativity just upended a fundamental rule of chemistry

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

A discovery by chemists at Brown University is challenging a basic principle of chemical bonding that has appeared in textbooks for generations. The researchers have found direct experimental evidence that triple bonds involving heavy elements do not always behave according to the familiar rules taught in chemistry classes.

The study, published in the journal Science, reveals that Einstein's theory of relativity can fundamentally alter the structure of chemical bonds when atoms become sufficiently heavy. In particular, the researchers found that two types of bonds normally treated as distinct can begin to merge, creating a structure that conventional chemistry models do not fully describe.

Using a powerful analytical technique called photoelectron spectroscopy, the Brown team identified the unmistakable signature of these relativistic effects in bonds formed between carbon and bismuth, a heavy metallic element.

"This idea that relativity is important in heavy elements has been around since the 1970s," said Lai-Sheng Wang, a professor of chemistry at Brown and the study's corresponding author. "But we show direct spectroscopic evidence that what we learned in high school about chemical bonding isn't true in heavy elements."

Why Einstein's Relativity Changes Chemical Bonds

To understand why the discovery matters, it helps to look at how chemical bonds normally form.

Atoms can connect by sharing electrons, the negatively charged particles that surround their positively charged nuclei. In a simple covalent bond, each atom contributes an electron to a shared pair. The attraction between these negatively charged electrons and the two positive nuclei helps hold the atoms together.

Some atoms share multiple pairs of electrons, producing double or triple bonds. According to the standard textbook model, a triple bond consists of three separate connections: one sigma bond and two pi bonds.

The sigma bond is typically the strongest. It forms through a direct, "head-on" overlap of electron orbitals along the axis connecting the two atomic nuclei. The other two connections are pi bonds, which form through weaker, "side-by-side" overlaps on either side of that axis.

For lighter elements, this familiar description works well. However, the situation becomes more complicated farther down the periodic table.

Heavy elements contain nuclei with large numbers of protons, creating powerful electrical attractions that can accelerate nearby electrons to a substantial fraction of the speed of light. At these extreme speeds, effects predicted by Einstein's theory of relativity become important, changing how the electrons behave.

One particularly important effect is known as spin-orbit coupling. Electrons possess a quantum property called spin, which gives them a magnetic character often described as pointing up or down. Under ordinary conditions, spin and orbital motion can be treated as largely separate properties. But in heavy atoms, relativistic effects can link them strongly together.

That connection changes how electrons interact and disrupts the clear distinction between sigma and pi bonds that traditional chemistry relies on.

"The boundary between a sigma bond and a pi bond is now sort of smeared," Wang said. "We still have three bonds, but we don't really strictly have a sigma or a pi anymore."

Scientists Find Direct Evidence in Bismuth Bonds

To test whether this unusual bonding behavior actually occurs, Wang's research group, led by Brown Ph.D. students Deniz Kahraman and Jie Hui, created molecules containing carbon and bismuth.

Bismuth sits next to lead on the periodic table and is among the heavier elements in which relativistic effects are expected to play a major role. That made it an especially useful candidate for investigating whether the familiar rules of triple bonding begin to break down.

The researchers cooled the molecules to temperatures approaching absolute zero, the lowest temperature theoretically possible. They then examined the molecules using photoelectron spectroscopy, a technique that uses laser light to eject electrons from molecules.

By measuring the energy of the released electrons, scientists can determine how tightly those electrons were held in place. This provides a detailed picture of the electronic structure responsible for chemical bonding.

The results revealed a striking departure from the conventional model.

Rather than showing the expected combination of one sigma bond and two pi bonds, the carbon-bismuth triple bonds displayed a different arrangement. Their electronic structure was more consistent with one pi bond and two hybrid sigma-pi bonds.

In other words, Einstein's relativity had changed the nature of the chemical connections, blending two traditionally distinct types of bonds into something that standard textbook descriptions do not fully capture.

A Discovery That Could Rewrite Chemistry Textbooks

The findings provide direct experimental confirmation of relativistic bonding effects that scientists have discussed for decades. Wang believes this evidence could eventually lead to changes in how chemical bonding is explained in chemistry textbooks, particularly as research involving heavy elements continues to expand.

The implications could also extend beyond fundamental chemistry.

Bismuth has attracted growing scientific interest because it may offer a less toxic alternative to lead in next-generation solar cells. Researchers are also exploring its potential in quantum materials and quantum computing, fields that depend on understanding and controlling the behavior of electrons.

A more accurate picture of how heavy atoms form chemical bonds could help scientists investigate these materials and better understand the properties that make them useful for emerging technologies.

"Maybe this will become the new textbook idea as we are dealing with more and more heavy chemistry of the heavy elements," Wang said.
