Hyperlite Mountain Gear Unbound Ultralight Backpack Review

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

The updated Hyperlite Mountain Gear Unbound is one of the best lightweight backpacks on the market for any adventure. The new Dyneema fabric is extremely durable, and the large side pockets are some of the most functional we’ve ever used.

Large side pockets. These pockets are big enough to fit large bottles and other items within reach, while holding them securely enough for scrambling

Not for everyone. The Unbound misses some features that some hikers require, such as load lifters and a hydration port.

Big improvements to an already great ultralight backpack. New fabric and a better frame elevate the Unbound by boosting durability and reducing weight. Courtesy of the excellent pockets, it might be HMG’s best backpack.

Years ago, I reviewed what was then a brand new addition to the Hyperlite Mountain Gear (HMG) lineup of multi-day backpacks. It was called the Unbound, and although I initially had my doubts, it eventually became my all-time favorite backpack. Although it appeared similar to the brand’s core three packs (the Southwest, Windrider, and Junction), a few small tweaks significantly improved its utility.

Now, Hyperlite has overhauled the Unbound with a new Dyneema fabric and some other design improvements. Changes aren’t always upgrades, of course. Three years after falling in love with the original Unbound, would the 2026 version — with its fancy new fabric, burlier frame, and lower weight — become a new favorite? Let’s see how it all shakes out.

New Dyneema fabric puts durability where it matters, including the bottom of the Unbound.

MSRP: $420 Weight: 29 ounces (size M) Available sizes: XS, S, M, L, T (torso sizes: 15”-21+”) Volume: 55 liters internal, 9 liters external (40L version available) Max. recommended load: 40 pounds Pockets: 1 main compartment, 1+ front mesh, 2 hip-belt pockets, 2 side pockets, bottom mesh Frame: Two contoured aluminum stays Material: Dyneema Woven Composite 3.9 Country of origin: Mexico

The Unbound is designed for thru-hikers and backpackers who want to move efficiently. While HMG’s other backpacks have remained popular on long trails for over a decade and are worth a look, the Unbound refines the long-distance focus. Subtle yet substantial tweaks to HMG’s winning formula unlock significant advantages for those who spend most of the day on their feet, moving forward.

More versatile pockets are the most obvious difference, but flexible compression and the removable hip belt are other improvements. Ultimately, the Unbound aligns with HMG’s philosophy centering on gear that is lightweight, simple, and durable enough to withstand multiple long thru-hikes.

The Unbound was with me for a lot of butt-scooting over granite boulders. Photo cred: aeigenbrot

I would love to be wrapping up a SOBO thru-hike right now, carrying the new Unbound, ripe with a sweaty funk and ringed with salt. But alas, I am at home. Still, I managed to put my backpack through its paces, carrying more weight than a typical ultralight-inclined thru-hiker, and bashing it against a plentitude of rough boulders.

On a peak-bagging trip to the Wind River Range, I loaded it above the maximum recommended 40-pound limit and scrambled across miles of talus and steep boulderfields. It also carried all of my stuff during random Colorado overnighters and several SAR missions. Heavy loads, awkward loads, almost empty, on trail, scrambling, rain, snow, sunshine. It may not have been a long thru-hike, but it’s been a good summer, and the Unbound was with me all the way.

Big side pockets and customizable side compression are two of the Unbound’s distinguishing features. Photo cred: aeigenbrot

Dyneema construction: HMG’s new Dyneema Woven Composite (DWC) is a more durable, yet lighter, reimagining of their classic Dyneema Composite Hybrid (DCH). Hyperlite packs were already on the brawnier side of ultralight, and this new fabric takes it a step further. It’s also waterproof when new and won’t absorb moisture, so your stuff is well protected from the elements. However, it’s probably wise to use a pack liner as extra insurance.

Two-stay frame: Two removable aluminum stays sit vertically on the back panel. These are contoured and help maintain a slight air gap between my mid-back and backpack. Removing them saves roughly 2 ounces. The thin foam sewn into the back panel also adds some rigidity and comfort.

Large side pockets: These two pockets are larger than average and easily accommodate two Smartwater bottles each with room to spare. Heck, two classic Nalgenes will fit. A cinch cord helps keep items secure.

Dual-access mesh pocket: The standard large mesh pocket gets a slight tweak with an additional access point a few inches above the bottom. While still secure, this makes it easier to find small items that may have sunk to the bottom.

Removable hip belt: The Unbound’s hip belt is completely removable yet securely held in place behind the lumbar pad via hook-and-loop. You can shave 6 ounces by leaving it at home, or replace a damaged belt. HMG offers replacements in three different sizes to fit a wider variety of bodies.

Bottom pocket: There is a low-profile pocket on the bottom of the Unbound, made from the same Dyneema stretch mesh as the front mesh pocket. One side is open for easy, pack-on access, while a smaller opening at one corner is ideal for stuffing snack wrappers.

Roll top: This classic design helps seal out the elements and adjust pack volume as it fluctuates during a hike. The extension collar expands for those big food hauls, but rolls down for tidier loads.

Y top strap: Far from unique, yet functional. The adjustable Y strap clips over the top of the pack and is useful for securing bulky items such as a foam sleeping pad, bear canister, or bag of chips.

Adjustable compression: Two daisy chains running vertically in parallel up each side of the Unbound provide numerous attachment points for compression straps and/or straps for lashing bulky items. The included shock cord zig-zags help secure tall items in the side pockets — umbrella, tent poles, trekking poles, etc.

Two frame stays instead of one. That’s an improvement.

The updated Unbound is essentially the same as the original. However, three significant changes distinguish the new from the old. HMG made some other tweaks as well, like different sewing, a new color, and pack shape, and I apologize to the design team for glossing over them, but here are the big ones:

New Dyneema Fabric: In a world where folks get excited about new shoe colors every six months, HMG’s commitment to its classic DCH fabric has been remarkable. During the decade spanning my first thru-hike in 2015 until last year, not much changed with the brand’s famous white backpacks. They remained popular throughout that period, even without jumping on the trends that swept through the UL cottage industry.

However, in 2025, HMG redesigned the Southwest, Windrider, and Junction with a new fabric. Now the Unbound benefits from the same upgrade.

This new Dyneema Woven Composite (DWC) promises improved abrasion resistance, strength, durability, and flexibility, while weighing less than the outgoing DCH. This is achieved partly through 100% Dyneema construction, whereas the old DCH incorporated some polyester. Time will tell if it is a true improvement, but all the data and my experience back up the claims.

More Frame Stays: The original Unbound utilized a single frame stay. The new Unbound has two. Theoretically, this equals greater load-carrying capacity, although the maximum recommended load is still 40 pounds. I was satisfied with the old frame, but I’m happy to have the extra structure.

New shoulder straps: The updated straps are wider and more curved than HMG’s old design — more of an ’S’ shape than before. This promises more comfort for a wider range of body types by distributing the pack load more evenly.

The big side pockets carried a lot of weird stuff on this trip, and it all stayed secure, despite the scrambling. Photo cred: aeigenbrot

As I hoped and expected, the team at Hyperlite has improved the Unbound in meaningful ways without any compromises that I’m aware of. Everything I loved about the original backpack is still there, but with the new fabric, shoulder straps, and stouter frame, the second generation is more comfortable, durable, and capable. It’s also lighter. Who said there’s no free lunch?

That being said, backpacks are highly personal. I love the simplicity of the Unbound and similar packs, but if you want more than the most basic features, then you might want to keep looking. The Unbound is designed to excel at a specific purpose.

Without casting shade on the fancy new fabric (which I do like a lot), the excellent side pockets have been and remain the Unbound’s defining feature. I loved them on the original Unbound, and I love them now.

Many other perfectly good backpacks fall short with side pockets that are either too small, difficult to reach, or insecure (including other HMG packs). I hate struggling to squeeze a bottle into an unforgivingly tight pocket, and I think it’s ridiculous that some are only large enough to fit narrow Smartwater bottles. What about my pot? What about my large-capacity water bottle? How about my tent poles, fuel canister, or umbrella? Just give me space to jam all my weirdly sized crap, dammit.

The Unbound does give me that space. My weirdly sized crap jams in nicely, and a cinch cord keeps everything secure. All side pockets should be exactly like these.

The Unbound is as comfortable as any other lightweight pack that I’ve owned. It may not have trampoline-like suspension, but it holds loads well and resists barreling. The foam back panel also did a good job of protecting my back from weird points and metal climbing hardware when I got lazy with my packing.

While more cushioned shoulder straps and hip belts exist, the Unbound’s were adequately supportive, even when my pack hovered around 40 pounds. I can’t say that the new wider and differently shaped shoulder straps were obviously better, but they did the job without drawing attention.

Finally, the new DWC fabric is nicer to the touch than DCH. It’s more flexible and less crinkly, although no one will mistake it for velvet.

The bottom of the Unbound is DWC, putting durability where it’s needed most. The fabric is mostly fine after a punishing trip, but there are some scuffed fibers.

Combining my experience carrying the new Unbound, the old Unbound, and other Hyperlite backpacks, I’m confident that durability is not an issue. I never had any problems with abrasion or punctures in the past, and the new DWC fabric has survived plenty of abuse.

During my trip to the Winds, I banged and scraped the bottom of the side pockets across countless granite boulders without springing a leak. While the upper portion of each pocket is made from Dyneema Gridstop, the DWC is wisely deployed on the bottom where it is most needed. If not for this, I’m certain that there would be several holes.

In short, I was impressed with DWC, and putting it on the bottom of the pockets was a hero move by HMG. The Unbound should easily survive most thru-hikes and high routes.

The volume difference comes from the extension collar. Seen here, unrolled above the shoulders.

The Unbound is available in both 40- and 55-liter volumes. I typically prefer 40-liter backpacks. The smaller capacity keeps me honest. However, I opted for the 55-liter version this time for greater versatility above and beyond what I might need for a three-season thru-hike (i.e., winter trips, mountaineering, SAR, etc.).

Regardless of the pack volume, the dimensions are the same except for the height and top width of the extension collar. In other words, the 55L Unbound is the same as the 40L version, but with a taller roll-top. This extra fabric is easy to roll away and adds roughly 1.5 ounces to the total weight.

The way I see it, the extra weight is a fair price to pay for extra versatility, and it’s already come in handy on a few occasions. As always, I recommend considering your own needs honestly based on the expected usage and the rest of your gear list.

Both the Unbound 40L and 50L can handle your standard ~11-liter bear canister like most other backpacks in this category. Pack it inside vertically, or use the long Y-strap to secure it outside, on top.

While I don’t have anything to complain about, I would love to try a version of the Unbound that incorporates running-vest-style shoulder straps with even more pockets for snacks and smaller items. I believe this would align with the intended use quite well by keeping high-use essentials within reach, enabling longer distances between breaks. Sure, you can add shoulder pockets or wear a fanny pack, but running-vest utility is undeniable.

DWC kept the contents of my Unbound dry during a morning of rain. I still used a pack liner, though. Photo cred: aeigenbrot

Side pockets: Goodness gracious, all side pockets should be like the ones on the Unbound. Dare I say that they’re perfect? They hold a ton of stuff, yet remain secure and easy to access while wearing the pack for folks with average shoulder mobility. And having DWC on the bottom of each pocket puts durability exactly where it is needed most. Seriously, the Unbound’s side pockets have spoiled me. I now begrudge packs that I used to love because of their inferior pockets.

Removable hip belt: All bodies are different, and all bodies change, especially over the course of a long thru-hike. It might not be convenient to purchase a new hip belt, but at least the option is there. It’s also nice to ditch the hip belt sometimes without taking scissors to your fancy backpack.

Dyneema: The new DWC is a great backpack material. It’s plenty durable for most adventures yet is lightweight and weather resistant. Dyneema is a cornerstone of all HMG designs, and it’s hard to argue with that choice.

Updates: The new Unbound’s updates make a pack that I already loved even better. The fabric is tougher, lighter, and more flexible. The extra frame stay boosts carrying comfort. The new shoulder straps should fit more people comfortably. The old, single-stay frame was maybe the one reason to choose HMG’s other packs instead of the Unbound. That’s not a thing anymore.

Lightweight: Even with the extra frame stay, the Unbound is lighter than before. And it is still the lightest of HMG’s popular thru-hiking backpacks. There are backpacks out there that weigh less, don’t get me wrong, but the Unbound is a lot of backpack for under two pounds.

Dyneema fabric is awesome, but it is pricey.

No load lifters: HMG has never included these useful straps on their core backpacks, but that doesn’t mean I don’t miss them. Running from the top of the shoulder straps to the top of the back panel, they can lift some of the load off the shoulders and also adjust how close the pack conforms to the back. It’s possible that the Unbound’s frame isn’t compatible with this system, and that by keeping weight low you don’t need them. However, if you want load lifters, look elsewhere.

No hydration port: If you prefer drinking from a straw instead of bottles, then you’re out of luck. I could not find a hydration port on the Unbound, even though some of HMG’s other backpacks do have one.

Expensive: While the Unbound is priced similarly to other Dyneema backpacks, it still costs significantly more than non-Dyneema peers. For the upcharge, you get better durability for a similar weight and more weather resistance. A fair tradeoff, but the Unbound is not a budget pick.

Without knowing what lies ahead, it’s nice to be confident that the Unbound can handle it. Photo cred: aeigenbrot

The updated HMG Unbound is easily my favorite backpack. If I truly need to haul a gargantuan load, then I’ll reach for something with a more substantial frame. However, for all other applications, the Unbound has me covered.

Where the new version rises above the old is in the new DWC material and double-stay frame. These are significant improvements that increase durability and functionality while reducing weight. They also bring the Unbound up to spec with HMG’s flagship thru-hiking backpacks, which were first in line for the DWC upgrade and always had two frame stays.

Honestly, I now cannot conjure a coherent argument in favor of the Southwest, Windrider, or Junction over the Unbound. Maybe I’m missing something, so please let me know if you disagree.

Regardless, the Unbound is worth considering if you’re in the market for a new backpack. It’s simple yet functional. It’s lightweight and durable. Whether it’s a high route, long trail, or weekend getaway, this pack is ready to go wherever you want to carry it.
