Tempers flare as future of Social Security debated

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Sen. Ron Johnson, R-Wis., said he wants to fix Social Security, while also calling it a "Ponzi scheme." (Tom Williams/CQ Roll Call)

If Congress ever tries to fix Social Security to address a looming shortfall, a Senate Budget Committee hearing Wednesday showed how poisonous the politics could become.

Budget Chairman Ron Johnson, R-Wis., set the stage for a fierce partisan clash even before the hearing began by calling Social Security a "Ponzi scheme" in a Fox Business interview earlier in the day. Democrats erupted, accusing Johnson of scaring seniors and being unwilling to fix a financial lifeline for more than 72 million retirees.

“I think it’s outrageous,” said Sen. Mark Warner, D-Va., one of several Democrats on the panel who slammed Johnson for his comments. “Don’t go out there scaring Americans to make political points on Fox News. This is too fricking important.”

Johnson acknowledged making an “imprecise comment” on prospects for a fix during the Fox interview. But he also told Democrats that if they had read his “chairman’s memo” he sent them Tuesday they would know he wants to preserve the system and pass a law to prevent any cuts to benefits if the Social Security Trust Fund is depleted.

"This is a scam, it’s grotesque, and at this point there’s really no way to fix Social Security," he said on Fox Business. "It’s a Ponzi scheme. You can’t fix it. You just shut it down and hope you can recover some of your money.”

But Johnson appeared to contradict himself later in the same interview, saying there is a difference with a “government-sanctioned Ponzi scheme” in that “you can force investors to keep putting money into it and that’s what we’ll probably have to do … You can fix Social Security and make it solvent.”

In his opening statement at the hearing, Johnson said Congress should pass a law to prevent benefit cuts and then “roll up our sleeves and begin seriously addressing the main problem, our massive debt and deficits.” He said that is the only way to “ensure we have the financial wherewithal to honor promises made to seniors.”

Johnson also told the panel he used the term “Ponzi scheme” in part to draw attention to the importance of addressing the problem. “If I gotta pay a political price for that, I really don’t care,” he said.

There is no doubt that a fix is needed. Without any change, Social Security recipients face a 22 percent cut in benefits by the end of 2032, according to projections from the Social Security Board of Trustees. Johnson said Congress and the president won’t cut benefits even if the trust fund is exhausted.

And there has been no shortage of Social Security bills introduced to provide a fix, but none of them has gained momentum.

Sen. Bernie Moreno, R-Ohio, drew flak from fellow Republicans after he and Sen. Elizabeth Warren, D-Mass., proposed lifting the cap on income subject to the Social Security payroll tax so that the tax would apply to all earned income.

A bipartisan proposal, from Sens. Richard J. Durbin, D-Ill., Bill Cassidy, R-La., Tim Kaine, D-Va., and John Cornyn, R-Texas, would have put a plan in motion by requiring the Social Security Advisory Board to recommend a bill that would ensure Social Security is funded for at least the next 50 years.

Senators on both sides of the aisle said they were committed to fixing Social Security. Republicans stressed the importance of economic growth to bring in more revenue to the system and cautioned that tax increases would slow growth.

Democrats pitched shifting more revenue into the system, for example by raising the cap on the payroll tax or taxing other income.

“I do believe if we can be focused on fixing the Social Security stability issue, that could be a stepping stone to fixing broader problems,” said Kaine, who is anticipated to chair the Budget Committee when Democrats take over Congress next year.
