AI may own the start of the trip, but Booking.com is betting trust and payments decide who gets the booking. Its experience with superapps, where daily use never became travel spending, suggests generative chatbots may face the same problem.

Booking.com is okay with travelers starting their trip planning with AI. It just wants to be the platform to close things.

Travelers are increasingly researching trips with AI, but that doesn’t mean the booking follows. Booking.com frames its challenge in Asia-Pacific as staying relevant at the moment people are ready to pay, wherever they may begin.

A Booking.com survey conducted earlier this year found that 64% of travelers in the region use AI to plan trips. Yet Booking Holdings, its parent company, has said recently that traffic referred directly by large language models is rare, representing well under 1% of room nights.

Laura Houldsworth, Booking.com’s vice president for Asia-Pacific, told Skift the gap exists because travelers don’t stay in one chatbot. “They might be searching on Claude one minute, ChatGPT another, Gemini, then rounding out with Google, and then coming to us directly,” she said. Booking.com's Asia-Pacific chief, Laura Houldsworth, told Skift the company is comfortable with travelers starting trip research inside AI chatbots as long as the booking still closes on Booking.com. A Booking.com survey earlier this year found 64% of travelers in Asia-Pacific use AI to plan trips, while parent Booking Holdings has said traffic referred directly by large language models accounts for well under 1% of room nights. Houldsworth attributes the gap to travelers moving between Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, and Google before coming to Booking.com directly, so no single assistant gets credit. Her argument is that trust decides where the money lands — 400 million reviews, secure payments, and customer service that chatbots don't offer. The same logic shapes her view of superapps: daily usage hasn't translated into big-ticket travel spend, so Booking.com is plugging into platforms travelers already use rather than competing with them, starting with WhatsApp as a booking channel in India. Houldsworth also called intra-Asia the fastest-growing travel corridor in the world, with domestic room nights in Asia growing at a low double-digit rate against mid-single-digit growth companywide, which has cushioned softness in the Europe–Asia corridor tied to Middle East conflict. Alternative accommodation is about 37% of global room nights, with regulation the main risk, and the connected trip strategy depends on local payments landing first.
