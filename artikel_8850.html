Our Editors Rode Dozens of Electric Bikes. These 14 Are Worth Buying for Every Budget, From Under $1,000 to Premium

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Find the right e-bike for your budget, riding style, and storage needs with expert-tested picks.

Fall is our favorite season for riding bike paths, rail trails, streets, and singletrack. Whether they’re commuting to work or school, running errands, or spending time with family and friends, more people than ever are choosing electric bikes for their rides. E-bikes make cycling easier, less sweaty, and more fun.

I’m a former product director for bike companies and a shop mechanic. I’m also a lifelong cyclist and ride dozens of electric bikes each year. I use them for everything from grocery runs to concerts and even going to the airport. This summer, I went almost entirely car-free thanks to an electric cargo bike.

Whether you’re picking your first electric bicycle, upgrading to a newer model, or adding another e-bike to your garage, dozens of great options are available—and our editors and staff have tested many of them. No matter your age, cycling ability, or budget, brands offer e-bikes that meet needs.

Tip for New Buyers: Don’t overpay for an electric bike, but spend what you can comfortably afford. If an e-bike is fun to ride, you’ll use it more often. It’s more cost-effective to buy an e-bike equipped the way you want it upfront than to upgrade it later. Also, remember: you cannot change an electric bike’s motor system if you decide you want more power.

The Aventon Level is our benchmark electric bike. It’s a great e-bike for commuting, running errands, or leisure riding. Over several years, the Level platform has proven to be reliable and low-maintenance. Plus, its price doesn’t break the bank.

Aventon split the 4th-generation Level into two models. The REC features a 750W hub motor with 80 Nm of torque. Aventon also added regenerative braking, increasing the bike’s range up to 75 miles. The pricier ADV edition features a mid-drive motor; it bumps up the torque to 100 Nm and range to 110 miles. Both versions come standard with fenders, a rear rack, lights, and suspension.

Another feature users will find quite useful is the Level’s connected features. The Level 4’s ACU (Aventon Control Unit) adds several smart features to the already well-equipped platform. The wheel lock and motion sensor alarm are passive theft deterrents when you stop for a mid-ride coffee or run into the post office. A 4G chip in the ACU enables GPS tracking in Aventon’s mobile app, so you can locate your bike if you forget where you locked it. 4G connectivity also allows you to set geofence restrictions on the bike for teens or housemates.

This relatively affordable seven-speed model from Aventon delivers simplicity and style that is tough to beat. It’s even better than the original version, thanks to hydraulic disc brakes. A push of the throttle gets you started; then, the brushless hub motor gently assists your pedaling.

Along with disc brakes, the Soltera features integrated lights and turn signals, and the battery is hidden in the bike’s frame (rather than the bolt-on batteries found on many similarly-priced e-bikes). Best for smoother roads and bike paths, the Soltera delivers the essential features a city rider needs in a strikingly light 46-pound package.

Aventon also offers a singlespeed, Soltera 3 ADV with a Gates belt-drive system.

Tern’s cargo bikes are the gold standard for practical features, durability, and ride quality. This reputation makes the brand’s GSD (longtail) and HSD (short-tail) cargo models top picks for bike commuters, families, and riders looking to cut their dependence on automobiles.

The new third-generation HSD improves on its predecessor’s legacy. The HSD (short for “Haul Stuff Daily”) is a shrunken-down version of Tern’s GSD3 model released last year. The HSD3’s frame has a 419-pound carrying capacity (combined bike, rider, and cargo), added stiffness, and improved stability at speed compared to its predecessor. The P10e Sport configuration features the peppier Bosch Performance Line Sport motor and an electronic-shifting ten-speed TRP E.A.S.I. A10 drivetrain. The Sport motor’s 28mph assistance was a welcome upgrade for commuting and running errands alongside car traffic on local suburban streets. The 90 Nm of torque (15 Nm more than the standard Performance Line motor) also provides quicker acceleration from a dead stop and a little more oomph when loaded down with cargo or climbing hills.

While it’s the second-most affordable HSD3 model in Tern’s U.S. selection, the P10e Sport is certainly not a budget-priced e-bike. But for the extra cost over similar-sized cargo bikes, you get a proven and well-thought-out bicycle. The HSD’s features and design elements feel purposeful. A great example of this is the bike’s stowability: The HSD3 can be tilted and stored vertically, balanced on its rear rack. It also moves easily on its rear wheel when you need to park or store it. The handlebar and stem also fold down, making the HSD3 much easier to transport by car or SUV than almost any other e-bike.

For big city life, a simple bike that’s low-cost, easy to repair, and reliable usually beats out complicated designs. Lectric’s XP Lite2 is about as basic as it gets. It has a low-maintenance singlespeed drivetrain, a 300-watt hub motor, and a 375Wh battery. With its compact size, 20x2.5-inch tires, and twist-grip throttle, the XP Lite is perfect for zipping through traffic, commuting to the office, or heading to the gym or bar after work.

The XP Lite2 features only one gear and a 300W motor, so it’s not ideal for very hilly terrain. But with the throttle, you can get over small rises and bridges. Lectric also equips the bike with hydraulic disc brakes and front and rear lights. The XP Lite2 is also great for RVers and campgrounds, since it’s lightweight (about 48 pounds) and folds down to 36x16x27-inch dimensions.

It’s hard to beat the Portola Lite for the price. Ride1Up recently refreshed its mighty mini, and it has everything you need for quick commutes: power, speed, comfort, and portability. The Portola’s ride quality is premium, even though it costs thousands less than some high-end folding electric bikes. The bike is also solid: We’ve ridden the Portola for several hundred miles, and the only maintenance it has needed is air in the tires and chain lube. Runner’s World’s Runner-in-Chief Jeff Dengate has been testing the Portola for several months and notes, “It’s the perfect size for city commuting and doesn’t take up space in my garage when I’m not riding it.”

The updated Portola features a 750-watt motor with 90 Nm of torque; enough juice to ride even the steepest hills around town. Other changes over the previous generation model include integrated turn signals, dual-drive sensor pedal assist (cadence and torque), and Apple FindMy integration. The Portola Lite comes with a 100mm travel suspension fork, lights, and fenders. It also features upgraded hydraulic disc brakes—not found on every e-bike in this price range—which make stopping the 61-pound bike much safer.

Ride1Up offers the bike in four colors with two battery options. The 10aH (480Wh) internal battery gets up to 60 miles of range, but if you need more range or weigh over 200 pounds, opt for the 15aH (720Wh) battery for only $100 more.

Many “step-through” e-bikes don’t actually have a low step design. This makes it challenging for some riders to mount or dismount. However, the CTY e2.1, by REI’s Co-op house brand, has a fully step-through front triangle. The CTY is a great choice for older riders, those with reduced mobility, or others who seek an easy-to-ride e-bike.

REI equips the CTY e2.1 with a 250-watt hub-drive motor that provides smooth pedal-assistance up to 20 mph. It also features a suspension fork to smooth out bumps and potholes, an adjustable stem for a comfortable riding position, and hydraulic disc brakes for better stopping. For less than the cost of some e-bikes online, you can get a quality electric bike backed up by REI warranty and service.

The Current ADV is the priciest bike in this story, but for a good reason. Some electric bikes look a lot like mountain bikes, but that doesn’t mean they are built for real off-road use. Aventon’s new Current is the brand’s first full suspension E-MTB, and it is trail-ready right out of the box. Trevor Raab, Bicycling’s Senior Photographer and Test Team member, said, “The bike’s intuitive ride feel will please experienced mountain bikers and also benefit first-time mountain bikers.”

The Current ADV features an aluminum frame with 140mm rear wheel travel and a great-feeling motor with 750 peak Watts and 110 Nm of torque. (It also has a Boost mode that bumps power to 850W and 120 Nm of torque.) Rounding out the ADV are high-end components like a RockShox Psylo Gold fork, SRAM DB8 brakes, SRAM Eagle 70 Mechanical transmission, and a tubeless wheelset with Maxxis Minion EXO tires.

In his full review of the bike, “Aventon Goes Full Performance: The Current Electric Mountain Bike Is Surprisingly Dialed,” Raab particularly likes the Current’s easy-to-use interface. It’s loaded with ride data as well as the ability to tune the motor settings. If you don’t need rear suspension, check out Aventon’s updated Ramblas ADV hardtail E-MTB.

Getting into E-MTB trail riding on a budget is tough. Low-priced electric mountain bikes often aren’t built strong enough for off-road riding (or weigh half a rider’s weight), and trail-capable models typically cost more than $4,000. That’s what makes the TrailRush a standout bike—for less than $2,000, you get a trail-ready hardtail you can take on singletrack and have some fun.

Ride1Up equips the 29-inch-wheeled TrailRush with a Brose mid-drive motor. With 90 Nm of torque, it isn’t as powerful as many new, higher-priced E-MTB motors, but it gets the job done. The TrailRush also comes stock with a dropper seatpost, four-piston disc brakes, and tubeless-compatible rims with Maxxis Minion tires.

Of course, to hit this great price, Ride1Up made some equipment sacrifices. The 504Wh battery isn’t as large as some peers (like the Aventon Ramblas), so you get less ride time between charges. Additionally, the 11-36T cassette and 38T chainring limit your ability to climb steep hills.

Compared to earlier iterations of the Aventure, the third-generation model has a smoother ride and feels more like a premium bike (without the high price tag). Aventon’s new torque sensor allows more control over the acceleration of Aventure’s 750W rear hub motor. Lighter-weight testers found earlier models had too much torque, but this Aventure remedied that fault. Additional features include a new head-unit interface, integrated turn signals, a front light, a rear rack, and fenders.

The Aventure 3 is an excellent fat-tire e-bike for commuters—especially on snowy days and gravel paths. Off-roading, it excels on doubletrack trails and cinder paths, but with this burly bike’s weight and components, it has limited functionality on singletrack and more aggressive mountain trails.

Aventon also offers the Aventure M, a mid-drive version of the Aventure. It keeps the same big, burly character but adds a more premium feel, a smoother, more powerful motor, and an upgraded drivetrain—along with a higher price. Those refinements cost about $1,000 more than the Aventure 3.

“Lightweight e-bike” might sound like an oxymoron to some, but a sub-40-pound weight is impressive when many electric bikes exceed 60 or 80 pounds. And often, e-bikes this light are pricey. Yet, the Velotric Tempo is only $1,499 and boasts a 39 lb. (claimed) weight.

Velotric achieves this weight reduction by ditching the bulky suspension fork (suspension forks on lower-priced bikes often don’t work all that well, to be frank), swapping heavy tires for 700x42c gravel tires, and using a smaller battery. Riders who need to carry the bike up any stairs or load it onto a bike rack will appreciate the reduced weight. However, heavier riders may want to test ride the Tempo before purchase, as lower-powered motors can bog down and reduce battery range for bigger-bodied people.

Many step-through electric bikes aren’t low enough for riders to easily get on or dismount. However, Heybike’s Venus features a very low-step frame that’s similar to designs used on city bikes. The Venus also has a 90-degree adjustable stem, so it’s comfortable to ride. At only 50 pounds, this e-bike is light enough for many riders to move around or carry up stairs if needed, and its 27.5” x 2.2” tires roll smoothly over pavement and crushed stone paths.

Electric bike prices range from entry-level models for less than $500 to premium machines costing almost as much as a good used car. However, our years of testing have proven that many of the best and most practical models live squarely in the middle.

We find that many standout e-bikes are priced between $1,500 and $3,000. These models strike a good balance between reliability and practicality, without requiring you to overspend on features or technology you don’t need. Electric bikes costing less than $1,000 often take shortcuts to achieve this, either by using lower-quality parts or offering limited service after the bike is purchased.

Of course, you can also spend much more on electric bikes. Premium e-bikes typically offer improved motor performance, higher-quality components, lower weight, or more tech features. However, for most general riding purposes, these bikes are overkill—but they‘re certainly fun!

There are three electric-assist classes available in the United States, and the use of each class of bike can vary by state, city, and area you are riding. Be sure to check local regulations before buying and before riding your bike.

Class 1: 20 mph max assist speed; throttle is not standard equipment.

Class 2: 20 mph max assist speed; throttle comes standard equipment.

Class 3: 28 mph max assist speed; throttle is not standard equipment

What this means: Class 1 motors kick in only when you pedal and stop helping you at 20 mph. Class 2 also offers pedal assist up to 20 mph, but these bikes can also have a throttle-powered mode that allows users to engage the motor without pedaling. Class 3 is pedal-assist like Class 1, but helps you until you reach 28 mph.

Other countries classify electric bikes differently, and many do not allow e-bikes to assist riders beyond 20 miles per hour.

Electric bikes generally feature one of two types of motors: mid-drive motors, which are contained in the frame at the bottom of the bike between the pedals; and rear hub motors, which are built into the rear wheel.

Bikes with rear hub motors are generally less expensive but are also less powerful. Mid-drive e-bikes tend to be more expensive, but are typically more reliable and higher performance. Because the extra weight on a mid-drive motor is centered in the bike and lower to the ground, these bikes are generally easier to handle than bikes with hub motors.

Measured in Newton meters (or Nm), torque is a rotational measurement of force—and the number to pay attention to when you want an idea of an e-bike motor’s output. More torque means more power off the line and more boost to your pedaling.

The heavier the bike, the more torque it needs. Lighter road bikes typically have 30 to 40 Nm of torque, and trail and cargo models (generally) have at least 80 Nm. Most commuter bikes fall somewhere in between.

The size of an e-bike’s battery is measured in watt-hours (or Wh). This measurement represents the energy stored in the battery and how many watts it can deliver each hour. The higher the number, the longer the range, but the faster you go, the faster you drain the battery.

So, if a 504Wh battery paired with a 500W motor gives you one hour of ride time at the highest assist, riding at about half that power will double your range.

Many e-bike brands seamlessly integrate batteries to make the bike look sleeker (and more like a traditional non-assist bike). Most batteries lock to the bike and come with a key that lets you unlock and remove it, which serves multiple purposes: You can remove the battery and charge it off the bike, plus a locked battery deters (and hopefully prevents) a thief from stealing it. An e-bike with the battery removed is safer for hauling on a bike rack and lighter for carrying up steps.

Since e-bikes can maintain higher speeds for longer than standard bikes, you want extra control when riding. Wider tires provide better traction and the freedom to leave the pavement with little penalty, and a suspension fork will help tame some of the rougher roads you might explore. Good disc brakes are a must, too, for slowing a heavy bike at high speed. This is not a place to skimp.

Some e-bikes have an integrated lighting system that turns on when you power up the bike. While this is a great feature, it’s not a deal-breaker if your bike doesn’t come equipped this way. It’s just as easy to attach your own since so many great bike lights are available.

E-bikes need regular maintenance and repair to stay in good working condition. Because they’re heavier and go faster than non-assist bicycles, e-bikes often require more regular service on parts like brakes, tires, and drivetrain components. We recommend having a good relationship with a local bike shop experienced in e-bike repair to keep your equipment running smoothly.

E-bikes use electronics for their motors, batteries, and displays. These parts are often proprietary to bike brands or even specific e-bike models, making replacement more difficult than parts on non-electric bicycles. Make sure to use the correct electronic replacement parts to avoid damaging your e-bike (or it catching fire).

Established brands usually (but not always) have a good supply of these parts for replacement, even for years after a bike goes out of production. Bosch and Shimano are two e-bike motor manufacturers with some of the most compatibility between bikes and stock parts for previous generations of systems.

Look for e-bikes from brands that offer at least a one-year warranty on electronic components (many brands’ warranties are longer) and make items like replacement batteries for your bike. These parts are usually not cheap (sometimes up to half the cost of a new bike), but it’s the difference between having an e-bike that can be repaired or becomes a bicycle-shaped paperweight if something goes wrong.

Following a dramatic increase in fires caused by the lithium-ion batteries used in electric bikes, there is a push from local officials, regulatory agencies, and advocacy groups across the U.S. for improved safety certification of e-bikes, batteries, and motor units. In September 2023, a New York City law took effect requiring that any e-bike sold in the city “has been certified by an accredited testing laboratory for compliance with Underwriters Laboratories (UL) standard 2849”.

However, just because something is marketed as UL 2849 compliant, tested to UL 2849, or even “certified to UL 2849” does not mean it is UL Safety Certified. Ibrahim Jilani, UL’s Global Director of Consumer Technology, notes: “Certification is always earned by a manufacturer and not a given when they undergo a product submittal. The UL Mark, or any authorized certification mark, can only be issued upon successful demonstration of meeting the requirements of the safety standard.” You can find UL’s updated list of products certified to UL 2849 here. You can cross-reference OSHA’s Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratories for a deep dive into the topic.
