Up a few concrete steps and through the front door of a 200-year-old row house in New York City, I follow a crowd of people into the 178-seat SoHo Playhouse. As we filter to our seats, ushers hand each person a black drawstring bag with a pair of wired headphones connected to a small Auracast receiver. Then, soon after the play begins, we’re instructed to put them on.

The headphones are an integral storytelling tool of Love Me — a one-man show from the minds of stand-up comedian and former Saturday Night Live writer Drew Michael and director Prashanth Venkataramanujam. It’s a show about love and the bid for connection, told in five chapters about different relationships in Michael’s life, bound by the threads of his hearing loss and self-discovery.

Love Me’s experimental sound design is possible thanks to Auracast, a technology based on Bluetooth that can broadcast a high-quality audio stream to any number of nearby devices. As the show follows Michael from childhood through adulthood, sound designer Daniel Kluger uses the headphones in combination with the theater speakers to create experiences that wouldn’t be possible with a traditional setup. Tinnitus ringing and hearing test tones play in the music between sections as subtle clues as the show builds to Michael’s epiphany about his hearing loss. Kluger also explores the world through varying levels of sonic clarity. Sound playing through the theater’s speakers is sometimes intentionally muffled, which is then further dulled by the headphones, giving the audience a sense of what Michael is hearing in those moments.

But one sound that stays clear and pristine is Michael’s inner monologue, heard in the headphones throughout the show. The inner monologue consists of both prerecorded tracks and the live feed from Michael’s microphone on stage as he talks back and forth with himself. “Any time you’re in my head — anything in the headphones is coming through clearly,” Michael tells me after the show. “[We’re] trying to also highlight that the appeal of the inner world is such that it’s the highest fidelity space for this person. It’s more comfortable.”

The different sound sources and treatments of the sound can lead to intentionally confusing scenes for the audience. During one, where Michael is doing a stand-up set at a comedy club, three sounds compete for attention: The theater speakers play the crowd’s reactions (including a heckler whose voice is sometimes muffled and sometimes clear); his prerecorded inner monologue reacting to the crowd plays in the headphones; and on stage, Michael is performing the set, with his mic muffled so our focus is on his inner monologue. It’s one of the definitive moments where the audience feels the constant concentration necessary to parse what’s happening when sounds aren’t clear and become overwhelming.

But the biggest impact comes during a climactic scene the sound design has been building toward, a realization that also incorporates the visuals of a series of Michael’s audiograms over the years. “I had this massive breakthrough about the whole history of what I’ve been doing — the cognitive labor, the invisible to myself labor that was affecting every single minute of my life,” Michael says. “That was a huge moment.”

That’s when the audience is instructed to remove the headphones we’ve been wearing for nearly the entire show. As Michael comes to the recognition of his relationship with his hearing loss and the clarity of mind that gives him, what we’ve become used to for the entire show’s experience — the headphones — are now gone, as is that literal weight from our heads. It’s an emotional moment, one that I’ve thought about multiple times since seeing the show.

It also leads into the final act, where Michael begins to understand his own Deaf identity. “The deafness is not the culprit, it’s the denial of it.” he tells me. “My hearing currently has never been worse, but I’ve never been more self-affirmed or self-actualized or felt happier in myself. It’s a misconception, I think, about deafness as a tragedy, deafness as a deficit, deafness as a burden, deafness as something that plagues a person.”

The ability of Auracast to broadcast an audio stream that any number of headphones can connect to is what made Love Me a possibility. Any Auracast headphones can connect to the audience broadcast — I brought along the Nothing Pro 1 to test the ability and connected to the audience broadcast channel in seconds — but I used the provided Auri receiver and Austrian Audio headset for the performance. The show’s sound was designed with those in mind, and I didn’t want the differing isolation of the Nothing headphones to affect the intent of the experience.

Love Me is presented in partnership with Deaf West Theatre, which supports Deaf-centered storytelling in theatrical productions. In addition to the audience Auracast feed, there’s one for users of hearing aids and cochlear implants that’s designed for compliance with those devices, and iPads with captions are also available. During the performance, Michael connects to his own broadcast channel with Auracast-enabled Phonak Eon Sphere hearing aids. “We talked about using in-ear monitors, but if I use an in-ear monitor, then I don’t have amplification from my environment, so then I lose contact with the audience,” Michael says. “These function as two things: They’re hearing aids and also in-ear monitors.”

Love Me relies on the Auracast technology to tell its story, but it isn’t a story about hearing technology. The technology is there to tell the story of connection and discovery of identity. “I think right now … there are two things that are at odds — the hearing aid and audiological tech side of deafness and then the cultural identity side of deafness,” Michael says. Technological improvements, like cochlear implants and hearing aids, are cited as one of the reasons schools for the deaf have been on the decline, which leads to kids being mainstreamed (put into regular public schools) and losing out on Deaf history and culture. “I’m not against the tech,” Michael says, “I just think tech and Deaf identity should be a marriage rather than a divide.”

And that collaboration is why Love Me works as well as it does. The Auracast technology is there both for accessibility — in the same way it’s already used in other performances, lectures, or announcement systems — and to uplift the show’s story of love, acceptance, and acknowledgement of one’s own Deaf identity. It’s an inventive use of the technology that carries with it a powerful message.
