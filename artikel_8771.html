The 67-Year-Old Who Raced Two Full Ironmans a Week Apart

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Mary Mitchell won her age group in Kona and eight days later raced Ironman California, where she also won her age group. Here's how she did it. (Photo: Mary Mitchell, FinisherPix)

Fresh off a sixth-place finish at the Ironman World Championship in Kona, American professional triathlete Sam Long recently took to Instagram to ask his followers a question. Should he race Ironman Florida on November 7, or give himself another two weeks to recover and race Ironman Cozumel on November 22?

Long wants to secure his qualification for the 2027 Ironman World Championship early, but the question of how quickly to return to racing after Kona is a serious one. Among the responses to his post were plenty of suggestions that he take the extra recovery time and race Cozumel instead.

After all, Kona is no ordinary Ironman. Its combination of heat, humidity, unpredictable winds and exposed terrain takes a major physical toll, even on athletes who have spent their careers preparing for those conditions. Racing another Ironman just four weeks later is ambitious enough. What about racing one eight days later?

For 67-year-old Australian age-grouper Mary Mitchell, that was precisely the challenge.

On October 10, Mitchell won the women’s 65-69 age group at the Ironman World Championship in Kona, finishing in 13:11:46. Eight days later, she lined up at Ironman California in Sacramento, where she crossed the finish line in 11:32:45, more than 90 minutes faster than her Kona performance.

And she won her age group again. Even more remarkably, her husband, Andrew, 66, who had also raced in Kona, joined her in Sacramento and won his age group too.

What began as a somewhat improbable endurance experiment ended with three age-group victories between them.

The idea wasn’t actually Mary’s. Earlier this year, Mary and Andrew traveled to Morocco to spend time altitude training in the Atlas Mountains with a friend. During the trip, their friend floated an intriguing challenge. What would happen if a bunch of athletes in their 60s attempted two full-distance Ironmans a week apart?

“He persuaded us that it would be a good human experiment to do, to see whether, at our age, you could do two Ironmans in eight days, and what would that be like?” Mitchell explains.

The plan was for all three to qualify for Kona, race the World Championship, and then travel to Sacramento for Ironman California the following weekend.

There was just one problem. Their friend subsequently suffered a knee injury and withdrew from the experiment. But Mary and Andrew had already committed. Their flights and accommodation were booked and the race entries paid for.

“We thought, well, we might as well go,” Mitchell recalls. “How bad can it be?”

They also had the encouragement – or perhaps bad influence – of American friends Jill and Dougin Walker, serial Ironman competitors who have completed around 100 Ironmans apiece.

“We’ve fallen in with a bad crowd,” she jokes.

For Mitchell, who has completed around 30 Ironmans since her first in 2004, racing twice in eight days was uncharted territory. The closest she had previously come was roughly a month between Kona and Ironman Malaysia.

But Mitchell also had another reason to make the most of Kona.

Mitchell had previously won her age group at the Ironman World Championship in 2019. Since then, however, her attempts to return to the top of the podium had been repeatedly derailed.

In 2022, she was hit by a car while riding on the Queen Ka’ahumanu Highway just 10 days prior, preventing her from starting the race.

In 2024, at the women’s World Championship in Nice, she developed food poisoning the night before the race and abandoned roughly 140 kilometers into the bike.

Then in 2025, she returned to Kona, only to discover that what she initially believed to be a cold was COVID. She struggled throughout the race and eventually withdrew on the run.

“I had three years of thwarted attempts to do the World Championship,” she says.

Heading into 2026, Mitchell and her husband had decided this would be their final Kona appearance. They wanted to experience other races and destinations rather than continue returning to the Big Island.

And for much of the race day, Mitchell’s final Kona appearance looked unlikely to deliver another world title.

Mitchell had hoped to swim around 90 minutes, but the ocean conditions and what she admits was insufficient swim preparation conspired against her. She eventually emerged from Kailua Bay in 1:57:06.

“I had an appalling swim,” she says. “I hadn’t done enough long swimming, didn’t have enough endurance, just because swimming’s not my favorite thing.”

By the time she reached transition, Mitchell had effectively written off her chances of winning: “When I stood up out of the water, I thought, a competitive race is off the table now.”

Rather than chase positions, she decided to enjoy what she believed would be her last Ironman World Championship. Her mantra became simple: “Look after yourself and just keep chipping away at it.”

Despite the heat, wind, and particularly oppressive humidity, Mitchell began making steady progress through the field. She completed the bike in 6:38:10 and started the marathon feeling surprisingly good. She settled into a sustainable pace, making sure to take in adequate fluids and calories at every aid station.

With her husband, Andrew, also competing, there was nobody on the sidelines feeding her information about her position. It wasn’t until she reached the top of the Energy Lab, with roughly 12 kilometers remaining, that an acquaintance delivered some unexpected news.

“She said, ‘You’re first and running faster than anyone else in your age group.’ And I’m like, ‘What? No, come on.’”

Mitchell completed the marathon in 4:23:23, the fastest run in her age group, to secure her second age-group world title.

Ordinarily, the next few days would have been devoted to celebrating, recovering and enjoying the satisfaction of a long-awaited victory. Instead, she had another Ironman looming.

Following the Kona awards dinner and a celebratory glass of wine, Mary and Andrew flew to Sacramento, arriving on Monday with less than a week remaining before their next race.

There was no elaborate recovery protocol, no special preparation, and definitely no attempt to squeeze in additional fitness.

“We rode our bikes for an hour to an hour and a half along the river trail a couple of times, which was really nice,” Mitchell recalls.

The rides were deliberately easy, simply turning their legs over while enjoying the scenery and wildlife. She also completed a couple of short runs no longer than 30 minutes or demanding.

Her only swim was a brief dip in the river to test the water temperature and a new wetsuit, purchased after her previous one split during a 70.3 race in Leipzig, Germany, earlier in the trip.

More surprisingly, Mitchell wasn’t experiencing any crippling muscle soreness she remembered from her earlier Ironman days.

“It’s strange. They sort of ached, but they didn’t actually feel sore,” she explains.

She believes advances in running shoe technology have made a meaningful difference to how her legs feel after an Ironman. She raced in “super shoes” but suspects recovering from back-to-back marathons would have been considerably harder in the shoes available a decade ago.

Still, there was plenty of uncertainty. When asked whether she questioned the wisdom of attempting another Ironman, Mary laughs: “Oh, I thought about that just about every day.”

“If they’d had a half [Ironman] race, I probably would have dropped down to that distance.”

She reasoned that California’s current-assisted, wetsuit-legal swim would be far less demanding than Kona’s ocean swim. The flat, relatively straightforward bike course also appeared manageable.

But the marathon was another matter entirely.

“My big concern throughout the whole week was the run. Can you do two marathons in eight days with having raced in Kona with the conditions that are notoriously one of the more difficult races that you can do?”

Mitchell had trained specifically for Kona and made no adjustments to prepare for the double. Whatever happened in Sacramento would simply happen.

As expected, the California swim was a very different experience. Thanks to the river current and wetsuit, Mitchell emerged from the water in approximately 55 minutes, effectively halving her swim time from Kona.

But the bike wasn’t entirely without incident. Early in the ride, Mitchell developed significant pain in her quadriceps muscle and her shin. By the end of the first 90-kilometer lap, she was seriously considering pulling out.

“I was thinking, should I stop? It’s really quite sore,” she recalls. “Am I causing damage?”

She decided to continue to the next aid station and reassess, knowing she could stop at any point. Eventually, the pain subsided.

With no expectations, and already holding her Kona qualification for the following year, Mitchell approached the rest of the race with an unusually relaxed attitude. She chatted with volunteers, stopped when necessary, and enjoyed the atmosphere.

“I was just chilled the entire race because I didn’t have anything to prove,” she explains.

The marathon, her greatest concern, proved surprisingly manageable. Rather than experiencing the crushing fatigue she had anticipated, Mitchell found herself settling into a rhythm. The crowds were enthusiastic, the aid stations welcoming, and she passed the kilometers chatting with other competitors.

Her marathon time, 4:23:30, was almost identical to the one she had run in Kona eight days earlier. When she crossed the finish line in 11:32:45, she had not only completed the experiment but had also won her age group again. Andrew had done the same.

“To our astonishment, we both won our age groups,” Mitchell says.

Perhaps even more surprising was how they felt afterward.

“When we first started this sport, you do an Ironman and basically couldn’t walk for three days. Getting up out of a chair was really difficult and painful.”

This time, within 24 to 48 hours, both felt remarkably normal.

Mitchell discovered triathlon in her 30s, partly because Andrew was already involved in sport and she was tired of being left at home while he trained. She bought a cheap bike, started entering local short-course events and quickly fell in love with the sport and its community.

Her first Ironman came in 2004 at Busselton in Western Australia. Like many newcomers, she initially assumed it would be a one-off experience. More than two decades later, she’s still going.

Now retired and living in Adelaide, Mitchell trains up to 20-25 hours per week during her biggest blocks. She has worked with a coach for many years and increasingly recognizes the importance of strength training and nutrition, particularly in preserving muscle mass as she ages. Around five years ago, she also began working with a sports dietitian to improve her fueling, gut training, and overall nutritional approach.

But Mitchell is quick to point out that endurance sport doesn’t consume her entire existence. She enjoys good food, wine, socializing, reading and traveling with Andrew. And while she is competitive, she says curiosity is an even greater motivation.

“I just want to see how far I can push myself,” she explains.

Since retiring, Mitchell has challenged herself to do “at least one thing each year that takes me out of my comfort zone.”

Last year, that meant racing Patagonman in Chile, confronting her dislike of cold water by jumping from a ferry into a freezing cold fjord to start the race. This year, it was the Kona-California double. Next year, she’s already entered the 100-kilometer Tarawera ultramarathon in New Zealand.

For Mitchell, the biggest reward of the double wasn’t another age-group victory. It was discovering that, at age 67, she could still surprise herself.

“I think it resets your mind. It resets your thinking about what your body can achieve. And what mentally you can achieve,” she reflects.

The experience also reinforced what she loves about triathlon: the places it takes her, the friendships it creates, and the opportunities to challenge assumptions about her own limitations.

“It’s taught me that you’re stronger than you think you are, mentally and physically.”

Would she ever attempt two Ironmans in eight days again?

“No. Absolutely not. There’s no need to repeat the experiment!”

For all the satisfaction of the achievement, Mitchell admits she would have liked more time to savor her second Kona world title. Instead, almost immediately, she was packing up and preparing to race again.

“It would have been nice to just chill and enjoy it a little bit more,” she explains. “Have a couple more glasses of wine, soak it in.”

But the experience did change one thing: Despite initially declaring that 2026 would be their last Kona, Mary and Andrew have both accepted qualifying slots to next year’s World Championship.
