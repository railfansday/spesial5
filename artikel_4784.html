When Violence Is the Point

Updated November 20, 2026 | Reviewed by Lybi Ma

By Avery Ellis and H. Colleen Sinclair, Ph.D.

On January 4, 2025, a 14-year-old boy in Borås, Sweden, walked up to a 55-year-old woman who was out walking her dog and stabbed her. He streamed the attack live on Discord to an online group he desperately wanted to join.

The attack wasn’t his idea. The group first gave him an initiation task: Slash car tires. When he did, they told him it wasn’t enough. Find a human target, they said. The woman survived after two surgeries. Streaming viewers tipped off police, who arrested the boy that evening. When asked why he did it, he said he wanted to belong.

Was this extremism? A random assault? A gang initiation? Child exploitation? Unsettlingly, it may be all four. U.S. law enforcement has a name for this kind of threat: nihilistic violent extremism, or NVE, a label the FBI and Department of Justice introduced in 2025. It describes loosely connected online networks, many of them branching out of a sprawling ecosystem called the Com (short for “the community”).

This ecosystem is also linked to TCC, the “True Crime Community,” dark fandoms (soon to be featured on 60 Minutes). But unlike the images that the TCC descriptor might bring to mind of keyboard sleuths obsessed with crime documentaries, these individuals are more interested in committing than solving crime. Their modus operandi is recruiting young people through gaming platforms and social media sites to push them toward harming themselves and others. It is grooming for violence.

The threat is growing fast. The Polarization and Extremism Research and Innovation Lab (PERIL) counted eight NVE-linked plots and attacks worldwide in 2023. By mid-November 2026, it had counted 69 for the year, an increase of 763 percent (Jensen, 2026). Schools were the most common target, and outside the United States, the median perpetrator age was just 16.

These threats take more than one form. Just this month, Bavarian authorities arrested two teenagers from the German state of Hesse. They are suspected members of the Com who pressured three girls aged 13 and 14 into filming sexual acts and self-harm (News.de, 2026).

A stabbing in Sweden and online sextortion in Germany look like very different crimes, yet both fall under the same label. That is part of the problem. Researchers, police, schools, and tech platforms all use the term NVE, but they don’t always mean the same thing. Here is where they appear to agree versus where they don’t.

Hatred of humanity in general. A recent review of NVE definitions found that most share a small set of core elements (Zdjelar, 2026), and misanthropy leads the list. Unlike prejudice aimed at a specific group, misanthropy is hatred of people in general. The group No Lives Matter calls ordinary people “mundane” and urges members to spread “terror to all who are mundane.” One recruiter put out a call for “true misanthropic individuals” (Njohsp).

Status earned through harm. The second widely shared feature is clout-chasing: gaining attention and rank by committing cruelty, or coercing others to do so. Leaked chat archives from 764, one of the most notorious NVE networks, show a hierarchy in which members climb by producing violent content (Argentino, 2025). Victims, often young girls, are groomed, pressured into sending explicit images, and then extorted into harming themselves on camera. That footage is collected in “lorebooks” that circulate through the network to control victims and raise the status of perpetrators (Hwang, 2026). The harm is the content, and the content is the currency.

Young people recruiting young people. NVE participants are overwhelmingly young, and so are their victims. Across Com-linked arrests worldwide, the median offender is 19, and the median victim is 13 (Argentino and Lindsay, 2026). Recruitment happens on the platforms where teenagers already spend their time.

Is there an ideology? The word “nihilistic” suggests these groups believe in nothing, and some researchers agree, describing NVE as violence without a clear ideological motive (ISD, 2025). Others argue that nihilism has become an ideology of its own, with rules and justifications for violence (Gill, 2025). Still others see a “salad bar” of borrowed imagery, such as Nazi symbols, Satanism, and school-shooter fandom, used for shock value rather than belief (Argentino and Lindsay, 2026; Sivignon, 2026). The evidence cuts both ways. A study of 29 adjudicated Com-network cases found that genuine ideological motives did appear in some pathways to offline violence, but often only after violent tendencies were already present (Clemmow and colleagues, 2026).

Is the violence a means to an end? The FBI’s definition says NVEs seek to bring about society’s collapse. Some groups at the edge of this ecosystem fit that description. The Order of Nine Angles (O9A), a satanic neo-Nazi movement, promotes ritual “culling” of people it deems worthless as a path to societal breakdown (Gartenstein-Ross, 2025). Other researchers argue that most NVE actors have no strategic goal and that chaos is the point (Argentino and Lindsay, 2026). Whether groups like O9A fall under the NVE umbrella, or are better understood as accelerationists, is debatable.

Is copycatting a defining feature? Many NVE attackers idolize earlier killers. PERIL found documented veneration of past attackers, most often in online tribute posts, in 62 percent of NVE plots and attacks. Columbine alone was cited in 88 cases across 20 countries (Jensen, 2026). In some online dark fandoms, mass shooters are called “saints.” Yet sextortion networks like the one implicated in Germany may never reference a past attack. Is veneration a core of NVE, or the signature of one branch of it?

Where does NVE end and something else begin? NVE overlaps with a crowd of related labels: the True Crime Community, sadistic online exploitation, participatory memetic violent extremism, and “mixed, unclear, and unstable” ideologies, to name a few. Some researchers treat these as parts of a single NVE ecosystem. Others see distinct problems that call for distinct responses.

Debating definitions can sound like academic hair-splitting. It isn’t. A school counselor, a prosecutor, and a content moderator can look at the same teenager and see three different problems: a mental health crisis, a criminal, a terms-of-service violation. Define the threat too narrowly, and we miss the warning signs. Define it too broadly, and the label sweeps in kids who need help, not handcuffs. Many of the young people involved are both victims and perpetrators.

NVEs pose a rapidly growing and increasingly alarming threat to youth worldwide. Despite the groups’ definitional ambiguity, experts largely agree that misanthropy and clout-chasing sit at the core of nihilistic violent extremism. Other definitional elements are still being debated. Experts should work toward definitional agreement and do so quickly. After all, a threat left undefined is a threat left unchecked.
