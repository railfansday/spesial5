What Does It Mean to Believe in God?

In his Ethics, Aristotle says that we should seek to understand each thing according to the kind of account it permits. In solving an arithmetic problem, for example, we should expect an answer that is both accurate and precise. In the moral life, by contrast, we should seek an account that accords with what a practically wise person would have to say.

If we are adding or subtracting single-digit numbers, we can reasonably expect a reliable answer from a third grader. If, however, we are talking about friendship, marriage, or the relationship between a parent and offspring, we should turn elsewhere for insight, perhaps to the works of George Eliot or Leo Tolstoy.

A similar shift in expectation is appropriate when we think about belief in God. If God were a phenomenon susceptible to the sort of reasoning that we find in an arithmetic problem or a proof in symbolic logic, then it should be relatively easy to construct a knock-down argument for the existence and nature of God.

If, however, God is not quantifiable, then arithmetical approaches are not likely to serve us well, and if God is not like the major or minor premises of a syllogism (such as: All things we can conceive of exist; we can conceive of God; therefore, God exists), then we should not expect to “solve” God like a proof.

Likewise, if God is not made of matter, we should not expect to be able to reproduce the divine presence through scientific means, whether physical or chemical. The mere fact that God does not have mass, dimension, shape, location, or color does not establish that God is nothing more than a figment of human imagination.

After all, we believe in numbers, although they lack all the physical properties just mentioned, and we also believe in love, justice, beauty, and truth, although they too cannot be counted, logically proved, or reproduced in a laboratory flask. There are things we believe to be real that we cannot see with our eyes or touch with our hands.

If sacred texts are any guide, the best means we have of accounting for God are not the methods of mathematics, logic, or science, but those more closely aligned with literature or narrative. The Bible, for example, is full of stories, and while a few of these stories include God directly, many more address the effects of God’s presence in the unfolding of the human story.

In one well-known metaphor, God is not something we directly apprehend, but we can sense God at work in the world much as we see and hear the breeze rustling the leaves on a tree. When Moses first senses God’s presence, it is in the form of a bush that is burning but not consumed by the flames, something only he has the curiosity and patience to see.

At this point, a skeptic might ask, If God is real, is it too much to ask that the divine should simply appear before us, work a few miracles, and thereby remove all doubt? But what if the problem is not God’s but ours? What if God cannot be put into any humanly constructed box because the divine is far beyond our meager intellectual and imaginative capacities?

What if we are talking about a reality that undergirds all of reality, not only as we know it but also far beyond our capacity to comprehend? What if we are seeking not just a clockmaker who set things in motion billions of years ago but a creator who sustains the universe from moment to moment as it continues to unfold even now in space and time?

Even the physical universe is far beyond our capacity to keep tabs on. A mature human organism consists of about 40 trillion cells, each one of which performs about 1 billion chemical reactions per second, which in turn requires the presence of about 100 trillion atoms of hydrogen. Of course, these are just round numbers – only God knows for sure.

It is quite possible, perhaps likely, and based on past experience, all but certain that there is a great deal that we do not yet know about even the physical universe. It was only in 1895 that we first learned of the existence of x-rays and only in 1962 that we verified extra-terrestrial x-ray sources. What else still awaits our discovery?

Yet even to say that we still have a great deal more to discover hardly captures the full extent of the problem. For one thing, there may be more out there and in here, even if it is susceptible to our awareness or understanding, than we will ever uncover. And it is also possible that the greater part of it does not conform to our ways of knowing.

Perhaps being itself, as well as its source and end, is not an entirely anthropomorphic thing, in the sense that it is not built for our minds. It may have many other purposes far beyond us. And yet, here we are, blessed with the ability to perceive and understand some truly remarkable things, even if we can’t begin to get our hands around the whole thing.

Perhaps when it comes to God, poetry, music, art, and narrative are the best we can hope for – intimations, always allusive and never definitive. And yet, even here, we find truth and beauty for which we have every reason to be deeply thankful, for perhaps it is above all when we sense God’s presence, maybe with the aid of Eliot and Tolstoy, that we come most fully to life.
