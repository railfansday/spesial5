Why Curlews Are the New Canaries in the Coal Mine

One would have to be totally out of touch to think that the Earth's diverse ecosystems are healthy and thriving. Numerous habitats are in dire straits, and many nonhuman animal residents are trying to tell us we must act to save everyone.

Grassland animals can tell us what is happening if we would only listen to them, and in her new book titled The Grassland Queen: An Avian Love Story in the American Plains, naturalist, filmmaker, and author Priyanka Kumar does just that by focusing on the lives of long-billed curlews, "an elegant shorebird with a rounded body and an improbably elongated bill—a highly intelligent bird that mates for life." I think of these birds as the proverbial canary in the coal mine, telling us that things where they live aren't going well, and Kumar carefully explains why we must listen to what they are saying.

Marc Bekoff: Why did you write The Grassland Queen?

Priyanka Kumar: As a naturalist and educator at the University of New Mexico, it’s my passion to research birds in the context of their ecosystems. The Grassland Queen shows how a community of birds can grow close to a naturalist’s heart, like they are extended family. Over years of researching curlews in grasslands, I returned to my daily life and found myself wondering how my birds were faring in the blazing fields, with heat radiating from dirt and stone.

The long-billed curlew is admittedly a strange-looking bird, with an impossibly long bill, like a creature out of prehistoric times. Curlew parents are insanely devoted to keeping their chicks safe from predators such as ravens, coyotes, foxes, and Swainson’s hawk. A curlew father is a Shakespearean character, dithering one moment, dive-bombing the next—I saw twists and turns and surprising bursts of drama on my days of fieldwork.

MB: How does your book relate to your background and general areas of interest?

PK: My work as a naturalist is deeply informed by my Himalayan childhood and how I experienced and loved nature as a child. I first wrote about long-billed curlews and a dozen other species in my book, Conversations with Birds. I am grateful that the book continues to resonate with readers and inspires them to deepen their engagement with nature.

Over time, I kept coming back to curlews. I wanted to observe them deeply. Long-billed curlews form lifelong pair bonds; the male and female may winter in different coastal areas, but they miraculously return each summer to the same grassland, find one another, and attempt to raise a family. They’re such relatable birds.

PK: Our national grasslands are one of the country’s best-kept secrets—the wildlife is exquisite, from the pronghorns to the burrowing owls. Grasslands have been very generous to humans—more than half of the human diet comes from grasses and their seed heads such as wheat, rice, rye, and oats. It’s time to return the favor and care about how grasslands are changing.

Grassland birds are in trouble—they’re the fastest-declining group of birds in North America. The ecosystem that the curlews have relied on for so long—they coevolved here with bison—is being relentlessly fragmented by industrial agriculture and development. Even the grasslands that remain are sometimes too degraded to support birds. My narrative about the curlew's life also shows a wider community of underappreciated grassland animals.

MB: What are some of the topics you consider and what are some of your major messages?

PK: It’s a marvel that curlews eke out a living in North American grasslands, which can sometimes look so scorched and bleak. Parts of the Great Plains have been in a megadrought, which means fewer grasshoppers and beetles for curlew chicks. These chicks need to eat, grow, learn to fly, and migrate back to the wetlands—without their parents—over one fraught summer.

It’s critical to understand the bird communities that live in the American Plains and how global warming can unravel ecosystems. After oceans, grasslands are the largest ecosystem on Earth. I take readers into the heart of this unfolding drama so that we can understand what the birds themselves may be experiencing.

MB: What challenge did you face while writing the book?

PK: The hardest part of writing this book was leaving the grasslands and all those lovely birds behind. In one of the last chapters, when I’m in the grasslands, I’m in wildfire smoke. With all the climate stresses, this ecosystem is pretty vulnerable to overheating and out-of-control wildfires.

Eighty percent of American grasslands are private, and we have no concerted national strategy—though there’s broad consensus that we need one—to incentivize private landowners to become more bird-friendly, whether through rotational grazing or creating wild margins at the edges of agricultural fields.

MB: Are you hopeful that as people learn more about this and treat birds with more respect and compassion?

PK: Literature and storytelling catalyze us to empathize with the lives of others. I hope this book pulls readers out of their everyday lives, transports them, and makes them feel affection for long-billed curlews. I’ve felt a sustained love for these birds over more than two decades.

I created an impressionistic painting: If you look closely, you’ll see many dots, which are the field observations. But hopefully, when you step back, you get the sweep of the grand landscape and the dramas playing out among wildlife and between wildlife and humans. This book is about being fiercely present. Will we choose to be present for the birds and the degradation their ecosystems face? It is painful to be present, especially when you find yourself looking for curlews on a shooting range, but there’s also a great joy that sneaks up on you just when you’re about to give up.

In conversation with Priyanka Kumar, an award-winning author whose writing has appeared in The New York Times, The Washington Post, Los Angeles Review of Books, Orion, and Sierra. An internationally acclaimed naturalist and filmmaker, she has taught at the University of California, Santa Cruz, and the University of Southern California and serves on the Advisory Council of the Leopold Writing Program.
