The Problem With Being Told “You’re Right”

If you’ve ever vented to friends about something aggravating that your partner is doing, or an issue you’re having with a coworker, your friends may immediately take your side: “I can’t believe they’d do that!” or “That’s sooo annoying.” We often turn to our friends for this type of support, and doing so can make us feel connected and seen. While validation is important, there’s a difference between validating feelings and being told that our viewpoint is right. When validation turns into just being told we’re right, it can get us stuck in our views and less likely to resolve conflict effectively.

For example, if you tell your friend that your child never listens to you, and your friend just sympathizes with how annoying that is, you may think that they are being a good friend, but chances are, it won’t change how you interact with your kid. That’s where a good therapist (or friend) comes in—they could both validate your feeling of frustration and help you become curious about the situation, which might be more helpful for changing your behavior, your kid’s behavior, or how you relate to your kid. It’s the difference between agreeing that your child is being annoying (as a fact) vs. seeing that maybe they’re getting hungry or tired, or even identifying how your own stress may be playing a role in how you are showing up with them. Adopting the latter mindset promotes emotional flexibility.

There is a large body of research on the value of emotional and psychological flexibility in improving both mental health and relationship outcomes. Flexibility is a skill that we can train, such as through cognitive reappraisal exercises. It’s also something that naturally develops through new experiences like having an open-minded disagreement with someone else.

The problem is that today there are fewer and fewer opportunities to have our thinking effectively challenged. Social media creates echo chambers, and AI chatbots like ChatGPT and Claude have been found to have an issue with sycophancy, an excessive form of agreement. If you’ve ever used an AI chatbot, you may have received responses like, “You’re asking exactly the right questions!” or “Honestly, you’re not overreacting,” which are types of sycophantic responses. At its extreme, sycophancy can even lead people to believe things that are more and more disconnected from reality, leading to “AI psychosis.”

This issue is not just contained to isolated incidents. Anthropic, one of the major AI companies, put out a report and found that their AI model (Claude) was sycophantic in almost 25 percent of conversations having to do with relationships. Another research study in Science found that AI affirmed users’ actions 49 percent more often than humans, even in situations involving deception, illegal behavior, or other harmful actions. The study also found that a single chat with a sycophantic AI chatbot decreased people’s willingness to take responsibility for their actions or repair conflicts with another person, while increasing their belief that they were right. Given that the number-one use for chatbots is for therapy and emotional support, this is a very concerning finding.

So what can we do about all this? One thing we can do is to intentionally seek out viewpoints that disagree with us and challenge our thinking. This could come from friends, from conversations with people we encounter day to day who hold opposing views, or from a therapist who can offer a fresh perspective. A similar approach extends to our use of AI. Instead of asking an AI chatbot, “Am I right?” we can try intentionally prompting for things like “What might I be overlooking?” or “Suggest a different view that challenges my thinking.” Overall, being open to seeing things from different angles is more likely to help us think broadly rather than get stuck in one way of thinking, which can contribute to more emotional flexibility and better mental health.
