How to Navigate Seasonal Affective Disorder as a Couple

As the weather cools and the leaves begin to change color, another change can occur: The depressed moods that come along with Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD) can begin to arise. SAD can do more than just dampen one’s mood in late fall and winter; it can also negatively impact one’s relationships. As fall arrives, one may want to get out and see the changing leaves, visit a pumpkin patch, or indulge in some holiday shopping. However, their partner may just want to crawl back under the covers and hibernate until the spring. Approximately 5% of people in the United States experience symptoms of SAD as the days grow shorter. Symptoms usually begin to show around mid-fall and carry on through the winter, beginning to relent around spring and summer. When one partner struggles with SAD, it can create feelings of frustration and resentment within the relationship. The fatigue, irritability, social withdrawal, and lack of sexual desire that can come with SAD may change how couples spend time together or interpret one another’s behavior. Here we discuss some practical ways to acknowledge our loved one’s feelings and SAD-related symptoms while still maintaining a connection and making room for how each person approaches the changing seasons.

SAD is officially defined as a major depressive disorder that has a seasonal pattern, following a regular cycle that typically begins and ends at the same time every year. People who experience SAD during the colder fall and winter months can have persistent low moods, low energy, sleepiness, changes in appetite, and a desire to withdraw from social activities.

The symptoms of SAD can be more severe than the “winter blues” that many of us deal with as the days get shorter and it gets darker earlier. For those who grapple with SAD, the activities of daily functioning are disrupted — and this can include their intimate relationships. Before judging a partner too harshly for what may seem to be a sudden mood change that comes as colder weather sets in, perhaps suggest that they meet with a professional to have an assessment done for seasonal affective disorder. Framing it as something psychological rather than assuming that one’s partner has lost interest in the relationship as a whole can help both partners approach the issue more productively.

SAD can have an impact on all aspects of daily life, from getting up in the morning to performance at work to interactions with the ones we love. This can create an imbalance when one partner is dealing with the negative effects of their SAD, and for the other partner, it is business as usual. SAD can lead to a decrease in motivation, which can look like disinterest from the outside looking in. One of the best ways to salvage a relationship when one person has SAD is to prepare before the symptoms set in. Focus on open communication between one another and ask questions such as “What kind of support feels most helpful when you are struggling?” or “How can we best stay connected when your energy is low?” Emphasize listening, sincere reassurance, and respectful communication over trying to solve a problem that may not be immediately solvable. The partner with SAD should come away from discussions feeling like their perspectives, needs, and opinions matter. For couples navigating seasonal depression, there are ways to connect when moods are low. The important thing to remember is to remain flexible. Suggest several options for time together, such as a short walk, a simple meal at home, or an affectionate touch that does not carry the expectation of sex. Be prepared to offer “low-energy” or “high-energy” versions of plans to work around the fluctuating symptoms of SAD. Offer invitations instead of hard and fast plans and try to stay away from statements that suggest the person needs to try harder to “break out of their seasonal funk.” It’s important in the relationship to support the partner with SAD without becoming their therapist. By keeping empathy and communication at the forefront, you can weather the seasonal depression period together.

Even the most well-intentioned partners can’t have all the answers. While keeping to a strategy of empathetic support and simple routines, seeking the advice of a professional can also go a long way in helping people with SAD. Treatment options such as psychotherapy, light therapy, and antidepressant medications can help people withstand the most challenging aspects of SAD until the symptoms begin to lessen as the days grow longer and the weather becomes warmer.

The colder months bring a lot of time for togetherness, from holiday parties to enjoying outdoor adventures in the snow. However, when a couple is navigating seasonal depression, the fall and winter seasons can look different. By sticking with small, flexible ways to stay connected and keeping the lines of communication open, couples can help make the season easier without allowing seasonal depression to define their relationship.
