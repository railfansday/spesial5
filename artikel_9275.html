What Your Doctor Fails to Tell You Can Definitely Hurt

When you think about your physical health, it’s natural to think of all the ways a physician or similar healthcare provider should be able to help you. If you or a loved one have developed a chronic disease that requires monitoring, surgery, or a switch in medications, you’ll probably feel that this is enough to get things back on track on the road to recovery.

As it turns out, a number of medical conditions and treatments have important psychological connections that are equally important to promote healing. Whether it’s depression, cognitive changes, or anxiety, you may not realize that these are natural outcomes of various types of interventions. Unfortunately, healthcare professionals aren’t always either (a) aware of these connections or (b) prepared (or have the time) to address them.

In a review of studies “at the intersection of psychiatry and cardiology,” Medical University of Silesia’s Kamila Klimek-Ociepka and colleagues scanned various databases to identify publications over the 15-year period of 2010 to 2025 in which findings were reported on the conjunction of cardiac device implantation (such as pacemakers) and assessment of patient symptoms in the domains of depression, anxiety, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and cognitive disorders. A total of 44 studies made it to the final set used to analyze the main findings.

Key to understanding these studies is “heart-focused anxiety (HFA).” As you might imagine, having a procedure done to your heart, even if it is meant to reduce your risk of dying, is hardly a minor episode in your life. Anxiety and depression are reported in as many as 44 percent of patients treated with internal devices meant to keep the heart beating regularly.

In particular, HFA occurs when people experience “excessive anxiety related specifically to cardiac sensations, symptoms, and anticipated cardiovascular events.” People with certain types of implants, the ones that perform internal defibrillation (to get the heart started, are particularly likely to have HFA, fearing being harmed by the voltage from the shocks the device performs. Instead of engaging in what would be heart-healthy exercise, they instead decide they could hurt themselves by making the heart overwork, leading to an actual worsening of their condition.

Depressive symptoms, for their part, can also become highly prevalent among cardiac patients, with estimates ranging up to 64 percent, especially when they first learn of their diagnosis. Their depression can be expressed as sleep disturbances and loss of energy, or in the emotional realm as hopelessness and pessimism about the future.

Finally, PTSD can also develop in patients who experienced sudden cardiac events or invasive therapies. There are probably few traumas that can occur in daily life as frightening as having your heart literally stop for a second or two. But because they don’t remember the event, their PTSD may be expressed as “hypervigilance, anxiety, and avoidance behaviors rather than intrusive memories or flashbacks.”

In reading about these psychological consequences of cardiac events and treatment, you might be wondering why you’ve never heard anyone speak about them in the context of treatment. Shouldn’t you (or your relative) have been warned or at least educated about these risks? Considering all the precautions listed on any kind of medical consent form, you would think that it might be worth adding these—along with an explanation.

Additionally, as pointed out by Erzurum Bolge Training and Research Hospital's Murat Özmen, physicians may not realize that depression can be associated with the low physical activity, high stress, obesity, and hypertension that are part of the disease picture. Just as physicians and other healthcare professionals instruct their patients about implanted devices themselves, the authors claim they need to educate them about the importance of sleep, developing coping methods to reduce stress, and participating in exercise as warranted by stage of recovery.

The experience of cardiovascular disease is clearly life-threatening and scary, but even more ominous is a diagnosis of cancer. Considering how highly prevalent cancer is within the population, it’s likely that someone you know (or you) has received this diagnosis.

It may seem almost self-evident that the uncertainty of a cancer diagnosis, along with the stress involved in tests, procedures, and therapy, would be enough to lead a patient’s mind to travel to existential questions about the possibility of surviving into the future. Fortunately, those who treat cancer patients are tuned in to the psychological risks. University of Houston’s Haejeong An and Yoonjung Kim note that this “general understanding is expanded by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), which specifically defines distress as a multifaceted concept covering physical, psychological, social, or spiritual discomfort that emerges during cancer survivorship, which affects how survivors think, feel, or act.”

Unfortunately, though, the concept of distress is not well-defined in actual practice. Therapeutic approaches fail to separate distress from anxiety, depression, and stress, meaning that interventions will fail to target this piece of the cancer journey separately and effectively. Starting with a database of more than 2,700 studies, the authors analyzed the final 14 that met all the rigorous criteria for inclusion.

The methods used in these studies were all over the map, ranging from standard psychological interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) to virtual reality, mindfulness, and hypnosis. As a result, there were inconsistent findings regarding effectiveness, a problem made worse by lack of a clear definition of distress.

Compared to cardiac disease, the scope of psychosocial interventions for cancer patients is far more advanced. However, the U. Houston findings suggest that practitioners do not have clear pathways for reducing the distress of their patients, if they indeed decide to implement some type of intervention.

These examples of research in the area of psychological symptoms and serious disease should alert you to the idea that if you (or a loved one) are headed for some type of intervention, it’s important to be aware of possible emotional consequences. In an era of overburdened healthcare providers, where you may feel lucky to get an appointment at all, finding ways to receive this support may seem impossible.

However, if it’s not from the provider itself, consider seeking support from other sources. There is a wide number of support groups associated with specific diseases, but if there isn’t one in your community, you can also draw from online resources. You can even start your own group.

An added piece of irony in the equation is that behavioral medicine often focuses on ways to help patients adopt healthier lifestyles. Less often does the field seek connections between physical disease and mental health. The An and Kim article suggests some causes of optimism given the large number of intervention studies in the area of breast cancer. The problem in this area is not lack of recognition, but lack of definitions and clear methodologies, leading to the exclusion of most available research.

To sum up, the next time you draw up your list of questions to ask your healthcare provider before going in for an appointment, think about whether your stress, anxiety, and depression might also be important to mention. Your mental health and your physical health can only stand to gain.
