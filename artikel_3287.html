Will The Congressional Blue Tsunami Help Or Hurt The Plan To Save Hollywood?

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

American voters issued a stunning rebuke of the Trump Administration on Tuesday night by electing Democrats to take control of both chambers of Congress come January. Among the many implications for Hollywood in the 2026 midterm election results, the efforts to enact a federal tax incentive for U.S. film and television production might have just hit an unexpected roadblock.

Lawmakers introduced the bipartisan Motion Picture, Television, and Entertainment Revitalization Act in September to create a first-of-its-kind tax credit designed to lure production back to the United States. The hope has been that the legislation could be attached to an end-of-year budget bill facing a vote by December 11 and land on the President’s desk soon after.

There is no clear answer as to whether that is still the plan, and once they establish an overwhelming majority in both chambers of Congress, Democrats will be flooded with warring priorities. Industry insiders are split on whether or not this spells danger for the national incentive efforts. Watch on Deadline

“It makes it harder,” one insider who has been involved in the push to pass the incentive said of the effects of the blue tsunami on the chances of relief for Hollywood.

That might be surprising to hear, given Democrats were first to champion the federal tax credit. But, after sweeping up Congressional seats left and right on Tuesday, the party’s overall strategy is now up in the air, and that could have interesting effects on this effort.

Another source working on the lobbying effort said that the blowout results were a much better case for the legislation’s prospects than if the election was closer, something that could have led to a protracted battle that consumed attention.

The source also said they were encouraged that, in interviews on election night, President Donald Trump seemed open to deals with the Democrats, a sign that there may be an appetite for legislation whose supporters cross party lines. That said, there is still a degree of uncertainty, including on timing, with things probably not getting clearer until Republicans resolve their leadership battle after Mike Johnson decided to step aside.

That’s a sentiment shared by Scott Budnick, who is a film producer and a chief champion of the California film tax credit.

“I think the midterms have little effect on the passage of the Federal credit,” the founder and CEO of 1Community told Deadline. “Before the midterms, high ranking Republicans and high ranking Democrats were working together to make it happen. And President Trump had already posted on Truth Social that he will definitely sign the bill. So I think that bipartisan sentiment still exists now more than ever.”

Here are a few of the scenarios that the federal tax incentive push could face over the next few months.

It is still a possibility that the Motion Picture, Television, and Entertainment Revitalization Act will pass as intended before the end of the year. Congress is required to pass an end-of-year funding bill, or the government faces another shutdown. It is unlikely, a year on from the longest government shutdown in modern history, that lawmakers will march into another one, so the major question here is whether the MPTERA will maintain its bipartisan support over the next month.

That’s the ideal scenario not just for the production workers it would impact but for the lawmakers who have already gotten the legislation this far.

“Every day that passes without a federal film tax credit is another day America remains well behind 65 other countries who are outbidding us for our signature industry. It’s another day that talented crews, writers, musicians, and small businesses go without work,” Rep. Laura Friedman tells Deadline. “We have a real mandate to get things done, and the President has said he is willing to work with Democrats. This is a great opportunity to put action behind his words, pass this bipartisan national film tax credit, and get this done, so that we can bring these critical jobs and investments back where they belong.”

Friedman was re-elected on Tuesday, as were bill sponsor Rep. Nathaniel Moran (R-TX) and several other bipartisan cosponsors including Rep. Brian Jack (R-GA), Rep. Linda Sánchez (D-CA), and Rep. Ted Lieu (D-CA).

If it does retain support, then there is a good chance at getting the tax credit legislation attached to the annual funding bill — but that is a big “if.”

Republicans in particular have acted only upon receiving a green light from Trump, who finally endorsed such legislation in August. That got the ball rolling on the bipartisan bill that is now making its way through both chambers. If Trump pulls his support outright, or simply turns his attention elsewhere, it would require Republicans in Congress to break rank from the White House to continue to champion the legislation.

The question for Congressional Republicans is: Will they choose to wave a white flag and signal early efforts to reach across the aisle as their own reign of power over both chambers comes to an end?

Trump’s continued backing is not the only thing that’ll make or break this legislation. There are several avenues by which the federal tax incentive could lose some of its bipartisan support in Congress over the next month or so. All eyes are on Republicans in the lame duck, but both parties could see their priorities shift over the final weeks of the legislative session.

It is unclear how the federal tax credit legislation now fits into the Democratic agenda, or where it will be on the priority list for Republicans in the remaining weeks they have in power. Among other things, with Republicans losing control in the Senate, in addition to the House, the push likely will be to get as many nominations through the upper chamber as possible, taking up legislative time.

Some of the cosponsors, including Rep. Mike Carey (R-OH), Rep. Brian Fitzpatrick (R-PA) and Rep. Marianette Miller-Meeks (R-IA), also lost their races on Tuesday night. Not only could their focus shift in the coming weeks, but any efforts taken up next year would require significant re-education if Democrats hoped to keep this bipartisan (although that would no longer be entirely necessary, as noted below).

For Democrats, the size of their perceived mandate is much larger than anticipated, which could prompt their strategy for this lame duck session to completely change. The major questions emerging for Congressional Democrats who are currently in office are: Do they have leverage to do enact something better once they take control? Or do they want to get “smaller” issues out of the way to focus on only the most glaring priorities when they start?

Democrats Could Take Up the Mantle Come January

Once Democrats control both chambers of Congress, there would be a very easy path to passing a federal film tax incentive, should it remain a priority. Congressional Democrats would have enough votes to do so without a lick of Republican support, though there is no real reason to believe that none of the elected Republicans would back it, given it would positively impact workers in their districts.

In doing so, the Democratic party would be able to place the legislation on the President’s desk fairly quickly. Sources indicate that this is a possibility if Democrats decide they could significantly improve on the legislation, given the size of their perceived mandate post-midterms.

It’s worth noting that President Trump warned amid his party’s midterm rout that he would be lording the power of the veto over Congress moving forward. That could start with this bill. While it is designed to support domestic film and television production across the entire country, it is still largely viewed as a plan to “save Hollywood” — a community that Trump and the Republican party have time and again associated with Democrats. So, there is always the possibility that it stalls there. However, getting that well-supported legislation across the finish line would be a pretty easy win for his administration during a time when their power will be severely limited.

Undoubtedly, the Democrats will enter the 120th Congress of the United States with a long list of priorities, including healthcare, U.S. intervention in the Israel-Palestine conflict, and the overall economy. This is in addition to the expected investigations into the Trump administration’s activities that incoming House Oversight Chair Robert Garcia has already promised with this two word statement on Tuesday: “Lawyer up.”

A federal film tax incentive could remain a priority as part of the Democrats’ agenda on the economy, or it could be placed on the back burner in favor of issues that Congressional Democrats find more pressing. In any case, total abandon is unlikely.

In an interesting twist, Senator Adam Schiff might be the key to preventing it from falling by the wayside entirely. The California-based senator was among the first to advocate for a national incentive, but he took a backseat this year given his adversarial relationship with the White House, opening a path for others who were less likely to draw the President’s ire to take the lead.

This is yet another example of how the tides have massively turned. If the Senator takes on a leadership position in the new year, he might be very well positioned to move this along. Effectively, he could go from persona non grata to one of the only people who could keep this effort from being tossed to the wayside. Deadline has reached out to Senator Schiff for comment.

While it would be a big win for production workers if Congress went back to the table and pushed for even more relief for the U.S. film and television industry, one insider acknowledges to Deadline that a new and improved federal tax incentive would fall “probably pretty low” on the totem pole of priorities for the new, blue Congress.
