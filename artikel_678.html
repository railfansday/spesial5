Hollywood’s Top 10 Animation Schools

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

From CalArts to Ringling, these are the programs turning out animation’s next generation — and getting them in the door

More than a year after KPop Demon Hunters became a global sensation — a human-made antidote to the AI slop flooding the Internet — billion-dollar hits like Toy Story 5 and The Super Mario Galaxy Movie and acclaimed work like Wildwood, Flow, Blue Eye Samurai, Arcane and X-Men ’97 make the case that the human touch is still what moves animation audiences. Breaking in, though, has never been harder. The studio system is contracting, AI threatens to rewire production pipelines, and new platforms and producers (see: YouTube and Glitch) are redrawing the map.

Professional connections, fearless experimentation and creative innovation are key. Animation schools play a critical role, preparing emerging talent to be ready on day one to build upon existing pipelines of voices and projects that’ll define the industry’s next chapter. “The great thing about the industry is that because it’s made up of artists, it’s first and foremost a meritocracy,” says Sam Goff, animation talent manager, recruiter and educator. “Networking and industry access also play a huge part, but you could have all the connections in the world, and if you don’t have the skills and can’t actually do the work, you’re not getting hired.” Related Stories Movies Hailee Steinfeld on Recording Music for New Movie 'Hexed' and Becoming a Mother in the Process Movies Magnolia Pictures Takes French Animated Gay Comedy 'Jim Queen' for U.S.

The storied CalArts offers courses tied to animation history, theory, screenwriting, character design, sound and, yes, Disney movies, in classes that are small and taught entirely by working artists. The Patty Disney Center for Life and Work uses a mix of support — one-on-one career advising, an entrepreneurial incubator program, a career and opportunity festival, animation portfolio day, weekly guest speakers and a robust internship program. When Amy Hurwitz, a veteran of LAIKA’s recruitment team, reached out to Bowie State University about a potential partnership around the start of the pandemic, Tewodross Melchishua Williams, professor, program and internship coordinator for the school’s Department of Fine and Performing Arts, was “immediately excited about what that could mean for our students,” he says. “HBCUs have extraordinary talent, but we don’t always have the same access to specialized facilities, equipment, funding, or direct industry relationships as other institutions.”

The partnership with the Oregon-based studio helps close some of those gaps, offering the students in Visual Communication and Digital Media Arts (VCDMA) access to Bowie’s first stop-motion course. Through it, around 20 students annually learn sculpture, cinematography, fabrication, and storytelling alongside character and puppet design, set construction and lighting, frame-by-frame animation, and post-production.

The school is also now home to Studio Ayoka Chenzira, funded by LAIKA and named in honor of Dr. Ayoka Chenzira, a pioneering filmmaker, animator, and department chair at Spelman College. “For me, that name matters,” says Williams. “It connects the space to an African American woman whose work helped expand what animation and experimental filmmaking could be, and it gives our students a legacy to learn from.”

Both initiatives have unlocked access to industry professionals, professional workflows, summer intensive workshops, and internship opportunities, with this year’s LAIKA movie Wildwood counting three very recent alumni as either crew or interns. “I tell students that talent matters, but so does curiosity, discipline, collaboration, and professionalism,” the Bowie State professor says. “Seeing our alumni contribute to a feature like Wildwood makes those lessons tangible.”

For LAIKA, the partnership has resulted in more facilities and access to “talented emerging artists whose backgrounds and perspectives can broaden the field,” says Williams. “The industry needs technical skill, absolutely, but it also needs more voices and different perspectives.”

The collaboration has also inspired similar efforts, like a partnership between Gobelins Paris, Netflix, and Guillermo del Toro, with the support of animation studio ShadowMachine. These serve as “a powerful reminder of the artistry, patience, and human craftsmanship at the heart of animation” in the age of AI, notes Williams. “For students, that’s an important lesson: even as technology changes, foundational skills in movement, design, lighting, storytelling, and collaboration remain essential.”
