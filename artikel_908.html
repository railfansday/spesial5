Athers: Cummins won't resign as Australia Test captain

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Pat Cummins is facing scrutiny over his role as Australia's Test cricket captain following his denials of Usman Khawaja and Cameron Bancroft's claims that the entire Australian cricket team knew about ball tampering during Australia's 2018 tour of South Africa

Michael Atherton does not believe Pat Cummins will resign as Australia's Test cricket captain "immediately" in the wake of Cameron Bancroft's explosive Sandpapergate revelations.

Cummins is facing scrutiny for his denials of both Usman Khawaja and Cameron Bancroft's claims that the entire Australian team knew ball tampering was taking place during their 2018 tour of South Africa.

The Australia captain dismissed Khawaja's claims during a press conference on November 13, saying they were "100 per cent wrong", but Bancroft later issued a 4,000-word statement supporting the allegations, adding Cummins' denials left him "angry, betrayed and alone".

Cummins' management have issued multiple statements since, saying there was "nothing in the [Bancroft's] statement that hasn't been answered" and later adding that he has been "consistent and adamant" from day one.

He is facing calls to resign but when asked if he believes Cummins' position is untenable, Atherton told the Sky Sports Cricket Podcast that he believes there is no "immediate threat" to the bowler's role as Australia's Test captain.

"I don't think so, yet," Atherton said. "A few things are obvious. One, he has the support of Cricket Australia. David Boone [Cricket Australia interim chair], about a week ago, was very clear about that. On Wednesday, Boone and [Cricket Australia chief executive, Todd] Greenberg reiterated that very strongly.

"He's obviously got a lot of credit in the bank in terms of performance as captain, having transformed the culture and kept Australia at the top of the tree, if you like.

"So I don't think there's an immediate threat there."

Atherton added that public reaction to Cummins' comments will be "important" in the discussion.

"He [Cummins] is very much the 'blue-eyed boy' of Australian cricket, isn't he? He's been that way ever since he came into the team," Atherton said.

"Obviously [he] is a great bowler and a top-class captain now.

"But the public reaction will be interesting because the sympathy now is almost going towards the lads who took the fall, particularly Bancroft.

"If the sympathy moves that way, it'll move against the others who are in the other camp, of which Cummins is one. That public reaction will be interesting. It will probably be important.

"Organisations are pretty fickle, aren't they? They support you until you don't. If the public turn on you, that can be a different thing.

"I don't know what he said today, but they, the cameras, were chasing him today.

"It's very much that kind of febrile atmosphere at the moment."

Atherton added: "That gives a sense of crisis, doesn't it? But I don't know. Maybe I'm wrong, but I don't see that [his resignation] happening immediately."

Bancroft, who was banned for nine months after the saga, also delved into discussions about the use of sandpaper in the Australian dressing room during the tour of South Africa, claiming the side was also using sandpaper in the second Test in Port Elizabeth as well as the third Test in Cape Town.

Bancroft said he recalled conversations with Australia's pacers, Cummins, Starc, Josh Hazlewood and Mitchell Marsh about "whether it was swinging enough", and that all players and coaches were present when media manager Kate Hutchinson expressed her opposition to the plans.

"There is still some doubt about exactly who knew what," Atherton said, when asked if he had sympathy for Bancroft.

"It seems to me that two things are absolutely clear. One is the build-up to it. And I've read Usman Khawaja's book, Sabr.

"It's a very interesting book in a broader sense than just the sandpaper stuff. But reading his book, reading what Cameron Bancroft said in that 4,000-word statement, it's clear that [there was in] the build-up to the South Africa tour, this almost institutional pressure that was put on the team, this win-at-all-costs mentality, is how they phrased it.

"In fact, Todd Greenberg, the chief executive of Cricket Australia, did say that Cricket Australia bore some accountability and responsibility for that.

"It was a build-up of pressure. And that was the dressing room that Cameron Bancroft walked into. If you remember, he made his debut in the 2017 Ashes series, the first Test of that series in Brisbane.

"His 4,000-word statement actually starts there, because he said, you need to know the context of the dressing room I was walking into. And it was clearly a team with a toxic dressing room culture at that point.

"That had been building up, and that's background to South Africa. That seems to me a really important part of this story."

Reflecting on events that unfolded in the wake of the saga, Atherton added: "As we say, more than three players must have known and yet the team let those three players carry the can and take the rap.

"That seems to me incredible. So those two aspects are the things that have made this story."

He added: "It's that the context of the build-up to it and the toxic dressing-room culture meant Cricket Australia would hand down heavier sanctions, that there'd be an ethics committee, an ethics investigation, and the whole thing blew up in that sense.

"And then what happened afterwards, the public, I think, cannot take the fact that they allowed three players to take the rap. That's a very long answer to your original question, I think, which was, do you have sympathy for Bancroft?

"And the sympathy is that he was a young kid who was one of three who were allowed to take the rap when clearly there was a broader knowledge there."
