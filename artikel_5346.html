What a Tiny Chorus of the ‘Bring Back Jensen’ Tech Bros Gets Wrong

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

A small band of rogue tech bros has ridden to Jensen Huang’s defense amidst a report from the Financial Times that Huang is huddling with allies, plotting a comeback as Nvidia CEO days after resigning, with a petition demanding “due process, not mob justice,” signed by a minority of tech voices who have gotten outsized attention, such as Elon Musk and Atreides Management managing partner Gavin Baker. One tech bro even said, “Nvidia would be wrong to fire Jensen. His marital indiscretions are between him, his wife, and a reprimand from the board,” as if that was all there is to the story.

But this small circle of rogue tech bros is wrong. This is not a story about a woke inquisition or prudes regulating marital indiscretions and sex – and a Sam Altman-style comeback does not make sense. We were supportive of Altman’s comeback and knew Steve Jobs personally – and supported his firing and subsequent comeback. But these circumstances are materially different.  There is no comeback here.

Start with the conduct the defenders wave away, of Huang’s workplace affairs with direct subordinates. Huang admitted infidelity on Nov. 8. Days later, the New York Times reported a relationship with one of his direct reports. Nvidia’s code of conduct, which opens with a letter from Huang himself, names “personal relationships” as a conflict of interest. We had firsthand knowledge of Mark Hurd’s removal at HP over expense reports that concealed a personal relationship with a contractor, which was the right removal. Boards at McDonald’s, Intel and Nestlé similarly removed CEOs over relationships with employees. Workplace romances with bosses having affairs with married direct reports put those subordinates in an inherently compromised position; they are deleterious to the morale of those around them, raising questions of merit and favoritism.

But when it comes to Huang and Nvidia’s woes, workplace romance is the least of it. The relationship that should alarm Americans the most was Nvidia’s “culture of noncompliance” on chip export restrictions to China, as Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent put it on Sunday with Larry Kudlow, irrespective of whether or not Alice Huang, who helped set up Megaspeed, was an actual Chinese spy. As Bessent said, was Nvidia “pressing these seemingly illicit sales to goose their revenues and their earnings?”

The paper trail when it comes to banned Nvidia chips illegally making their way into China at scale, evading U.S. restrictions, is particularly damning for the company.

Shipment records reviewed by the Wall Street Journal tie Megaspeed to nearly $12 billion in orders of Nvidia’s most advanced chips for Chinese buyers between early 2024 and mid-2025, despite a balance sheet that showed a measly $33 million in 2023., according to Culper Research. Documents reviewed by Bloomberg show that a company Alice Huang founded told China’s own central-bank credit registry that it held servers carrying banned H100, H200 and H800 chips in a Shanghai data center. Some were first sold to an official Nvidia cloud partner in Singapore and then subsequently laundered into China. In March, the Justice Department indicted Super Micro’s co-founder, who has pleaded not guilty, for allegedly smuggling $2.5 billion of Nvidia-powered servers through a Southeast Asian front, with dummy servers staged for inspectors.

And where do such chips end up once they make their way into China? The Chinese Communist Party and the Chinese military, where they are used directly against the US.

Reuters found that Northwestern Polytechnical University, blacklisted by Washington since 2001 and one of China’s “Seven Sons of National Defense,” bought two Nvidia H200s just last month. It researches missiles and military aircraft. A WireScreen study of more than 3,800 Chinese military and government procurement records found export-controlled accelerators, including Nvidia’s A100s and H100s, going to buyers across the People’s Liberation Army, including its Rocket Force, its Cyberspace Force and the equipment arm of the Central Military Commission.

Worse yet, China is training its own supposedly superior AI models on banned Nvidia chips, and likely distilling U.S. frontier models in order to create supposedly lower-cost “open source” models. A former researcher from OpenAI, in an original forensic analysis and GPU “fingerprinting,” found patterns strongly suggesting that six AI models from four of China’s leading labs were initialized on Nvidia Blackwell chips, which are barred from export to China – a highly disturbing finding which raises questions about whether Chinese AI “innovation” is actually just stolen U.S. intellectual property masquerading as Chinese ingenuity. By the researcher’s own account, the findings do not show where the chips were located or how access was obtained.

So many of these tech bros have it exactly backwards. Beijing did not want Jensen out. Beijing wanted Jensen’s chips, and got them.

All the while, Huang assured the world there was nothing to see. “There’s no evidence of any AI chip diversion,” he disingenuously told Bloomberg in May 2025. That October he declared Nvidia’s AI-chip business “100% out of China.” Nvidia now says it put Megaspeed on an internal block list in June 2025. Yet for months afterward its spokespeople kept vouching for the customer, insisting spot checks found every chip where it belonged.

Culper Research revealed just how disingenuous Nvidia’s claims were. Its review of Singapore and Malaysian filings found Megaspeed’s Malaysian arm had been financed through a Singapore entity, Apex Enterprise Solutions, that Culper ties to Alibaba, and had imported $4 billion of servers from Aivres, the U.S. arm of Inspur, whose Chinese parent Washington blacklisted over its work for China’s military. Nvidia’s assurance that Megaspeed had no Chinese shareholders said nothing about who was paying for the chips.

That does not even begin to factor in the possible charges of accounting fraud that plaintiffs’ attorneys will surely bring against Nvidia as the truth emerges, given how Nvidia was accounting for all these Asian transactions and the possible impact to its revenues, earnings and stock price. Nvidia reports China at about 7% of revenue, or $12.4 billion in the first half of this fiscal year. Culper, the shortseller that flagged the Megaspeed nexus six months before the board did, estimates that more than 20% of Nvidia’s compute revenue in fiscal 2026 was really driven by China, through Southeast Asian intermediaries and outright diversion, and quotes a former Nvidia employee putting it as high as 40%.

Then there is the line item that vanished in Nvidia’s revenue disclosures. In the quarter ended July 2025, Nvidia billed $10.2 billion, or 22% of its revenue, through Singapore. Weeks after the Times first reported the Megaspeed investigation, however, Nvidia’s next quarterly filing switched its reporting basis to customer headquarters, and the Singapore line vanished. Nvidia says Singapore was only an invoicing hub for mostly American customers. Really?

That claim rings especially hollow when one notes that the change came within months of its biggest Southeast Asian customer landing on the block list. Columbia accounting professor Shiva Rajgopal calls the timing “curious.” A shareholder class action filed last week alleges investors were misled about Megaspeed for more than a year.

In summary, Nvidia told investors its Chinese AI-chip business was “zero” while its biggest Southeast Asian customer had lined up $12 billion in orders for Chinese buyers; it blacklisted that customer in private while vouching for it in public; and it then retired the one line in its filings that would have shown the trail of money and chips flowing to Southeast Asia and then demonstrably onwards to China. A plaintiffs’ lawyer would note that is the definition of securities fraud. And at an absolute minimum, there is enough evidence to make clear that this is a disclosure failure of historic size, and there needs to be accountability at Nvidia.

When Nvidia reports earnings on Nov. 17, it should restore the Singapore breakout and tell investors how much revenue ultimately serves Chinese end users. It knows the number. According to the Journal, Nvidia has told many overseas cloud partners to hold Chinese customers under a ceiling that has ranged from 30% to 49%. A company that polices the ratio obviously knows what the answer is, and they can and should disclose that transparently as a first step towards accountability.

Many defenders of Huang point out that he could be merely carrying out Trump’s wishes as part of a broader implicit trade deal with China where the U.S. continues to provide cutting-edge chips and in exchange, the Chinese continue to provide the U.S. with rare earth minerals. But this is a misleading and misguided argument on several counts.

First, the U.S. is not nearly as dependent on China for rare earth minerals as many imagine, with the U.S. possessing tremendous reserves of rare earth minerals but merely needing some sustained investment to extract and process them. If there is a national conversation about permitting reform, we should have that conversation and not just sweep it under the carpet in a drug deal done in the dead of night.

Second, this implicit deal did not apparently restrain other cabinet members, such as Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Treasury Secretary Bessent, from registering their concerns.

Third, Trump himself has endorsed the widely admired former CEO of Apple, Tim Cook, as the next CEO of Nvidia, echoing our own earlier endorsement of Cook. Cook has scrupulously abided by national security policies while capably and successfully balancing commerce in China with protection of U.S. national security, and there is nothing to suggest that Cook would knowingly undermine U.S. national security by continuing the illicit flow of chips into China.

Unsurprisingly, many of Huang’s allies who are attempting to paper over these genuine national security issues, disingenuously presenting the issue as one of a woke mob seeking to regulate sex, are talking their own book.

Fund managers Brad Gerstner, of Altimeter, and Gavin Baker, of Atreides, held roughly $1.9 billion and $250 million of the stock at midyear, respectively. It is Altimeter’s largest disclosed equity holding, and surely he would not welcome the inevitable drop in Nvidia stock if chip export restrictions to China are enforced more stringently.

Even more importantly, both Gerstner and Baker are significant holders of the “neoclouds” who are both backed by and are significant customers of Nvidia. Amidst rumors that Colette Kress, the interim CEO of Nvidia, had challenged Huang’s own son-in-law and Huang on neocloud spending before Huang’s ouster and amidst rumors that she may be prepared to usher in a more disciplined era of capital spending for Nvidia, pulling back on excessive spending commitments to neoclouds, these neoclouds could be in for a world of pain without Huang, who was seen as one of their biggest champions.

Normally, when a chief executive resigns after misleading his company about his ties to a customer under federal investigation and is caught days later in an undisclosed relationship with a direct report, his allies do not march on Washington to demand his job back. Yet within three days of Jensen Huang’s exit from Nvidia, supporters had assembled a “war room” to pressure directors and the White House for his reinstatement, backed by an open letter claiming more than 131,000 unverified signatures.

Why the panic? Because the campaign to restore Huang is about far more than absolving a revered founder. With just 3.6% of a single-class stock, Huang cannot vote himself back into power, and returning him requires ousting the directors elected to oversee him, in a replay of OpenAI’s 2023 board collapse except on a $4 trillion stage. If the petitioners win, Nvidia becomes an autocracy in all but name. And for the insiders leading the charge, keeping one-man rule intact may be the only way to protect an unstable empire of handshake deals, circular financing, and national-security blind spots from a long-overdue reckoning.

Telegram’s Pavel Durov captured the ethos of the reinstatement campaign in two sentences: “Jensen made a rookie mistake. He had a board.” Around that creed, a small circle of founders and investors—several of them major Nvidia customers or shareholders—has tried to cast basic corporate governance as historic malpractice. Of course they might add that the other “rookie mistake” is that they took other people’s money to build their business, which requires such fiduciary oversight.

Epic Games founder Tim Sweeney called the exit “an Apple firing Steve Jobs level of idiocy,” while Y Combinator’s Garry Tan signed the petition and Andreessen Horowitz’s Martin Casado lamented a “dark, dark day.” Closer to Nvidia’s balance sheet, Elon Musk, a top chip buyer whose SpaceX counts Nvidia as a $21 billion shareholder, signed as well, while fund managers Gerstner and Baker pressed directors in a more measured letter to weigh every option, including “the potential return of Jensen Huang.”

What these defenders portray as a boardroom coup was actually a reluctant last resort. Nvidia’s directors stood behind Huang on Nov. 8 and reversed course only when general counsel Tim Teter insisted, after Huang’s own candor gave out. Having denied for 13 months that his tie to an executive at Megaspeed—a customer under federal investigation—went beyond business, Huang came clean to Teter only under press scrutiny and assured him there were no other relationships. Days later, when the Times revealed a relationship with a direct report, Huang stepped down. An independent Wachtell inquiry is already delivering the due process his backers claim to want, and even one ally working to reinstate him admitted, “having an affair with a subordinate is a fireable offense.”

Why do such accomplished people care so much? Because under Huang, Nvidia stopped merely selling chips and became what The New York Times called “a central banker for start-ups that needed chips and funding.” Equity stakes in OpenAI, Anthropic, SpaceX, CoreWeave, Intel, and others ballooned from roughly $7 billion to $99 billion on the balance sheet in a single year through July. Beyond its own cash, Nvidia backstopped an OpenAI-leased Ohio data center campus with up to $105 billion in conditional guarantees and enlisted Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR under nonbinding memoranda to marshal over $500 billion in outside capital for its buyers. Because nearly every recipient of that financing also buys Nvidia’s silicon, the boom rests on a circular wager where, as Bloomberg observed, the investor “loses twice” in a downturn.

Huang’s allies fear the web will unravel without the man who spun it. Musk gave the game away when he noted that many supply deals “were a handshake with Jensen, not a written contract.” His defenders offer that as tribute, but fiduciaries should read it as an indictment. Customer and supplier ties, as D.A. Davidson’s Gil Luria rightly argues, “should transcend” any single executive, and AI’s promise is real enough to outlast every founder building it today. A $4 trillion enterprise held together by one man’s handshakes is the condition a board exists to cure, and financing that survives only under a single personality was built wrong.

Heavy as those balance-sheet risks are, the national security record weighs heavier still. Since 2022, Washington has barred Nvidia’s most advanced chips from China. But Megaspeed, whose former executive a classified U.S. intelligence memo reportedly tied to China’s Ministry of State Security, bought more than $2 billion of Nvidia technology in roughly a year. Even after the federal investigation into Megaspeed became public in October 2025, Nvidia kept the customer and listed it as a cloud partner. All the while, Huang lobbied successfully to loosen the very rules at issue.

To be sure, no charge has been filed against Huang or Nvidia. The company denies knowing of any diversion, and China dismisses the espionage claim as a smear. The unresolved questions argue for letting investigators finish their work, and the loudest calls to keep Huang’s return alive come from those with the most to lose. Reinstating Huang by petition before the inquiry into his own conduct concludes would place the subject of the investigation directly above the people conducting it.

That inquiry leads straight into Nvidia’s own financial filings. Nvidia maintains that customers merely centralize invoicing in Singapore and that US-based buyers accounted for more than 99 percent of the controlled data center revenue billed there.

However, knowing who paid the invoice leaves open who ultimately used the chips. Malaysia has drawn more than $34 billion in data center investment, with ByteDance and Alibaba identified as customers at Nvidia-equipped sites, a legal rental arrangement that investors were never shown.

AI will reshape medicine, science, and industry, and Nvidia’s engineers will help build that future under whoever leads next. Durable technologies scale on contracts, compliance, and candor, the unglamorous machinery that outlasts any founder. Nvidia’s directors spent three decades deferring to one man and one week governing. Shareholders should insist the governing continue.

Ultimately, Jensen Huang built one of the great companies in American history. But that does not earn him a waiver from U.S. national security, chip export laws, securities laws or his own code of conduct, no matter how much noise a small circle of rogue tech bros make on X.

This tech bro mob must think that Huang should embrace Cornelius Vanderbilt’s retort, “Law, what law?  I am the law.”
