Stop agonizing over the ‘right’ choice—to avoid regret, save time and reduce stress, take these 3 steps

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

If you want to be successful in your career, your ability to make a decision, map out a plan and take swift action is crucial.

In 70% of the 20 fastest-growing occupations, as identified by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, judgment and decision-making are considered key soft skills, according to a ResumeNow report published Nov. 12. A November 2025 report from the Burning Glass Institute found that decision-making skills — including problem-solving, risk analysis and strategic thinking — were "one of the most enduring and economically valuable human capabilities" in the age of artificial intelligence, with 41% of more than 6.8 million U.S. job postings from 2022 to 2024 explicitly mentioning them.

When the time comes to make an important decision, you naturally want to get it right. So you might research, examine every variable and analyze the data, only to find yourself completely unable to move forward. This analysis paralysis is often rooted in perfectionism, as you tell yourself: "I need to make the absolute best choice…"

The problem with this mindset is that there's no such thing as a perfect choice. As a cognitive behavioral psychotherapist who specializes in anxiety and works with high performers, I know that maximizing — the never-ending quest to find a flawless or ideal choice — is a trap.

In my experience, the happiest and most successful professionals don't waste time hunting for a flawless path or agonize over making the "right" decision. They pick a direction and channel their energy into making that decision work.

Landmark research by psychologist Barry Schwartz, author of "The Paradox of Choice," and his colleagues shows that "maximizers" who obsess over finding the single best option end up experiencing higher levels of regret and depression and lower well-being than "satisficers," who settle on a solid choice that meets their core standards. Maximizing squanders precious time, energy and resources, and leads to choice overload, elevated anxiety and lower overall satisfaction.

Coming from a cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) perspective — where the core idea is that our thoughts affect how we feel, our feelings affect how we behave and our behaviors reinforce our thoughts — maximizing is rooted in unhelpful thinking styles called cognitive distortions. These patterned, habitual ways of thinking aren't necessarily based on fact, but feel compellingly true.

Specifically, maximizers get caught in black-and-white thinking, where a choice is framed as strictly good or bad, right or wrong, smart or dumb, and in catastrophizing, which means focusing exclusively on the absolute worst-case scenario.

These rigid, all-or-nothing thought patterns and worst-case predictions directly fuel chronic anxiety, emotional paralysis and avoidant behavior, according to foundational CBT research by Aaron Beck and Judith Beck. For example, a maximizer might spiral down this road: "If I don't make the perfect choice, I'll get fired and lose my reputation, and Brad will get my office!"

The reality? Initial decisions rarely need to be as set in stone as we might imagine. Success is rarely about choosing the single "correct" door on the first try. It's determined by making a sound decision and executing it with a flexible, dedicated approach.

To avoid the maximizing trap, take these steps:

Maximizing is appealing because it gives you the illusion of productivity; you feel like you're working hard by pouring energy into finding the single right choice. In reality, exhaustive research can be a waste of time and mental energy.

This is why establishing "good enough" criteria is essential, based on Nobel Prize-winning economist Herbert Simon's concept of satisficing. It may feel risky at first, especially if your decision-making is being hijacked by catastrophic thoughts about worst-case scenarios. But over-analyzing is actually the riskier path.

Start by identifying the non-negotiable criteria for success. Then make a rule that you'll spend no more than two hours on background research, consult only three resources or review just two or three vendor proposals.

Once an option meets, say, 80% to 90% of your benchmarks, shut down the search phase and lock in your decision. Save the rest of your energy for planning and execution.

Indecision and its close cousin, procrastination, function as avoidance strategies I see people use to temporarily escape short-term stress and anxiety. Time fuels analysis paralysis, and your options will feel endless if you don't set boundaries.

Psychological research on procrastination confirms that delaying decisions can be an emotional regulation strategy rather than a time-management issue.

To manage this anxiety loop, set a strict deadline on your calendar and commit to making a call once time is up. You can also create smaller time goals for different phases of the process, such as data gathering and resource identification.

Remind yourself: A solid, well-executed decision made today beats a perfect decision delayed until next month.

Every decision you make will eventually be subject to change. The more valuable skill isn't finding the ultimate answer, but cultivating flexibility and adaptability.

Clinical research on psychological flexibility shows that the ability to adapt your thinking and pivot in real time is associated with lower anxiety and greater resilience under pressure. When you learn to shift your focus from pre-decision evaluation to post-decision adaptability, you can make almost any sound choice the right choice.

When you have thoughts like "I have to make the right choice," try to reframe them by asking yourself instead: "How will I pivot if this doesn't go according to plan?"

It makes sense to want to get things right from the start. But even the most successful people aren't fortune tellers. So instead of front-loading the task by pouring valuable time and energy into maximizing, top performers save their precious resources for execution — and have the confidence to know they can handle the outcomes as they unfold.
