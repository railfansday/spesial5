Singapore Airlines CEO May Rethink New Business Class Colors, And He Should

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Singapore Airlines has made a mistake with the colors of its new business class cabin. The gray and black surfaces with bright yellow accents look more like a cheap hybrid of Spirit Airlines and Scoot than the premium airline whose name is on the aircraft. Now CEO Goh Choon Phong has left the door open to changes. I hope Singapore Airlines takes that opportunity.

When I covered the new Singapore Airlines business class seat, I welcomed several functional improvements, particularly the redesigned footwell. Singapore has addressed complaints about sleeping comfort and added features passengers increasingly expect.

I keep looking at the photographs and wondering how this particular color scheme made it through the approval process. Singapore Airlines has spent decades cultivating an identity associated with understated luxury. Its warmer, muted cabin colors help communicate that identity before a “Singapore Girl” even shows up to offer a welcome drink.

The new business class looks disconnected from it. The yellow accents against the gray and dark surfaces suggest a budget airline or a gaming chair. Neither association strikes me as particularly useful for Singapore Airlines.

During the launch Q&A, executives defended the decision to move in a different visual direction.

A post shared by Brandon · The Points Wanderer (@thepointswanderer)

Vice President of Product Experience Ong Wah Li said customer research had identified a desire for a more contemporary appearance. She described the resulting palette as “modern and refreshed, yet elegant and refined.”

Chief Commercial Officer Lee Lik Hsin emphasized that the Singapore Airlines experience extends beyond cabin colors. He defended the different palette, saying:

“We do not feel that a different colour palette creates any kind of inconsistency.”

Of course the Singapore Airlines experience involves more than colors. Service and comfort are far more consequential over a long flight. But the airline does not invest in cabin design because appearance is irrelevant. It understands that the surroundings help establish what passengers think they are buying.

Asking for something “modern” does not mean asking for this. Singapore Airlines could refresh its interiors while preserving the warmth and restraint that make them recognizable. I simply do not understand why contemporary design should require such a sharp departure from the brand. Plenty of modern interiors feel inviting and expensive without a bright yellow accent running through the seat.

At a November 11 employee town hall, Goh acknowledged the negative reaction. According to The Business Times, he said:

“We’ll look at it, we’ll see what is feasible.”

He also expressed confidence that passengers would appreciate the seats once they experienced their comfort and practical features.

They may well do so. I hope I do too. But enjoying the seat would not make the color choice better. I can appreciate a more comfortable bed while wishing the cabin looked like Singapore Airlines…and the fact that I say that underscores what a brand Singapore has built and should be careful not to fritter away over idiotic consultants who are out of touch with Singapore’s customer base.

Goh has not promised a redesign, and I would not describe this as a reversal. Still, his willingness to consider what can be changed is welcome. We all make mistakes. There is considerably more dignity in acknowledging one and correcting it than spending years defending it.

The contrast with the new Singapore Airlines first class suite makes the business class decision even stranger.

PriestmanGoode designed first class, while Designworks, a BMW Group company, designed business class. Factorydesign handled premium economy and economy.

First class looks like a natural evolution of Singapore Airlines. The warmer finishes and batik details give the new suite a connection to the airline without making it look old. It demonstrates that you can introduce a substantially different product while retaining a recognizable identity.

I worked closely with PriestmanGoode during my time at Star Alliance and have a great appreciation for its work. Looking at these two cabins, I would ask the firm to help bring the business class finishes into the same visual family.

Designworks does have aviation experience, including work with STARLUX and EVA Air. Nevertheless, I think it has badly misjudged this assignment. The BMW association does not automatically make the result luxurious, and this particular result does not look worthy of Singapore Airlines.

I am not particularly impressed with the new premium economy aesthetic either, though that is a different firm’s work. Ultimately, Singapore Airlines approved all these designs. The responsibility for how they fit together belongs to the airline.

There is nothing inherently wrong with yellow. It works for Scoot because Scoot has a different identity and sells a different proposition. It worked for Spirit Airlines (until it didn’t…).

But Singapore Airlines has little reason to make its premium cabin evoke its own budget subsidiary. Nor does the comparison with Spirit help communicate the experience it wants passengers to pay for, right?

That is particularly strange in Southeast Asia, where travelers have plenty of experience distinguishing a full-service premium carrier from a budget airline. Singapore Airlines should reinforce what makes it different.

The yellow stripe will not suddenly send loyal passengers to another carrier: I don’t think people will book away from Singapore because they don’t like the seat color. But why should that be even a consideration? Singapore Airlines has the resources to deliver both comfort and an interior that reflects its reputation.

The earthy, muted colors have worked because they convey quality. This new palette feels like a needless attempt to be different, with too little consideration for what is being lost.

I welcome the functional improvements in the new Singapore Airlines business class seat and look forward to trying it. But the color scheme is a mistake. Sorry, it is. To my eyes, it cheapens the appearance of a product intended to command a premium.

Goh has indicated that the airline will consider what is feasible. My suggestion is straightforward: retain the improvements to comfort, ask PriestmanGoode to help reconsider the finishes, and give business class an appearance consistent with the airline passengers have chosen. Dark brown would work well.

Singapore Airlines does not need to spend the next decade explaining why a cabin that reminds people of Scoot actually represents luxury. It has time to reconsider. I hope it does.
