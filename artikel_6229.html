What Republicans don’t know about Black voters’ resilience cost them

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Sen.-elect Scott Colom speaks during a victory rally at Tougaloo College on Nov. 11. (Brandon Bell/Getty Images)

The campaign of Mississippi’s Sen.-elect Scott Colom was rightfully praised for its clever and brutal ads. The “pick another protein” spot will forever be stuck in the brains of a huge swath of Americans, especially Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith. She underestimated Colom and his team, and will soon be an ex-senator.

However, it was fitting that the Democrat’s closing message traded the edge for a solemn reminder of who and what brought him so far on his journey to his election as Mississippi’s first Black U.S. senator since Reconstruction.

The ad delivered an all-American lesson about history, born of bravery and sacrifice.

Filmed at the Jackson, Miss., home of slain civil rights leader Medgar Evers, the ad featured Colom recounting the story of Evers, a World War II veteran who survived Normandy, only to be shot in the back by a racist assassin, resentful that a Black man dared to exercise his rights as a citizen in the country he fought for.

“He was killed for helping Mississippians vote,” Colom said. “On Tuesday, use your vote and honor everyone who died for that right.”

In a state where voting is still racially polarized, it was clear that Colom would not win over a majority of white voters; he won an estimated 25 percent, better than Democrats in recent elections and cutting into Trump’s margins, but certainly not a path to victory.

Black voters in Mississippi made the difference, as they often have. With their allies in the cause of justice, they heeded the call. They showed up despite the state’s stringent and often confusing voting restrictions, purges of the voting rolls engineered by Republican Secretary of State Michael Watson (who used unverified data from a credit bureau), and the U.S. Supreme Court Louisiana v. Callais decision that led to a wave of Southern states breaking up Black-majority districts.

When the Mississippi purges resulted in “affidavit ballots” that required verification to count, as The Washington Post reported, young Black women tracked down those voters, one by one, to do everything they could to ensure voters’ efforts were not in vain.

Black voters mattered in states all over the country, but especially in the South, where low turnout has plagued Democrats, particularly in midterm elections.

Keisha Lance Bottoms was elected the first Black female governor in the country in Georgia, where Democrats won up and down the ballot. Newly elected senators Annie Andrews in South Carolina and James Talarico of Texas campaigned for and earned support from Black voters with proposals that rose above platitudes. And don’t sleep on North Carolina, where voters flipped seats the GOP tried to gerrymander into a hardened majority. The sight of hundreds of students from HBCU NC A&T State University, denied a campus early voting site, marching to the polls, must have made the ancestors smile.

In Florida and Louisiana, where Democrats had little financial support and lost their statewide races, Black voters brought them tantalizingly close to victory and energized for the contests to come.

I still don’t expect Republicans to give up the election shenanigans, done in the name of “election integrity” and color blindness but in reality designed to demoralize as much as suppress. Why try to win over a diverse electorate with an inclusive, policy-driven message when manipulating Black-majority districts can still work?

The number of Black U.S. House members in the next session of Congress will fall, offset by a few Democrats who survived in redrawn districts, including Shomari Figures of Alabama and Don Davis of North Carolina.

But you can’t redistrict a state, and you can’t skip town halls and expect tradition, support for the Confederacy and an “R” after your name to forever save you — the Hyde-Smith playbook.

"I blocked Scott Colom from being a federal judge, and you blocked him from being U.S. senator," she bragged, too soon, on election night.

Maybe she should have emphasized affordability, saving rural hospitals and serving all Mississippians, as Colom did.

What did GOP officials in those Southern red states think they would accomplish by focusing on their base and ignoring everyone else in states they were supposed to be serving?

Did they not pay attention in history class, or maybe they attended one of those post-integration private schools where an unedited version of America’s past might not have been on the lesson plan?

Did they believe that voters who remembered Evers, Fannie Lou Hamer, Vernon Dahmer and countless others would shrug and say “you win” when legislatures and elections officials quickly and cynically pounced to quiet their voices?

That would be disrespectful to all the people who worked for, sometimes died for rights they knew they may never enjoy, yet had a belief in America and the rightness of their cause.

Scott Colom and the Evers family remembered. He was emotional in his reaction, in an MS NOW interview, when asked about the statement from Reena Evers-Everette, daughter of Medgar and Myrlie Evers. “Their courage helped make this moment possible,” she said.

Colum agreed: “I absolutely stand on their shoulders. And from the bottom of my heart, I’m going to do everything I can to make them proud.”

Post-election, he has been very gracious, saying that he intends to serve all Mississippians, including the woman whom I suspect would hit a different note if the election had turned in her favor.

I have no doubt Scott Colom means it. He knows who paid the price to get him this far.
