How to Inspire the Thanksgiving Spirit Year-Round

The tradition of Thanksgiving is an inspiring example of community and sharing that has strengthened North America for centuries. In February and March 2026, Gallup and the Walton Family Foundation asked 6,732 American citizens what motivates them to give to their communities. They wanted to know whether opportunity, another powerful American driving force, is still going strong. They asked whether people believe they can shape their own futures to build better lives and stronger communities, and how those views influence their willingness to give back.

They found that regardless of location or demographics, 72% of U.S. adults surveyed say the area where they live is worth investing in, even though nearly half do not agree that people in their community look out for one another or work together to solve common problems. Only 1 in 3 believe that if they had an idea to improve their community, someone would listen, and 60% do not trust their local leaders to act in the community's best interest. However, if they believe they will be heard, 2 out of 3 respondents are motivated to give back.

Beyond being listened to, citizens cited other factors that shape their love for and support of their communities. More than 8 in 10 say access to nature improves their quality of life. Americans living in places with closer interpersonal ties are more likely to feel like they can shape the direction of their lives. More than three-quarters believe housing is unaffordable, and nearly half say their communities do not offer enough stable jobs.

The researchers believe these responses reflect the psychological concept of agency, defined in the Cambridge Dictionary as the ability to take action or choose what action to take. They find that self-determination, or agency, is the most important predictor of adults’ assessment of their life satisfaction. Their data show that in communities that build strong foundations and connections among their residents, well-being and optimism flourish.

The foundation’s statistics are affirmed by the 2026 findings of Payne & Schimmack, professors at the University of Toronto's Psychological & Brain Sciences department. They also study the impact of opportunity, or agency, on individuals. They surveyed 1,200 adults from Canada and the United Kingdom to measure their positive and negative feelings, life satisfaction, and three psychological traits: autonomy—the feeling of being free to make choices; competence—feeling effective and capable; and relatedness—feeling close and connected to others.

Their findings unwrap the statistics and responses reported by Gallup and Walton. They find that whether happy or sad, autonomy—the sense of freedom to make choices—is the strongest indicator of life satisfaction. "Those who felt more autonomous were more satisfied with their lives," said Payne, who said the findings have practical implications for public policy. "Programs and interventions designed to improve well-being may succeed in improving feelings, but if they restrict people's choices, then they could ultimately backfire and lead people to judge their lives as worse overall.”

The Walton Family Foundation sums it up: “Communities grow stronger when people have a voice and a way to contribute.” They find that those closest to challenges are also closest to local solutions for local problems if they are supported to speak up and contribute. “When we listen to the people who know their communities best, we can turn their ideas into lasting change.”
