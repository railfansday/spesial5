Curiosity: The Most Underused Clinical Tool

Imagine a patient who works as a delivery driver. After a couple of months, the patient returns for a follow-up appointment with blood pressure that remains above the goal the physician set. The physician prescribed the appropriate daily medication, covered how to take it, and confirmed understanding. Despite this, the patient reports only taking the medication a few times a week. What the physician doesn’t know is that the medication increases trips to the bathroom and interferes with his tight delivery schedule.

What often happens in these scenarios? The healthcare provider spends the next several minutes of the appointment reiterating why taking the medication as prescribed is important, crucial even, for lowering blood pressure and all the risks associated with not taking it correctly. Before going through that conversation, another question worth asking may have saved both a lot of time: “Can you walk me through what happens on the days you don’t take your medication?” or “How are the days you don’t take medication different from the ones when you take your medication?” The answer can significantly change the conversation and provide the clinician with information tied to a particular issue or set of issues that can be addressed, while just another conversation about why it is important to take the medication as prescribed would have left the problem unaddressed.

Questions like these hold a spark of curiosity that allow the healthcare provider to get into the contextual layers of patients’ lives and uncover what the initial assessment may have left out. This is why curiosity deserves more attention as a legitimate clinical tool.

With experience, clinicians learn to recognize and identify patterns that help them interpret symptoms, anticipate potential complications, and develop evidence-based treatment plans. This is how medical students have been learning and how researchers and clinicians have been advancing treatment options for years. This is also how AI comes into play, through its processing power. The risk of relying solely on pattern recognition, without questioning and without curiosity, is becoming so comfortable with familiar patterns that we stop asking whether it fits the person across from us.

It becomes easy to interpret a missed appointment as a lack of commitment, or to read medication hesitation as resistance or failure to change a pattern, or even see unhealthy eating habits as a lack of motivation. The descriptor seems plausible, particularly when it feels like the pattern has been seen before. However, it is critical to remember that plausibility and confirmation are not the same thing.

Consider for a moment another patient, one who hasn’t scheduled a recommended evaluation. There are several potential explanations. Maybe transportation is an obstacle, or their work schedule is prohibitive. The patient might be terrified that the evaluation will confirm they have an illness that took their parent’s life. Each situation calls for a different conversation and different types of support.

The answers we receive may challenge our initial assumptions, and we need to be ready and willing to change the plan of action when they do.

In a 2013 article in the Annals of Internal Medicine, Weiner and colleagues conducted an observational study exploring how adapting care to a patient’s circumstances affected treatment outcomes. They found that when physicians asked the right questions, or gave room for patients to share context information, the problems the patient came in to address were significantly more likely to improve. Findings such as these highlight the importance of curiosity and using what we learn about patients’ lives to shape the care we provide.

So, for that patient working as a delivery driver, what would that look like? It could mean reviewing medication concerns and discussing medically appropriate prescription options that fit with his day-to-day responsibilities. For another patient who simply cannot afford the prescription, it could mean finding a more affordable alternative. And for someone who fears medical tests, it could mean creating space for the patient to understand and explain their fear and providing resources and support on how to move forward.

A thoughtful, curiosity-driven question is the starting point of this work. Its value will depend on what happens after the answer.

Including psychologists in the process can help care teams turn curiosity into a more holistic understanding of the patient. Psychologists’ training emphasizes examining how beliefs, emotions, habits, relationships, and their daily lived experience shape behavior. When a patient struggles with a treatment plan, that expertise can help the team move beyond initial impressions to investigate the factors making the plan difficult to follow.

In integrated care environments, this may mean taking time to explore a fear the patient has not yet expressed, understanding factors reinforcing a longstanding habit, or identifying where a prescribed routine fails to align with the realities of a patient’s daily responsibilities. Working alongside the patient as part of the care team, the psychologist translates that understanding into a care plan that properly fits the patient’s daily life.

Of course, appointments are usually short, good documentation takes time, and when an open question is asked, we risk revealing problems requiring more help than a single visit can provide. The care team is also often already stretched thin, with little time and too much to do.

These challenges are real. Curiosity isn’t going to solve the staff problem a clinic faces; it also won’t help create affordable housing, provide a replacement for lost insurance, or create healthier and more accessible food and exercise options everywhere.

Still, there are moments during an ordinary visit when a simple question can help redirect the work. “What about this plan has been hardest for you? “What concerns do you have about this treatment plan?” “Is this a plan you feel can fit in and work with your typical day?” Being curious and asking a few open-ended questions can help a patient feel seen, feel more part of the treatment process, and lead to better outcomes.

Before explaining, again, why a patient should follow a prescribed treatment plan, be curious and investigate what is making the plan difficult to follow.
