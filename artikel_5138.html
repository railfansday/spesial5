The UK FCA is taking a stricter stance on DeFi authorisation than the US and EU, citing fraud, consumer recovery gaps, and crypto money laundering.

London is taking a harder line on decentralised finance than both Washington and Brussels. Under its upcoming UK FCA DeFi regulation framework, the Financial Conduct Authority plans to require platforms carrying out regulated activities to obtain full authorisation.

Officials point to heightened fraud, scam, and crypto money laundering risks, alongside limited recovery options for retail victims.

Major DeFi builders are already pushing back against how the regulator defines control. In its November 18 consultation response, Aave Labs warned that the FCA’s proposals pull developers in opposite directions.

Regulators encourage platforms to monitor risks and screen illicit flows. Yet Aave argues the draft guidance could treat basic protocol maintenance, risk management, and sanctions screening as signs of centralisation, triggering full DeFi authorisation requirements even when software remains non-custodial.

The regulatory push gained urgency after October’s $6.3 billion Binance hack. Jane Moore, head of the FCA’s payments and digital assets department, warned that the speed and scale of stolen funds moving through DeFi protocols can frustrate efforts to freeze or block transfers.

U.S. court records filed on November 23 back up that concern. Federal prosecutors moved to forfeit roughly $1.1 billion in USDC tied to North Korean hackers, detailing how attackers routed stolen assets through THORChain, XRP swaps, and Bitcoin mixers.

While the breach hit a centralised exchange, officials remain focused on how quickly funds disappear across decentralised rails.

Despite recent headlines, the FCA opened its GC26/7 guidance consultation on October 21, which was eight days before the Binance attack, and closed responses on November 18.

The draft clarifies existing rules rather than writing new law, meaning writing code alone stays unregulated unless someone facilitates financial services.

The FCA plans to publish final guidance in January 2027. Authorisation applications close on February 28, 2027, ahead of the broader cryptoasset regime taking effect on October 25, 2027.

Until then, UK FCA DeFi regulation comes down to identifying who operates a regulated service, not whether a platform calls itself decentralised.
