Uniswap freezes $300,000 in stranded fees, halting automatic token burns

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Uniswap proposes a new bridge and declining UNI payment threshold to release roughly $300,000 in stranded X Layer fees.

Uniswap’s proposed repair for fees stranded on the X Layer network would change both the bridge used to burn UNI and the payment traders make to claim the backlog.

Uniswap Labs disclosed on Nov. 6 that protocol fees had been accruing on X Layer but could not be released, leaving the corresponding UNI unburned. Implementation reviews on Nov. 13-14 describe a higher initial UNI payment designed to reduce the windfall available to the first trader who clears that backlog.

Uniswap’s fee system relies on outside traders, known as searchers, supplying UNI in exchange for selected fee assets held in a TokenJar, a contract that collects those assets.

The payment goes toward burning UNI, and the searcher keeps the recoverable asset value above its UNI payment and execution costs.

Converting accrued fees into a completed UNI burn requires a searcher to execute the exchange and the bridge to settle it. The searcher’s net surplus is a separate return, so a release does not require Uniswap itself to buy UNI on the open market at that moment.

X Layer's governance messages use the OP Stack bridge, while token transfers use Agglayer. According to Labs, the installed relayer followed the usual OP Stack token-bridging pattern, but X Layer’s version rejects ERC-20 transfers, including UNI.

A searcher receives fee assets and supplies UNI. X Layer’s disclosed bridge mismatch interrupted the second leg; the proposed repair changes the route and payment.

Uniswap Labs said on Nov. 6 that fees accrued, but the installed OP token route prevented releases and corresponding UNI burns.

The plan uses Agglayer for UNI transfers and a declining payment. These are proposed mechanics; successful execution and settlement remain unestablished.

Accrued fees, searcher surplus and a settled UNI burn measure different things.

The currently edited description of Uniswap protocol-fees implementation PR 167, opened Nov. 2, estimates that roughly $300,000 of fees could be swept into the jar. It warns that restarting with the existing 2,000 UNI payment could hand the accumulated assets to the first searcher at that fixed price.

Uniswap’s collection architecture requires explicit collection calls for v3 and v4 fees, so a jar’s wallet balance alone doesn't capture everything accrued in pools.

PR 167 describes an initial threshold of 80,000 UNI, a 2,000 UNI floor and a two-day half-life. Its description estimates about 10.8 days to reach the floor.

The mechanism starts with an expensive claim and reduces the required payment over time. A searcher can act when the assets it can recover justify that payment and its costs, so competition could direct more backlog value into UNI payments instead of leaving the margin created by a low fixed threshold.

The proposed schedule remains inconsistent across the published plans: the Nov. 6 forum post says roughly six days after execution, while the reviewed implementation describes about 10.8 days.

Governance would start the decay after the TokenJar points to the new releaser. Anyone could update the threshold, and searchers could bundle that update with a release.

The setter would temporarily own the releaser, returning ownership and threshold-setting control to governance on the first update at the floor. The reviewed design removes a separate reclaim function, so elapsed time alone would not restore control.

As of Nov. 15, PR 169’s combined implementation remained a draft, listing the setter and replacement releaser deployment as blockers. The releaser and setter PRs remained open, while the official deployment registry still listed X Layer’s original releaser.

For scale, Strata Terminal reported $7.8 million in UNI burns over the preceding 30 days as of Nov. 15, and 14.1 million UNI in recurring burns overall, separate from the one-time 100 million UNI treasury burn.

That recurring flow gives the proposed one-time release context, but the older $300,000 backlog estimate uses a different observation date and valuation method. A direct conversion ratio would conflate an estimated stock of fees with realized burn flows.

The proposed repair’s economic test is how much UNI the release payment ultimately sends to Ethereum’s burn address.
