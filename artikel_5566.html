Ianalumab Extension Study Confirms Benefit in Sjogren's, Though Still Modest

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

— Much will hinge on third phase III study now underway

For patients with Sjögren's disease, a second year of treatment with the investigational anti-B cell drug ianalumab boosted response totals, raising the likelihood that it could eventually win approval.

Research presented at the American College of Rheumatology's recent annual meeting showed further improvements in EULAR Sjögren's Syndrome Disease Activity Index (ESSDAI) scores after already improving significantly, relative to placebo, with an initial 48 weeks of treatment.

Ianalumab targets B-cell activating factor (BAFF) and is also lethal to B cells. The expectation is that this double whammy will make the drug more durably effective than other B-cell depleting agents like rituximab (Rituxan). It's also being tested in a range of other conditions marked by B-cell pathology, including lupus and systemic sclerosis.

As previously reported, participants in the so-called NEPTUNUS-1 and NEPTUNUS-2 randomized trials saw an average 6.5-point decrease from baseline in ESSDAI scores after the first 48 weeks of ianalumab treatment at 300 mg monthly. Continuing on that regimen in the open-label extension study for another 60 weeks brought an additional 1.0-point improvement, according to Thomas Dörner, MD, of Charité Universitätsmedizin Berlin, and colleagues.

More impressively, responder totals also increased substantially in the extension. Whereas 58.9% of patients in the main trials had achieved at least 5-point reductions in ESSDAI scores, the proportion rose to 68.4% by week 108. Numbers for the fraction of patients achieving ESSDAI scores less than 5, indicating low disease activity, were identical -- 58.9% at week 48 and 68.4% at week 108.

As well, almost one-quarter of participants initially randomized to monthly ianalumab achieved clinical remission (ESSDAI=0) at week 108, up from 13% at week 48.

Patients randomized to placebo in the main trials were re-randomized in the extension to receive ianalumab, at doses of 300 mg given either monthly or every 3 months. Among those getting the drug monthly, they too saw substantial improvements. Basically, by week 108, they reached the same degree of symptom relief that the original ianalumab groups had achieved at week 48.

All this is good news for ianalumab's sponsor, Novartis, for which the original NEPTUNUS findings were somewhat disappointing. Yes, the primary endpoint of a significant advantage over placebo in change-from-baseline in ESSDAI score was met in both trials, but just barely (P=0.0496 in NEPTUNUS-1). And other outcomes either failed to show a significant difference from placebo or the effect sizes were too small to matter much.

Although safety did not look to be a substantial problem -- and the extension data appeared to confirm that -- the modest-at-best efficacy did not bode well for FDA approval. So Novartis is now sponsoring another placebo-controlled phase III trial focusing on patients with a high symptom burden, the better to see a clear difference between the drug and placebo in effectiveness.

Two ianalumab doses were tested in the NEPTUNUS trials, 300 mg monthly or every 3 months. Those patients continued on these doses, while the placebo groups were re-assigned between them. The latter got their first ianalumab dose at week 60, meaning they then were under active treatment for 48 weeks at data cut-off for the current report.

Of the original 666 patients in the two studies, 610 entered the extension and 551 remained through week 108. In total, the extension is slated to last 5 years beyond the initial 1-year, double-blind period. The current report focused on those patients receiving monthly dosing in the extension, a total of 358, of which about one-third were from the original placebo groups.

A key secondary outcome was change in salivary flow. Participants with a modicum of flow at baseline did better than those with only minimal flow in the primary results, and this remained true in the extension. Dörner and colleagues stratified participants according to whether baseline salivary flow was more or less than 0.4 mL/min. The low-flow group saw little benefit from ianalumab, a 0.1-mL/min increase by week 48, and it remained at about that level through week 108 among those receiving the drug throughout. Not much improvement was seen, either, in the original placebo group after switching to ianalumab.

It was quite different in the group with baseline flow >0.4 mL/min. Those patients saw a 0.2-unit boost in flow by week 48, with an additional jump of 0.2 mL/min at week 72, although the mean value with continuous ianalumab at week 108 fell back to the level seen at week 48. Flow levels actually fell with placebo during the main trials, but recovered back to baseline levels with ianalumab in the extension.

No new side-effect concerns arose in the extension. At the time of data cut-off this past June, some patients had already been on the drug for 4 years (mean 135 weeks); 3% had experienced a serious adverse event considered related to therapy, with 1% stopping the therapy because of such events. Systemic reactions to drug injections occurred in 12%-14% of patients; these appeared to wane as patients continued with treatment.
