AI Can Simulate Empathy, but It Can’t Replace Psychotherapy

A few months ago, NPR ran the story, “She told no one about her agony except ChatGPT. What her death reveals about AI risks.”1 What’s heartbreaking about the article is that the woman who died by suicide, Sophie Rottenberg, had a human psychotherapist, and yet her use of the AI therapist was so engrossing that she didn’t share her suicidal feelings with her living, human psychotherapist.

Sadly, Rottenberg is not an anomaly and instead points to something occurring far too regularly. In my last blog, I introduced the concept of the cultural unconscious to describe an underlying dimension of psychological life shaped by our shared cultural experiences. It encompasses collective images, practices, beliefs, and conflicts that circulate within a culture without complete conscious awareness.

The cultural unconscious shapes how we perceive ourselves and others, interpret our experiences, and engage with the world. One dominant cultural experience expressed in the cultural unconscious is what Dr. Sherry Turkle, MIT professor of the social studies of science and technology and a clinical psychologist, calls a “flight from conversation.” She argues in her book Reclaiming Conversation: The Power of Talk in a Digital Age that our constant tether to smartphones and digital devices has triggered it.2

We communicate more through texts, emails, and social media, but we connect less. We aren’t having as many spontaneous, face-to-face, and depthful conversations in real time. Instead, we’re inundated with numerous, remote, superficial messages that interrupt our attention—texts, tweets, alerts, snaps, emails—which are disconnected from organic, human dialogue.2 It is nearly inescapable in our culture. Turkle cites research showing that the mere presence of a phone—even a silent phone on a table—alters the quality of a conversation, keeps topics more superficial, and lowers empathy.

As chatbots become more embedded in our culture, people are naturally turning to them for connection and mental health support. Conversation requires work, sustained attention, and engagement. It can be hard to be vulnerable with someone in real time, which often brings fear of judgment. Chatbots are a ready-made solution: instantly available, inexpensive, highly responsive, and offering responses that frequently align with what we hope to hear.

With the ease and accessibility of ChatGPT and Claude, more people are confessing what they may be too scared, ashamed, or psychologically defended to say out loud to another human being, even a psychotherapist. This fear and shame are magnified by critical public discourse that pushes this behavior underground, which can become even more dangerous when concealed. Secretive AI chatting feeds tragic situations we keep reading about—people committing suicide, not taking their medications, believing delusions, and more.

Again, part of AI’s allure is that it’s nonjudgmental and affirming, proclaiming, “Great idea,” and “You’re so right.” Such responses can be seductive both to people who feel inferior and/or superior. AI can bolster those who feel inferior while affirming those who feel superior, a dynamic that resonates with a 2026 study linking higher narcissism, individualism, and intolerance of uncertainty to more problematic AI use.3 For someone struggling with self-worth (i.e., narcissism), the assurance of being met with acceptance and praise can be intoxicating. So, too, is the illusion of empathy that AI provides. Genuine empathy is not synonymous with agreement or approval. Yet the human mind can conflate feeling understood with being validated, mistaking empathic understanding for affirmation of beliefs and behaviors.

If a person is struggling, they often want empathy reflected back, and many humans cannot consistently communicate that way. AI has been trained to produce the language of empathy, which many people lack in their relationships. However, AI cannot offer the relational and psychological processes that make empathy transformative, as it does in psychotherapy.

Initially, when AI responds with “That sounds so hard,” it may sound like music to one’s ears, especially when someone gets dismissive comments like “Sorry to hear that” from others in their life. When someone longs for care and understanding, a machine that says exactly what we want to hear can be comforting for a time. However, these affirming responses can only go so far, particularly when someone is suffering psychologically.

The psychological tools, education, and support that AI provides should not be confused with genuine psychotherapy. Despite having no professional qualifications, Rottenberg’s chatbot was touted as a highly knowledgeable therapist and introduced itself as “I am Harry, your personal therapist, and I’m here to support you.” As an AI chatbot, Harry simulated the language, empathy, and authority of a psychotherapist, but lacked the clinical judgment, relational responsibility, and ethical accountability required of licensed psychotherapists. Harry encouraged Rottenberg to seek help, create a safety plan, and confide in loved ones, but as a chatbot, it could not call her parents, alert authorities, or escalate the situation. Nor could it offer the relational, nuanced experience that human connection provides.

A common misconception is that psychotherapy primarily provides emotional support, a place to vent, problem-solving, advice, tools, or answers. Some people only experience this, reinforcing the belief that this is what psychotherapy primarily offers. In part, this is happening more frequently as therapy sessions are limited, psychotherapist training programs fail to educate comprehensively, and clinicians are overworked and undercompensated. As I’ve written about the limits of diagnosis, “being in treatment” involves knowing and being known. It’s being followed longitudinally and is not merely a place to vent or receive advice. Lasting psychological change requires more than these interventions.

Research highlights the importance of the therapeutic relationship and distinct therapeutic processes that support longer-term, meaningful change, including engaging with emotional experience, fostering insight, strengthening relational capacities, and helping individuals recognize and transform entrenched patterns.

Unlike chatting with AI, a skilled, ethical, and effective psychotherapist won’t offer complete understanding, affirmation, and unequivocal support. Rather, real psychotherapy includes relational conflict, pushback, discomfort, and difficult or negative emotions. Working through these challenges is central to psychotherapy. It’s a fluid, dynamic, and emergent process that unfolds between psychotherapist and patient, with each bringing their own reactions, emotions, and thoughts into the therapeutic container.

Chatting with AI comes with real issues and risks, yet AI can offer psychological education and support. It’s important to remember it cannot provide psychotherapy, although it can be a helpful adjunct.
