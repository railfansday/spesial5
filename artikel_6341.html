Older adults get tailored plans for fall prevention, but a simple leaflet may work just as well

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Across 110 trials, tailored fall prevention plans for older people worked no better than basic advice, and people on them fell slightly more often.

A doctor who worries that an older patient might fall can order the full workup. Balance, eyesight and medicines get checked, someone looks at the loose rugs at home, and a plan is then built around those findings. It sounds like the gold standard of fall prevention.

However, pooled across the trials that have tested it, that tailored plan probably prevents no more falls than a leaflet of good advice. People on the plans actually fell slightly more often.

The result comes from a new Cochrane review, a kind of study that gathers every reliable trial on a question and pools the numbers. This one covered 110 trials and 48,919 people aged 60 and over. All participants were living at home rather than in care.

Lianne Wood and Cynthia Swarnalatha of the University of Exeter led the review. The co-authors were at the University of Oxford and the University of Sydney.

In an interview with Earth.com, Wood said the next job is to work out what the tailored programs contain that plain advice doesn’t.

“At the moment we are not sure why the results show that education is possibly more effective than tailored interventions,” she said.

Researchers call the tailored approach a multifactorial program. A health professional checks a person for two or more risks that can be changed, then treats or refers each one.

That might mean an exercise class for weak legs, a medicine review, or a handrail for the bathroom.

Seven trials with 9,901 people compared that approach with falls advice or education alone. The advice side was deliberately basic, often a standard leaflet such as Age U.K.’s Staying Steady, sent by post or handed over in a session.

People on the tailored plans had about 9 percent more falls per year than people given advice. The review rated that evidence as moderately certain. The chance of falling at least once, and of falling two or more times, was the same in both groups.

Against usual care, meaning no special fall prevention at all, the tailored plans did better, but not by much. Across 29 trials and 9,442 people, they cut the number of falls by about a quarter. In 17 trials, the risk of falling two or more times was about 13 percent lower.

The chance of falling at least once barely moved, across 38 trials and 12,774 people. Fall-related fractures and hospital stays didn’t change either.

The review rated all of those results as low-certainty evidence. This was because results varied from trial to trial and many trials had weaknesses in design.

Asked by Earth.com what surprised her most once the trials were pooled, Wood said it was what did not happen.

“We were surprised that there were no other big changes to the previous review, despite adding many more people and trials of higher quality,” she said. “Much of the pooled results are very similar to the 2018 review with low quality evidence.”

The review also tested the untailored version, called a multiple component program, in which everyone gets the same bundle, regardless of their own risks. Forty-four trials used that design, and one tested both approaches.

Against usual care, those bundles made little or no difference to the number of falls, though they may have lowered the chance of falling at least once by about 12 percent. Against exercise alone, and against advice alone, they made little or no difference on any measure.

Side effects were generally mild, mostly short-lived aches from exercise, and serious ones were rare.

The components themselves were familiar. Of the 66 tailored-program trials, 53 included home safety changes or assistive equipment, 49 included exercise, and 36 included a medicine review.

Wood was asked what a health service that runs these programs should do on Monday morning.

“So, until we know more, we’d advise people to continue as normal,” she told Earth.com.

Asked what the best evidence does support, she named two things. “We know that falls prevention education and advice to start with exercise appears to be effective, so this may be the area we need to put more emphasis on.”

Her view fits the direction guidelines have already taken. The 2025 guidance in the United Kingdom puts strength and balance training at the center of prevention, and the 2022 World Falls Guidelines call exercise a core intervention.

Exercise and standard advice may also take less time and money to roll out across a whole population than individual assessments.

The review pooled every tailored program together, so it can’t say whether some combinations of components work better than others. The authors wrote that this question needs a different kind of analysis.

In most trials, participants knew which group they were in, and most relied on people reporting their own falls. Six of the comparisons showed signs that trials with weak results were less likely to have been published.

Trials that counted harder outcomes, such as fractures, have generally shown disappointing results, the authors wrote.

The evidence also comes from a narrow slice of the world. The tailored-program trials came from 22 countries, but most were run in Australia, the United Kingdom and the United States. Only 17 of those 66 trials reported participants’ ethnicity, and some trials excluded people with dementia.

“There still remains a real lack of studies in low to middle income countries and people of different ethnicities, so understanding the effects of treatments in these groups remains a real priority,” Wood said.

About one in three people over 65 fall at least once a year. In the United States alone, falls in that age group led to about 4.5 million emergency department visits in 2024, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

Previous falls, muscle weakness and poor balance all raise the risk. A fall can also leave a fear of falling again, which the review links to faster physical decline and a retreat from social life.

Co-author Sarah Lamb of the University of Exeter said the results show how much is still unknown about why falls happen in the first place.

“We’re now conducting a major study to understand why people have unstable walking and are at higher risk of falls,” Lamb said.

“Combined with this review, it will help us design the best prevention programs in future, to reduce frailty and help people live well for as long as possible.”

Next, the authors want a single, large trial that tests home changes and exercise, alone and together, against usual care.

They also want wearable sensors recording falls instead of diaries. Until that trial runs, the pooled record says a tailored plan and a leaflet do about the same job, and nobody can yet say why.

The full study was published in the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews.
