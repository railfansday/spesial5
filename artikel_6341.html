Scientists discovered a tiny furry predator from 165 million years ago that hunted slippery prey in the water

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

This Jurassic mammal relative swam like a platypus and hunted with three-rooted fangs, and its throat was ready for swallowing and nursing.

In most pictures of the dinosaur age, early mammals and their relatives are tiny creatures keeping out of the way. Many did weigh just a few grams.

One of those relatives was a swimming predator that weighed a few pounds and hunted with fangs anchored by three roots each.

The animal’s throat bones were the bigger surprise. They carried the anchor points for muscles that today’s mammals use to swallow, drink and nurse, which puts key parts of the modern mammal throat long before modern mammals existed.

The animal, named Megacauda sungei, comes from rock in Inner Mongolia, China, that formed in the middle of the Jurassic period, when dinosaurs dominated life on land.

That rock is between 163 and 168 million years old. Scientists know the animal from a single skeleton, now held by the Paleontological Museum of Liaoning.

Researchers from Shenyang Normal University, Shandong University of Science and Technology, the University of Chicago and the University of Bonn described it. Thomas Martin of Bonn, Zhe-Xi Luo of Chicago and Chang-Fu Zhou of Shandong led the work.

Megacauda belonged to the docodonts, an extinct group of animals closely related to true mammals. The name means “big tail,” after its broad, flat tail.

The team estimated it weighed between 2.2 and 4.4 pounds (1 and 2 kilograms). That makes it the largest known mammal relative from the Jurassic.

It was about twice the weight of its closest known relative, and 150 to 200 times heavier than a tiny docodont found in the same area.

The upper canine teeth stood about 60 percent taller than the lower ones, and each had three roots, a setup that no known mammal shares. Grooves and ridges along those fangs likely strengthened them for puncturing prey.

Farther back, the cheek teeth had blade-like points that sliced past each other, much like the teeth of seals. The team thinks Megacauda was a major predator that ate salamanders, fish and small water creatures without backbones.

The skeleton shows a body made for water. The tail was wide and flattened, with an outline closer to a giant otter’s than to the rounded tail of a beaver or platypus.

The hind feet were long, with long outer toes like those of a platypus or a sea otter. Its hip carried the kind of muscle attachments a platypus uses to hold its legs close to its body when it swims.

Up front, the limbs had features for digging. The team concluded that it lived much like a platypus does, hunting in water and sheltering in burrows on land.

Megacauda probably wasn’t as strong a swimmer as an otter or a beaver. Its tail muscles didn’t extend as far down the tail as they do in modern swimmers, so its tail likely gave it less push.

The rarest part of the fossil is the roof of the throat. CT scans showed the bones in three dimensions, including a small, bony hook at the back of the skull.

In marsupials and placental mammals, a muscle wraps over that hook and tightens the soft palate, the flexible back of the roof of the mouth. That firm palate gives the tongue something to press against when a baby suckles milk or an adult drinks.

A groove beside the hook likely anchored a second muscle. In living mammals, that muscle pulls the throat up against the soft palate during swallowing, sealing food and drink off from the nose.

Saddle-shaped bones under the throat, like those in many living mammals, completed the set.

Alongside the modern-looking hook, the skull kept older ridges seen in earlier mammal relatives.

Muscles don’t fossilize, so the team rebuilt them from where they attach in living animals, including an opossum. Based on that, they think the swallowing, drinking and nursing equipment arrived together, not one piece at a time.

Other docodonts lived in trees or burrowed underground like moles. Over about 100 million years they spread across the northern continents. Megacauda is the earliest known case of a mammal relative living partly in water.

“This shows how widespread docodonts were in the Northern Hemisphere and that this diverse group of animals developed very different lifestyles at an incredibly early stage,” Martin said.

The same mix of swimming and digging turned up again about 120 million years later, in an extinct group of mammals called pantolestids. About 30 million years after that, it arose once more in the group that includes otters and weasels.

Every conclusion here rests on one animal. The weight is an estimate from its skull and from similar-sized platypuses, and the soft palate and throat muscles are inferred from bone.

Whether Megacauda actually nursed its young, or how often it drank, can’t be read from bone alone.

Answering that would take more docodont fossils with their throats intact, along with the feeding structures of the earliest mammals for comparison.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.
