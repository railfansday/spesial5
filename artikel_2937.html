Why Data Residency Is Not Resilience

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Sharad Kumar is Co-Founder and CEO of FluidCloud, which uses Cloud Cloning to move enterprise workloads across any cloud in seconds.​

A few years ago, our company was acquired, and we had to move our infrastructure from one cloud account to another on the same provider, using the same tools.

Our team had spent years building cloud security software and knew the provider’s APIs better than most customers ever will. I assumed the move would take a few weeks. It took nearly a year.

This project comes to mind every time an executive tells me their company could leave its cloud provider if it had to. I’m the co-founder and CEO of a company that helps enterprises move workloads between clouds, and I often ask, “Have you tried to switch providers?” Almost nobody has.

Most sovereign cloud programs focus on which country the data sits in. That’s “residency,” which is the easiest of the three considerations hidden inside the word “sovereignty.”

The second consideration is operational. Who administers the environment? Where do those decisions get made? Could the estate keep running if an external control plane becomes unavailable?

The third consideration is about independence: Can you keep operating without any single provider? An estate can satisfy residency and operational control, and the answer to that one can still be no.​

Moving a critical workload to a sovereign provider can leave you less able to recover.

The confinement to one jurisdiction is deliberate, as it’s exactly what you’re buying. It also removes the mechanism hyperscalers use to survive a regional failure: replication to a site that is geographically and legally distant. Two data centers inside one country don’t give you that. A provider that crossed the border to give it to you would be breaking its own promise.

Provider choice is the second problem. If a hyperscaler disappoints you, you have alternatives with comparable capability. If an in-country sovereign provider disappoints you, you have a short list, and each option runs its own partly proprietary stack. Leaving a sovereign cloud is often harder than leaving the hyperscaler you left to get there.

So, a migration presented to a board as reducing dependence on big tech often only moves concentration risk rather than reducing it. If you document it as a deliberate trade-off and assess the residual risk, that’s a defensible decision. If you present it as a risk reduction, it’s an audit finding waiting to happen.

European rules have turned this from a strategy discussion into an evidence requirement.

The Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) ​took effect for EU financial entities in January 2025. Its Article 28(8) requires exit plans for information and communication technology (ICT) services supporting critical or important functions to be “comprehensive, documented and … sufficiently tested and reviewed periodically.” The key word is tested. A plan nobody has run doesn’t clear that bar, however long it is.

The EU Data Act has applied since September 2025 and approaches the same problem from the provider side. It lets customers switch providers with no more than two months’ notice, and switching charges disappear entirely on 12 January 2027. That removes some of the contractual and commercial locks. The technical locks stay exactly where they are. The right to leave and the ability to leave are two different things.

Testing an exit plan doesn’t need a replatforming program. Take one critical workload, rebuild it somewhere else and put traffic through it. Use a sandbox if a live target carries too much risk. A dry run that surfaces the problems while they’re cheap to fix still beats a binder.

In my experience, you’ll likely see four things break during this process, in roughly this order.

Identity breaks first, because each provider has its own way of proving which workload is which and what it can touch.

Networking follows, because topology, routing and private connectivity have to be rebuilt on the target.

Encryption and key custody come next, because where the keys live and who can use them are provider-specific answers.

The fourth problem is the one people resist most. Some services have no equivalent on the other side. Managed AI, advanced analytics and vendor-specific governance tooling often don’t exist on a smaller provider at any price. An honest exit test produces that list. The usual conclusion is a divided estate, with regulated workloads in-jurisdiction and the rest wherever the capability exists. That’s a legitimate answer, even if boards rarely welcome it.

The list is uncomfortable to read. It’s also the first real information a resilience program has, and it’s cheaper to produce on a random Tuesday than during an incident.

Infrastructure you can move is infrastructure you control.
