Ryan Grim Is Bringing the Manosphere Bros and Socialists Together

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Anyone still struggling to understand how Bernie Bros and MAGA voters overlap on the political Venn diagram might find answers in Ryan Grim’s audience.

Grim, a veteran journalist and self-described “leftist,” has cultivated a rather politically diverse and incongruous fan base over his two decades in journalism. On election night in Los Angeles earlier this month, a room full of socialists erupted in cheers when Grim appeared on the big screen at the Democratic Socialists of America’s watch party (Grim was a guest that night on Hasan Piker’s livestream). The next day, a friend who religiously listens to Tim Dillon, a podcaster credited with helping Trump win reelection in 2024, and Tony Hinchcliffe, a comedian who performed at a Trump rally that same year, told me that he was also a huge fan of Grim’s.

After working at Politico, HuffPost, and The Intercept, Grim, 48, cofounded Drop Site News, an independent nonprofit news organization, in 2024, and he’s also a cohost on the antiestablishment political podcast Breaking Points. He’s developed a reputation for being an equal opportunity critic of both parties, which Grim says helps explain why people all along the spectrum are attracted to his work. Even Steve Bannon (who called Grim an “intrepid reporter”) and former Republican congressman Matt Gaetz have occasionally thrown compliments his way.

“I think there’s a huge overlap with Breaking Points, Drop Site, and the whole podcast-bro world,” Grim tells Vanity Fair. “It does feel like we’ve basically become the primary news source for the podcast bros, which is not how you would have necessarily understood the world several years ago.”

But lest anyone think that Grim has changed his views to accommodate new fans, his frequent criticism of Israel’s war in Gaza has helped make him a target of Fox News host Mark Levin, who has called into question Drop Site’s nonprofit status.

In a wide-ranging conversation, Grim discusses his unlikely popularity among the podcast bros, why he thinks the Epstein files helped propel Democrats in 2026, why he doesn’t understand the protests against Jerry Seinfeld, and what his favorite jam band, Phish, taught him about tolerance.

This conversation has been edited and condensed for clarity.

Vanity Fair: You’ve talked about how being a leftist rather than a Democratic partisan has helped you build credibility with conservatives. Why is that distinction important?

Ryan Grim: [The conservatives] never thought I was a Democratic partisan, because I wasn’t. I think if you have a view of the world, people are willing to say, Okay, that’s his view of the world, and I’m going to metabolize what he’s reporting while taking into account that he has a particular view of the world.

But if you’re a partisan for Democrats or Republicans, that’s different because then you’re playing, like, strategic games with your information, and your view of whether or not something is good or bad changes with whether or not it’s good or bad for Democrats or good or bad for Republicans, rather than being grounded in some type of idea about the world.

And you’ve been criticizing Democrats for decades.

Yeah, I think what frustrates our critics is that they can’t be like, “Oh yeah, well, you didn’t say that when Biden was president or when Obama was president,” and it’s like, “Oh no, in fact, we said this and worse,” because it didn’t matter the color of the jersey. We believed that drone strikes were wrong, or shutting down ACORN is bad, and we’re going to say it no matter who’s in office.

Jeremy [Scahill] and I have been doing this for so long that we have that track record, so I think that has really helped us build that audience across the spectrum. It’s been pretty gratifying to develop that conservative audience. It’s weird sometimes to hear a Steve Bannon or a Matt Gaetz applauding your journalism. But it’s like, hey, that doesn't necessarily mean they agree with us about our conclusions. That’s not what we’re here for. We’re here to do the reporting.

You grew up around a lot of Moral Majority conservatives in rural Maryland, and you’ve said that even though you disagreed with their politics, you didn’t necessarily think they were bad people. Is that something the left has lost sight of?

They were almost universally pretty good people whose views I didn’t share as a young person and don’t now. But that’s just who they are.

I’ve talked to other people who still live in these rural areas, and they’re like, “How do I deal with these people who have these wretched beliefs?” I say, “Just deal with them as people.” They were kind of born believing that, and a lot of them are rethinking things all the time.

You seem to have a lot more patience for people you disagree with than some of your fellow leftists. How do you reconcile that with your own pretty uncompromising politics?

I try to live by the creed of [left-wing political commentator] Michael Brooks. He used to say, “Be ruthless to systems, be kind to people,” and that can be very hard. But to me the most important thing to do is try to understand that people operate within systems, and it is the system that you’re against, not the people.

I think you’re more likely to win converts by understanding what their role is in the system and keeping your criticisms at that systemic level.

I think sometimes people go a little bit crazy. I don’t agree with Jerry Seinfeld’s politics when it comes to Israel, but I don’t understand the value of protesting Jerry Seinfeld. Maybe he’s given some money or something, but like, in general, he’s not a decision-maker. What is the upside in making him a target of protest? That part I don’t understand.

Speaking of people who disagree with you, Mark Levin has been going after Drop Site News’s nonprofit status. How seriously are you taking his efforts?

So if tomorrow Levin succeeded, and we were no longer a nonprofit, we would then carry on as a for-profit supported by our readers.

I know the underlying hostility is he doesn’t like our reporting—he doesn’t want it to exist. But stripping our nonprofit status wouldn’t change that. It would just turn it into a lucrative for-profit company, which I don’t understand. Like, that’s his preferred outcome? That we make a bunch of money doing this? I don’t think it’s very well thought through.

But couldn’t this become a much bigger problem for journalism? There are organizations like ProPublica and The Texas Tribune that could theoretically face similar attacks.

Yes, and certainly the Trump administration has been very publicly saying that it plans to go after the entire nonprofit complex, and everybody from Sierra Club on down, because they consider them to be political enemies.

But [the Trump administration] has also gotten a lot of pushback on this from the conservative-nonprofit world, which is actually much more lucratively funded than the progressive side, because the conservatives have said [Democrats are] going to come for organizations like The Heritage Foundation when Republicans are no longer in power.

Let’s talk about the midterms. Moderates saw the blue wave as a vindication of the party’s affordability-focused strategy, while progressives saw it as a mandate for a more ambitious agenda. What was your biggest takeaway?

I think people are overlooking the role that the Epstein files and Trump’s cover-up, in particular, played. That was really a major break that eroded trust from his base and from independents.

We even did a survey right after he launched the war and asked people if they thought he was going to war with Iran to distract, at least in part, from the Epstein scandal. And by a 50-to-42 margin, the majority of the country said yes, he is.

Whether true or not, the Epstein scandal bled into Iran, which then drove up gas prices and created all of this intense anxiety. Meanwhile, Trump is focused on the ballroom, which he’s using to collect bribes.

People believed when they elected him that he was going to drain the swamp, release the files, start no new wars, and focus instead on bringing down prices. That was what people understood he was going to do. And on all four, he just did the exact opposite, in, like, an aggressive, in-your-face, I-don’t-care-about-you kind of way.

You recently discussed a forensic expert’s analysis challenging the official account of Jeffrey Epstein’s death. What interested you about the coverage?

It was certainly interesting that The New York Times was willing to cover it.

Their headline was funny. The one that the Times went with said something like, “Against Conventional Wisdom,” comma, “Forensic Expert Says Epstein Was Murdered.” And I was like, What? What conventional wisdom are you talking about here?

I don’t think the conventional wisdom was challenged there at all.

We live in this interesting world where the public can get to, like, a 70-30, 80-20 belief in something without The New York Times ever giving permission for that belief. And so then when they finally come around to it, like with this article, they say that the story they’re writing goes against conventional wisdom. The conventional wisdom may be wrong, but the conventional wisdom is that he was killed.

What does that say about the state of journalism?

The depressing part, from my perspective, is there’s almost nothing I can report anymore that our readers will admit they’re surprised by, and there’s pretty much nothing you could report on Epstein that would ever live up to what people just assume is true about him.

All right, something considerably more fun: We’re both Phish fans, and I’ve noticed a growing MAGA contingent at their concerts. Does that bother you?

Well, it’s the same audience. You know, they’re just going through changes in their lives. Doesn’t frustrate me at all. All are welcome.

I actually talked to Phish about this in 2002. I met the band after they played at Saturday Night Live. At the time, I was very agitated about the fact that they had not said anything about the Iraq war, and the drummer, Jon Fishman, told me they had said something, one political thing, several years earlier, and they just got flooded with letters from Republican fans who felt hurt.

They’re like, You know what? We’re just not gonna go there anymore. Stop at a stop sign. This is about all we’re gonna say.

And you know what? It’s fine. We don’t need Phish to tell us what they think about the world. They can just create a space for us to think about it.

Phish would not have been able to stop the Iraq war.

But doesn’t some of the anti-Muslim and transphobic rhetoric on the right run counter to the ethos of Phish?

Yeah, but I don’t think any of their audience goes into that.

So you think they’re more fiscal conservatives?
