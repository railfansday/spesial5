Scientists squeeze record-breaking X-ray power into an incredibly tiny spot

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Scientists have achieved a new world record by producing extraordinarily intense X-ray flashes that last just 300 attoseconds, an almost unimaginably brief period of time. Using the European XFEL, a powerful X-ray research facility, the team concentrated the flashes into an area roughly 100,000 times smaller than the cross-section of a typical human hair.

By squeezing so much X-ray energy into such a tiny space, the researchers created an exceptionally powerful beam that could open new possibilities for studying the behavior of electrons inside atoms and molecules. The breakthrough could eventually help scientists observe the rapid movements of electrons during chemical reactions and potentially even learn to control them.

The experiment took place in April 2026, and the findings have now been published in the scientific journal Optica.

Record-Breaking X-Ray Flashes Last Just 300 Attoseconds

"This is a world record in intensity and fluence for a hard-X-ray attosecond pulse," says Ichiro Inoue from the University of Tokyo, first author of the publication.

Hard X-rays are a form of light with extremely short wavelengths. Scientists use two measurements to describe their extraordinary power. Fluence refers to the amount of energy delivered to a particular area, while intensity also considers how quickly that energy is delivered.

In this experiment, each X-ray pulse lasted an estimated 300 attoseconds (an attosecond is a billionth of a billionth of a second). Such incredibly short flashes offer researchers a way to investigate events that unfold far too quickly for conventional observation.

To concentrate the X-rays, the team used a specialized optical arrangement called an advanced Kirkpatrick-Baez, or AKB, system. It consists of two exceptionally precise mirrors that focus the beam in different directions. Each pulse underwent four reflections in total, with one mirror focusing the X-rays vertically and the other horizontally.

The result was an astonishingly small focal spot measuring just 180 × 330 nanometers (a nanometre is a billionth of a meter). By directing the energy into this microscopic area, the researchers were able to achieve record-breaking intensity.

"A few years ago, we demonstrated terawatt-scale hard X-ray attosecond pulses at the European XFEL," says DESY scientist Jiawei Yan, one of the corresponding authors of the study. "But generating powerful pulses is only the first step. For experiments, what really matters is what we can deliver to the sample. With this advance, we can now use attosecond X-rays to explore highly nonlinear interactions between X-rays and matter."

These nonlinear interactions occur when extremely intense X-rays cause matter to respond in ways that would not normally happen under weaker illumination. Studying such effects could reveal new details about how atoms and electrons behave under extreme conditions.

An Extraordinary Reaction Inside Copper Atoms

To confirm the exceptional intensity of their X-ray pulses, the researchers directed them at a thin sheet of copper. The experiment provided independent evidence that the focused flashes reached peak intensities exceeding 10²⁰ watts per square centimeter.

When the X-rays struck the copper, they knocked electrons out of the atoms' innermost electron shells, leaving behind empty positions known as 'holes'. Electrons from other parts of the atoms then moved into these vacancies, releasing energy in the form of X-ray light.

Under ordinary circumstances, this emitted light travels in many different directions. However, when the incoming X-rays become sufficiently intense, something remarkable happens. The emitted light can stimulate other excited copper atoms to release additional X-rays traveling in the same direction, producing an amplified beam.

Scientists call this phenomenon amplified spontaneous emission, or ASE.

"It is almost magical to see this happening," says Anders Madsen, group leader at the MID instrument, where the study was conducted. "Normal fluorescence goes in all directions, regardless of the direction of the incoming beam. But when the ASE threshold is crossed, you see the light cone starting to condense into the forward direction."

The team also observed another important signature of extreme intensity. As they increased the energy of the X-ray pulses, the amplified signal initially became much stronger. At higher energies, however, the increase slowed and eventually leveled off, a behavior known as saturation.

This pattern is expected only under exceptionally intense X-ray illumination. Its appearance provided additional confirmation of the record intensity calculated from the pulse energy, duration, and size of the focused beam.

A New Way to Explore the Hidden World of Electrons

Beyond setting a world record, the breakthrough could significantly expand scientists' ability to investigate some of nature's fastest processes.

Electrons play a central role in determining how atoms bond together, how molecules interact, and how chemical reactions unfold. Their movements happen on extraordinarily short timescales, making them difficult to observe directly.

By combining ultrashort X-ray flashes with extremely high intensity, researchers hope to capture information about electrons before the incoming radiation has time to substantially alter the material being studied. This could provide a clearer picture of electron behavior inside atoms and molecules and, in the longer term, potentially lead to methods for influencing electron motion during chemical reactions.

The advanced Kirkpatrick-Baez mirror system used for the record-setting experiment was transported from Japan. European XFEL is now preparing to receive its own AKB system, with delivery expected in early 2027.

The new equipment is being funded through a collaboration with the University of Hamburg, with additional support from the Federal Ministry of Research, Technology and Space (BMFTR).

Once the system has been installed and commissioned, scientists from around the world using the MID instrument are expected to gain access to these exceptionally brief and intense X-ray flashes. The upgraded capabilities could allow researchers to investigate electron behavior at a level of detail that has previously been difficult to achieve, bringing them closer to understanding and potentially controlling the fundamental processes behind chemical reactions.
