Robert Brokamp: Helping charities and family members while lowering your tax bill. This week on the Saturday Personal Finance Edition of The Motley Fool Hidden Gems Investing Podcast. So this month, we're going to discuss tax-smart ways to give to charities, and how to give investments to family members because nothing says I love you like compounding growth. Here to discuss how to be generous while also lowering your tax bill is my foolish colleague and fellow Certified Financial Planner® practitioner, Stephanie Marini. Welcome back, Stephanie.

Stephanie Marini: Thanks for having me. I'm glad to be here.

Robert Brokamp: It's the time of year when many nonprofits are going to begin requesting donations, and they often emphasize the tax benefits. However, this year, there have been a couple of changes. Tell us about them.

Stephanie Marini: As part of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, there were two rules shifted for 2026. So first, taxpayers who want to take the standard deduction can now deduct up to $1,000 of cash gifts to charity. And then that would be $2,000 for married couples filing jointly. So the catch is the gifts cannot be to donor-advised funds. They have to go directly to a charity, and it has to be cash. It cannot be a gift of appreciated stock. Those taking the standard deduction, they can take a charitable deduction. And then on the itemizers’ side of things, it's been made a little bit more difficult because now there's a hurdle. Only giving that's above 0.5% of adjusted gross income is deductible. So at $200,000 of AGI, the first $1,000 of that produces no deduction. Timing your giving really matter this year more than others.

Robert Brokamp: Gifting appreciated stock, which I think really everyone who has a regular taxable brokerage account should consider doing if they're charitably inclined. Explain the benefits and how it works.

Stephanie Marini: Let's say you want to give to charity $10,000. And you have $10,000 worth of individual stock. If you sell that stock to get the cash to give to the charity, yes, there will be a charitable deduction for you, but there will also be capital gains due, and which could be quite significant, depending on how long you've held the stock. However, if you gift that stock, you will avoid the capital gains while still getting that charitable deduction, come tax time. Like we mentioned earlier, I want to make sure we point out, there is now that AGI floor of 0.5%, and there is a cap for those itemizing at 30%, but it is another option for those charitably inclined.

Robert Brokamp: Point out a few other things to keep in mind. So this has to be a stock with a long-term capital gain. So you've had to have held it for longer than a year. To take that deduction, you do have to itemize. But even if you don't itemize, you still get the tax benefit because you're passing on the capital gain to the charity. And if you donate more than that 30% of your AGI cap, you actually can carry forward the excess to future tax years for up to five years. But again, this only benefits you if you itemize. What's great about this strategy is that you can then buy the stock back, Nvidia in your example, right away if you want. You don't have to wait 30 days for tax-loss harvesting, but now at a higher cost basis. So that's an additional tax bonus. You mentioned previously donor-advised funds, and I suspect many people have heard about them but maybe not know all the details. So tell us about donor-advised funds.

Stephanie Marini: This goes along the lines of gifting a stock. It can be gifted from your account into a donor-advised fund. So backtracking a little bit, a donor-advised fund is an account specifically set up for charitable giving. Many of the big companies have it, so Fidelity, Vanguard, Schwab. And it's a way to go ahead and claim the deduction now when you put the money in, but you don't have to decide exactly where it's going yet. Especially as we're coming up on the end of the year, if we want to go ahead and put money into a donor-advised fund, you can claim that deduction for 2026. But make the decision later as to what charity gets the funds. Like I mentioned, unlike a cash deposit into a donor-advised fund is not eligible for the charitable deduction. If you're one of the standardized deduction takers, appreciated stock can be put into a donor-advised fund. So it's another major benefit, because then you can also try and time contributions. You can bunch them. So you can lump-sum here at the end of the year and maybe count for 2026 and 2027. Because the standard deduction is so high, it can make sense to deposit multiple years to then try and reach that itemized limit and claim those deductions.

Robert Brokamp: This whole bunching strategy really does depend on the standard deduction. Let's list them real quick here so everyone knows them. For 2026, they are $16,100 for singles, $24,150 for head of household, and $32,200 for married folks filing jointly. Those who are 65 and older or blind get an additional standard deduction on top of that. If your total itemized deductions, including charitable deductions, are less than standard deduction, you don't really get a tax benefit for them. The theory behind bunching is that you put two or more years’ worth of deductions all in one year, pushing you over that standard deduction would at least allow you to get some tax benefit from those deductible expenses. With a donor-advised fund, you're maybe putting two or three or four years’ worth of your charitable contribution in one year. You would do this maybe if you're not sure who you want to get the money yet, or you don't want them to get that money all at one time. You do have some say over how that money is invested, and it grows tax-free until it's eventually donated to the charity. So definitely something worth considering. Let's highlight something that is available to folks who are 70.5 or older and have traditional IRAs, because they have another option. They're called Qualified Charitable Distributions or QCDs. So, Stephanie, tell us about those.

Stephanie Marini: With the QCD, the money would go directly from your IRA to the charity, and it has to be a qualified charity. So that money then is excluded from taxable income. So it's essentially a tax-free distribution. And that amount that you transfer to the qualifying charity, does offset any required minimum distributions dollar for dollar. That said, you can't double dip and count the qualified charitable distribution and a charitable deduction. And there also is a cap on how much you can contribute via a QCD. So for 2026, that limit is 111,000 per person. But this really comes into play, too, for seniors that are maybe over that 73 limit that are getting required minimum distributions that, if you have a high 401(k) balance, could be quite high, could be more than you need. If you are so charitably inclined, reducing that amount that you have to take as taxable income by having that go directly to a charity, which, again, you might have been wanting to do anyway, helped solve that problem.

Robert Brokamp: You mentioned 401(k) balances, and you definitely have to stack RMDs for 401(k)s. One of the issues here is that a QCD can only be made from an IRA. So you're definitely considering the 401(k), but it has to be made from an IRA, which is one of the reasons why when you retire, it might make sense to move some or all of your money to an IRA after you've retired. Let's move on to some of the boring nitty-gritty when it comes to all this. Because like all things with taxes, and especially deductions, you have to keep good records, and in case the IRS comes snooping around or whatever. What do people need to know about the paperwork they should keep when donating to charities?

Stephanie Marini: All of it. Document it all, keep it all always. I think for me, as a good rule of thumb, if the charity is going to provide you something, keep it, because there's a reason they're giving it to you. For the appreciated stock, you're going to need to provide documentation showing what that cost basis was. So, when it was purchased. And also, that would account for that time frame to make sure you held it for the long term, more than a year. But you will also need to file an additional form with your taxes. It's Form 8283, but make sure to keep all documentation for those three years or for however long that carryforward deduction is still being used. So whichever of those is longer, keep the documentation.

Robert Brokamp: Then I'll just add to that that anytime you are involved in a financial services firm, like donating appreciated stock or the QCD, you want to notify the charity to expect that coming in and to make sure that they know what came from you. In most situations, Fidelity, Vanguard, Schwab, all those folks will say it's coming from whatever, Josephine Smith, but you want to make sure that they just didn't get a check from some financial services firm. They don't know who did it. And I also say that you have to be very careful when doing your tax return to make sure that you make it known to the IRS that distribution was a QCD, because if you don't do that right, it's going to be considered taxable.

Let's move on to giving money, and particularly investments to friends, family, whomever. So not charities, but people you just want to help out. After all, the holidays are coming up, and we're all going to be giving gifts to people, and you may be inclined to give something that appreciates in value rather than something that just eventually ends up in a landfill. Some listeners may be saying to themselves, "Yeah, that sounds like a great idea, but who actually gives stocks as gifts?" I know someone, and it's you, Stephanie. So tell us about how you have given the gift of, hopefully, long-term wealth to your younger relatives.

Stephanie Marini: Here's the thing. My first nephew was born in a very, very dangerous time, the year I started working in a bank. Because it is at that time that things like compounding and growth and probably my own 401(k) knowledge was really starting to click on a personal level. And I was seeing firsthand by being in a bank branch, how people really do struggle with money. And so I'm at the risk of being long-winded, but I think it matters. I grew up with Dave Ramsey parents. They weren't super, super strict, but I knew when I saw envelopes coming out, like what that meant. And I say that because I honestly think it was the greatest form of generational wealth that they could have passed down to me. Because going into college, coming out of college, I knew what words like budgeting, debt, tracking, allocating. I knew what they meant, and it was all clicking for me right at that time.

But when I started working at that branch, I saw how all kinds of people, doctors, all kinds of professionals, old, young, everybody. I really didn't know how money worked or how to grow money in any way. So long way to say, I knew that if I could start compounding for my nephew early, that's the greatest thing I could do. I wasn't going to compete with Grandma and Grandpa with my little money that I could give him for a cheap plastic toy. What we did was set up a brokerage account for him. With my brother-in-law, we went, and I explained how boring I was about to be, and we got it set up over the course of the Christmas week. It might not sound like a lot because it's $50 here for Christmas and maybe $50 for his birthday, but it was getting that account set up and making it more simple for not just me, but other family members or even my nephew's parents to be able to transfer it in that really got the ball rolling.

Because the account already exists, it's easy to then ask for things. And now my nephew is in his teens, and he's gotten more involved with it, which is really cool. When we started, it was something simple like an S&P 500 ETF. Just again, to get the compounding started. But as he's gotten older, he's been able to look at the account, see how things grow. He's added individual stocks to it, things like row blocks that he's been interested in. And I know that he's at minimum, having the conversation, and he knows that this exists for him.

Robert Brokamp: I love that story so much, and your nephew is very lucky to have you as an aunt. Let's briefly touch on tax issues regarding giving money or investments to people. What will be the tax consequences for the kids and their families? What should people be aware of?

Stephanie Marini: For 2026, the gift tax exclusion is $19,000 per person. And thankfully, my nieces and nephews are still young enough where they're not listeners, so I can use as an example without being held to it. If my husband and I wanted to max out our gift, we could each gift that 19,000 to each niece and nephew. So 38,000 from my household to each of my niece and nephews. Nineteen from me, 19 from him. Even if we won whatever the Powerball is right now and exceeded that 38,000 in the year, really, the additional amount would need to be recorded on a tax form, Form 709. But what it does is reduce my lifetime limit, which is $15 million per individual or 30 million per couple to each receiver. 529's, I want to point out, do allow for a one-time five-syear lump sum contribution. So for 2026, using these numbers, it would mean 95,000 per year per person, or 190,000 as a couple. For maybe grandparents, if anybody on my side of the family is listening, that'd be great option for my kids. They could do a lump sum contribution into a 529 and get them set up.

Robert Brokamp: And I'll point out that that also applies to parents. When you're giving money to a 529, that is a gift. And if you do that five-year lump-sum contribution for the 529, you do have to fill out Form 709. But I just want to emphasize that if you do give more than $19,000, it doesn't mean you owe taxes this year. I think that's often misunderstood. You have to fill out the form, and it eats into that lifetime state gift exclusion, $15 million per person, $30 million for a couple. So, frankly, most people don't need to worry about it. I'll talk a little bit very briefly about taxes in the case of minors, because once they have investment accounts, you do have to start paying attention to what is called the Kiddie Tax. I'm going to be a bit technical. But basically, it applies to children under age 19 or under age 24 if they're full-time students. In 2026, the first $1,350 of unearned income -- so interest, dividends, capital gains. That's tax free. The next 1,350 is taxed at the child's rate, and anything above 2,700 is taxed at the parent's marginal rate. So for most kids, that means that $2,700 of their investment income is tax-free. Which means that along the way, the custodian of the accounts for the kid, can do a little bit of tax-gain harvesting over the years, selling an investment, realizing a capital gain that is taxed at 0%, and then immediately buying it back, resetting the cost basis to a higher number.

Again, it can be a bit technical. So do your research to make sure you understand the tax consequences and maybe even the financial aid consequences if the kid is getting ready to go to college. All this that we're talking about presumes that you're using a taxable brokerage account. You do have other options. If the kid or any other person you're giving money to has earned income, you maybe contribute to a Roth IRA for them. Nowadays, there's the option of 530A accounts, better known as Trump accounts, which I think definitely should be open for kids who will get free money, either from the government or an employer or someone else. But as they currently stand, I actually don't love them otherwise for gifts from parents or other adults. I don't want to get into all the details here, so I'll just draw your attention to the February 21st episode from this year in which I discussed all the pros and cons of different types of investment accounts for kids, and then the July 11th episode from this year, in which we did a really deep dive on Trump accounts. Stephanie Marini: I think my biggest addition for all of this would be, there's a lot of the way this helps that's not associated to a dollar. So a recurring transaction or a deposit when you get a bonus, it's easier to ask for it from aunts and uncles. Once a kid is older, having those talking points and growing is starting that financial literacy earlier and earlier, and it's going to make such an impact.

Robert Brokamp: A culture of investing, wealth accumulation, being smart with the money, and I just love it. Besides cash, you can gift shares to people of any age. What do people need to know when it comes to gifting investments? Because people may say, "You know what? Stephanie Marini: The biggest thing to know about whenever you're going to gift an investment, specifically a stock, is that the original cost basis does follow that stock.
