An open structure-guided RNA foundation model for generalizable prediction

Nature (2026) Cite this article

RNA language models have achieved strong performance across diverse downstream tasks by leveraging large-scale sequence data. However, RNA function is fundamentally shaped by hierarchical structure, making structural information important for pre-training. Existing approaches often depend on noisy annotations or task-specific objectives that can limit generalizability. Here we present structRFM, a structure-guided RNA foundation model trained on 21 million RNA sequence–structure pairs by integrating base-pairing interactions into masked language modelling through a pair-matching operation. We also introduce MUSES, a multi-source ensemble of secondary structures designed to mitigate predictor bias, and a dynamic masking ratio that balances structure-guided and nucleotide-level masking. structRFM learns joint sequence and structure representations as sequence-level, nucleotide-level and pairwise features for broad downstream adaptation. It ranks highly in zero-shot homology classification and establishes new benchmarks for secondary-structure prediction. Its tertiary-structure adaptation, Zfold, consistently improves both three-dimensional predictions and their extracted secondary structures, with performance comparable to AlphaFold31 across CASP15-natural, CASP16 and RNA-Puzzles. structRFM also improves performance on function-prediction tasks and generalizes to newly released RNA families and long non-coding RNAs. Together, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of structure-guided pre-training and highlight multimodal RNA language models for robust structural and functional inference. The complete 21-million-pair dataset, model weights and source code are openly available to support multimodal RNA foundation-model development.

a Ternary simplex visualization of the MUSES performance landscape across weighted combination configurations. Each vertex corresponds to a single standalone method (BPfold, ViennaRNA or CONTRAfold), and interior points represent weighted ensemble models. We normalized weighting coefficients for proportional equivalence and applied linear interpolation over a dense grid to smooth the INF metric distribution. Colour gradients (blue to red) represent INF values, with contour lines for enhanced resolution. This visualization illustrates how ensemble weighting modulates MUSES performance for robust secondary-structure annotation and identifies optimal weight-coefficient regimes with associated trade-offs. b Benchmark evaluation of six established multi-source RNA secondary-structure prediction methods23,24,25,26,34,44,45,79 on the Rfam66,67 and full ArchiveII37 (n = 3, 975 independent RNA sequences) datasets, stratified by three sequence-length categories (≤ 500 nt, 501–1000 nt, > 1000 nt). c Linear fits extrapolating prediction performance for the six methods on both datasets. Lines are linear regression fits, and shaded bands represent 95% confidence intervals. A substantial performance degradation is observed with increasing sequence length, highlighting the current challenge of modelling RNA secondary structures for long RNA sequences.

a Head-to-head comparison of structRFM with other models using ϵRMSD72, TM-score73, GDT-TS74 and INF75. Circle size indicates the absolute difference between the two methods. b Violin plots of RMSD values. For each method in b, n = 8 independent RNA targets for CASP15-natural, n = 13 independent RNA targets for CASP16, and n = 20 independent RNA targets for RNA-Puzzles. The inner box plots show the median (white centre line), 25th and 75th percentiles (lower and upper bounds of the thick line), and minimum and maximum (lower and upper thin whiskers).

The ϵRMSD, TM-score and GDT-TS of tertiary structures, together with the INF of secondary structures extracted from tertiary structures, are annotated. In the secondary-structure diagrams, stems are shown in green, multiple loops in red, interior loops in yellow, hairpin loops in blue, and 5′ and 3′ unpaired regions in orange. In general, structRFM performs better on natural targets than on synthetic targets containing dense long-range interactions, but sometimes fails to predict short local stems (in green), resulting in large multiple loops (in red).

The ϵRMSD, TM-score and GDT-TS of tertiary structures, together with the INF of secondary structures extracted from tertiary structures, are annotated. In the secondary-structure diagrams, stems are shown in green, multiple loops in red, interior loops in yellow, hairpin loops in blue, and 5’ and 3’ unpaired regions in orange. In general, structRFM performs better on viral and aptamer targets but performs worse on multimer and bacterial targets. For targets marked with blue circles, the sequences used for CASP16 prediction differ substantially from those of the PDB ground-truth structures because regions unresolved in the experimental structures were not modelled.
