A Woman Left Her Belongings in Pompeii Nearly 2,000 Years Ago. Archaeologists Just Found Them.

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

A Pompeii resident’s wardrobe and other personal affects were frozen in ash, offering a snapshot of a personal beauty routine from 79 C.E.

In a centrally located home in ancient Pompeii’s Via dell’Abbondanza (translated as Street of Abundance), one woman curated a cozy—and intimate—dressing room, obscured by a façade. The identity of the woman is itself now obscured and lost to time, but the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 C.E. froze her personal habits and routines in ash. 2,000 years later, archaeologists found it exactly as it was.

The house in question likely belonged to Cornelius Maxium, based on inscriptions no longer visible but seen during a 1912 excavation. Archaeologists recently ventured behind the home’s façade, and in doing so, opened a wardrobe last opened in 79 C.E.

Archaeologists spelled out the find in an online excavation journal from Pompeii Sites, highlighting how the investigation revealed the woman’s personal beauty routine and daily habits. The wardrobe contained cosmetic tools of tweezers and a mixing palette, a glass perfume vial, an incense burner, and a bronze oil lamp. The excavation also revealed vessels and pots of bronze, glass, and ceramic, as well as a small wooden box contained lead weights, which were likely used in trading.

“Pompeii is not just a place of great works of art; it is also a place where the most ordinary objects reveal the human dimension of the past,” Gabriel Zuchtriegel, director of the Pompeii Archaeological Park, said in a translated statement. “The value of this discovery lies in the ability to piece together various traces of a life—personal habits, work, and memories. While we cannot reconstruct the identity of the house’s inhabitant with certainty, these fragments bring us closer to their daily existence.”

The woman whose wardrobe this was, for example, may have had a great affection for pinecones. In addition to the discovery of a small jug decorated with a pinecone motif, there was also a necklace with at least 24 miniature charms made from faience, bone, glass paste, and rock crystals. Some of those charms also depicted pinecones.

Tucked inside a cloth pouch inside the wardrobe, archaeologists may have found the most personal item of all: a necklace with amulets known as “crepundia,” which literally translates to rattles. Typically worn by children for ritual protection—the amulets were thought to contain spells that offered protection—the items also audibly rattled so adults could better track their children’s movements.

Roman-era experts said adults often kept childhood crepundia as a keepsake. “The Roman world was characterized by a high degree of geographical mobility at every social level, from slaves to wealthy merchants,” according to the statement. “As a result, many people found themselves living far from the parents, relatives, and the places where they had grown up.”

Amidst the personal items at Via dell’Abbondanza, archeologists may have located the woman’s proto-piggy bank, as excavations revealed a woven container holding 38 bronze coins.

That such deeply personal items, to say nothing of the hoard of bronze coins, were there within the wardrobe to be found centuries later suggests that the owner, whoever she may have been, fled the eruption and subsequent devastation with no time to extract them, and never returned to retrieve them. That, of course, is assuming they survived to even attempt it.

It’s possible the owners were not home at the time of the eruption, as the room housing the wardrobe appeared to be under some level of renovation when the disaster occurred. Even in its mid-reno state, however, the room featured expensive Italian paintings, highlighting the potential wealth of the occupants that match the prime real estate along one of the ancient city’s main thoroughfares. The excavation also uncovered the remains of some of the home’s smaller residents when Vesuvius rained down its devastation. There was a pair of young wood mice skeletons inside an amphora on the home’s mezzanine level, showing that even the finest urban homes in Pompeii clashed with rodents. Evidently, there was no crepundia warding them off.
