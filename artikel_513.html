Hexed review: Turning Red meets Harry Potter, minus the problematic politics

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Spooky season lives on as Disney unfurls Hexed, a witches' tale opening over the Thanksgiving holiday. And lucky us, their latest is a magical adventure that might pull some inspiration from Harry Potter, but in a fresh and fun way (that doesn't line the pockets of unrepentant transphobe J.K. Rowling).

Co-directed by Jason Hand, Fawn Veerasunthorn, and Josie Trinidad, Hexed offers a story about a child destined for greatness. However, her path to mastering magic is marred by an evil, power-hungry fascist who is surrounded by a flock of vicious mythical beasts. However, rather than focusing on a sad orphan in England, Hexed's Potter-like tale centers on the Ohio-raised daughter of a mother whose love language is smothering.

This combination mixes the coming-of-age comedy and mother-daughter drama of Turning Red with a magical realm reminiscent of Harry Potter (and the underrated Disney show Owl House), creating an entertaining elixir that's fun, imaginative, and moving.

Hailee Steinfeld lends her voice to Billie Doe, a teen girl introduced through a heist in which she steals her high school's mascot (a befuddled owl) so he can be set free. This seemingly bad behavior — which includes "borrowing" her mom's car without a driver's license — is more than an act of rebellion against school uniforms, her grumbling principal (Brett Gelman), or her overprotective mother, Alice (Rashida Jones). Scored by Queen's rapturous anthem "I Want to Break Free," it's a symbolic act that plays as this Disney heroine's "I wish" song, though this Disney heroine doesn't get an original song of her own. Billie wants to be free to spread her wings and be herself.

For years, Billie's mother has given her endless "guidelines," building around her a world of rules from which Billie is desperate to escape. So, when a magical portal opens in her bedroom, of course she rushes on through without looking back. But this won't be a story where Billie pulls a Dorothy Gale in Oz, wandering around alone while her mother figure Auntie Em is none the wiser. Shortly after Billie has tumbled into the magical realm of Hexe and made a spectacle out of herself by flying with style, her mother Alice follows.

Together, they will battle against the vicious Queen Celeste (Jodie Foster), and her fleet of half-raven/half-human Morphlings. (Imagine if the Wicked Witch's flying monkeys were chic, side-eyeing goth girls with shapeshifting abilities). But to join forces, they first have to understand each other, literally traveling into each other's minds to see each other's perspectives. That's a bit of magic we could all use.

Bound to Billie, we're quickly made aware that she doesn't fit in. A school photo shows her not only off in the corner with a glum look on her face as the other kids smile in unison, but also with more vibrantly colored hair and skin that sets her apart. She's not regarded as beautiful for this, but strange and worthy of shunning.

She loves plants, attracts stray cats, and possesses a reckless streak that makes her one of the rowdiest Disney heroines since The Little Mermaid. And while her classmates might scoff and her teachers might sneer, Billie is dedicated to being herself. But when her mother sneers at her, she almost literally shrinks.

It hurts to believe your mother sees you as a screw-up. This big feeling becomes a crucial incentive for three of the film's lead characters, having sprawling consequences. Maybe we can't choose to not be hurt by a parent's rejection. But we can choose how we respond. Will we cave to their pressure? Will we dull our own shine or corrupt our hearts to distort ourselves to meet their expectations? Or will we soar above?

The plotline of mothers and teen daughters struggling to see each other outside of the parenthood dynamic puts Hexed on the same path as Brave and Turning Red. In each of these Disney animated films, the mother seems cold as she urges her daughter to assimilate to societal norms. But in each instance, it's with the goal of protecting her from hardship.

However, Hexed gives deeper space to the idea that being forced to be like everyone else by the very person who should believe you are special is its own kind of hell. And it's not one invented with each new generation, but a trauma that can reach back, scarring those who come after.

For this intense central drama and all its macabre elements, Hexed is nonstop entertaining. Steinfeld recaptures the teen angst and rebellious spirit she showed in the phenomenal live-action coming-of-age comedy The Edge of Seventeen. Jones brings her wry wit to Alice, while Foster's sharpness cuts like a knife through velvet. You can practically hear her targets' self-esteem being shredded to ribbons.

While there's plenty of noteworthy names in the ensemble cast, including Dan Stevens, Tracey Ullman, Randall Park, Stephen Fry, and Henry Winkler, it's Fallout's Walton Goggins who proves an unrepentant scene-stealer. Slathering on a Cajun accent like it's barbecue sauce, Goggins brings a surly but sweet attitude to a three-eyed cat familiar named Beef Roger Crummchuk. If that name alone has you intrigued, you'll love Hexed. Chaotic, intriguing, and silly, this character's name is a solid distillation of the movie itself. Plus, Goggins and Steinfeld have terrific chemistry as an overeager protégé and a wizened mentor.

The filmmakers have bypassed the typically clean Disney aesthetic, which demands its heroines be soft, round, and always pretty, and worked in some alt elements to give Billie an easy-to-clock baby goth look. In a beautiful and meaningful element of the movie's color story, the teals and fuchsias that Billie favors in her Ohio life are reflected in Hexe, along with golds and purples that all suggest she belongs, as she visually fits in this world she's never known. But when it comes to the colors of the queen, they are chillier, more muted, speaking not only to the difference between heroine and villainess, but also to the distinction in their energies.

With a spirited sense of humor and a pop music soundtrack, Hexed brings an inviting verve to its story of generational trauma. And in that, it helps the spoonful of medicine go down, for the children and parents in the audience alike. For those kids not old enough to be having this kind of psychological connection to Hexed, they'll be delighted with the daffy physical humor, rousing action sequences, and an array of colorful characters who'll frighten and delight. Hexed is spell-binding.
