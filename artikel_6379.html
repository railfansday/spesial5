Early galaxies in the newborn universe spread elements like carbon and oxygen way faster than anyone expected

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Webb found carbon, oxygen and silicon in moving gas around young galaxies, so heavy elements had already spread just 500 million years after the Big Bang.

In its first moments, the Universe held almost nothing but hydrogen and helium. Carbon, oxygen and other heavy elements came later, made inside stars.

Yet those elements had already spread into the gas around a galaxy seen just 500 million years after the Big Bang, and around two others seen a little later.

So the recycling that carries material from one generation of stars to the next was already working in at least some galaxies. The Universe was less than 4 percent of its present age.

Yongda Zhu, a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Arizona and its Steward Observatory, led the work with colleagues there and at UC Riverside and the University of Michigan. Their sample is three bright galaxies, so the team can’t yet say how typical those galaxies were.

Light carries a record of whatever it passes through. Each element absorbs its own set of colors, and those colors show up as thin dark gaps when the light is spread out into a spectrum.

Zhu’s team used the starlight from each galaxy as its own light source. On its way to Earth, that light crossed the gas around the galaxy, and the gas left its gaps behind.

Catching gaps this faint took NASA’s James Webb Space Telescope. The light had been traveling for more than 13 billion years, and Webb collected it for about 29 hours with the setting that captured those colors.

All three came from a deep Webb survey of one patch of sky, and they were the only galaxies in it bright enough for this kind of measurement. The team chose them on brightness alone, before checking for any gaps, and all three turned out to have them.

The gaps matched oxygen, carbon and silicon. Some of the oxygen atoms were intact. Some of the silicon and carbon atoms had lost one electron, and others had lost three, which takes harsher, more energetic conditions.

All of those versions showed up at overlapping speeds. A single calm cloud of gas inside a galaxy would have a hard time producing that mix. It points instead to gas in several states, moving together and tied to each galaxy.

The pattern of gaps looked broadly like the one astronomers see in star-forming galaxies from more than a billion years later. Elements like these went on to build later stars and rocky planets.

In an interview with Earth.com, Zhu tied the moving gas to stellar feedback – the push that massive stars and their explosions give the gas around them.

“You can picture young galaxies as both factories and distributors,” he said. “Stars inside them produce elements such as carbon, oxygen, and silicon, and stellar feedback then pushes some of this enriched gas outward.”

The gaps didn’t land exactly where they would for gas at rest alongside each galaxy. They were shifted slightly toward bluer colors, which happens when gas is moving toward the telescope.

The gas lay between each galaxy and Earth, so moving toward Earth meant moving away from the galaxy. The shifts match speeds of roughly 31 to 155 miles per second (50 to 250 kilometers per second). Even the low end would cross the continental United States in about a minute and a half.

“In our JWST spectra, we see this gas moving relative to the galaxies themselves, showing that these elements were already being spread into their surroundings,” Zhu said.

The speeds can’t show how far out the gas reached. A wind blowing close to a galaxy and gas spread farther around it would produce the same shift, and churning motions inside the galaxy could add to it.

The most heavily stripped atoms may have been moving faster than the rest, but sharper spectra will be needed to confirm that.

Webb had already found heavy elements inside galaxies even older than these three, including oxygen in one of the most distant galaxies known.

Outside galaxies, most earlier measurements came from later times. They relied on light from extremely bright, distant galaxy cores shining through random stretches of gas, which were only loosely connected to any one galaxy.

“The speed of the process was the most striking part,” Zhu told Earth.com.

“Previous JWST observations had already shown that very early galaxies could produce heavy elements, but our results show that galaxies were also beginning to redistribute those elements into their environments remarkably early.”

The very first stars would have formed from pure hydrogen and helium. Astronomers have spent years looking for them, and they’ve proved hard to find.

Zhu said the result may help explain why. Once the first generations of stars began enriching their surroundings, he said, “chemically pristine environments may have disappeared rapidly.”

The heavy elements needn’t have come mostly from those first stars, though. Other researchers’ models show that a later generation of stars, with some reaching 200 to 300 times the mass of the Sun, could make carbon and oxygen this quickly.

Three bright galaxies can’t show how common this was. Some other gas clouds seen in the early universe hold almost no heavy elements, so the spreading was probably patchy, reaching some places well before others.

At this level of detail, Webb’s spectra also blur fine structure, and the team couldn’t map where the gas ends. An actively feeding black hole in one of the galaxies can’t be fully ruled out either, though the spectra show no clear sign of black hole activity.

“The next major question is how common this process was,” Zhu said. He wants deeper observations of more galaxies, to learn whether outward-moving enriched gas was common in the early universe. Sharper spectra could also separate the motions of the gas in its different states.

For now, the finding rests on three of the brightest galaxies of their time. Larger samples will show whether the rest of the young universe was spreading its elements just as early.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Astronomy.
