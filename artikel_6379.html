One in Four Consumers Would Pay $225 More for Salmon-Safe Tires. Would You?

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

In a West Coast survey, one in four consumers said they would pay $225 extra for salmon-safe tires, bringing wildlife conservation into an everyday buying decision.

Buying new tires could someday give drivers a way to help protect salmon. A new study suggests that many consumers would pay extra for tires labeled “salmon safe,” potentially creating a market for products designed to reduce harm to fish.

The idea comes from a troubling discovery made several years ago. Research co-led by Washington State University (WSU) scientists found that 6PPD, a chemical added to tires to prevent them from breaking down too quickly, is toxic to coho salmon. When tire debris from roads reaches freshwater streams, the chemical can kill the fish.

Tire manufacturers are now exploring new formulations that would eliminate 6PPD. As that work continues, economists at WSU wanted to understand whether consumers would voluntarily spend more on tires that could help protect salmon.

“The tire doesn’t exist yet, but we wanted to know how the general population might value the tire after it’s been developed,” said Joe Cook, a professor in WSU’s School of Economic Sciences and lead author of the study. “As the tire industry explores reformulation, we hope this demonstrates there is a potential market for ‘eco-labeled’ tires.”

Cook, who works at WSU’s Puyallup Research and Extension Center, conducted the research with Wisnu Sugiarto, a professor at Whitman College and former WSU graduate student. Their findings were published in the journal Marine Resource Economics.

The researchers surveyed nearly 700 residents of California, Oregon, and Washington, asking how much they would be willing to pay for hypothetical salmon-safe tires. They then compared those amounts with what participants reported spending on their most recent tire purchases.

The median willingness to pay was 10% above the previous purchase price, equivalent to roughly $76 more for a set of four tires. That means half of respondents were willing to pay a larger premium, while the other half would pay less.

Some participants placed an even higher value on protecting salmon.

“Approximately 25% of people said they would pay a premium of $225 for a set of four salmon-safe tires,” Cook said. “This shows that protecting salmon is very important for a sizeable group of consumers in the three states.”

The results suggest that environmental concerns could influence an ordinary purchasing decision, even when consumers would have to spend more. For tire manufacturers considering alternatives to 6PPD, that willingness to pay could point to a potential market for products advertised as safer for salmon.

However, the researchers emphasized that what consumers say they would voluntarily pay for an environmentally friendly product is different from what manufacturers might charge if regulations required them to remove 6PPD.

“This study is about voluntary eco-labeling for a hypothetical product,” Cook said. “We aren’t saying that prices would go up this amount if 6PPD is regulated out of tires — that ultimately would depend on what it costs for tire manufacturers to reformulate and how competitive the tire industry is.

“Our results couldn’t be used in a benefit-cost analysis of a state or federal regulation,” he continued. “That would require a different survey approach, but we would be excited to look at that and well-placed to do that work.”

The researchers hope to continue examining the economic consequences of removing 6PPD from tires, including questions that would help policymakers assess potential regulations.

Reference: “Measuring Consumer Demand for Salmon Safe Tires” by Joseph Cook and Wisnu Sugiarto, 5 November 2026, Marine Resource Economics. DOI: 10.1086/743478

The study was funded by the nonprofit Salmon Safe, which provides “salmon safe” certifications to products like wine and beer and organizations like farms and construction contractors.
