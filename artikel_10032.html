Daily disruptive outages that ground planes and halt work – welcome to the new normal

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

NSA, Apple add to a week of high-profile network outages, largely attributed to 'routine maintenance'

Another day, another disruptive network outage (or two) as government officials and company execs scramble to assure an anxious populace that these are not coordinated cyberattacks – and security experts call this the new normal.

A classified network that the US National Security Agency uses for internal communications, called NSANet, went offline on Tuesday in what the agency described as a “temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance.”

“While some internal systems were affected, mission-critical capabilities remain fully operational and our ability to defend national security continues,” the NSA said in a statement following media reports about the outage. “NSA's infrastructure is designed with multiple layers of redundancy to maintain operations during planned maintenance or unexpected events.”

Around the same time, Apple users began reporting issues across multiple services – including Apple Support, iCloud, the App Store, Apple Pay, and more – with Apple retail stores worldwide also reportedly experiencing disruptions that halted sales and in-person support.

A day earlier, customers at major US banks including JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, and US Bank reported service outages that Chase attributed to “a software bug in on-premise hardware systems - there is no evidence this is cyber-related.”

All of these high-profile outages followed last week’s US Federal Aviation Authority (FAA) order grounding all planes on Friday due to problems with copper wiring during an L3Harris network upgrade, and a Microsoft 365 outage on Thursday.

Redmond later blamed the massive service disruption on a faulty software update it pushed back in July.

Despite the weekend respite from network outages, your humble vulture still had to field is-this-the-beginning-of-the-end questions from friends and family all day Saturday and Sunday, most of whom suspected Russia or China – maybe even Iran – caused the spate of downed systems and services.

A highly sophisticated, coordinated nation-state hack would be a much more exciting cause for the disruptions. The real culprit, however, appears to be aging, under-funded infrastructure, riddled with decades-old bugs and held together by digital sticks and glue.

“As someone who started out as a hacker, I know how tempting it is to connect the dots when major systems fail one after another,” Remedio CEO Tal Kollender told The Register, adding that there’s “no credible public evidence that these outages were part of a coordinated cyberattack,” and most have been attributed to technical failures.

“What concerns me more is what these incidents reveal about the fragility of our digital infrastructure. We've built an interconnected world on layers of aging systems, complex configurations, and dependencies that organizations don't always fully understand. A single misconfiguration, failed update, or broken dependency can create disruption on a massive scale, even without an attacker,” Kollender said. “The uncomfortable reality I think we're grappling with is that attackers don't always have to break our systems. Sometimes, our systems are already capable of breaking on their own.”

For people who couldn’t withdraw money from their bank, board a flight, or use email at work on Friday, “the disruption was real regardless of the cause,” she said.

“Cybersecurity has spent decades investing in detecting threats, identifying vulnerabilities, and generating reports,” according to Kollender. “Yet we still allow for these preventable weaknesses and configurations that can bring entire systems down.”

Cynthia Kaiser, senior VP of Halcyon’s Ransomware Research Center and former deputy director of the FBI’s Cyber Division, told The Reg that the recent disruptions show “how deeply interconnected our digital infrastructure has become, and how a single technical failure or software update can cascade with outsized effects across critical systems.”

Then we go and add AI to this already delicate balance, and everything essentially blows up.

AI-assisted bug hunting and exploit development has surfaced more than 100,000 CVEs so far this year, with Microsoft’s Patch Tuesday disclosing more than 1,000 vulnerabilities in each of the past two months. AI has also shrunk the elapsed time between the public release of a security patch and attackers developing working exploits.

“We are in an era where AI is accelerating the discovery of legacy software flaws, forcing organizations to race to patch vulnerabilities before adversaries can exploit them, which inevitably creates friction and disruptions,” Kaiser said. “Even when an outage isn’t caused by a cyberattack, these moments of operational distraction leave dangerous gaps open for threat actors and opportunists to take advantage of, making it crucial for both institutions and everyday individuals to remain on high alert against social engineering and unsolicited outreach.”

Even when attackers aren’t using AI to facilitate intrusions, the technology itself has become a source of risk to organizations.

“Autonomous agents can execute actions, change configurations, access sensitive information, and interact with critical systems at machine speed,” Kollender said. “When those systems lack proper governance and safeguards, a mistake can develop just as quickly as an attack.

Former US Department of Defense network exploitation and vulnerability analyst Mike Wiacek, when asked if these regular system outages are the new normal, told us: “The brutal truth is yes. Especially in the AI world that we live in.”

After the DoD, Wiacek founded Google’s Threat Analysis Group. He's since founded Stairwell, a threat-intel firm he serves as chief technology officer.

“The idea that we are going to patch ourselves to security is, dare I say, an antiquated concept,” he told The Register.

One of Stairwell’s engineers was doing regular, daily software development tasks on an open source library with Anthropic’s Claude Mythos, and the model told the engineer about a buffer-overflow vulnerability in the code.

“We weren't even looking for it,” Wiacek said. “It found a vulnerability in open source software by accident. You used to need specialized bug hunters and really, really technical security folks to discover and develop vulnerabilities and exploits. Now, it’s almost too easy to do it.”

This new reality, he said, necessitates a total rethink of how we build and maintain digital infrastructure.

“How do we get to a world where we assume compromised by default, and continuously recompromised, and then develop the architectures and the systems necessary to make what was the expense of incident-response investigation really fast and cheap? Because we will not be able to patch our way out of it.”

It requires vulnerability managers and system administrators to patch as soon as software updates become available. But from an engineering perspective, it also means building in “as many fail-safes as possible,” Wiacek said.

“An elevator doesn't have just one cable hoisting it,” he continued. “There's a lot of redundancy in those systems. Enterprises and organizations and governments haven't really had to operate like that. But that's going to be the new norm, whether we want it to be or not. Pandora's box is opened, and it's not going to go back inside.” ®
