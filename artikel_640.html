How to Get Started on Making a Difference as Tax Rules for Charitable Giving Change

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Philanthropist Rick Baker and Blank Rome tax expert Sean Weissbart offer inspiration and advice on navigating the season of giving on Variety's 'Strictly Business' podcast

Rick Baker is a wealth management expert by profession, advising many of Hollywood’s A-list on managing the money and portfolios for the long term in a volatile industry.

For Baker himself, the concept of wealth management means giving some of it away through a scholarship program that he launched more than 10 years ago in his hometown in rural Alabama. On the latest episode of Variety‘s “Strictly Business” podcast, as we ease into the winter holidays, Baker offers an inspirational story of how those with means can get started making a difference in the lives of others. And Sean Weissbart, a tax expert and partner at Blank Rome in New York, explains some significant changes to charitable giving federal tax rules that take effect for 2026.

Baker’s own life has been a fist-pumping story of a person from humble means seizing the opportunity through education after he received a scholarship to Alabama’s Auburn University. That set him on a path to building a tony wealth management practice in Beverly Hills over the past 35 years and counting. Baker has never forgotten the hard times he experienced growing up on a small farm in Crossville, Ala.

“I grew up in rural Alabama with two parents who were Depression kids. My dad could not read or write,” says Baker, who is today a managing director and wealth management advisor and portfolio manager with Merrill / Bank of America.

“I was fortunate enough to get an academic scholarship to Auburn University, and then later I attended the Wharton Business School. During that path, I learned firsthand how important education was. And so I decided to go back and start giving back in my hometown of Crossville, Alabama. A very, very rural community, about 3,000 in population. It has one red light that’s actually not even a red light. It’s a blinking yellow light. And I go back and I try to do things that will change that community and make it better and just pay it forward,” he says.

The foundation issues scholarships for secondary education tuition. It also has a mechanism to support local youth for food, housing and transportation costs that can stand in the way of the student attending school. Baker can’t hide his emotion in explaining what it means to be able to help remove even small obstacles to achievement for underprivileged kids. “I remember what it was like in rural Alabama and not having anything. I remember what it was like. And I never forget that because there’s such a thin line between being mega rich and mega, mega successful and not having anything — being the guy that’s struggling from paycheck to paycheck. It’s a thin line,” Baker says. “And we just have to remember that if we are fortunate enough to be able to do something for others, we need to do that because it could easily be taken away.”

Weissbart, in a separate conversation, weighs in with perspective on the new charitable giing tax rules for 2026 and beyond that were enshrined by Congress in last year’s so-called Big Beautiful Bill.

“There are a whole new set of rules that were enacted in 2026 that changed the landscape for how one goes about getting an income tax deduction for charitable giving,” Weissbart says.

Among them is a stipulation that the first half-percent of an individual’s adjusted gross income “is not eligible to be deducted as a charitable deduction,” he says. Translation, per Weissbart: A half-percent of a person’s adjusted gross income is taken off the top of any tax deduction for a charitable donation. For a person making $500,000 a year, one-half percent is $2,500. Therefore, if the person makes $10,000 in charitable donations, only $7,500 qualifies for a write-off.

After a roller-coaster week on Wall Street that saw the Anthropic IPO generate enormous wealth and a bumper crump of multimillionaires (at least on paper), Weissbart notes that stock gifts to charity can be a great offset to support a worthy cause and avoid high capital gains taxes.

“We’re talking about a very, very significant savings that actually mitigates the loss of that asset on your balance sheet,” he says.
