Big Tech takes a beating as online safety experts accuse companies of doing bare minimum

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Accessing banned content is as easy as whipping out a thesaurus, Parliament's Communications and Digital Committee hears

Technology companies are doing just enough to show a degree of compliance with the UK's Online Safety Act, but are failing to meet the needs of the individuals and young victims the legislation exists to protect, experts say.

A panel of online safety pros assembled before Parliament's Communications and Digital Committee on Tuesday, taking aim at the likes of Google, Microsoft, Meta, and ByteDance for failing to act on important matters.

Clare McGlynn, professor of law at Durham University and expert on violence against women and girls (VAWG), was especially critical of major search engine providers for their inconsistent downranking of harmful content search terms.

"Rape porn," for example, is correctly downranked and no user sees any relevant links until the tenth page of results, although adjacent terms such as "unwilling porn" can be found as early as the third page, McGlynn said.

"I just think if someone spent a little more time thinking 'I really want to minimize the risk of this material appearing,' I would take a systematic approach," she told the committee. "I would downrank 'unwilling' and 'unwanted,' and I would downrank 'asleep porn' as well… because it just means that it's easily findable.

"So, they don't rank some of them, but just not systematically or wholly consistently."

McGlynn went on to say that during her own research, "force porn" was rightly downranked heavily, although terms as similar as "forced porn" were not.

"If you were trying to think of the words that would be similar to say rape, and rape porn is extreme porn and it's not supposed to be on those platforms anymore, you would think of words like unwilling and unwanted," she added.

Google inevitably took much of McGlynn's flak due to its dominance of the search engine market, although the professor said the same issues can be observed on Bing, too.

Alexandra Whiston-Dew, partner at law firm Miscon de Raya, argued that search engines have such an important role in regulating the OSA, noting the limited resources at Ofcom's disposal.

She said that more needs to be done from the tech side to ensure different parties in the chain of publication are detected early and downranked or blocked accordingly.

There are publications, for example, that actively advertise themselves as websites that host illegal content, and these appear to be missing from the search engines' crosshairs.

"So, they are spotlighting the fact that they are publishing illegal content, and this is going under the radar not only from the search engines but also the other platforms and the businesses in the chain of publication," said Whiston-Dew. "Also Ofcom needs to really pull all of their resources together and attack that receptacle of illegal content."

Similarly, McGlynn raised the issue of porn sites continuing to host extreme porn, as they are legally allowed to do, to make it available to less regulated territories.

With the harmful content still on the site, all it takes is a VPN connection to access it, and bypass the age assurance mechanisms in the process.

As well as a piecemeal approach to downranking extreme content, experts said that tech platforms initially showed a strong appetite in quickly handling content-takedown requests, although this appears to have waned substantially.

Citing data from domestic violence and VAWG charity Refuge [PDF], Janaya Walker, interim director at the End Violence Against Women Coalition (EVAW), said that before November 2025, the success rate for these kinds of reports made using Refuge’s Trusted Flagger team stood at 53.7 percent. Over the past 12 months, however, this dropped to just 18.2 percent.

Further, since November 2025, zero percent of reports made to Meta have been successful, according to Refuge’s figures.

The charity’s Trusted Flagger team works closely with social media platforms to report content that victims may have reported to it. The idea is that if the report comes from an authority like Refuge, it is acted upon more quickly.

Walker said there was real concern about how platforms behave and whether they comply with existing regulation, and about what that means for specialist services supporting survivors in limo, particularly without an online commission to advocate for victims' and survivors' interests.

Meta did not immediately respond to questions about its takedown rate, but according to its Community Standards Enforcement Report, it removed 27.8 million posts related to adult nudity and sexual activity between April and June 2026. The report does not include a category devoted to VAWG-related reports, so take the data with the appropriate level of scrutiny.

Neither Google, Microsoft, nor TikTok responded to requests for comment.

The findings from Refuge and the discussion in Parliament this week reflect the wider concern about the efficacy of the OSA, and how online safety experts are frustrated with how little impact it has had, more than a year after the new duties came into force.

England's Children's Commissioner, Dame Rachel de Souza, told the same committee in September that the OSA "has made absolutely no difference" to children's access to harmful content online, as evidenced by the accounts of young people themselves.

Amid discussions from the panel of experts this week regarding the need for a civil redress scheme, McGlynn pointed to British Columbia's system as an example of how other countries are going further to protect people from online harms.

She said: "...an individual still does not have the right to demand an image be taken down. And that is where I think we just lack that capacity to say to an individual, 'this is what you need to do, and you can bring this legal action, for example, against X, against the perpetrator, you can get the material deleted.'

"In British Columbia in Canada, they have a swift online civil redress system where you can make these claims, and you can get instant orders to get this material taken down, and you can even get compensation for some of the harm suffered.

"So other countries are doing more, if you like, to give the individuals that right as well as all the other measures at the platform level." ®
