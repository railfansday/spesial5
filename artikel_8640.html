Want Stronger Bones? Weight Training May Only Be Part of the Solution

For decades science has been demonstrating, quite effectively, that weight training is good for your bones. It’s one of those physical truths that has become so thoroughly accepted nobody bothers arguing about it anymore. Lift weights, build muscle, strengthen your skeleton, and keep pushing. Excellent. Another axiom etched in stone.

Except for the fact that science has this annoying habit of reopening cases we thought were long closed.

A new meta-analysis published in Sports Medicine—Open looked at 24 clinical studies involving 1,238 healthy adults and found something you wouldn’t expect. When researchers examined biological markers associated with bone formation, the biggest responses weren’t coming from walking, jogging, step aerobics, or even conventional resistance training. They came from high-intensity activities involving acceleration, impact, sudden changes of direction, and explosive movement. Recreational soccer and handball produced some of the strongest responses, with certain bone-formation markers increasing by as much as 45 to 46 percent.

Impressive data for sure, but I wouldn’t go abandoning weight training just yet.

Bones are living tissue. They respond to mechanical stress, just like all the other structures involved, but apparently they pay particular attention when that stress arrives suddenly and from different directions. Running, jumping, cutting, stopping, accelerating, and changing direction produce forces that conventional lifting doesn’t produce, despite being extremely demanding muscularly. A brutally slow leg press can make your quads beg to be amputated, while your skeleton experiences something much less severe than it would if you were dribbling a soccer ball downfield dodging opponents.

That does not mean lifting weights is useless for bones. There’s plenty of existing evidence supporting resistance exercise for skeletal health, and strength training also improves muscle, balance, and physical function—all things you’d rather possess when gravity eventually reminds you who’s boss.

It means this particular analysis suggests resistance training may not be the entire answer.

There’s also a practical problem with telling older people worried about osteoporosis to immediately start jumping, cutting, and playing handball. The people most concerned about fragile bones are precisely the people we shouldn’t suddenly send sprinting through an obstacle course. For younger lifters, however, I think there’s a useful message here. Somewhere along the way, bodybuilding taught us to eliminate unnecessary movement. Machines got better. Exercises became more controlled. We learned how to isolate a muscle so effectively that the rest of the body barely has to participate. That’s great if the sole objective is hypertrophy.

Maybe occasionally sprint. Jump. Carry something. Change direction. Play a sport. Do something that requires your skeleton to deal with forces that don’t arrive on a perfectly greased track at exactly the same angle every repetition.

I’m still going to lift weights. You should too. But apparently your bones may appreciate being reminded that the human body was designed to do more than sit on a machine and push.

It’s really quite simple, if you want to look and respond like an athlete then you have to train like an athlete, which means you have to do athletic stuff. One trick ponies don’t age well.
