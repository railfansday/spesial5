Your Net Worth Isn’t Your Self-Worth – Measure Your Money Against Your Own Goals

Tying your sense of worth to your net worth makes every money setback feel personal. Separate the two on purpose. Write down what you value that money can’t measure, judge your finances only against your own goals and last year’s numbers, and talk to a therapist if money shame won’t let up.

It happens at every income. Someone behind on savings dreads opening the banking app, a high earner leaves dinner with wealthier friends feeling like a failure, and both are tempted to spend to look successful.

For many people, the tie between money and worth starts in childhood, with a parent who treated a big house or a new car as proof of doing well. Social media then puts other people’s vacations and remodels in front of you every day. And the hedonic treadmill, the way each raise or upgrade soon feels ordinary, keeps pushing up the amount that feels like enough.

Measured against other people and a bar that keeps rising, no amount will ever be enough. Your own goals and last year’s numbers stay put, which makes them the one fair measure of how you’re doing with money.

Picture someone who stops scrolling a friend’s vacation posts and starts a yearly check of her net worth against her own goals. She feels steadier before her numbers change at all.

For shame about debt, the first step is opening every statement and telling one person you trust.

Shame that has you hiding statements from a partner or avoiding money decisions altogether needs more than new habits. Talk to a financial therapist, who works on the feelings behind how you handle money, and use the Financial Therapy Association’s directory to find one who meets in person or online.
