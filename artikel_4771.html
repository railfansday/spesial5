Bye-Bye, Beige! This 517-Square-Foot Apartment Became a Creative Retreat Full of Light and Color

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

“My previous home was dominated by shades of beige,” Jaco Kroon says. “And it no longer suited me.” After a career in marketing, Kroon decided to head in a different direction and devote himself to a new creative pursuit: designing furniture for pets. He found the perfect setting for this fresh start in a 517-square-foot apartment in Amsterdam. Together with Elza Wandler, a designer and founder of her eponymous accessory brand, he created an interior that reflects his evolving vision. “I wanted a place that underscores my new direction—a more colorful, more creative, and more surprising home,” explains Kroon.

A vase by ceramist Pol Chambost brings contrast to the table.

“I’d seen a few articles about Elza Wandler’s home and immediately thought: That’s exactly the kind of environment I want: creative, personal, and full of objects that tell stories,” Kroon recalls. What began as creative interest soon developed into a friendship. During a lunch together in the designer’s garden, the two hit it off right away. In addition to their passion for interior design, they also share a love of dogs—they both own poodles.

As Kroon got to know Wandler’s home better, he was particularly struck by the fact that every object had its own unique character. They wanted to create a similar atmosphere in his apartment, which led to a close creative collaboration in which they both contributed their ideas and visions. For Kroon, his home soon became more than just a place to live: “While the work was underway, I was developing my own line of pet furniture and realized just how much the new interior was influencing my creative work as well.”

Jaco Kroon’s love for animals has come to shape his career. He’s seen here with his faithful toy poodle, Elio.

The biggest challenge of the project was making the best use of the limited space. The 517-square-foot apartment combines a kitchen, living room, dining area, and sleeping area without feeling cramped or having to make any sacrifices. According to Wandler, the key was to view the spaces as a cohesive ecosystem: “In such small spaces, every single object interacts with the others. Even a four-inch-tall vase can completely alter the balance of a room.”

The bedroom is decorated in shades of salmon, with custom cabinets framing the bed. The wallpaper is by Kelly Wearstler. Wall sconces by Contain and the bedding is by Bonsoirs.

This gave rise to the project’s motto: “Every item must be a Beyoncé”—that’s how Wandler and Kroon jokingly describe their design philosophy. It’s their way of expressing the idea that every piece should bring its own personality and presence to the home without disrupting the harmony of the environment. The color scheme also follows this principle with intense yet carefully coordinated tones—such as the eggplant in the kitchen and the warm, velvety salmon pink in the bedroom—paired with textured woods, vintage fabrics, and custom details. “At first, the idea of a burgundy-violet kitchen really put me off,” Kroon admits with a smile. “But the further we got into the project, the more I felt my horizons expanding.”

A blue bowl by Wandler x SolidNature and a ceramic basket by Ferm Living add splashes of color to the burgundy kitchen. The art on the wall is by Laura de Wilde.

One distinctive feature of the project is the interplay between functionality and aesthetics: Practical solutions blend so naturally into the design that they are barely recognizable as such at first glance. The historic Amsterdam townhouse, with its large windows, had very little storage space. That’s why Wandler took advantage of nearly every opportunity to create additional storage solutions. Custom cabinets frame the bed like an architectural backdrop, while the TV disappears into a specially designed piece of furniture made of zebrawood.

“Elza has turned what would normally be a purely functional piece of furniture into something beautiful. She even convinced me to hide the TV behind the doors of a zebrawood cabinet!” jokes Kroon. “Everything seems to have been designed for aesthetic reasons, but in fact, every element is tailored to make optimal use of the available space.” The decision to use zebrawood also follows this philosophy: Its striking grain lends the interior a vertical dynamism and makes the room appear more spacious. “I wanted to use the wood to create movement and depth,” explains Wandler. “In a small room, you always have to consider how to increase the sense of space.”

Colorful accents, such as the stool by Rutger de Regt, make a bold statement in the interior. The vase is by India Mahdavi and the sculpture on the wall is by Floor Merjenburgh.

As is often the case with Wandler’s projects, restored vintage pieces, contemporary design, and works by young artists all find a place side by side here. The large vintage sofa was completely reupholstered, while the artwork and decorative pieces come from a network of emerging designers and artists supported by the designer through Kamer—her curatorial platform dedicated to collectible design.

The dark wood of the floor complements the chocolate-brown armchair by Norr11.

“It’s important to me that I feel happy in my home,” says Kroon. “And that it stimulates creativity.” Wandler sought to create this feeling through her selection of artwork and a carefully coordinated lighting design. The latter plays a central role in the living concept: Since the apartment faces multiple directions, the natural light changes constantly throughout the day, causing colors and materials to appear different depending on the time. This creates the impression that this small apartment was conceived as a place that evolves and can accompany personal transformation; it embraces new ideas, new ambitions, and new identities. “The greatest compliment,” Wandler concludes, “was when Jaco said to me, ‘Now I feel like I’m finally living as the version of myself that I want to become.’”

The dining area, with a table from Westwing, is connected to the living room. The vintage sofa by Cini Boeri for Arflex has been reupholstered; the vase in front of the window is by l’Objet.
