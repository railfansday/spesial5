'Wifi Motion Sensing' Can Track Everything You Do Through Your Router

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

A simple wifi feature could let ISPs track your location and movement patterns within your home. "Wifi motion sensing" turns your ISP’s router into a motion detector, letting providers know exactly how many people are in the house, where they are situated, individual movement patterns, and, in some cases, even body shapes that could approximate a person’s identity.

Wifi routers rely on radio signals to connect wirelessly to your devices and appliances. Those same radio signals are also absorbed and reflected by the human body as they travel in all directions from the router. With the right software, ISPs can track how those wifi signals travel around your room, which can be studied to detect presence and sense motion patterns.

As long as your ISP’s router model supports this feature natively, it doesn’t require any additional hardware to be installed in your home. For example, Xfinity’s version of the feature pairs your router with five other connected appliances in your room to create oval-shaped sensing zones that can track your presence and movement within those spaces.

It isn’t a new feature, either—companies like Cognitive Systems and Origin Wireless that supply networking hardware to ISPs have been offering wifi motion sensing for years now, while early research prototypes from the MIT date back at least as far as 2015. In 2025, IEEE updated its Wi-Fi 802.11 standard to include motion sensing and lay out the architectural guidelines for it, which makes it likely that even more routers will now ship with the feature.

In its current consumer form, wifi motion sensing can help you control smart home apparatus like lights or thermostats, get pet safety notifications on your phone, set up a fall alert system for elderly users, and trigger appliances remotely based on human presence. But enabling it carries risks.

Most providers, including Cognitive Systems, claim that the technology isn’t sensitive to identity and can’t be used to track specific individuals. But even if true, that’s more likely to be a product decision rather than a technical limitation. Karlsruhe Institute researchers have shown that even ordinary wifi signals can identify individuals with a 99.5% accuracy.

Also worth noting: ISPs’ legal wording suggests that they may disclose the collected and stored motion data at any time to law enforcement, government agencies, regulators, and courts in response to subpoenas or during an emergency. ISPs may also retain your motion data in the cloud long-term in case they are required to facilitate a subpoena in the future.

As far as major ISPs are concerned, Xfinity (Comcast) is the only broadband provider that currently offers this feature across its commercial routers on the XB7, XB8, XB9, XB10, MXB1, and XER10 gateways. It’s opted out by default, but in case you or a family member already switched it on, you can disable it again by navigating to Home > WiFi Motion on the Xfinity app, click on the gear icon on the live view page, and tap Remove WiFi Motion.

Verizon Fios customers also had access to a Home Awareness feature on their routers that could be toggled on or off using the MyFios app, but that’s since been discontinued. In an official disclosure published shortly after the discontinuation, Verizon notified customers that it has deleted all associated movement logs from this service and no longer retains any motion data from users.

But there are other regional ISPs that offer it, too. For example, Breezeline (Atlantic Broadband) offers a feature called Motion within its Plume-powered application that alerts customers when movement is detected in their homes, among its other features. MidCo, a telecom company in the Upper Midwest, has a feature called Sense which can be toggled on or off using the MidCo Wi-Fi app. Plume, ADT-owned Origin Wireless, and Airties—all of whom supply technology to ISPs—have also been marketing motion sensing to customers for the last few years.

If you’re unsure if your wifi router or ISP has motion sensing features or opts you in by default, look for a feature called “Sense,” “Motion,” or equivalent in your broadband’s mobile app. Exact steps differ greatly from one ISP to another, so it’s best not to assume that they will all opt you out by default, or that they collect the same types of data. With the 802.11bf update to IEEE standards in 2025, this technology will be accessible to even more router manufacturers, so expect adoption to grow among internet providers as well.
