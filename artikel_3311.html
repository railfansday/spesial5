IVs, Jazz Bands, and Ad Reads: 24 Hours Inside the Gronk Industrial Complex

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

R ob Gronkowski is in a familiar place as magic hour concedes to Friday-night lights in Los Angeles. He is pacing in a locker room on the campus of Sierra Canyon School, a high school that counts various Jenners and LeBron James’ sons among its alumni.

The future Hall of Famer tight end is crew-cutted and cut. At 37, he is just five months older than Travis Kelce, who is still hauling in touchdowns from Patrick Mahomes, and he still looks like he could grab six catches for 107 yards and two TDs. Alas, Gronk — like Jesus and Cher, he goes by one name — exited the game at 33 with four Super Bowl rings.

He has the full attention of the gathered players. Gronk hits all of his marks in his pregame talk. Be accountable. Trust your teammates. Make the play. “You’re going to remember Friday-night football games for the rest of your lives,” says Gronk.

He speaks in a cadence that, for the clueless, suggests a brain pan both punch-drunk and super high.

Gronk gets serious, or as serious as someone named Gronk can get. He talks of injuries — Gronk lost a college season to back problems and major parts of his NFL career to a list of injuries that reads like the medical chart of a guy who got T-boned by a Ford F-150 while driving a Nissan Versa.

“If you’re injured, you can’t participate,” says Gronk. “You’re worthless to the team. You gotta take care of your body. You gotta rehab hard.”

“And speaking of that, we’re talking DNA Vibe,” says Gronk. “It’s a jazz band and consists of red-light therapy. It has vibrational frequency on it, too. And every single one of you here in this room on this team is getting one. Use the jazz band; it works to help the healing process naturally in your body.”

The players look confused. No matter. Gronk finishes up.

“Thank you for having me, and kick some ass!”

“There’s different levels of being yourself. I’m not gonna tell ‘69’ jokes on tv — no chance.”

I’m scheduled to talk with Gronk the next afternoon at the Waldorf Astoria Beverly Hills. That morning, I look up the product he mentioned the night before. It has the cumber­some official name of DNA Vibe Jazz Band and has nothing to do with learning to play bebop during fifth period at Millard Fillmore Junior High and everything to do with helping you recover from injuries while using red-light therapy that I do not understand. You may not be surprised to learn that Gronkowski is DNA Vibe Jazz Band’s primary spokesman. At the hotel, Gronk happily shakes my hand, his massive forearms covered by matching bandages.

“I get really dehydrated when I travel, so I get an IV,” says Gronk. “I also got ozone therapy, where it takes out the blood and then you put ozone in it.”

I smile as if I have even the smallest idea of what the hell he is talking about. I try to move to more familiar ground. I’m about to ask him about his role on the top-rated Fox NFL Sunday pregame show when a bushy-haired man approaches us. Gronk makes the introduction.

The guy is serial entrepreneur Lance Collins, who co-founded the sports-drink company BodyArmor, whose remaining 85 percent Coca-Cola bought for $5.6 billion in 2021. (The soft-drink company bought the first 15 percent in 2018.) Gronk’s endorsement deal included equity, earning him a big payout. Collins and Gronk are now on to another sports drink.

There are celeb endorsers who observe a firewall between a theoretically journalistic interview and hawking products. That’s not Gronk. “Put it in the story,” says Gronkowski. “Come on, let’s put it in the story.”

“Recover 180,” Gronk repeats. “It’s an organic sports drink, and it’s filled with electrolytes.”

Gronk calls the drink delicious and tasty. Collins suggests “delicious and nutritious,” an improvement, and Gronk instantly repeats the revised copy.

I’m beginning to think Collins being in the lobby is not coincidental.

Collins pulls out his phone and begins showing me ads for the drink. He only stops after I theatrically look at my watch for a third time. “I’m gonna make a ton of money, and he’ll make a lot of money,” says Collins. He mentions he has a tequila line that also features Gronk.

Rob Gronkowski was the rare tight end who could pancake a linebacker on one play and then one-hand a pass from Tom Brady in the end zone on the next. All of this while partying his ass off, often with his four brothers and, sometimes, his dad, who built a state-of-the-art weight room in the family basement for his boys. They all had stints as pro athletes. The Brothers Gronk liked to prank one another with stunts like throwing forks at each other hard enough to lodge in someone’s elbow. And then there was the time at the oldest brother’s Vegas bachelor party that one of them — or someone in their crew — took a dump in a chip bag. Their dates were unamused. (I learned all of this while reading his memoir, It’s Good to Be Gronk, a not-exactly-Nabokovian account of his salad days.)

But that’s all in the past. Gronkowski has now lined up for the next trick play: erasing the line between sincerity and commerce with his buffoonish charm. It is both impressive and a bit icky.

Longtime friend and former New England Patriots teammate Julian Edelman says it’s all about his work ethic. According to Edelman, Gronk arrives on time and knows his lines better than anyone on commercial shoots.

“I’m a slapdick compared to Rob Gronkowski.”

OK! Welcome to the Gronk Industrial Complex. Keep your head on a swivel or you’re gonna get blindsided.

From the start, Gronk understood something that many famous dudes never do: Shtick is tolerated, but only after brilliance has been established. Like Dennis Rodman and Tom Cruise before him, Gronk knows the world will let you be a total weirdo if you perform like a superstar. But before you jump on the proverbial Oprah’s couch you must — in Gronk terminology — kick some ass.

Here’s a Gronk creation story. Edelman was in his second year with the Patriots when Gronkowski arrived from the University of Arizona, where he lived in an off-campus house with a hot tub in the front yard. But Edelman didn’t see that side of Gronk in their first training camp together. Initially, Gronk grinded away at the playbook and impressed head coach Bill Belichick with his dominant blocking.

Then the team broke camp and Edelman saw Gronk Unchained. A bunch of players hit a nightclub, and within minutes there was the real Gronk.

“When someone says, ‘I thought this guy was going to be an idiot,’ I just start laughing.”

“He’s dancing in the middle of the dance floor, going crazy, having the time of his life, being Gronk,” remembers Edelman. “And I walk in, and I’m like, ‘Oh, my God, what is this guy doing?’ Rob comes over, and there’s a waiter walking by, and the waiter has a bunch of drinks and finger food. Gronk just grabs a handful of shrimp and eats the shrimp. He’s like, ‘What’s up, Jules?’ And I’m like, ‘Oh, my God, what is this?’”

Let it be noted that Gronk did not grab the shrimp until he showed his value.

“No one could say anything because of how hard he worked and his production,” says Edelman. “Even the coaching staff — they’re like, ‘Well, that’s just fucking Gronk. If he’s going to score three touchdowns and be the point of attack for our running game, then whatever he does, we don’t care.’”

Bill Bradley was a legendary basketball player and one-time presidential candidate who shares nothing in common with Gronk. Bradley once said domination on the court and life depended on situational awareness.

“You develop a sense of where you are,” said Bradley.

That’s Gronk. Here’s an example. For his entire public life, Gronk has made “69” jokes, with the legend being he once bought $69,000 of Apple stock in 2014 on the recommendation of his contractor, and then forgot about it for years. (Depending on when he bought it, that $69,000 would be worth roughly a million dollars today if it wasn’t sold.)

I ask him if it is hard for him to rein in his inner 69 on the Fox pregame show, which is decidedly G-rated.

“There’s different levels of being yourself, 100 percent,” says Gronk. “I’m not gonna tell the ‘69’ jokes on TV — no chance.” He smiles his goofy smile.

“But a podcast or something, that’s the place to say all those types of jokes.”

He also knows not to bite the hand that feeds him. I ask him if he is worried about the impact of CTE, a degenerative brain disease linked to repeated blows to the head. He dissembles with the skill of a tobacco lobbyist.

“You hear all the studies about CTE, and there’s a lot of science behind it,” says Gronk. “You’ve got to believe in the science, but it’s still kind of a myth as well, because how can you truly pinpoint anything to football?”

To the casual observer, Gronk comes across as a cartoon character with a caveman name. That’s how he likes it.

“The best position I love to be in is when I’m underestimated,” says Gronk as he exchanges a bro handshake with his IV guy. “When someone says, ‘I thought this guy was going to be an idiot,’ I just start laughing.”

“That’s when I know that that person is truly an idiot.”

The next day, Gronkowski arrives just after 6 a.m. at the Fox NFL Sunday studio. He’s the kid on the show — 18 years younger than Michael Strahan, 29 years younger than Howie Long, and more than 40 years younger than Terry Bradshaw, all Hall of Famers. Still, he seems to have the lowest energy level of the bunch when they emerge for a run-through shortly after 7 a.m. Maybe it’s the crimson-checked suit and tie he had to don. The entire panel is wearing suits from the Collection by Michael Strahan (available at JCPenney).

The atmosphere before airtime feels less like a billion-dollar television property than the greenroom at a 55-and-over-community talent show. Bradshaw sings snatches of Marty Robbins’ “Big Iron,” a country & western song released in 1959. Turns out Strahan knows the song from his dad. The two sing the chorus: “And he’s here to do some business/With the big iron on his hip/Big iron …”

Long looks on like an anthropologist studying an ancient and backward tribe. Gronk doesn’t seem to notice, his head buried in his cue cards, trying to memorize the points he wants to make. A few minutes later, Bradshaw tries to knock the cue cards out of Gronk’s meaty hands.

“I love messing with him,” Bradshaw will tell me. “He’s so dialed in. I try to un-dial him.”

A half-century ago, Bradshaw turned a country-bumpkin persona into a half-century career as a pitchman, musician, and NFL broadcaster. He recognizes a fellow traveler.

“The camera loves him,” says Bradshaw. “He’s harmless. It’s easy to like him.”

Harmless is not the word I’d apply to a man who spent 11 NFL seasons beating the shit out of defenders, but Bradshaw has a point. Gronkowski’s secret power is that he conjures up Yogi Bear rather than Tony Soprano. No man eating Frosted Flakes out of a giant bowl in their man cave is going to worry that Gronk thinks he is smarter than them.

Television did not come naturally. Gronk understood football instinctually, seeing linebackers and safeties the way Jaws might size up a baby dolphin. Explaining what he saw in TV soundbites was another thing. During his first years at Fox, he prepared for TV as if he was the awkward lead in a high school play.

Looking back on his playing days, Gronk says, “I’m shocked I was able to do it all.”

“I would remember a whole paragraph,” says Gronk. “I would say word for word the whole paragraph. It wasn’t fun being on TV like that.”

His producer, Bill Richards, could see Gronk laboring. Richards reminded him that the show was not a three-act play. He told him just to listen to the other men at the desk and then react to what they said.

“This is live TV,” Richards told him. “We want you for what you think at the moment.”

Richards bristles slightly when I wonder if Fox hired Gronk for comic relief, a Chris Farley type talking about the NFL’s best edge rushers. Sure, there are the stunts — Gronk once jumped from a military helicopter into the Pacific Ocean for the show, which led Strahan to observe that Gronk hasn’t “learned what to do is say no when asked to do stuff.” Still, Richards wants the audience to remember that Gronk the crash-test dummy was also one of the greatest football players ever.

“He’s not just the frat guy,” says Richards. “He knows football.”

Being Gronk requires a willingness to look ridiculous. That’s A-OK for Gronk. In a segment that initially was filmed as an in-house joke, the Fox crew invented a Dallas Cowboys tight end and asked Gronk to analyze him, since he played the position. Gronk searched his memory for the name. Nothing. The player did not exist, but Gronk did not panic. He pivoted to the Cowboys’ actual tight end, Jake Ferguson, and explained how quarterback Dak Prescott would continue looking for him. The prank worked because Gronk was simultaneously dumb and smart. (Gronk loved the mischief, and the segment ran on the actual show.)

On set, Bradshaw needles him constantly. Gronk doesn’t mind; this is how ex-jocks express affection, through humiliation. Bradshaw clearly has great affection for Gronk. He worries about him like an agony aunt.

“There was a time when I was the hottest young thing out there,” says Bradshaw. Now, Bradshaw watches Gronk go from show to ad shoot to the airport and back. “He’s gone, gone, gone,” says Bradshaw. “Commercial, commercial, commercial, all over the place.”

Bradshaw warned Gronk to pace himself. At the Super Bowl in 2025, Gronk hit the wall.

“He was knocked out,” says Bradshaw. “He was so tired. He’d been going, going, going. For the Super Bowl, he must have had 88 events.”

Gronk rejects the premise. He has brought professional discipline to the business of being Gronk. He sleeps, does hot yoga, does his ozone thing, and restores himself before the next encounter.

I ask Gronk if it gets exhausting being, well, Gronk for the masses.

“I can’t always be on, and I can’t satisfy everyone,” says Gronk.

This sounds like common sense until you understand Gronk’s predicament. His fans approach him and want good-guy Gronk who spikes footballs, makes 69 jokes, and invites them in ads for FanDuel to blow their paychecks and join the Sportsbook Reward Club. They don’t want to see a glowering Gronk wearing sunglasses in first class.

Edelman knows when his buddy hits his limit. An exhausted Gronk, he says, is like a dog whose owner has left him alone for three days. He is still Gronk, but the light has gone out of his eyes.

Gronkowski gives a dismissive wave when anyone suggests he works too much. He insists that he and his girlfriend, Camille Kostek, a former Patriots cheerleader, love to put their phones away and just chill at their New England home.

I don’t buy it. Work is both the cause of that exhaustion and the cure for something else. Football ended abruptly for Gronkowski, as it does for nearly everyone in the NFL. He first retired at 29, returned to win another Super Bowl with Brady in Tampa, and left for good at 33.

All his life, somebody told Gronk where to be and what to do. At Patriots training camp, his days began at seven in the morning and ended at 10 at night. Practices, meetings, treatments, meals, and film study swallowed every waking hour. He remembers that existence now with something close to wonder.

“I’m shocked how I was able to do it all.”

But the absence of structure proved just as disorienting. After retirement, he would look at an empty week and initially feel liberated. Then he’d get restless.

“I’m already bored,” Gronk remembers thinking. “What do I do with myself?”

Football provided the structure that made the fun possible. Now, the business of being Gronk serves the same purpose. The difference today is there is no offseason.

It’s now around 5:45 p.m., and Gronk sheds his suit and heads to a decidedly less-flashy part of the Fox studio, one filled with Alexi Lalas memorabilia. Gronk and his friend Edelman prepare to tape their podcast, The Gronk & Jules Show. Both have been working for 12 hours. Now, they must reconstruct the day in football and push premium dog chow.

The podcast is where Gronk’s football intelligence and Gronk’s foolishness peacefully coexist. He explains how a strong running game pulls linebackers forward, opening the middle of the field for big plays. But then he guesses that Saints quarterback Tyler Shough was drafted in the fifth, sixth, or seventh round. A producer informs him Shough was the 40th overall pick.

“Might as well be the sixth,” says Gronk. “Might as well.”

He is now babbling like a giant toddler who missed his afternoon nap, but it works. Gronk says an AI version of himself must have made one of his incorrect predictions. He theorizes that Elon Musk named Grok after him but removed the “n” to avoid paying for the naming rights.

Eventually, it is time for ad reads. One is for Carhartt. Gronk whispers to Edelman.

Edelman tells him it’s work clothes for men.

A minute later, Gronk is wearing a Carhartt sweatshirt, riffing about the protective qualities of the garment for Buffalo Bills fans diving on folding tables, as they like to do.

“You know you’re not going to break your arm,” says Gronk. “And if you do, it’s just not going to hurt as much.”

Someone up the chain of command has decided that the ad reads should be preceded by some classic Edelman and Gronk banter. Today, that means a lengthy read for Sundays for Dogs devolves into a discussion of how dry dog food produces dry dog shit while wet dog food produces — you guessed it — wet dog shit.

Finally, the two are done. Gronk is out on his feet. Before he disappears into a waiting car, I ask him how tired he is.

Gronk begins explaining the advantages of recording the podcast immediately after the Fox show, when the games are still fresh in their minds. Then he stops.

“The best position I love to be in is to be underestimated,” says Rob Gronkowski.
