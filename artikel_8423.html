Need Life Insurance? Only If Someone Relies on Your Income or Shares Your Debts

You need life insurance if your death would leave someone short of money, such as a spouse or kids living on your income, a partner on your mortgage, or a co-signer. If no one fits, skip it. If someone does, buy a term policy, which covers a set number of years, while you’re young and the price is low.

To see whether anyone fits, list everyone who would lose income or pick up a bill if you died next month, and how many years each would need help.

The obvious names are a spouse and young kids, plus anyone who shares a debt with you, such as a partner on the mortgage or a parent who co-signed a loan. Easier to miss are a business partner who would need cash to buy your share of the business from your family, a parent whose rent you cover, and a child with a disability who will need support as an adult. A stay-at-home parent needs coverage too, since the family would have to pay someone for the childcare and housework.

If no one makes your list, you can skip the policy, provided your savings would pay for your funeral. Debts in your name alone don’t change that. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says only your estate, the money and property you leave, owes them, though some state laws make a surviving spouse pay certain debts.

If your list has names, check whether your employer’s coverage is enough before you count on it. Then work out how much the people on your list would need, and buy your own policy soon.

Most buyers choose level term, the Insurance Information Institute notes. Your age and health when the policy starts set its premium, the price you pay, and that price stays the same for the whole term. Say a 30-year-old man who doesn’t smoke shares a $300,000, 30-year mortgage with his partner. A $300,000 policy that lasts 30 years would let his partner keep the house, and Ethos, which sells life insurance online, estimates it would cost $24 to $45 a month for a man that age in average health.

Buy coverage that lasts as long as the longest need on your list, such as a 30-year term policy for a new 30-year mortgage, or one that runs until your youngest child finishes college.
