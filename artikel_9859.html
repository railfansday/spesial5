Before You Spend More on Ads, Find Out What Happened to Your Last 300 Leads

I worked with a company selling and installing photovoltaic systems for homeowners just as a government subsidy program was preparing to bring a sharp increase in demand. At the time, the business was receiving between 10 and 20 leads a month. The website needed substantial improvements, enquiries came through different channels, and the sales team had no CRM to manage them in one place.

Preparing for the campaigns involved working on the commercial process alongside the marketing activities. The company needed a way to collect enquiries, make them available to sales and keep track of what happened after the first contact. These decisions had to be made while the website, campaigns and sales procedures were being developed.

During the campaign, the company generated approximately 10,000 leads, around 3,300 per month. The government program created exceptional demand, while the advertising campaigns and website improvements helped capture that interest.

My role covered marketing strategy and the coordination of its implementation. I managed the Meta campaigns, coordinated the agency responsible for Google Ads and worked with the web developers on the website. On the commercial side, I worked with the founder and sales team on selecting and integrating the CRM, defining how enquiries would be handled and preparing the information salespeople needed in their conversations.

The volume eventually reached a level that would have been difficult to manage through the previous arrangements. The preparation of the commercial process became an important part of making that increase manageable.

When someone fills in a contact form, the information may pass through several systems before reaching a salesperson. The website receives the submission, an advertising platform may register a conversion, an automation tool transfers the data, and the CRM creates or updates a record.

Each system records a different stage. A successful conversion in the advertising account tells marketing that somebody completed the intended action. The sales team needs the information to appear in the right place, with enough context to understand the enquiry and continue the conversation.

The difference becomes important when the number of requests increases.

At 10 or 20 enquiries a month, a small team may be able to manage follow-up through emails, spreadsheets and direct conversations. People can compensate for an imperfect process because the volume is relatively low. When several hundred requests arrive in a week, keeping track of them requires a shared system and clear responsibilities.

There is also a reporting limitation worth understanding. A CRM can show the records it has received, including their status and assigned owner. An enquiry that disappears before reaching the CRM may be absent from its reports altogether.

For that reason, it is useful to keep a record of submissions at the point where they enter the process, such as the website form or the advertising platform, and compare it with the records created in the CRM. The comparison helps identify requests that were submitted successfully but did not reach the intended destination.

The company also needs a consistent way to distinguish legitimate enquiries from internal tests, spam and duplicates. Otherwise, the numbers can become difficult to reconcile, especially when the same person contacts the business through more than one channel.

These details affect the quality of information available to both marketing and sales. They become increasingly relevant when management is making decisions based on lead volumes and campaign performance.

In the renewable energy project, we brought enquiries from several sources into a common CRM. Website forms, paid campaigns and other communication channels needed to feed into a commercial process that the sales team could use consistently.

Zapier was part of the setup, transferring leads from paid campaigns into the sales workflow. I coordinated the CRM selection and implementation, the integration requirements and the work between the people responsible for the different parts of the process.

Once the enquiries reached the CRM, we also needed to establish how they would be handled.

Who would contact the customer, what information should be available before that conversation and how should the team deal with requests requiring technical clarification? These decisions involved the sales function and had to reflect the company's actual capacity.

The customer questions were also specific to the market situation. Homeowners wanted to understand whether they qualified for the subsidy program, what documentation was required, how the financing worked and what installation involved. A salesperson needed accurate information to explain the process and identify whether an enquiry could become a viable commercial opportunity.

We worked on sales scripts, answers to recurring questions and procedures for handling the incoming requests. We also introduced qualification logic to help the team prioritize enquiries as volumes increased.

The CRM provided a common place for information and follow-up. The value of the setup depended on how the team used it and whether the information reaching sales was sufficient for the next step.

This is an important consideration when selecting software. A company can compare CRM features and automation options extensively, yet still encounter difficulties if the people involved have different expectations about how a request should be handled.

For a business preparing to scale acquisition, the practical requirements deserve attention early in the project. They influence the choice of integrations, the CRM configuration, the procedures used by sales and the information that marketing needs to collect.

Marketing reports provide detailed information about the activities that generate enquiries. Impressions, clicks, conversion rates and cost per lead help evaluate the campaigns and identify areas that need improvement.

Once a person submits a request, the commercial process introduces additional measurements.

I would separate the time needed for an enquiry to reach its assigned owner from the time until the first human contact attempt. The first depends largely on the integrations and routing procedures. The second is influenced by staffing, workload and the way the sales team organizes follow-up.

A record showing that a lead was created in the CRM says little about when somebody actually tried to contact that person. Keeping the two moments separate helps management identify where a delay occurred.

The distinction between an attempted contact and a successful conversation matters as well. The team controls when it makes a call or sends a personal response. Whether the customer answers depends on circumstances outside the team's control.

Response time has been studied in connection with online lead generation for many years. In The Short Life of Online Sales Leads, published in Harvard Business Review in 2011, researchers audited 2,241 US companies by sending test enquiries. They found that 37% responded within an hour, while 23% did not respond at all.

The research provides historical evidence of how frequently online enquiries went unanswered or received delayed responses. For an individual business, current measurements are needed to understand how its own process performs.

Working hours are another part of the calculation. A request submitted on Friday evening may follow a different schedule from one received on Monday morning. The relevant departments should agree on response expectations and apply them consistently.

These measurements are useful when they reflect how customers are actually handled. They can also reveal differences between acquisition channels or periods of unusually high demand.

If a particular form regularly produces records with missing information, the problem may require changes to the form or integration. If leads are assigned correctly but remain untouched for long periods, management may need to examine workload, priorities or sales capacity.

The data gives the teams a common basis for discussing where improvements are needed.

Before increasing the acquisition budget, I would recommend testing the route followed by a real enquiry, using the same forms and channels available to customers.

Submit a request and record when it was sent. Then check when it appeared in the CRM, whether the information was transferred correctly and which person received responsibility for follow-up. The test continues until the first human contact attempt has been recorded.

The timing should be assessed against expectations agreed in advance. For an automatic transfer between systems, a company might initially test a target of a few minutes. Human follow-up needs its own target, taking into account working hours, the type of enquiry and the team's capacity.

Each important source deserves a separate test because the routes can differ. A website form may be connected directly to the CRM, while a paid social lead form may use an automation platform. Email enquiries can require yet another procedure.

After confirming the normal route, I would check several situations that can expose weaknesses in the setup.

A duplicate submission should be recognized without creating unnecessary confusion in the sales process. A request arriving outside working hours should follow the agreed schedule. An enquiry assigned to somebody who is absent should have a way to reach another responsible person.

I would also test the situation in which a customer receives an automatic confirmation, but the request fails to reach the sales team. This is particularly important because the confirmation can give the customer the impression that the process is moving forward.

The purpose of testing these scenarios is to establish how the company identifies a problem, who receives the relevant information and which person or department can resolve it.

A single successful test cannot establish how reliably the process operates under normal conditions. The company also needs to examine records collected during actual activity, particularly when volumes increase.

Together, these checks can show whether the integrations and procedures are functioning as intended and where additional preparation may be necessary.

Consider a business generating 300 enquiries a month and planning to increase its advertising budget substantially.

I would begin by reviewing what happened to those 300 enquiries. How many reached the CRM correctly, received an owner and were contacted within the agreed time? How many became meaningful commercial conversations, and what happened to the remaining requests?

The answers help determine whether the next investment should go primarily toward acquisition or whether the commercial process needs attention first.

A company may discover that the campaigns are generating relevant enquiries, while the sales team has limited capacity to follow up. Additional advertising would then increase the number of requests entering the same process.

In another situation, the handoff may work well, but few enquiries become qualified opportunities. That would justify examining the targeting, the offer, the information collected through forms and the conversations taking place afterward.

These assessments require the teams to look beyond their individual reports.

In the renewable energy project, the rapid growth in demand made coordination with the commercial operation especially important. We worked on the lead-handling process and the materials used by sales while the campaigns and website were being developed. The company also had to adapt its operational capacity as demand increased.

The subsidy program created unusual market conditions, so I would be careful about applying the campaign's numerical results to other businesses. The questions regarding capacity and the commercial process remain relevant whenever management expects a substantial increase in enquiries.

The same reasoning applies to promises made in marketing materials.

A response-time claim, such as a commitment to answer enquiries within an hour, should be supported by measurements showing that the company can achieve it consistently. I prefer to evaluate performance over a meaningful period, including busy weeks and staff absences, before recommending a measurable promise to customers.

Delivery expectations, installation schedules and quotation times deserve similar attention because they depend on several activities within the business.

For acquisition planning, I bring marketing performance, lead handling and sales results into the same management discussion. Each provides information about a different part of the commercial process, and the connections between them can influence the decision to increase spending.

An increase in cost per lead deserves an examination of campaign performance and market conditions. A growing number of uncontacted enquiries calls for a review of the handoff and available sales capacity. When contacts are timely but qualification remains weak, the investigation moves toward the offer, targeting and commercial conversations.

Before recommending a higher acquisition budget, I want to understand how the business handles the enquiries it already receives and what additional volume its people and processes can support. That requires examining what happens after submission, including the requests that have yet to become sales conversations.
