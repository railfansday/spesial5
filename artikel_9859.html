Media relations is usually managed as a communications function. The work is planned around announcements, interviews and thought leadership. Success is recorded as placements, impressions, share of voice and perhaps social engagement.

That leaves a great deal of commercial value on the table. Experience points toward a different model, earned media can function as revenue amplification when used right.

The principle is straightforward. A credible third party saying something important about a company, its customers or its executives gives a revenue organization an asset that its own marketing cannot manufacture. The best revenue leaders put that asset to work and demand more.

This is increasingly how the best businesses structuresprograms. In one proposal we’ve seen, the explicit business outcome is that coverage becomes proof of a company’s “access, insight and influence,” creating assets for sales and renewal conversations. Another framework  in our sample calls for media relations to be mapped directly to opportunity progression and messaging for late-stage buyers, rather than treated as a parallel marketing activity.

Maybe the most revealing example, however, comes from the work with one particular CEO and a health tech company.

The traditional PR operating model has a natural endpoint: pitch, then interview, then coverage, and then report the result.

A revenue-driven model adds an entire second half: business objective, and then differentiated point of view or proof, and then media result, and then internal activation, and then customer activation, and then executive amplification, and then prospect engagement, and then opportunity progression, and then measurement.

Okay, that’s more steps. And more isn’t necessarily more.

The process here starts by confirming business goals and determining how media relations, content and social can accelerate those goals. It then identifies a business-aligned point of view, milestones and external angles and tracks the engagement and response generated by results.

The expert revenue leader displays an instinct that sophisticated revenue organizations should study. When there is an important story, she frequently pushes immediately beyond the publicity itself.

Consider the direction around a customer announcement. She wants a company-wide message, a customer communication and a webinar with customer executives, extending a single communications moment into several channels and constituencies.

Around another announcement, she asks for the news to be posted through corporate channels, amplified through additional company accounts, reposted by her personally and communicated to the broader organization.

This is organizational activation. The press result becomes an occasion for employees, customers, partners and prospects to encounter the same evidence from different directions. There is also an important discipline behind the urgency. Ahead of a major story, the Revenue Leader explicitly directs the team to focus the media conversation on the “big industry changing story” rather than reducing it to product features and functions.

That is exactly what strong revenue leadership needs from communications. Features answer what does your product do? But a category-level media narrative answers the much more valuable questions. Why does the market need you? Why now? Why should an executive pay attention?

This becomes especially powerful in complex B2B markets. A salesperson can tell a prospect that a marquee customer trusts the company. Marketing can put the customer logo on a slide.

However, credible customer participation plus independent media coverage changes the quality of the evidence. Take the example of a health system where a health tech company serving them records national healthcare coverage, customer executives publicly discussing the health tech company, accompanying thought leadership and more than 20 healthcare leaders proactively engaging with the Revenue Leader after the announcement, including people commenting on the significance of the customer relationship.

A related case-study we’re familiar with describes the same outcome as 20+ healthcare-leader engagements immediately following the announcement and notes that the resulting campaign expanded into a marketing effort led by the client’s Chief Revenue Officer. Here’s that sequence, customer success, and then customer advocacy, and then media validation, and then market attention, and then revenue activation.

Revenue leaders should stop evaluating media exclusively through audience size. For a complex enterprise business, 20 conversations with relevant health-system leaders can matter far more than hundreds of thousands of undifferentiated impressions. The same principle appears elsewhere in my experience. Let’s take a client roundtable and thought-leadership ecosystem contributing to approximately $880,000 in ARR from resulting customer relationships.

This suggests a better hierarchy for measuring B2B media relations:

Level 2: AttentionDid the right audiences see or engage with it?

Level 3: ConversationDid customers, prospects, partners, investors or influencers respond?

Level 4: Commercial movementDid those conversations create meetings, introductions, opportunities, expansions or renewals?

Level 5: RevenueCan opportunities or revenue be credibly connected to the motion?

Impressions still have value. They simply sit near the beginning of the measurement chain rather than the end.

That process can impose useful discipline on strategy. A Revenue Leader we know has repeatedly emphasized measurable outcomes in her external positioning. A recent company social-content record captures her view that transformation should be assessed by whether it changes how people work and improves outcomes, rather than simply tracking deployment or adoption activity. Revenue teams can apply exactly the same standard to communications. The objective is a measurable change in what important people know, believe or do.

When executives, salespeople, marketers and customers repeatedly encounter the same externally validated ideas, the revenue-led organization gradually becomes clearer about what it sells and why customers care. PR strategy should flow backward from that objective.

Reporters rarely need another company claiming leadership. What they and their editors need is something that advances a story their audience is following, or should be following that is often bigger than one business. Therefor you need a methodology explicitly testing stories for relevance, timeliness and novelty, while asking what headline or tension will earn attention. Revenue leaders should bring communications teams the raw materials: Customer evidence. Data. Product usage. Unexpected market observations. Buyer objections. Regulatory changes. Competitor gaps. Economic pressures. Operational results.

The highest-value enterprise stories frequently involve customers. But the story should advance the customer's agenda too. In fact that should be the primary goal. Framing a story around the buyer's own transformation priorities encourages that customer to step forward publicly as an innovator, and thereby better validate the technology or service provider.

Do not wait until a story appears to ask, “What should we do with this?” Before publication, designate:

CEO/internal note explaining why it matters

Talking points + link + target account list

Incorporate proof into upcoming meetings/webinars

Use in renewal and expansion conversations

This is one of the clearest lessons from the Revenue Leader approach. Distribution is part of the strategy, rather than cleanup work after the PR team finishes.

“Thought you might like this article” is weak activation. Provide sellers with a specific connection:

We have been talking about the operational challenge your team raised around X. This piece gets into how another organization is approaching exactly that issue. Thought it would be useful before our next conversation.

The media result creates permission to reengage. The account context turns permission into a useful conversation.

For major earned results, concentrate activation.

CEO, company and participating customer amplification. Internal distribution. Priority-account outreach begins.

Revenue leaders make personal contact with high-value relationships. Sales uses the story with open opportunities. Marketing examines engagement for relevant names and accounts.

PR captures reporter reactions and follow-on opportunities. Revenue captures buyer reactions. Communications and sales compare signals and identify next actions. Then repeat selected outreach at 7, 30 and 90 days wherever the article remains relevant.

Consider tracking the following. First you have the result, that goes to target-account engagements, that goes to executive responses, that goes to conversations, that turns into meetings, then you get to opportunities influenced, there’s a pipeline value, and then you get to closed revenue.

The process becomes a loop. Revenue intelligence leads to a stronger media narrative, that leads to earned authority, which gets you to conversations, which lands you with new revenue intelligence. There is an important caution. Commercial urgency should not turn the PR function into an amplification machine that sacrifices journalistic credibility or customer trust.

Here's where professional media management remains as close to essential as we can get. Internal discussions around high-profile customer opportunities emphasize maintaining clear roles and guardrails when navigating customers and reporters because poorly coordinated intervention can reduce available media options or undermine trust.

That produces another best practice. Revenue should determine where the organization needs to go. Skilled communications professionals should determine how to navigate the media environment necessary to help get it there.

The two functions need to operate closely without collapsing into one another.

Choose three revenue priorities, ten to twenty strategic accounts and three defensible market narratives. Map customers, data, executives and upcoming milestones against them.

Develop reporter relationships around the issues rather than pitching indiscriminately. Advance the customer evidence, executive POV, proprietary data and third-party validation that support the commercial narrative.

For each significant result, execute internal, executive, social, customer and account-level activation. Record responses in the CRM and hold a monthly media-to-revenue review between communications, marketing and revenue leadership.

At 90 days, ask a harder question than “How much coverage did we receive?”

Ask which relationships are materially stronger because of the authority we earned?

Media relations cannot close an enterprise deal by itself. Nor should every article be assigned a fictitious revenue number. But the opposite assumption, that earned media sits somewhere above the revenue engine generating vague “awareness,” is increasingly difficult to defend.

The smartest revenue leaders build an operating model that turns earned authority into more and better conversations, better conversations into opportunities, and opportunities into measurable growth. That’s where media relations becomes a revenue discipline.
