Elon Musk’s Cybertruck Is Dumb, Ugly, and Mercifully Dead

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

In March 2024, a Tesla Cybertruck speeding down Sunset Boulevard slammed head-on into the Beverly Hills Hotel’s iconic entrance sign just before midnight. Passersby winding through the Flats slowed down to watch as Elon Musk’s statue to his own insecurities—the stainless steel embodiment of nuance-free American ego—demolished in front of Hollywood’s unofficial canteen.

Every decade gets the iconography it deserves. At just about the midpoint of the 2020s, ours had arrived, fully formed.

And now the Cybertruck is dead, which is wonderful news. Musk, who’s having a historically bad November, announced late last week that Tesla will be “winding down” production of the Cybertruck around mid-2027, putting an end to the most hated car in America. I was going to call it the most polarizing car in America, but honestly, no one likes this thing. And it’s not entirely due to anti-Elon sentiment: Tesla’s general sales have been relatively steady, going down a bit as Musk went full DOGE mode in 2025 before rebounding in 2026. People like Elon’s cars. They just do not like the Cybertruck.

True, its death has been mourned by some—especially the self-selecting devotees who installed bulletproof glass in their Cybertrucks before shooting them with rifles. These people exist. But mostly, the news has been greeted with a rush of enthusiasm, relief, and, more than anything else, blue-pilled schadenfreude.

Now would be the right moment in the news cycle for an art writer such as myself to make a contrarian argument: that the Cybertruck was secretly a design masterpiece—some kind of neo-Donald Judd breakthrough unleashed ahead of its time.

I am not going to make that argument, because that is not true. Not even close. No one in the future will look back on the Cybertruck as an unheralded art object. It’s The Jetsons for meatheads, a Batmobile for people who secretly root for the Joker. It looks like a birdhouse designed by Zaha Hadid, which I would love to have as a vessel for feeding hungry birds at my (sadly hypothetical) Zaha Hadid house. But I would not want it as a vehicle to park in a driveway. It is a bad car.

And yet, that crashed Cybertruck in front of the Beverly Hills Hotel is seared into my brain, playing on repeat like the most un-subtle Lana Del Rey song ever written. Even beyond that crash, the Cybertruck is the ultimate symbol of this surreal and cruel decade, one that started with a global pandemic and a government suggesting the possibility of a bleach injection as treatment. It’s a decade that wound through four years of a former president denying the lawful election of a current president, only to see that former president return to power. Through it all, we’ve sunk deeper into our collective psychosis—one that’s forced us into rooms with our devices, addicted to social media and artificial intelligence, consuming half-real information and spewing it back out into the world.

It’s a perfect world for the Tesla Cybertruck to define.

The Cybertruck arrived right at the dawn of the decade, in what we can now see as a completely different timeline.

Musk unveiled the car in November 2019 at Tesla’s design studio. Head designer Franz von Holzhausen attempted to demonstrate the vehicle’s shatterproof glass by hurtling a metal ball at a window at full speed. The window shattered. Great omen! It was also pretty weird that Musk was introduced by a hologram of someone called “Cybergirl,” who people speculated was actually a hologram of Grimes, the pop star Musk was dating at the time.

Plenty of design press fawned over the shiny new thing—“Long live the Cybertruck. See you in the Thunderdome,” wrote Dwell. But Wired wisely asked the opinion of an actual expert: Raphael Zammit, who chairs automobile design at Motor City’s own College for Creative Studies.

“I was like, ‘Oh my goodness! What did Tesla do? What did they do?” he said. “It literally breaks every rule we tell to our students. It’s what we tell them not to do.”

Regardless, the Cybertruck started to infiltrate the cultural atmosphere. It was lampooned on The Simpsons. There were countless memes. It was embraced, very early, by rappers. Weeks after the unveiling, Travis Scott used his personal connections to the Tesla CEO (they did Christmas together with the Kardashian-Jenners) to snag a Cybertruck for his video for “Gang Gang,” a posse cut with the Cactus Jack crew.

Three years later, Pharrell Williams, 21 Savage, and Tyler, the Creator featured the Cybertruck in their video for “Cash In, Cash Out”—rendered in Claymation. Offset’s video for “Swing My Way” has Anok Yai sitting atop a Cybertruck. Bad Bunny said, “Me monté en la Cybertruck y me acordé de la Montero de los viejos tiempo.” Meek Mill said, “They was doing cyber attacks, so I bought a Cybertruck.”

The cars fascinated artists too. Julia Wachtel’s CyberChrist, on view at Todd Von Ammon’s DC gallery, von ammon co, earlier this year, pictures Jesus Christ pulling an elongated Cybertruck behind him. I recall with great happiness the perverse thrill of Frieda Toranzo Jaeger’s Cybertruck, a painting in her 2022 show at MoMA PS1. These artists were certainly taking a critical eye toward the Cybertruck, but for some art collectors, there was nothing ironic about it. At least, a specific kind of collector. When I arrived at the convention center for Art Basel Miami Beach in 2024, the first full year of Cybertruck distribution, there was a line of Cybertrucks wrapped around the block.

But that’s the thing about the Cybertruck: There was always more supply than demand. Musk initially hoped to sell 250,000 units per year. According to automotive data platform Kelley Blue Book, Tesla moved nearly 39,000 of them in 2024, a decent start. In 2025, sales dropped, then dropped again in 2026.

Perhaps the Cybertruck could have endured the ridicule of design experts, car enthusiasts, and aesthetes with even just a whiff of good taste if it weren’t for the massive backlash that accompanied Musk’s move into radical right-wing politics, and his full-throated support of Donald Trump. After his efforts at DOGE resulted in the gutting of USAID and other agencies, along with unprecedented and remorseless mass firings, Cybertrucks started getting vandalized around the country. Some owners tried to protect their weird boxy investments with mea culpa stickers—“I bought this before Elon went crazy,” etc.—but no one believed that, or really cared. The cars got defaced. No one stepped in to intervene. The Cybertrucks deserved it.

There was also a bleaker incident that parallels the Cybertruck at the Beverly Hills Hotel: the dystopian hellscape of the Cybertruck that exploded on the Las Vegas Strip, in front of the Trump International Hotel & Casino. The man responsible for that explosion served in the US Army, and authorities believe he suffered a self-inflicted gunshot wound just before the vehicle detonated. The Beverly Hills crash was an accident in which no one was harmed—but the charred, deadly remains of the truck in Las Vegas seemed downright terrifying, a vision of American capitalism and greed that had met its grisly moment.

Perhaps now, with a new era of Washington poised to define the final years of this most delirious of decades, we’ll eventually look back at the Cybertruck with at least some nostalgia for its silliness, its boxy stupidity, its pop culture ubiquity. If it does linger in the annals of history, maybe we’ll think of the Cybertruck as a roadbound folly of the world’s first trillionaire, and a footnote in Musk’s legacy, for better or for worse.

Put it this way: By the time Hollywood studio heads arrived at the Polo Lounge for their Monday power lunches, the mess of the Cybertruck that had crashed at the Beverly Hills Hotel the night before was gone. Almost as if it never happened at all.
