LNG Trading Profits could Turn Negative in Next Supply Glut

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

SINGAPORE, Nov. 18, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Global liquefied natural gas (LNG) trading profits could turn negative during the next supply glut, expected to last from 2030 to 2034, according to new analysis by Bain & Company. This is a sharp reversal from the $20 billion or more in annual gross trading profits earned during recent supply disruptions, as a growing number of LNG buyers become traders themselves and compete for a shrinking pool of margins.

Bain forecasts that annual gross LNG trading profits could fall to approximately $5.5 billion during the next glut, compared with an estimated $20 billion in 2026. However, holders of Henry Hub-linked free-on-board (FOB) volumes must still pay liquefaction fees on cargoes they cannot place. If sanctioned production capacity arrives on time, approximately one in six US cargoes could go unlifted, resulting in around $5.8 billion in annual cancellation fees. These losses could push the aggregate trading profit pool below breakeven, even before accounting for additional losses on cargoes lifted and sold below cost.

"The LNG market is getting crowded in the middle. After every shock, buyers did the sensible thing and locked in flexibility. Many became traders without meaning to, and this set up the next glut. Japanese companies now resell about 40% of the LNG they handle; importers in China, India and Turkey are following, and midsize European utilities are building portfolios of their own. Today's LNG crunch may be traders' last windfall before the glut," said Chung Yen Wong, partner in Bain & Company's Energy & Natural Resources practice.

The shift follows successive supply shocks that prompted LNG buyers to secure more flexible supply. Between 2022 and 2025, buyers signed long-term contracts of 15 years or more worth a combined 211 million tonnes per year of LNG, more than half from US projects and many on FOB terms. Meanwhile, spot and short-term deals rose from 34% of global LNG trade in 2019 to 43% in 2025, according to GIIGNL data. In addition, Bain's Intersectsm model estimates liquefaction capacity utilization could fall from 90% in 2026 to below 75% between 2030 and 2034, even counting only plants already operating or under construction. That would be about as deep as the 2020 glut, but last five years rather than one. Additional planned capacity would further increase the risk of oversupply.

As more participants compete in LNG trading, Bain expects margins on flexibility, logistics and credit services to shrink first. The remaining margins will increasingly depend on managing mismatches in contract length and pricing indices, which can generate profits during supply shocks but losses during a glut.

The eventual outcome will vary significantly across market participants. Books matched or hedged in timing and price could retain much of their margins, while portfolio holders with US volumes they cannot place, or buyers contracted beyond their own needs, would bear the costs.

"In a crowded middle, copying the competition is the one strategy guaranteed to fail. The winners will start from what they bring that others cannot: assets, customers, balance sheet and information," said Wong.

Bain's analysis also points to the potential emergence of a new service model, where established merchant traders take on smaller players' surplus for a fee or a share of the upside, rather than buying cargoes outright. Consolidation is also likely through the glut.

Bain underscores the need for LNG companies to reassess their business models, risk appetite, and trading capabilities in the current supply crunch. This includes setting limits on uncontracted cargoes, separating industrial portfolio management from short-term optimization, and strengthening the systems and analytics needed to monitor exposures and manage increasingly complex trading portfolios. Investors looking at LNG trading books, portfolios and projects should test who really carries the volume and price risk when the glut arrives.
