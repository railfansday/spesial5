Why floods kill more people than tornadoes or lightning in the U.S.

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Why floods kill more people than tornadoes or lightning in the U.S. on long-term average, from flash flood speed and driving into water to storm surge and dam failures

Water, not wind or electricity, is the more consistent weather killer in the U.S., on long-term average. National Weather Service (NWS) fatality records show floods claiming more lives than tornadoes or lightning over that span. The pattern is easy to miss. Tornadoes and lightning arrive with visible violence, while floodwater can look calm, shallow or distant until people are already in it.

The comparison needs a clear scope. It holds for the U.S. when long-term NWS averages are set side by side. It does not hold in every year. In 2011, tornadoes killed more than 500 people in the U.S., more than flooding killed that year. That season included the tornado that struck Joplin, Missouri. A violent tornado season can upend an annual tally. Over decades, though, the average tilts toward water.

Understanding why floods kill more people starts with how water behaves. Flash floods can build within minutes to hours of heavy rain. They often reach places where no rain is falling. Moving water also carries more force than its depth suggests. A road can be washed out beneath a surface that looks passable. Many flood deaths happen inside vehicles.

Human choices and the built environment shape that risk. Pavement sends rainwater toward low points, including underpasses and underground transit. Dams and levees can fail. Hurricanes, often remembered for their winds, can kill through storm surge on the coast and freshwater flooding far inland. Once the water recedes, cleanup brings its own hazards.

The exposure is global. Large populations live on floodplains and along coasts worldwide. When rivers overflow in densely settled regions, a single flood season can take lives across an entire country.

The 10 slides that follow are a selection of reasons and examples, not a ranked or complete account. They cover the speed of flash floods, the physics of moving water and the decisions drivers make at the water's edge. They also explain why lightning and tornadoes tend to kill fewer people on average. Hurricane rainfall, storm surge, dam failures and urban flooding each get their own slide. Each slide addresses a separate part of the same question: why water so often turns out to be the deadlier hazard.

A flash flood can build within minutes to a few hours of heavy rain. Its water often reaches places where no rain is falling at all. People downstream may get almost no time to react.

That short lead time separates a flash flood from a river flood. A river swollen by days of steady rain or snowmelt rises slowly enough to track. A flash flood arrives as a sudden surge. It can turn a dry creek bed or a quiet street into a fast channel before anyone nearby sees the danger.

Intense rainfall over a small area is the usual trigger. Slow-moving or repeating thunderstorms can drop rain faster than the ground can absorb it. Soil already saturated from earlier storms takes in very little more. Rock, clay and pavement take in almost none. The excess runs downhill and collects in the lowest available path.

Terrain decides how fast that runoff moves. Steep slopes, narrow canyons and small streams funnel water together and speed it up. In the arid U.S. Southwest, dry washes known as arroyos can fill with water from a storm miles upstream. Hillsides burned by wildfire add another risk. Scorched ground sheds water easily and has lost the plants that would otherwise slow it down.

The result is a flood that can arrive under clear skies. Hikers in a slot canyon or families camped beside a creek may hear no thunder and feel no rain. Meanwhile, a surge from a distant storm can be moving toward them. A flash flood that strikes at night is harder still to see and to escape.

The National Weather Service uses tiered alerts to match that speed. A flash flood watch means conditions favor flash flooding. A flash flood warning means flash flooding is happening or about to happen. The highest tier is a flash flood emergency. The NWS reserves it for rare cases where a flash flood poses a severe threat to human life and catastrophic damage is occurring or expected.

The more serious flash flood warnings can also reach mobile phones through the Wireless Emergency Alerts system. The NWS tells people under a warning to move to higher ground right away instead of waiting to see the water rise.

The National Weather Service says six inches of moving water is enough to knock an adult off their feet. The agency's thresholds climb quickly from there. It says 12 inches can carry away a small car, and two feet can carry away most vehicles, including SUVs and trucks.

Those depths run against instinct. Six inches of water sits well below the knee of any adult. Standing water at that level is a nuisance. Moving water at that level is a hazard. The current pushes against the legs, while the ground underneath offers little grip.

Water is heavy. A cubic foot of fresh water weighs about 62 pounds. When that mass moves, it presses steadily against anything in its path. The push grows sharply with speed. In basic fluid physics, the drag force on an object rises with the square of the current's velocity. Water moving twice as fast pushes roughly four times as hard.

A person wading through a current has only the friction under their feet to resist that force. Mud, gravel, slick pavement and submerged debris all make that friction unreliable. Once a person falls, getting back up in moving water is difficult. The current can then carry them downstream.

Vehicles face a second problem: buoyancy. A car body traps a large volume of air. As water rises around it, the car displaces that water, and the water pushes upward on the vehicle. The lift reduces how firmly the tires press against the road. Less pressure on the tires means less traction. A modest current can then shove the vehicle sideways or float it off the road.

Buoyancy is one reason a small car can be carried away by about a foot of moving water. Larger SUVs and trucks sit higher and weigh more, which gives them some margin. NWS guidance still says two feet of moving water can carry away most of them. That depth reaches around or just above the knee of a standing adult.

Depth is also hard to judge from above. Floodwater is usually brown with sediment, which hides whatever lies beneath the surface. A stretch that looks uniform can conceal a dip or a ditch. Speed is just as deceptive. A smooth surface can mask a fast current. Floodwater also often carries branches and other debris that can strike a person or a vehicle.

The National Weather Service and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention report that more than half of flood drownings happen when someone drives a vehicle into hazardous water. That puts one decision at the center of many flood deaths: whether to drive forward.

The choice often comes quickly. A driver reaches a flooded dip, underpass or creek crossing. The water looks like a thin layer over the pavement, and the driver decides to keep going. The surface shows little of what lies underneath.

Floodwater can scour a roadbed from below while the water on top looks calm. The asphalt may be undercut or gone entirely, leaving a drop-off where the lane used to be. A car that enters at what seems like a safe spot can end up in a channel far deeper than the driver expected.

Muddy water hides the edge of the road, the centerline and any debris moving in the current. Darkness adds to the problem. Headlights glare off the surface and flatten any sense of depth. A familiar route offers no guarantee. A single storm can change the ground beneath it.

Low-water crossings raise the danger in parts of the U.S. where roads dip straight through creek beds. In central Texas and much of the Southwest, many of those creeks sit dry for long stretches and then fill after heavy rain. Drivers who cross them dry most of the year can misread how much conditions have changed.

The NWS built its Turn Around Don't Drown campaign around this moment at the water's edge. The message asks drivers to treat water covering a road as a reason to stop and choose another route. The slogan is four words long and easy to recall when there is little time to weigh options.

Road closure barricades carry the same message. Driving around one puts the driver into the water and can pull rescue crews in as well. Swift-water rescues expose firefighters and emergency teams to the same currents that trapped the motorist.

If a vehicle stalls in rising water, NWS safety guidance is to get out and move to higher ground if it is safe to do so.

Lightning kills fewer people than flooding in the U.S., on long-term average, because each strike hits a single point, endangering one person or a small group at a time. U.S. lightning deaths have also fallen over the decades. Flood danger, by contrast, can spread across whole valleys and neighborhoods at once.

The difference comes down to geography. A flood can cover miles of road, homes and farmland in a single event. Anyone inside that footprint is exposed, whether they are at home, at work or trying to leave. A lightning bolt discharges at one spot. Its harm reaches the person struck and anyone close by, through current that travels along the ground or jumps from a nearby object.

The danger also has a clear end. A thunderstorm moves through, and the electrical threat passes with it. The National Weather Service tells people to wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before going back outside. Floodwater does not follow that kind of timetable. It can stay high, or keep rising, for hours or days after the rain stops.

The NWS sums up its guidance in five words: "When thunder roars, go indoors." If thunder is audible, the storm is close enough to strike. The agency treats no outdoor spot as safe during a thunderstorm. A substantial building with wiring and plumbing offers protection. So does a hard-topped metal vehicle with the windows closed. Open-sided shelters, tents, picnic pavilions and lone trees do not.

Lightning can strike well away from the heaviest rain, sometimes from what looks like clear sky overhead. That is why the NWS ties its advice to the sound of thunder rather than to rainfall or dark clouds.

Most people struck by lightning survive, though many live with lasting injuries such as nerve damage, memory problems and chronic pain. A person who has been struck carries no electrical charge. Bystanders can safely start CPR and call for emergency help right away. Fast CPR can save a victim whose heart has stopped.

Tornadoes kill fewer people than floods in the U.S., on long-term average, because each one damages only a narrow path. Warnings and sturdy shelter also reduce the risk for people inside that path. The rare violent tornado that strikes a populated area is the main exception.

A tornado concentrates its destruction. Even a large tornado with a long track leaves the ground on either side of its path largely untouched. One neighborhood can be flattened while streets a short distance away keep their roofs. Floodwater spreads differently. It can fill whole valleys, road networks and low-lying districts at the same time.

The warning system gives people in a tornado's path a chance to act. The National Weather Service issues a tornado watch when conditions over a region favor tornadoes. It issues a tornado warning when a tornado has been spotted or detected by radar. A warning is the signal to take shelter right away.

The NWS advises moving to a basement, a storm cellar or a small interior room on the lowest floor of a sturdy building, away from windows. Mobile homes offer little protection. The agency advises leaving them for a stronger structure before a tornado arrives. It also advises covering the head and neck to guard against flying debris.

None of this rules out mass casualties. The Tri-State Tornado of March 18, 1925, tracked across Missouri, Illinois and Indiana and killed 695 people. It remains the deadliest tornado in U.S. history. It struck decades before public tornado warnings of the kind used today existed.

Modern forecasting has not removed the danger. On May 22, 2011, a tornado rated EF5, the highest category on the Enhanced Fujita scale, tore through Joplin, Missouri. It killed 158 people. Sirens sounded in Joplin before the tornado arrived. The death toll still placed it among the deadliest U.S. tornadoes on record.

The Tri-State and Joplin tornadoes show how tornado deaths tend to arrive. Most tornadoes cause no deaths, and many touch down over open country. The toll comes mainly from the few violent tornadoes that cross towns. That pattern makes tornado deaths uneven from one year to the next.

Rain from a hurricane can kill more people than its winds, often far from the coast where the storm made landfall. Hurricane Harvey in 2017 and Hurricane Helene in 2024 both became deadly freshwater flood disasters.

Harvey came ashore on the middle Texas coast in late August 2017 as a Category 4 hurricane. It then weakened and stalled over southeast Texas for days. That slow movement let rain bands pass over the same areas again and again, including the Houston metro area.

One Texas location recorded more than 60 inches of rain from Harvey. That total is a record for any tropical cyclone in the U.S. Most of the storm's deaths came from freshwater flooding, not from wind.

Helene followed a different path to a similar result. It made landfall in Florida's Big Bend region in late September 2024 as a Category 4 hurricane. It then moved north into the mountains of the southern Appalachians.

Western North Carolina, hundreds of miles from the landfall point, took the worst of the flooding. Rivers and streams in steep mountain valleys rose fast. Communities around Asheville saw roads, homes and businesses swept away. Helene killed more than 200 people across several states.

A hurricane's category does not capture this risk. The Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale rates storms by sustained wind speed alone. It says nothing about how much rain a storm will drop or where that rain will land. A weaker or slower storm can still produce extreme flooding.

Forward speed also shapes the flood risk. A storm that crawls, as Harvey did, keeps dropping rain on one region. Terrain plays a role too. When moist tropical air is pushed up mountain slopes, as happened during Helene, it cools and releases more rain onto the ground below.

Distance from the coast does not remove the danger. Harvey and Helene showed that a storm's deadliest water can reach cities and mountain towns well away from any beach.

Storm surge, not wind, is the hurricane hazard most likely to kill people along the coast. The National Hurricane Center has long described the surge as the greatest threat to life when a hurricane reaches a shoreline.

Storm surge is an abnormal rise in seawater pushed ashore by a storm's winds. It comes on top of the normal astronomical tide. When the surge arrives near high tide, the combined water level is known as the storm tide, and it climbs even higher.

The height of a surge depends on more than a storm's strength. Its size, forward speed and angle of approach all play a part. So do the shape of the coastline and the slope of the seafloor offshore. A broad, shallow continental shelf, like much of the northern Gulf of Mexico, lets water pile up higher than a steep drop-off would.

The Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale does not account for any of this. It rates hurricanes on sustained wind speed alone. A large storm with a lower category can still push a destructive surge far inland.

Surge water does not behave like a slowly rising river. It arrives with breaking waves riding on top. The force of that moving seawater can knock buildings off their foundations. It also floods escape routes and cuts off people who did not leave in time.

Hurricane Katrina showed how surge and failing defenses can combine. In 2005, the storm drove water against the levees and floodwalls protecting New Orleans. Those barriers failed. Much of the city, which sits partly below sea level, flooded. Katrina killed more than 1,800 people.

The National Weather Service sums up its coastal advice in a short phrase: "Run from the water, hide from the wind." Coastal evacuation zones in the U.S. are based largely on surge risk rather than wind. The National Hurricane Center also issues storm surge watches and warnings separately from hurricane watches and warnings.

A storm surge warning can cover stretches of coast where hurricane-force winds are not expected. That separation lets forecasters flag life-threatening inundation on its own terms.

A dam failure releases stored water all at once, and a steep canyon can turn one thunderstorm into a wall of water. The Johnstown Flood of 1889 and the Big Thompson Canyon flash flood of 1976 are one deadly U.S. example of each.

Johnstown, Pennsylvania, sat in a narrow valley where the Little Conemaugh River and Stonycreek River join. Upstream stood the South Fork Dam, an earthen embankment holding back a reservoir called Lake Conemaugh. The lake served as a private retreat for the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club. Its members included Pittsburgh industrialists Andrew Carnegie and Henry Clay Frick.

On May 31, 1889, after heavy rain, the dam gave way. The reservoir emptied into the valley below and surged toward Johnstown. It collected trees, rail cars and the wreckage of homes along the way. More than 2,200 people died. The disaster ranks among the deadliest in U.S. history.

The aftermath drew a national relief effort. Clara Barton brought the American Red Cross to Johnstown, in one of the young organization's first large relief operations. The flood also raised public questions about who was responsible for maintaining a private dam above a populated town.

Big Thompson Canyon in Colorado involved no dam at all. On July 31, 1976, a slow-moving thunderstorm stalled over the canyon. The canyon carries the Big Thompson River between Estes Park and Loveland. Its steep rock walls shed the rain straight into the river channel. The river rose into a torrent that tore through campgrounds, cabins and the road along the canyon floor.

The flood killed 144 people. The canyon was crowded that evening with visitors marking the weekend of Colorado's centennial. Many of the dead were visitors who had little warning before the water reached them.

The two disasters share a basic physical problem. In each case, a large volume of water was concentrated and sent through a narrow space with people in its path. In a canyon flash flood, climbing the walls on foot to higher ground offers more protection than staying on the canyon floor. That floor is where roads and campsites sit. It is also where the water runs deepest and fastest.

Cities can turn heavy rain into deadly floods quickly because pavement and rooftops shed water instead of absorbing it. On July 20, 2021, floodwater poured into a subway line in Zhengzhou, China, and trapped passengers underground. Fourteen of them died.

In a field or forest, much of the rain that falls soaks into the soil. In a city, roads, parking lots, sidewalks and roofs block that path. Rain runs off these hard surfaces almost as soon as it lands. It then moves toward the lowest places it can reach.

Storm drains and sewers are built to carry that runoff away, but every system has a limit. When rain falls faster than the pipes can take it, the excess stays on the surface. Streets begin to behave like channels. Underpasses and dips in the road fill like basins.

Underground spaces sit at the bottom of this system. Subway stations, rail tunnels, underground garages and basement rooms all lie below street level. Water that enters them often has nowhere to drain. The people inside may have only one or two ways out. Those exits lead up through the same openings the water is coming down.

Zhengzhou is the capital of Henan province in central China. During extreme rainfall in July 2021, water flooded Line 5 of the city's metro. Passengers on a stopped train were left standing in rising water inside the tunnel. Many were rescued.

The same pattern has appeared far from China. In September 2021, the remnants of Hurricane Ida brought intense rain to New York City. Water filled basement apartments, and residents drowned inside their homes.

Urban flash flooding does not need a river or creek nearby. It can develop blocks away from any stream, wherever runoff gathers faster than drains can remove it. That makes the danger harder to read from a window or a sidewalk.

The Zhengzhou disaster shows how a city's layout shapes flood risk. During intense rain, the spaces built below ground are among the first to fill and among the hardest to leave.

Flooding kills on a global scale partly because so many people live where water naturally spreads. In 2022, floods in Pakistan killed more than 1,700 people.

Floodplains have drawn settlement for thousands of years. The land is flat and easy to build on. Floods leave behind silt that keeps soil fertile for farming. Rivers supply drinking water, irrigation and routes for trade. The same features that make these places productive put homes, farms and roads in the path of high water.

Coastlines follow a similar pattern. Many of the world's largest cities sit on the sea, at river mouths or on deltas. Low elevation and dense development leave little room for water to go when rivers rise or the sea pushes inland.

Pakistan's 2022 disaster showed that exposure at national scale. The floods followed an exceptionally heavy monsoon season. Water spread across farmland, villages and towns in the Indus River basin. Much of the country's population and agriculture is concentrated there. Many people lost homes, crops and livestock at once.

A flood's danger also continues after the water stops rising. Floodwater mixes with sewage, fuel and chemicals. It can contaminate wells and public water supplies. Drinking or washing in that water can spread illness. The risk is highest where clean water and sanitation were already limited.

Damaged power lines create a separate hazard. Downed or submerged lines can energize standing water. People wading through flooded streets or basements can be electrocuted without seeing any sign of the danger. Any downed line can still carry current, even when it looks dead.

Carbon monoxide poisoning is another cleanup risk. When grid power fails, households turn to portable generators, gasoline pressure washers and fuel-burning heaters. Running that equipment inside homes, garages or basements lets the odorless, colorless gas build to deadly levels. People can be overcome before they notice symptoms.

Each of these hazards can claim lives well after the floodwater has drained away.
