Trump said colleges would ‘go to hell very quickly’ without foreign students. New enrollment just fell 18%, costing them billions in tuition

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

New international students at U.S. colleges fell 18% in the 2025–26 school year, to 228,123, the Institute of International Education said Monday in its annual Open Doors report. The first look at this fall points to a second straight drop. In IIE’s fall 2026 snapshot of more than 800 schools, new international enrollment fell another 15%.

The total looks calmer: 1,146,163 international students, down 3%. But that count includes 335,785 graduates on Optional Practical Training, the program that lets them work in the U.S. after their degree. That group rose 14%, and it pays no tuition. Take it out and the number of international students enrolled in classes fell about 8%, by Fortune’s math. That’s about 73,000 fewer students paying tuition.

The president saw this coming. At a cabinet meeting in August 2025, defending a plan to admit 600,000 Chinese students, Trump said that without them, “our college system would go to hell very quickly and it wouldn’t be the top colleges. It would be colleges that struggle on the bottom.” His base pushed back. Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene said that if keeping Chinese students out made some schools fail, “these schools should fail anyways because they are being propped up by the CCP.”

Since then, his administration has moved the other way. In May 2025 the State Department paused student visa interviews, then restarted them with expanded social media vetting. A travel ban that started with 19 countries grew to 39, plus the Palestinian territory, on Jan. 1. In July, DHS finalized a rule replacing open-ended student status with a fixed four-year limit and called it the end of “foreign student visa abuse.” A federal judge in Massachusetts blocked it in September. Among schools that reported declines this fall, 88% blamed the visa application process and 70% blamed travel restrictions, according to IIE’s snapshot.

The losses were concentrated in one corner of the campus. Graduate students fell 13% to 423,078, and master’s students fell 22%. Doctoral students rose 3%, and undergraduates slipped 0.3%. Indian graduate students, the biggest group, fell 34%.

IIE President and CEO Jason Czyz said the data “reaffirms the enduring importance of international education to the United States.”

Colleges don’t report how much tuition they lost to the drop, so here’s a rough estimate. University of North Texas President Harrison Keller has said each admitted master’s student who didn’t enroll would have brought his school between $20,000 and $25,000. Multiply the roughly 73,000 missing students by that range and you get about $1.5 billion to $1.8 billion in lost tuition. That’s a floor. UNT is a public university, and private schools charge international students much more.

Counting everything students spend, including rent, food and health care, NAFSA estimates international students’ economic contribution fell 7% in 2025–26, to $39.9 billion. That’s about $3 billion less than the year before. NAFSA, the trade group for international educators, puts the cost of this fall’s decline at another $2.4 billion and 31,000 jobs. Its members depend on these students, and its projections rest on surveys.

The money hurts more than the headcount suggests, because these students pay more than their share. Princeton research cited by the Hechinger Report found international students made up 6% of enrollment but 12% of college revenue. They usually pay close to full price and get little aid. In IIE’s survey, 60% of institutions said they recruit them partly for their “financial contributions.”

For most schools, the loss is manageable. Moody’s found in 2025 that a 15% drop in international tuition would barely affect operating margins at 77% of the universities it rates. But 15% of rated universities have more than 15% international students, and some have more than 30%. Under a 20% drop, Moody’s said, 15 universities could lose 2 to 8 points of operating margin. Moody’s and S&P have negative outlooks on higher education for 2026, and Fitch calls the outlook “deteriorating.” “We see revenue growing for the sector next year, but not as much as expenses,” Moody’s analyst Patrick Ronk told The Bond Buyer.

Trump’s “bottom” turned out to include Denton, Texas. UNT projected a $45 million shortfall as international enrollment fell, on top of a $32 million cut in state funding over two years, and it has eliminated 71 academic programs, the Hechinger Report found. “About 60% of UNT’s growth over the last few years has been in international graduate students,” Keller told the board in August 2025. “We’re overexposed compared to other institutions.” UNT had more than 1,300 fewer international students this fall.

The public flagships aren’t immune. International enrollment fell 15.6% at Indiana this fall and 12.7% at Ohio State, Higher Ed Dive reported, and 12% at Pitt, according to the Pittsburgh Post-Gazette.

Domestic students are covering part of the gap. Syracuse, Northwestern, USC and the New School all raised tuition 3% to 4% while losing international students, Hechinger found. “It leaves a really big hole in the budget, which has to get filled one way or another,” UC Santa Barbara economist Dick Startz told the outlet.

College closures are speeding up too, but for a different reason. Hampshire College in Amherst, Mass., will close at the end of this semester, after enrollment fell by almost half since 2015 and it couldn’t refinance a $21 million bond. It’s one of nine colleges set to close in 2026, by The College Investor’s count. Huron Consulting Group says more than 440 of the country’s 1,700 private nonprofit four-year colleges are at risk, as the number of 18-year-olds starts to shrink.

The schools that close are mostly small undergraduate colleges with few international students. The schools losing international master’s students are bigger, and they cut jobs and programs instead. Public institutions “hardly ever close,” Higher Ed Dive noted in covering a Philadelphia Fed model. Fortune reported in August that the country’s high school graduating class is projected to shrink 13% by 2041, so both squeezes are arriving at the same time.

This year, the OPT boom kept the total from looking like a crisis. That’s the program the administration went after next. On Oct. 8, DHS proposed making schools pay $70,000 the first time they recommend a student for OPT and $30,000 for each recommendation after that; schools could pass those costs on to students or employers. In IIE’s survey, 95% of institutions said prospective students would look elsewhere if OPT were restricted. In the fall snapshot, OPT fell 6%, its first decline in five years. The full count for this fall comes in next November’s Open Doors report.
