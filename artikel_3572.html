Dolly Parton Is Gone, But Dollywood Will Always Love Her

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

“RIP Dolly Smoky Mountain Angel” was one of the first signs we saw as we pulled up on the side of a motel in Pigeon Forge, Tennessee. Just down the same road was another memorial to her on a Burger King sign. This was in early October. Dolly Parton, the country singer and actor, widely considered a Great American Who Everyone Loves, had died, at the age of 80, in late August. I am not the kind of person who cries over celebrity deaths, but after I heard that she died, I went to my closet to find a Dolly Parton T-shirt I had bought the one time I saw her live, at a concert of mostly acoustic songs in Queens in 2016. I couldn’t find it. I blamed my brief flirtation with the Marie Kondo method, but there was no way that T-shirt hadn’t sparked joy.

Early October in Sevier County, in far eastern Tennessee, where Parton was born, is full of cabins on some parcels of land that dot the ancient Appalachians. But among the rolling green expanses are Mexican supermarkets, coffee shops, apple orchards, and pumpkin patches. This is where she opened the amusement park Dollywood in 1986. The Great Smoky Mountains National Park draws masses about 13 miles from Dollywood. The fancier of my friends identified the area as where the Blackberry Farm resort is. But Pigeon Forge has a feeling of a Jersey Shore boardwalk in Appalachia, with a strip of businesses that sell vapes and ATV rides and Moon Pies a block from a Margaritaville hotel.

Parton opened Dollywood in 1986 at the foothills of the Great Smoky Mountains. Today, it draws in millions of people annually—loving fans who don her iconic pink cowboy hat or accessories honoring the park's famous Grist Mill Cinnamon Bread (a cult favorite).

Locals heap the kind of praise on Parton that we’ve come to expect knowing her reputation as a goddess among mortals. “People don’t understand what she did for this place and meant to this place. She threw lots of money at it and went out of her way to take care of this place,” an Uber driver named Eric told me as we exited the highway into Dollywood, with its script logo and “love every moment” greeting signs. “I got to meet her three times at the park because she would just show up and stand there with a crowd of people. For someone who’s been famous as long as she’s been famous you’d expect a certain air of haughtiness,” he says. “But I was stunned at how kind she was.”

Parton’s job title at the park was dreamer-in-chief, and part of Dollywood’s allure is that it’s a shrine to her life and career. There’s a replica of the two-room wood cabin where she grew up a sharecropper’s daughter, in close quarters alongside her parents and 10 siblings. Her home, not even 500 square feet, had no electricity nor running water. (The original home still stands about 10 miles away but isn’t open to the public.)

Parton wrote the country ballad “I Will Always Love You” in 1973 as a heartfelt farewell to her longtime business partner Porter Wagoner. Now, fans honoring Dolly's memory have used the slogan for the park's new annual festival, which opens in March 2027.

That modest beginning is plenty known, but Dollywood also celebrates Parton as she became a household name and wildly rich. The Dolly Parton Experience is a museum devoted to her trajectory, from Nashville ingenue to a celebrity among celebrities (photos show her with Mick Jagger in the ’60s and Whitney Houston in the ’90s). It has a gallery devoted to her Imagination Library, which has donated more than 300 million books to children around the world. Another building across a landscaped path celebrates her role in the workplace revenge comedy 9 to 5 (September 25 has now been christened a holiday in Sevier County) and houses a half dozen of her wigs, sorted by decade, all platinum, all voluminous. A plaque commemorates her wig stylists, starting with her Aunt Colleen. Parton’s costumes are scattered around the tributes, including a black flared jumpsuit with a hot pink shirt and neck ruffle so intense it looks Elizabethan. One wall features one of Parton’s famously self-effacing quotes: “It costs a lot of money to look this cheap.”

In her mobile home, which guests line up around to take a tour, she has a closet with a hanging yellow diamanté dress, a full-size bathtub because she preferred baths to showers, mirrored ceilings because she was claustrophobic, and cheeky digital clocks that display her most hallowed time zones: Dollywood (5:53 p.m. ET) and Nashville (4:53 p.m. CT). I ask our tour guide, a woman in a sparkly vest, how she and the park employees have fared without Parton. “Today is the first day I haven’t cried,” she admits.

Dollywood is more than a museum—it's an amusement park. The 165-acre Pigeon Forge carnival features over 50 rides and Dollywood's Splash Country, a 35-acre water park.

But more than a museum, Dollywood is an amusement park, and a much bigger one than I had realized. It’s a place where you can stand in line for 45 minutes to buy a corn dog while listening to a four-person singing-and-dancing group in the vein of the Mickey Mouse Club does peppy but half-assed covers of Fleetwood Mac and Van Halen. Looping rollercoasters and water tube rides leave families soaking wet in matching T-shirts commemorating their vacations. Dondre, my colleague and our photographer for this journey, waits probably a full hour to squeeze into a vintage muscle car with me so I can simulate steering around a track. It wasn’t until deep into our wait time that we realize this is a children’s ride. At this point, the two of us notice that, in the sea of people, we are the only ones who are childless, wearing dark colors, and audibly bemoaning that Dollywood is dry. (Parton was not a strict teetotaler, but the park bills itself as assertively family-friendly. “Weapons and alcoholic beverages are not permitted on Dollywood Property” is the only explanation given on its website.) No one else seems to care. The ride is quick, but we are ecstatic, high on refillable Diet Cokes, kettle corn, and driving a car, which neither one of us knows how to do in real life.

Some park employees have not fared well without Parton. One tour guide admits: “Today is the first day I haven’t cried." Parton passed away on August 25 at the age of 80 after a battle with cancer.

We don’t want real life at Dollywood. We want the dreamer-in-chief’s dreams. For dinner, we hit the 8:30 show of Dolly Parton’s Stampede in Pigeon Forge, an equestrian version of dinner theater where guests are served a whole chicken, pulled pork, corn, greens, a biscuit, pie, and unlimited soda, all to be eaten by hand while the not so vague perfume of manure wafts through the air. Horses with cowboys and cowgirls astride them jump through rings of fire, someone does wire work while dangling from the ceiling, and American flags fly in abundance. We catch a pig race. Sooo-ey! At another point, a volunteer from the audience disappears from a barrel before our eyes. The whole thing feels psychedelic, and we peak when performers in butterfly wings ride around to Parton’s song “Love Is Like a Butterfly.” I squeal as a few actual buffalo take the arena. After the show, I ask a cowboy how the majestic creatures are as animal coworkers, and he shakes his head. “There’s a reason I stick to horses.”

Even in the perfect bubble of Dollywood, in the wake of her death, Parton’s spirit has already faded into the nostalgia.

There isn’t enough acknowledgement of Parton’s death at the park for my taste; no candlelit vigil or impersonators we can hug. By the second day, my mania has subsided, giving way to contemplation. I ask fellow parkgoers if they have come to pay respects. Every single one replies that their trip has been planned long before. My attempts to make small talk with strangers doesn’t result in the fantasy of one collective mourning. Families in seasonal T-shirts seem shy and uninterested in chatting with me, a strangely inquisitive middle-aged woman who wants to ask them if they’ve heard the rumor that Dolly Parton was secretly covered in tattoos. (They haven’t.) A friend group of two lesbian couples from Pennsylvania brushes off my line of questioning around whether Parton might have been more than just queer-friendly (rainbow Dollywood shirts and backpacks and trucker caps are for sale). Too soon, they say, and shake their heads—too salacious to be airing gossip. I ask other strangers if they have heard the family drama over Parton’s estate. Most dismiss me, but another Uber driver, this one named Austin, says, “She has a huge estate and a big family, and it was always going to happen. We’ll see how it plays out.” (In November, the lawsuit was dropped, and Parton’s nephew said he was happy to put the issue behind him.)

The butterfly is a key part of Dollywood's logo, representing Parton's personal emblem. As a little girl growing up in the Smoky Mountains, she was fascinated by butterflies and related to their gentle grace. She cemented the motif into her career in 1974 with the hit song, “Love is Like a Butterfly.”

Maybe, I say to Dondre, no one will talk to me because I, unlike about 50% of the people there, am not wearing a T-shirt emblazoned with a Bible verse, a military slogan, or a celebration of America’s 250 years. I keep pointing out paintings of crosses and eagles and American flags—often all combined on one canvas together—for sale. Dondre says, in a kind way, that nothing is more cliché than hearing someone from New York talk about other parts of the country. Maybe what made Dolly Parton so winning—her apparent lack of judgment—is not something I inherently have. It can’t hurt to try to be a little more like Parton in our own daily lives.

Maybe she had an optimism no one can ever replace. And that’s ultimately okay because you can try to channel that anywhere. Even in the perfect bubble of Dollywood, in the wake of her death, Parton’s spirit has already faded into the nostalgia, a curio like Graceland, but with a vintage steam train ride.

I succumb and buy a Christmas ornament with Parton dressed as Santa and eat a chicken salad sandwich served on a butterfly-logo-shaped pretzel. I guess I enjoy Dollywood the way I enjoy going dancing in Berlin gay clubs or karaoke birthday parties: It is an experience, and a fun one, but not what I would choose. I will never, in the vein of a Disney Adult, be a Dolly Adult. More power to those who cherish their season passes. But Parton doesn’t even have to be core to your identity for you to enjoy an intricately branded amusement park. Its unchanging presence is a comfort. If you need to commune with her earthly treasures, there is always Dollywood. May her costumes and wigs remain.

Dollywood will always be a place for fans and dreamers alike to marvel in the wonder of the country icon’s extraordinary career and charming home.
