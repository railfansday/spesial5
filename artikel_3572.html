‘The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping’ Director Francis Lawrence on the Film’s Final Scene

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

When Francis Lawrence, the filmmaker behind The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, greets me over Zoom, he’s aware that his darkly lit, gothic backdrop makes him look a bit like the villainous President Snow. “All I need is a big eagle back here,” the white-haired, smartly dressed director jokes.

Lawrence has helmed almost every film in Lionsgate’s blockbuster series, based on Suzanne Collins’s best-selling novels about a dystopia where a bloodthirsty dictator forces children to fight to the death for the elite’s entertainment. He joined the franchise for its 2013 sequel, Catching Fire, and has directed only three non–Hunger Games movies in the decade-plus since. In that time, one could argue, the world has tilted further toward the oppressive future depicted in Collins’s novels—see, for instance, Donald Trump’s Hunger Games–esque Patriot Games. And while Lawrence isn’t naming names, he’s never lost sight of the movies’ larger political message. “I feel really lucky to be able to tell stories that matter, that are wrapped in these commercial packages,” he tells Vanity Fair. “So anytime one of these things comes around, I jump at it.”

Just as the original Hunger Games film made a star of Oscar winner Jennifer Lawrence (who returns for a scene in the new movie), Sunrise on the Reaping introduces the world to Australian newcomer Joseph Zada. He plays the younger version of Woody Harrelson’s Haymitch Abernathy, a victor of the Hunger Games who later serves as a mentor to actor Lawrence’s Katniss Everdeen. Making a reflective movie prompted director Lawrence to reflect on his own Hollywood trajectory. “The biggest difference is that I’m a lot more relaxed. I was nervous as hell on Catching Fire,” he says. “It was my fourth movie. It was a movie I was inheriting. It was a big movie, and it was a massive cast. So I was really intimidated. Now I have much more fun, I will say. I’m much more comfortable with actors, and the amount of actors, and with logistics. And there’s definitely a family that’s built into it, which is very important for me. There’s, what, 14 years of my life spent making these movies? So it has to be fun.”

This interview has been edited and condensed for clarity.

Vanity Fair: When did you become aware of Sunrise on the Reaping, and at what point did you and Suzanne Collins start discussing an adaptation?

Francis Lawrence: When we did the original series of movies, and we finished the last Mockingjay, she said she was done, and we thought that was it. Then she surprised me and Nina [Jacobson], the producer, with Ballad, however many years later. And she never tells us when she’s writing. Even when she has something to read, she never tells us what it’s about. She’s very superstitious about it all. So we got the call, which was very exciting. And she said, “Go to my agent’s house. There’s a manuscript. Let’s talk after you read it.”

I got to his house, sat in his backyard, and there was a manuscript and a copy of Catching Fire sitting on top of it with a few tabs in it. I opened to the tabs and saw that it had the portion where Katniss and Peeta were looking up the Haymitch Games, that sequence from the book. And I was like, Oh, she’s written the Haymitch story, which was very exciting. And that started the process that we always have, which is that I read the manuscript in one sitting, then read it a second time after having a conversation with Suzanne.

Then I fly to New York and meet her at Scholastic, and we sit up in a conference room for about a week going through the book, beat by beat, of what we think the movie is going to be. She and I lay it all out and chat, and she sits on her computer and writes a scriptment. Then that’s the thing that we turn over to the screenwriter.

How have you built that trust with the author of these books, having adapted them for more than a decade now?

That working process happened accidentally when I signed on to do Catching Fire. I had never done a sequel to someone else’s first movie before. And when Lionsgate and the first director, [Gary Ross], parted ways and I got the call, they didn’t have a script, but they had started the process of developing it before I was on board. And so I signed on based on the book and had planned a trip to New York to meet with Suzanne.

The script came on the day that I was traveling to New York. I read it, and it was not the movie that I wanted to make—because I loved the book, and it was not the book. Luckily, nobody else wanted to make that version of the movie either. Our meeting of me just picking her brain turned into, “This script doesn’t work. Let’s go through, page by page, and figure out the version of the movie we want to make.”

That’s what really formed the bond—just working really, really closely with her. Spending 8 or 10 hours a day for a week or two in a conference room—me riffing on visual ideas and her talking about the story. We think the same way in terms of storytelling, structure, and she seemed to take to my visual ideas.

There’s this weird dynamic that happens as we get closer to production. We’re usually developing the script pretty much within a week of shooting. But the production is also building. So you have thousands of people working, figuring out locations, traveling in different parts of the world. The stress levels are building.

Suzanne and I have related the relationship to almost like a mother-son relationship. It starts really lovely and creative, but then as we get closer and closer, it’s me being that late teen who’s ready to leave the home, like, Okay, enough’s enough. I’m going to go make the movie now. I have to say, I love the process. It’s not without its stress sometimes, but that’s part of the closeness of it all that I cherish.

How did this film’s futuristic-meets-1970s aesthetic come about?

There was an element [of the story] that reminded me a bit of Alice in Wonderland, which then made me think of Jefferson Airplane, that song “White Rabbit,” which brought me to the ’60s and ’70s. Then you can look at characters certain ways. Like, Haymitch, you go, Okay, he’s from, like, young Bruce Springsteen, or Lenore Dove [played by Whitney Peak], like a young Stevie Nicks. And you get this hippie vibe. And the Capitol is that Warhol Studio 54 crowd, and Snow like a ’70s Yves Saint Laurent.

How do you go about casting a young Haymitch, finding an actor who can anchor the film like Jennifer Lawrence did in the original Hunger Games film?

Our casting director, Deb Zane, had met him, and I want to say before he did East of Eden. She had a lot of faith in him. We liked his self-tape a lot. But we knew that he was going to have to pull a lot off. He starts as a very optimistic, fun, playful, young guy in love, and in this movie has to turn into somebody that’s much closer to the Haymitch that we’re used to—cynical and a drunk. He’s going to have to go through a lot of emotional trauma, plus have that kind of mischievousness, irreverence, all these different elements that Woody [Harrelson] has naturally. And so we really put him through the ringer. We did a lot of tests, kept bringing him back, bringing him back, bringing him back. He and I were modulating how much Woody we feel in his performance.

I want to make sure that everybody makes the role their own. That they find the spirit of the character and that it’s not trying to do an imitation of somebody. That’s true of Elle [Fanning] doing Effie, of Ralph [Fiennes] doing Snow, of Jesse [Plemons] doing Plutarch, Kieran [Culkin] as Caesar. Everybody has to figure out how to do it on their own. Then the weird thing is that Kelvin [Harrison Jr., who plays a young Beetee], has worked with Jeffrey [Wright] three times. So he’s fairly close with Jeffrey. When he got the role, he called and said, “You won’t believe this, but I’m actually playing the young version of you.” He could just conjure Jeffrey Wright. His voice, the speech patterns, the timbre of the sound of everything. It was pretty wild.

Were there storylines from the previous films that you felt you could plant Easter eggs for? I’m thinking specifically of the romantic chemistry between Effie and Haymitch.

There are certain Capitol characters where I give a lot of freedom for improvisation. Effie, Caesar, Drusilla [played by Glenn Close], there’s a lot of room for ad-libs. One of the elements that we definitely played with a bit came from an improvised moment in the [last] Mockingjay. We were shooting this scene in Atlanta outside of Snow’s mansion, which is one of the very last scenes of Katniss in the Capitol. She’s saying goodbye to Effie, and then Haymitch and Effie kiss.

Quite honestly, it was an idea because Woody just wanted to kiss Elizabeth Banks. But everybody thought it would be fun and that the fans would dig it, that Haymitch kissed her. Then it became this big thing of this hidden love that Haymitch and Effie have for one another. And Elle played into that a bit. So there’s, I would say, a hint of flirtation here between the two, less so on Haymitch’s side. His heart is obviously somewhere else in the story. But Elle definitely brought a bit of that to it. That was not scripted. That’s the fun of having great actors.

What was it like to reunite Jennifer Lawrence, Josh Hutcherson, and Woody Harrelson from the original movies?

Jen and I have stayed close over the years. We’ve done four movies together. Josh, who basically has moved to Spain, I haven’t seen in a long time. Woody I see off and on and text every once in a while. But there was something really special about the three of them being back together.

It’s also really, for the most part, the same crew from 2012, when we started making Catching Fire. It’s the same DP [Jo Willems], the same camera operator [David J. Thompson], the same AD [Christopher Surgent].

[When] they got to the hotel, Jen and Josh were in the bar having a beer or something, and I was like, Okay, I won’t even shower. I won’t get out of my rain gear. I’ll just come in. The next thing you know, there were 40 of us who had been together since Catching Fire, the gang back together. It’s like a high school reunion or something, but in the best possible way.

There’s a futility to what happens in this film, knowing where we end up in future movies. How do you handle that aspect of history being doomed to repeat itself?

Look, we’re doing a story where you know the lead character is going to make it out. It’s really about how he makes it out, and what he ends up being like at the end of it all. What I’m really pleased about is that the way Suzanne wrote the book, and the way I think we’ve ended the movie, there is a sense of hope that the tragedy is not for nothing. That was really important to me. And one of the beautiful elements in the story, which comes a bit later, is this sense of, everybody has to find their voice. Everybody has to participate.

It really deals with this thing that I think is happening a lot today, where you find apathy: people not wanting to vote, feeling even if they do vote that it’s not going to matter. This idea that you have to be active, and you can’t expect immediate gratification—that everybody needs to stand up and it may take generations, but you still have to do it.

Outside of my screening on the Upper West Side, there was a group protesting Elon Musk and data centers because the documentary about him was playing at the theater. It was a reminder that there will always be something to rise up against, and always people who will be willing to do it.

Yeah, exactly. And ideally, at some point, if everybody stands up, something will change.

During the 13 years that you’ve worked in this universe, dystopia has felt more and more familiar. How have real-world events influenced your filmmaking?

In the 56 years I’ve been alive, there’s never really been a time where I’ve felt like, Oh wow, everything is great. Nothing is wrong in the world. It always feels like there’s some dystopian sense about the world at any given time. And somehow Suzanne is always able to write these stories about it, which is what makes these movies feel timeless. A lot of people just see them as spectacles, but really they’re about our times very specifically. Almost in a subversive way, I feel like we’ve gotten to smuggle these ideas into these movies that a lot of people go see.
