I Watched AI Eat the Easy Half of an Industry. The Other Half Got More Expensive.

If you want to see what AI actually does to a professional services market, do not look at the think pieces. Look at logistics consulting. Over the past three years I have watched machine learning quietly absorb half of what this industry used to bill for, and the result is not what either the doomers or the boosters predicted. The analysts did not disappear. The market split, and the half that survived got more valuable.

A decade ago, a meaningful share of logistics consulting revenue came from work that was, honestly, computation: benchmark these freight rates, model this distribution network, forecast this demand curve, reconcile these invoices. It was skilled work, but it was pattern work, and pattern work is exactly what the current generation of models does at near-zero marginal cost.

Today a shipper with a data feed and a software subscription gets analysis that used to cost six figures. Arrival predictions come from models trained on historical movement data. Network scenarios run by the thousand. Document extraction reads the bills of lading that used to consume junior analysts. The report-writing tier of consulting, the firms whose deliverable was the analysis itself, is being hollowed out in real time, and nobody who buys consulting is mourning it.

The timeline matters for anyone mapping this onto their own industry. The hollowing did not announce itself. It showed up first as pricing pressure on commodity studies, then as procurement departments quietly canceling retainers for work their own tools now did, then as the mid-tier firms pivoting to implementation or disappearing into acquisitions. Three years, roughly, from capable models to restructured market. Professional services does not get a decade to adapt. It gets a product cycle.

A composite example, drawn from how these engagements actually run. An industrial shipper suspects it is overpaying for freight. Five years ago, step one was a six-week analytical phase billed by the hour. Today the benchmarking is largely done before the first meeting ends, because the consultant’s tooling has already scored every lane against live market data. What fills the engagement now is everything downstream of the analysis: deciding which carriers to pressure and which to protect, structuring a bid so incumbents actually compete, reading the counterparty across the table, sequencing the network changes so operations never hiccup.

Notice the economics. The part that was automated was the part that scaled with headcount, which is why junior analyst hiring in this field has cratered. The part that remains scales with reps, the hundreds of negotiations and implementations that calibrate someone’s judgment, and reps cannot be batch-processed. The pyramid business model died; the expertise business replaced it.

Here is the part the automation narrative misses. When the computable layer of a profession becomes free, the non-computable layer does not shrink. It becomes the entire product, and its price goes up, because buyers can finally see what they were actually paying for.

Economists have a name for the pattern: complementarity. Automate one input and its complements appreciate. Spreadsheets did not kill accounting; they killed ledger clerks and made judgment-heavy advisory the growth business. Databases did not kill law; they killed document review hours and raised the premium on courtroom strategy. The models are running the same play on analysis itself, across every industry at once, and freight just happens to be the cleanest place to watch it because the complement is so unmistakably physical.

In logistics, the non-computable layer is specific and stubborn. A model can tell you that you are overpaying on a freight lane. It cannot sit across the table from the carrier’s pricing manager, know from two hundred prior negotiations that the first offer is theater, and restructure the volume commitment so both sides sign. It can flag that your railcars dwell too long at a customer site. It cannot know that the fix runs through one specific unloading crew and a demurrage clause written in 2019. The deepest version of this is rail, the most contract-heavy and relationship-dense mode in freight, where specialist rail consulting practices staffed by former railroad executives are thriving in exactly the era that was supposed to automate them away.

Watch where the demand moved. Rankings of the best logistics consulting firms are now led by execution-focused specialists rather than report factories, and the fastest-growing firms in the field are boutiques built by operators, small teams of people who ran freight networks for decades, armed with the same AI tooling as everyone else, selling the one thing the tooling cannot produce: judgment with skin in the game. Many now tie fees directly to documented savings. You do not offer that pricing model unless you are confident you possess something the software does not.

The buyers repriced too, which is the half of the story nobody writes about. Procurement departments that once compared consultancies on day rates now compare them on referenceable outcomes, because the day-rate layer is software now. The RFP questions changed in three years from describe your methodology to show us the twelve-month results of your last three engagements. Markets learn fast when the alternative is free.

If you are building AI products for any operational industry, this split is your map. The computable layer is a feature war you will fight against every funded competitor, with margins trending toward zero. The durable business sits at the interface between the model output and the messy human system that has to act on it, and that interface is owned by whoever has the domain judgment.

Concretely, that suggests three build strategies that hold up. Sell to the experts rather than around them, because the operators-with-tooling model is winning and they are buying. Price against outcomes the way the leading consultancies now do, because outcome pricing is a moat software-only competitors cannot cross without the judgment layer. And if you must pick between a better model and better domain distribution, pick distribution, because the model advantage decays in months and the trust advantage compounds for years.

Which suggests the uncomfortable, useful conclusion: the most defensible asset in the AI era is not the model. It is the twenty years someone spent on loading docks and in negotiation rooms learning what the data cannot say. The software made that knowledge more valuable, not less, and the industries that figure out how to pair the two, as freight is doing right now, are the ones to watch.

Every knowledge industry is running this experiment with a lag. Freight just finished the first lap, and the results are posted: the analysts who were really computers lost, the operators who were really judges won, and the total value of the market went up. Plan your career, your product, and your hiring accordingly.
