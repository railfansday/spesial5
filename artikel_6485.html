Could a Nasal Spray Stop Alzheimer’s Before Memory Loss Begins?

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Protecting memory from Alzheimer’s may one day involve a nasal spray, with encouraging results in animals and evidence that its particles also protect human neurons grown in the lab.

Alzheimer’s disease damages the connections that allow brain cells to communicate, threatening memory. A nasal spray containing tiny particles naturally released by cells has helped protect those connections in an animal model of the disease. The treatment both prevented memory deficits and countered cognitive decline after it had begun.

The particles, called extracellular vesicles, are small packages that cells release into their surroundings. Those used in this study came from cells of the amniotic membrane, the tissue lining the sac that surrounds a developing fetus. Researchers at the Università Cattolica del Sacro Cuore, Fondazione Policlinico Universitario A. Gemelli IRCCS, and the E. Menni Research Center of Fondazione Poliambulanza reported the findings in Translational Neurodegeneration.

When administered through the nose to animals that mimic features of Alzheimer’s disease, the vesicles reached the hippocampus, a brain region crucial for memory and particularly vulnerable to the disease. There, they entered both neurons and microglia, cells that monitor conditions in the brain and help defend it. The treatment counteracted neuroinflammation and protected neuronal function, particularly at synapses, the connections through which nerve cells transmit information. These effects were accompanied by a significant improvement in cognitive abilities.

The choice of amniotic membrane cells grew out of years of placental research by Professor Ornella Parolini, a full professor of applied biology and scientific director of IRCCS Ospedale “Casa Sollievo della sofferenza.” Her work has focused on these cells and the substances they release, collectively known as the secretome, investigating their protective and regenerative effects in various experimental models. The E. Menni Research Center, led by Parolini, contributed expertise in isolating and characterizing the cells and identifying the molecules they produce.

The research combined the expertise of Professor Claudio Grassi, whose team at the Università Cattolica studies brain inflammation and plasticity, with work by Professor Salvatore Fusco on the vesicles’ effects in the brain. Lead authors Dr. Andrea Papait and Dr. Francesca Natale helped connect these findings, investigating how amniotic extracellular vesicles influence brain inflammation, neuronal function, and memory.

The findings also suggest that effective Alzheimer’s treatments may need to address more than the buildup of harmful proteins in the brain.

Professor Fusco explains: “The study suggests the need to direct research into Alzheimer’s therapies by focusing not only on counteracting the accumulation of toxic compounds like beta-amyloid and tau, but also by paying close attention to the cellular environment in which neurons live (e.g., microglia and astrocytes) and the molecular signals (e.g., cytokines or microRNAs) exchanged by these cells, which can influence synapse function.”

To investigate the protective effects in human cells, researchers used skin biopsies collected from patients with Alzheimer’s disease and healthy individuals at the Policlinico’s Memory Clinic. From those samples, they generated pluripotent stem cells, which can develop into different cell types, and then used those cells to produce human neurons.

The vesicles also showed protective effects in these laboratory-grown neurons, extending the evidence to experimental human models and giving the study a closer connection to patients.

Professor Grassi emphasizes that the findings are encouraging but still preliminary.

“These are preclinical results that require further validation in humans and do not yet represent an available therapy for Alzheimer’s disease, but they point to a very promising direction: understanding whether some of the mechanisms through which the placenta naturally regulates inflammation and protects tissues could offer new tools for the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases and, more generally, whether the use of extracellular vesicles represents a new frontier for the treatment of neurological diseases.”

Reference: “Human amniotic mesenchymal stromal cell-derived extracellular vesicles reprogram microglia and prevent neurodegeneration in experimental models of Alzheimer’s disease” by Andrea Papait, Francesca Natale, Antonietta Rosa Silini, Ida Nifo Sarrapochiello, Elisa Orecchini, Raimondo Sollazzo, Marco Rinaudo, Serafina Farigu, Nicoletta Garofalo, Giulia Mantini, Guido Maria Giuffrè, Pietro Romele, Elvira Ragozzino, Alice Dellaria, Luciano Giacò, Camillo Marra, Cristian Ripoli, Claudio Grassi, Ornella Parolini and Salvatore Fusco, 9 September 2026, Translational neurodegeneration. DOI: 10.1186/s40035-026-00581-1
