How does a flavor become a favorite? Scientists uncover a gut-brain signal

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

A slow dopamine signal helps mice learn to prefer flavors linked to calories, offering clues to how food preferences develop.

Coffee can be a hard sell on the first sip. It’s bitter, and people naturally tend to avoid bitter tastes, yet many grow to love it.

A new study offers a clue to how we acquire food preferences over time.

Working mainly with mice, scientists found a slow dopamine signal that helps the brain connect a flavor with calories arriving in the gut.

The signal reaches a small part of the amygdala, and human brain scans pointed to a similar spot.

James Grove, then a graduate student at UC San Francisco, ran most of the experiments in the lab of senior author Zachary Knight, a UCSF physiology professor.

“We’ve shown that this slow dopamine system is what drives us to learn to like certain foods over time, and we can see where and how that happens,” Knight said.

When we eat, taste and calories usually come together.

The researchers needed to separate them to see which one prompted the brain to release dopamine, a chemical messenger.

They fitted mice with a thin tube through the stomach wall and delivered sugar water or a milkshake-like liquid directly into the stomach.

About a fifth of a teaspoon went in over 10 minutes while sensors tracked dopamine in four brain regions.

Two regions that feature heavily in reward research, the nucleus accumbens and dorsal striatum, showed no response to the nutrients.

That matched an earlier experiment from Knight’s lab.

Instead, dopamine rose in the front of the basolateral amygdala. In mice, this structure measures only about a tenth of an inch (2.5 mm) from front to back.

The back half of the basolateral amygdala did not respond to nutrients. It did respond to a drug that made mice feel sick.

The front section’s signal also followed a different timetable from dopamine released in response to taste.

Taste signals come and go within seconds, but this one began about 48 seconds after nutrients entered the stomach.

It stayed high for about nine minutes after the infusion ended. That gave the researchers a way to distinguish the slower response to nutrients from the quick response to flavor.

Next, mice drank two saccharin-sweetened solutions, one lemon-lime and one grape. Licking one triggered glucose delivery into the stomach, while licking the other delivered plain water.

After six days, mice with normal dopamine activity strongly favored the flavor paired with glucose. Its share of their licks rose from about 35% to 81%.

When researchers used light to switch off dopamine-producing cells in the midbrain during training, the mice showed little change in preference.

The glucose-paired flavor’s share of their licks rose only from 48% to 53%.

These mice kept drinking, although they drank less of the newly paired flavor on the first day.

The experiment included roughly equal numbers of males and females. Both showed similar dopamine responses.

Turning off dopamine neurons didn’t stop every kind of learning. Mice that tried a new flavor and then received lithium chloride, which causes nausea, still learned to avoid it.

So the mice could still learn that a flavor made them feel ill. Learning to like a flavor because it came with nutrition depended on a different brain system.

The team then offered mice two calorie-free flavors and used light to activate dopamine fibers in the front of the amygdala 20 seconds after they licked one of them.

The mice learned to prefer that flavor even though no nutrients arrived.

The same stimulation in the nucleus accumbens, dorsal striatum, central amygdala, or back of the basolateral amygdala did not create a preference.

In the dorsal striatum, it had the opposite effect: the paired flavor’s share of licks fell from about 61% to 37%.

Stimulating the fibers in the front of the basolateral amygdala did not change how much the mice ate.

The mice also wouldn’t press a lever just to get that stimulation.

Unlike many dopamine signals, this one didn’t seem rewarding by itself, but pairing it with a flavor taught a lasting preference.

The gut’s response to sugar depended on SGLT1, a protein that transports certain sugars across the intestinal wall.

Sugars that activated it prompted dopamine release, while fructose and mannitol did not.

A drug that blocked SGLT1 stopped the response. Fat used a different set of sensors, and blocking three of them stopped the dopamine response to fat.

Other research has also found separate gut pathways for sugar and fat.

Using a microscope, the team watched individual amygdala neurons as mice drank. About 58% became active when the mice licked sucrose, beginning as soon as licking started.

The same cells responded to sucralose, a sweetener with no calories. But that response faded during a single session, while the response to sucrose held steady.

Sweet taste and signals from the gut reached the same cells, which kept responding when the sweetness came with calories.

Silencing those cells during training stopped the mice from learning a preference without stopping them from eating.

Until then, the researchers had recorded only the brain’s left hemisphere.

When they checked both sides in the same mice, nutrients raised the dopamine signal by about 18% on the left and about 5% on the right.

Activity at rest was similar on both sides. The difference appeared when nutrients arrived.

Human scans offered a similar clue: Dana Small at Yale reanalyzed a PET study of 13 men who drank a milkshake or a tasteless drink.

Dopamine release after the drink reached the stomach was concentrated in the left front amygdala.

Marc Tittgemeyer and colleagues at the Max Planck Institute for Metabolism Research in Cologne scanned 38 adults after a glucose drink. Activity increased in the same left-side area.

In mice, cutting the vagus nerve on either side roughly halved the response in both brain hemispheres. That suggested the left-right difference did not come from signals traveling along that nerve.

The team then traced the brain regions feeding into each side of the pathway.

The biggest difference appeared in the visceral cortex, part of the insula that processes signals from inside the body.

Scientists generally expect circuits for basic tasks such as feeding to work similarly on both sides of the brain.

Knight’s lab is now studying the connections between the insula and amygdala to understand why this signal is stronger on the left.

The human evidence is more limited, and scans after a drink cannot show that this signal teaches people to prefer a flavor.

Unlike the mouse experiments, the human studies did not deliver nutrients straight into the stomach and measure the dopamine response that followed.

The study also did not test GLP-1 drugs. Knight suspects they might reduce dopamine release in response to nutrients, making food less reinforcing over time, but that possibility remains untested.

“By understanding the steps between nutrient detection in the gut and learning in the brain, it might be possible to fine-tune them to subtly change our preferences for different foods,” Knight said.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.
