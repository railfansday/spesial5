20 Most Universally Loved Animated Shows, Ranked

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Animation is the great equalizer. Iconic animated shows like The Simpsons and Attack on Titan and Avatar: The Last Airbender have brought audiences around the world together. Part of that international appeal is simply that animation is a lot easier to dub, but it’s also because the limitless medium of animation allows artists to capture something unique and universal.

One Piece’s pirate adventure speaks to something much deeper about defying an oppressive regime. BoJack Horseman’s titular talking horse went on a destructive journey of substance abuse and self-loathing. These are the most universally beloved animated shows ever made.

It’s hard to understate just how big of a cultural juggernaut Rick and Morty was at the height of its popularity. This show had such dedicated fans that they were terrorizing McDonald’s employees for a taste of Mulan-branded Szechuan sauce.

It’s easy to see why Rick and Morty made such an impact. It’s hard sci-fi, with pocket universes and decoy families and interdimensional travel, but it also hits on a much deeper emotional level, with a frank look at mental illness, family dysfunctions, and terrifyingly toxic relationships.

Neon Genesis Evangelion is a deconstruction of the mecha genre by way of a Biblical allegory and both Jungian and Freudian interpretations of the human psyche. The series takes place in a post-apocalyptic future, where soldiers in giant biomechanical suits are tested and traumatized in their battles with beings known as “Angels.”

The last two episodes proved to be polarizing, so much so that the ending has since been remade in other media. But the show’s esoteric ending opens the door to a lot of discussion, much like the ambiguous final cut-to-black in The Sopranos.

Long before the Simpsons, long before the Griffins, long before the Belchers, there was The Flintstones. Hanna-Barbera took the basic premise of The Honeymooners and transplanted it into prehistoric times for the first ever animated series targeted at grownups.

The Flintstones is remembered for all the sight gags turning dinosaurs and reptiles into household items, but it was also a timely satire of suburban family life. As the theme tune highlights, they’re the modern Stone Age family; it’s the Stone Age, but they’re dealing with modern concerns.

X-Men: The Animated Series deftly translated the complex characterization and moral ambiguity and thematic richness of the X-Men comics to the screen. It’s essentially a superhero soap opera, balancing dazzling comic book spectacle with deeply involving human drama.

The cartoon recaptured the searing social commentary that makes the X-Men different. It updated the comics’ civil rights-era allegories, and redefined these mutant underdogs for a new generation. In the past couple of years, against all odds, its own reboot — X-Men ’97 — has done the same thing.

While The Simpsons and Family Guy went all-in on the comic absurdity, King of the Hill remained refreshingly grounded. No one fell in a gorge or fought a giant chicken; every episode was based on a relatable, real-life situation that real families might deal with: a boisterous new co-worker, a dad pushing his kid into sports, gossip getting around town.

King of the Hill is the cartoon equivalent of a Norman Rockwell painting. It captures a nostalgic, pastel-colored reflection of classic Americana: backyard barbeques, football viewing parties, fixing up a truck on a hot Sunday afternoon.

Charles M. Schulz had such a simple, soulful view of the world that not only do his Peanuts comic strips still hold up; their animated adaptation does, too. Those old Peanuts cartoons haven’t aged a day; they’re still just as wholesome and heartwarming as they ever were.

Charlie Brown and his friends are some of the most iconic characters ever created, and the animated series brought them to life. No matter how bad your day has been, Peanuts is the perfect pick-me-up.

Monkey D. Luffy has been searching for the One Piece for over 1,000 episodes, and he still hasn’t found it, and people are still tuning in, because it’s not really about that. One Piece started off as a fun riff on pirate adventures, but it’s since evolved into something much, much bigger.

It’s not just about a pirate crew searching for treasure. It’s about a tightknit group of friends defying an evil regime to live their lives freely and pursue their dreams unencumbered. It’s an inspiring tale of the oppressed turning on the oppressors.

George Lucas’ Star Wars prequels were criticized for getting bogged down in the political machinations of a galaxy far, far away. But the animated spinoff, Star Wars: The Clone Wars, took the pieces of that political narrative — a manufactured war being fought by two sides controlled by the same guy — and turned it into a genuinely compelling TV drama.

The Clone Wars has an awful lot of pew-pew laser fights and buzz-buzz lightsaber duels, but it also explores the futility of warfare, the endless cycle of eye-for-an-eye violence, and the ethical implications of following unethical orders. It satisfies as a space adventure, but it also digs into the deeper themes that Lucas’ movies spelled out in their dialogue.

Scooby-Doo, Where Are You! was a lot of kids’ first encounter with the horror genre. It introduced young viewers to spooky ghosts and swamp monsters and vampire bats through the lens of a heartfelt buddy comedy.

Initially, what makes you stop changing channels and settle on Scooby-Doo is the creepy atmosphere. But what makes you stick around is the camaraderie between the Mystery Inc. gang.

There was a lot riding on Matt Groening’s follow-up to The Simpsons, and he managed to live up to the hype. Futurama is just as biting and hilarious and endearing as The Simpsons, but it takes its satire of American culture 1,000 years into the future, where things have only gotten worse.

Futurama had one of the smartest writers’ rooms in the history of television. As a sitcom, it’s just as funny as The Simpsons, but as hard science fiction, it’s just as complex and thought-provoking as Star Trek or The Twilight Zone.
