Can You Talk Your Way Into a Job?

Job interviews can feel overwhelming, but a 2024 study by Heimann & Schmitz-Wilemy suggests that being yourself may be the key to landing that job.

Research suggests that interviewees who are seen as more genuinely themselves are more likely to get the job. But do people who are more authentic during job interviews actually perform better at the job itself?

Researchers analyzed video recordings from 181 employed adults who participated in structured mock interviews and obtained job performance ratings from each participant’s supervisor. The study distinguished between verbal authenticity cues (what applicants say) and nonverbal authenticity cues (how they said it, including facial expressions, vocal style, and gestures).

Authenticity is that which makes us human. Think of authenticity cues as things a robot—or even artificial intelligence (AI)—is unlikely to do. Examples include expressing personal opinions, discussing personal topics from your private life (like whether a spouse is excited about this particular job opportunity), and talking about emotions in a way that feels sincere.

For example, “I’m excited by this job opportunity and know I can rise to the challenge,” or “I know I don’t have some of the experience you’re looking for, but I’m eager to learn”: The latter feels or sounds more like a human connection because of the acknowledgment of a weakness and the desire to overcome it.

Applicants who openly shared personal information, clearly stated their opinions, expressed emotions, and communicated in an expressive manner were more likely to be viewed as genuine and authentic by observers.

To determine whether an applicant's “realness” carries weight further than simply a first impression, the researchers controlled for observers' initial ratings of warmth and competence. Authenticity cues continued to predict (perceived) authenticity even after these factors were taken into account.

Our perception of someone being real with us in the moment is not entirely based on whether we think they are likeable or able to handle the job. Instead, we find ourselves subconsciously drawn to people who seem sincere. In sum, your foibles and flaws make you human. Yup, the way you stumbled a bit as you gave your answer or hesitated before responding to a question—that’s the part that makes you seem genuine, likeable, and, most importantly, management material.

Why aren’t we prizing perfect answers over genuine answers? Because gaining someone’s trust is crucial in the animal kingdom—and in the human kingdom as well.

We need to know who the predators are so that we don’t feel like prey, and I’m guessing that job interviewers are often suspicious that candidates are blowing smoke at them in an attempt to secure the job.

This study found that job interviewers paid particular attention to whether candidates expressed genuine opinions, shared their emotions (both good and bad), and communicated in a way that felt sincere.

Pretty well—but not for all people all the time.

Perceived authenticity predicted who was most likely to get hired, but researchers also predicted that authenticity cues displayed during interviews would be associated with supervisor ratings of job performance.

But it turns out that this prediction was only partially supported.

Verbal authenticity cues showed a significant positive relationship with supervisor-rated job performance (r = .24), whereas nonverbal authenticity cues did not show a significant relationship (r = .09).

In other words, applicants who clearly expressed their opinions, discussed emotions, and avoided vague or superficial responses tended to perform better at work according to their supervisors. The superficial acts of appearing expressive, animated, or engaging through nonverbal behavior were not associated with better job performance.

There were three verbal behaviors that were important when it came to job performance: clearly stating personal opinions, providing specific rather than vague responses, and discussing emotions when appropriate. The idea is that clear, direct interaction is helpful in both a job interview and in facilitating effective work relationships. Effective work relationships with open and honest communication are more likely to result in a secure and successful work environment. When employees are happy and feel supported by management, their employment goals are more likely to be met, and their bosses are more likely to approve of their leadership skills.

Don’t be a robot during a job interview; just be yourself. Stop obsessing over the “right” answer to a question and go for the truth: I’m not perfect, but I’m always looking to improve. And use your real-life language and wording, not something you copied out of a manual or learned from your AI best friend. It appears that either consciously or subconsciously, interviewers respond not only to your on-paper qualifications but also to you as a person.

Heimann, A.L., Schmitz-Wilhelmy, A. Observing Interviewees’ Inner Self: How Authenticity Cues in Job Interviews Relate to Interview and Job Performance. J Bus Psychol 40, 237–256 (2024). https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-024-09949-4
