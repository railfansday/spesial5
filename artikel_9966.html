When Microsoft, Apple, and the FAA can't keep it up, it's a system of a down

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Outage after outage after outage is a syndrome that needs radical treatment

No Access November is going well this year.

Among the many organizations to adopt Outage As A Service (OAAS) were Microsoft, Apple, the FAA, and any number of European supermarkets. Even the NSA has succumbed. For us, at least, outage is word of the year. It's certainly deeply embedded in the public mind, with schoolchildren reportedly referring to the 19th century Irish famine as "the potato outage." There's no danger of an outrage outage.

OAAS is synonymous with infrastructure failure, a more useful term that begins to hint at cause and cure as well as effect. In all of the latest crop, what we know is depressingly familiar. Microsoft's O365 meltdown was a cascading failure caused by a botched upgrade that took stuff offline and melted down as it tried to come back. The FAA was a migration from Jurassic hardware that wasn't end of life so much as living fossil. The Eurofridge-cum-fondue-maker was a control system so badly written it fell apart if you raised an eyebrow at it. Clearly, nobody had. Until now.

These are all different classes of OAAS that have been around for decades. Security-based outages are still outages, and a packet flood is still a packet flood whether it's a botnet DDoS or finger trouble in the config dashboard.

Each of them has a root cause with obvious fixes – a faulty mental model of network/server interaction, chronic underfunding, a complete supply chain with little cyber-security expertise. All these, like the poor, will be with us always. They won't fix themselves. Infrastructure that works is invisible, and invisible things are forgotten until they break. What's needed is an infrastructure that watches itself, that is designed to be adaptive and resilient, one that refuses to connect to danger and nulls out any that gets through.

There have been attempts to create a ground-up architecture that is inherently risk resistant. One such is SCION (Scalability, Control, and Isolation On Next-generation networks). It redesigns routing between domains around explicit path selection, separate control and data planes, and mechanisms for trust and failure isolation. The result is a very resilient, very reliable, and very secure infrastructure that reflects the designers' tenet that you can't patch the internet into any of these things. Start from scratch or GTFO.

SCION started about 15 years ago, and it works well enough to be adopted by entities like the Secure Swiss Finance Network. If the Gnomes of Zurich trust it, no higher accreditation exists. Well, it was designed by ETH Zürich. Where you won't find it is almost everywhere else. The internet is like Switzerland in the same way the Amazon rainforest is like Central Park. There are trees and sometimes it rains. It is unwise to infer anything else.

Given you can't manicure existing infrastructure into true resilience, the only other option is evolution. A couple of billion years of continued life on Earth has proved this is the only way to re-engineer complex systems while they're usefully running. In this case, the current evolutionary endpoint that's of most interest is the human immune system.

An interlocking system of functions, it takes care of uncountably thousands of pathogen-based threats and internal malfunctions every day. You might think that the 30-odd billion computing machines making up today's Internet are impossible to monitor and secure, but that's peanuts to the human immune system. An average human has around 30 trillion cells, and roughly the same again in bacteria. When it works, the mechanics of the human infrastructure are invisible and out of mind. The immune system makes it so, and everything involved is driven by information, signaling and behavior. There is pattern matching, resource management, defense in depth and reinforcement learning in persistent models. Sounds familiar?

The analogy between this and modern digital infrastructure is so irresistible that it has been around for a lifetime. Viruses, infections, swarms, worms, system health are so familiar that they go unremarked. The term Digital Immune System has been around since the last century too, pushed by outfits like IBM, Symantec and Gartner. It hasn't taken off, probably because it's been marketing bunkum instead of describing the development of inherently resilient infrastructure based on insights from biology.

It has also been true that natural selection, the engine of evolution and this biology, only works in IT at the most macro, capitalist level of product and company survival. Everything else is selected through design decisions, market analysis, and insight versus delusion. The adoption of immunological concepts to digital infrastructure will have to be the result of rigorous exploration of complex systems and cross-disciplinary research. What would that look like?

There are academics thinking just these thoughts: see this paper [PDF] from Kay Smarsly at Technische Universität Hamburg. Like SCION, it takes an architectural look at what resiliency might look like, but with explicit reference to biological systems.

OAAS is a chronic syndrome with multiple causes and potentially fatal outcomes. It exists and persists because digital infrastructure too often fails to contain internal failures or external attacks. We have a working example of an approach to managing analogous problems in systems far vaster and more intricate than any we have built. We now have tools that may start to do systemic analysis and inference at a useful scale. In an industry prepared to bet billions on agents that do your shopping, there is every reason to kick off serious research into digital immune systems worthy of the name. Doctor, cure thyself. ®
