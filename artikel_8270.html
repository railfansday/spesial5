How to Clean Overhanging Routes Without Taking the Biggest Swing of Your Life

Permadraws are incredibly convenient, but sometimes they are not appropriate for an area. There are a few reasons for this. One is that permadraws are highly visible, turning a natural setting into an outdoor climbing gym. Permadraws also corrode rapidly in marine and coastal environments. Lastly, they are expensive.

So if there are no permadraws, how do you safely clean a steep sport route? Leaving a carabiner is the next easiest solution. But what if you don’t want to do that?

These nagging questions—as well as recent accidents—have compelled me to put together this long list of options for cleaning your next steep line. I’ve organized these methods into three categories:

Each method has pros and cons in regards to simplicity, speed, and safety.

Ultimately, no single “best method” exists. Though there is a singular worst method: rappelling. Cleaning steep routes on rappel is a huge hassle for the climber, and bystanders on the ground who inevitably have to assist. Your method of choice will come down to the circumstances of each route and your personal preferences on simplicity, speed, and risk tolerance. Many of these methods are best saved for niche scenarios, or rare “if-then” situations. Still, it is best to be prepared with knowledge and equipment for those odd cleaning scenarios.

Below, I will discuss several methods for cleaning on the way up. I choose this approach if a route has a roof or dangerous swing potential or if, for whatever reason, I’m not ready to clean the route on the way down. Cleaning on the way up can take more time, but it brings safety gains. Methods range from simple to complex. If cleaning on the way down looks like a nuisance, just lower down to the ground and deal with it later (i.e., on the way back up)

If the route is well within the abilities of someone in your party, toproping to clean is always a good go-to. Never turn down a free toprope, right?

Issues with this method arise when the climbing gets hard enough where the climber might fall on toprope, thus becoming stuck in space. If the climber is unable to get back to the wall and decides to lower, then you may end up needing to re-lead the climb to re-clip the draws/directionals, so someone can attempt cleaning on toprope again. This is a huge pain, but I see it very often. Thankfully, there are many methods that can help you avoid this scenario.

Firstly, when someone is toproping to clean, we always say they must promise to get to the top. That way, they won’t leave the rope unclipped from lifesaving directionals. This reminder helps incentivize the climber to “plan falls.” The worst time to fall is immediately after unclipping a bolt, when the swing will be the farthest, potentially leaving the climber stranded. If you think you might fall, it’s best to adequately rest hanging at a bolt before unclipping. Only unclip the bolt once you’re ready to charge to the next one.

What if you get unlucky and fall into space? You can try pulling on the belayer’s end of the rope if it’s within reach. However, this method is usually much less productive on steep topropes than on steep lead climbs. Oftentimes, pulling on the rope will get you nowhere.

If pulling on the rope is getting you nowhere, the next method is boinking. Usually performed on lead, boinking is a strategy a leader can employ after falling into space and becoming unable to reach the rock or a rope. But it does work (albeit sometimes less effectively) on toprope. It’s also tiring and best for short stretches.

The boinking method is simple, but often executed inefficiently. The belayer takes tight on the rope, ideally, and hovers above the ground, or over an edge where they can hang their full body weight. Then, the climber does a pull-up on the climber side of the rope to create a loop of slack. When the climber rapidly releases the slack, the belayer drops. If the belayer is too low to the ground, they will hit the ground, and usually blame the climber.

For maximum efficiency, the belayer can easily ascend the rope (using an ascender and their Grigri) the same distance that the climber needs to ascend. This obviates the need for timing the boinking/drops and is far more relaxing for both parties. It’s also far more efficient than the belayer jumping to take at the right time.

An alternative to boinking is climbing the leash, as it’s called by highliners. This is the main method highliners use to climb the leash after falling, but also works identically in climbing scenarios when you’re not too far below your clip. If you’re flexible, place your heel above your tie-in knot, then pull on the rope, until you can straighten your leg and reach high for the wall. Once you pull on the wall or a draw, the belayer must rapidly take in all the extra slack.

Have the aforementioned methods become too tiring or inefficient? Or is there too much rope drag or rope stretch in the system for them to be effective? The next method is for the stuck climber to ascend the rope. If I don’t want to do the climbing again, I’ll usually jump straight to this method since I always have an ascender with me at the crag. If it’s just a short section to ascend, a prusik or other autoblock will work fine. But for cleaning a full route, you’ll thank yourself for bringing a proper ascender or at least nano-trax, to the crag (they weigh nothing).

If it is too difficult to pull in and release a draw, you can relocate your ascender above the draw, then clip a PAS/draw into the top carabiner of the draw you wish to clean. Slack the rope from your Grigri to unclip the rope from the draw. If you forgot to move the ascender above the clip, it will get stuck here. Begin ascending again until there is a 90-degree angle between your PAS and tensioned rope. The closer this angle is to 90 degrees, the less muscle required to pull in and unclip the draw. When you unclip the draw from the bolt, it stays attached to your PAS as you go swinging.

Sometimes rope shenanigans can get a bit clunky, especially in roofs. If the bolts are close enough together, you can implement some aid climbing techniques as needed. Sometimes it’s easier to ascend via yarding on bolts, and top-stepping in slings than jugging when it’s really steep. Just do whatever it takes to get up there and reclaim those draws! This can be done in a toprope scenario or a jugging scenario, with a slacked rope, or tensioned.

The biggest error when cleaning steep routes is cleaning on the way down, then taking a dangerous swing low on the route. There are many ways to clean on the way down. We’ll start with the standard scenario, then dive into niche techniques for oddball scenarios.

Many routes have an anchor just below the steep climbing. If this is the case, once you finish cleaning the steep terrain, the first step is to go off belay at that anchor and untie. Then pull the rope from above, re-tie in, and re-lower. When bolting routes, I love adding this type of anchor, not only to provide an easier, shorter version of the route, but also to greatly simplify the process of cleaning lower down the route.

The most common method for cleaning a steep sport route while lowering is to employ a trolley draw (it goes by many names). This draw connects the belay loop to the belayer’s side of the rope. As you lower, you can pull in on the belayer’s line to clean the draws. Higher up on the route, call for your belayer to hold (stop lowering) when the rope below your trolley draw is nearly level. Lower down on the route, you’ll want to stop sooner, just above the bolt.

There are a few methods for retrieving the draws, but whatever you do, be sure to stop and think before unclipping anything.

The quickest method is to simply grab the wall, pull in (not up) to slack the draw and remove the draw from the bolt. If you can’t grab holds, often you can draw dyno: grab the draw with two hands and thrust in (not up) to momentarily slack the draw to remove it. In either of these cases, it is far more strenuous to remove the rope from the draw since your trolley draw will be yanking you away from the wall when you still have to clean the draw from the bolt. TLDR: If you’re tensioning the trolley, unclip the draw from the bolt.

If those methods aren’t working, go in direct to the draw you are hoping to retrieve. You can clip your PAS directly to the top carabiner of the draw. If using a draw to go direct, clip an additional draw from the belay loop to the lower carabiner of the draw you wish to clean. It’s best to resist the temptation to relocate the trolley draw into a tether draw. Note that if you remove your trolley draw and do not replace it, you could later unexpectedly fall into space or take a dangerous swing.

Once you’ve clipped direct to the draw, you can unclip the rope from the draw you wish to clean, calling slack if needed. Now, unlike other methods, the primary tension is no longer on the trolley, but instead directly on the bolt. Now pull down on the belayer’s rope to assist them in taking. That 90-degree rule applies here, too. Double check that your trolley is in place, assess the swing potential, then remove the draw from the bolt, and take the swing.

The lower you go on the route, the more potential energy—and extra rope—builds up in the system. If the trolley accidentally comes unclipped for any reason, it can be catastrophic. You could swing and hit a rock or a tree. The extra rope could even cause you to hit the ground. For this reason, as I get lower, I’ll often double-up my trolley draw. Why not?

Now let’s imagine we’ve lowered all the way down to the first bolt. As mentioned, the farther we end up from the plumb line, the higher the potential swing risk. Sometimes it is safe to unclip and take the swing. Other times, you might hit trees, rocks, or the ground. If both parties decide the swing is safe, the climber must first unclip the trolley draw before taking the swing. If you forget to unclip the trolley draw, you’ll drag the belayer along the ground as you swing. Very unpleasant!

If the belayer is firmly anchored you can opt to keep the trolley draw in place to minimize the swing. However, since most sport crags don’t have convenient ground anchors, this option isn’t always possible. Just remember, don’t expect your belayer alone to stop your swing!

If you’re unsure whether a swing is safe, it’s usually best not to risk it. The horizontal distance of a swing is only a small part of understanding the potential impact. A giant King Swing can be perfectly safe, while a seemingly harmless three-foot swing can be devastating. The greater the starting angle of the swing, the more gravitational potential energy the climber has.

Once past the rope’s vertical plumb line (the lowest point of the swing), the climber begins losing speed. However, impact force depends just as much on how abruptly the climber is stopped as it does on their speed. Finally, account for rope stretch, which can increase the swing’s radius and lower the climber’s trajectory. Always make sure your swing won’t put you into the ground or another obstacle!

Even if the swing is all air, there can still be a risk of sharp rock cutting the rope. For this reason, it’s best to avoid big swings if the rope is rubbing rock. Usually, it’s the swing back toward the wall where rope damage is most likely to occur. Ropes cut most commonly from sideways grinding or sliding. If the rope must press rock, I prefer to place the rope where the rock is perpendicular to the rope’s force.

If a swing looks risky, the safest and easiest thing is to leave a draw or a carabiner. In my 20-plus years of sport climbing, I’ve never left a carabiner, but I do love this method because it’s allowed me to collect hundreds of free carabiners over the years, which I repurpose as lower-offs on anchors.

Besides cost, there are additional downsides to leaving gear. It is annoying to clip a bolt that has a bail carabiner. Numerous reported accidents exist wherein a climber falls after clipping a draw to the hanger over an existing carabiner, causing the draw’s carabiner to break. For this reason, people climbing after you should hopefully know to slide their draw between the carabiner and the wall when clipping.

On coastal routes, solid gate carabiners will eventually corrode shut, requiring a saw to remove. On the coast, only leave wiregates unless the wall is frequently trafficked. Speaking of difficult to remove, quick links should not be left on lead bolts. They require someone else to stop their climbing to use two hands to clean up after you. They are not cheaper than carabiners, unless you use the cheap hardware store ones that rust over and eventually require a saw to remove. If you must leave something, it must be a carabiner. Buy a stick clip and you’ll get an endless supply of free carabiners!

Let’s say we’ve decided to leave a draw. Now just stay connected with your trolley and continue lowering to the ground. The system will experience extra friction with the zigzag in the rope, which is nice. If you want to leave just a carabiner, you’ll go in direct to the draw you wish to clean, slack the rope, and clip the carabiner to the bolt above the draw. Then insert the climber’s end of the rope to the carabiner. Take hard, then remove the draw, and lower.

With both of these methods, you can still retrieve your draw/carabiner. The fastest, and sketchiest way to do so is to solo up to the first bolt (and back down). If it’s easy climbing and you’re comfortable, it does get the job done quickly. Otherwise, removing the draw with a stick clip is much safer, and won’t take too much longer.

Now let’s dig into a couple specialized scenarios.

Technically, the advice that follows on reclaiming the quickdraw from the first bolt safely is part of cleaning a route on the way down. But it involves some niche skills. So, let’s imagine you don’t want to leave a carabiner or draw behind on the first bolt. And you also don’t want to solo up to the first bolt, nor do you have a stick clip. If it’s a big, rounded glue-in, you can do a lower-out off the first bolt. In short, a lower-out is using a length of rope to do a secondary lower that safely equalizes the climber onto the plumb line. The goal here is to cancel out the swing potential, not to lower to the ground, so the belayer should hold tight. Do not lower the climber until the lower-out is complete or you’ll use up more rope on the lower-out than expected.

There are several ways to set up a lower-out. My favorite method? Give myself a huge tail on a figure-8 on a bight when cleaning the anchor. I make that tail double the length of the distance from the bolt to the object I might hit on a swing. When I arrive at that bolt, I go in direct. Then I unclip the trolley and ask my belayer to relocate under the anchor so they don’t swing. Once they’ve taken hard, I pass the extra long tail through the bolt, tension the extra tail, unclip my attachment to the bolt, lower myself out, then pull the rope through the bolt.

Lower-outs can be done with a knot on the end of the lower-out strand or without. Remember, you should always have the knot in the end of the belayer’s strand! Having a knot on the lower-out chord can keep you from lowering off the end and taking a bad swing if the chord comes up short. However, other times, skipping the knot can sometimes be safer (more on that in a minute). This is where stopping and analyzing is important.

Lower-outs can be done with and without a belay device. If it is a pure roof or horizontal traverse, I will use a belay device to lower-out as the forces are significant. This also puts a lot of friction (wear and tear) on the hangers, and should be done sparingly. If the angle is less extreme, I often prefer to lower-out by hand without a belay device. With this method, it is absolutely imperative that the belayer does not lower the climber until the lower-out is complete. Otherwise, the force on your hands will go from very comfortable to dangerous.

But, why on earth would I ever prefer not using a belay device? If your lower-out chord is a tad short, finishing the lower-out by hand is often necessary to maximize your reach before letting go of the chord. The most dangerous option is to use a belay device and no knot; the issue being that it is very difficult to lower without your back facing the wall you’re trying to avoid hitting. Additionally, transitioning from lowering with the device to lowering by hand is risky. The safest thing is to knot the end, lower-out with an assisted braking belay device, hang on the device to clear the knot, then finish lowering by hand. If the pendulum angle is mild enough, I’ll skip those first steps and lower the whole way by hand.

If you use the lower-out method, make sure you have extra rope, as well as a knot in the belayer’s end of the rope. I’d recommend practicing lower-outs in no-risk scenarios first to understand the forces. You may be surprised how easy and safe it can be to two-hand the rope. After the lower-out, simply pull the rope to retrieve. If the belayer avoids lowering during the lower-out, the rope friction on the bolt is minimal and wear and tear on the bolt is non-existent. Still, it’s better to avoid making a habit out of this.

But what if it’s not a fat rounded glue-in bolt? In that case, I’d highly recommend against a lower-out due to the rubbing of the rope on the sharper hanger. In this case, I would do a rappel-out. You’d rig it the same as a lower-out, but you set up your extra tail as a rappel instead of a lower (find the midpoint of your tail). Similarly, the belayer must avoid the temptation to lower.

While you are rappelling out, negligible rope pressure exists on the hanger, but you won’t create any tensioned rubbing friction like you would when lowering. When equalized onto the plumb line, you can pull the rope through the bolt hanger just like before. To further lessen any friction of the rope on the hanger while pulling, flick the tail and pull when the rope is lightest. This way, there will be no wear and tear.

Let’s say you weren’t planning to do a lower-out or rappel-out. Your belayer can toss you the bottom of the lead line to use just the same. Just make sure that before you untie the stopper knot at the end of the line, that the belayer ties a new stopper knot on their break strand (an overhand on a bight is fine). This requires a bit more rope than the first method, so just be careful.

But what if we don’t have enough rope for a lower-out and you don’t have the means to retrieve the draw any other way? If the bolt is solid, you can rappel straight through the bolt hanger. Blasphemy! This is clearly a last resort, but if you’re going to do it, you should at least know the best method. Firstly, never lower through a bolt hanger. That creates much more friction on the rope and bolt than a lower-out or pulling the rope after rappelling.

I personally do not believe pulling the rope after rappelling would cause any wear and tear, especially if it is a fatty rounded glue-in. What about an expansion bolt’s hanger? Recent tests have shown this scenario to hold around 11kN. There is some mild risk to the rope when pulling it due to the sharper edges. Just like the rappel-out, you can flick the end of the rope and pull the rope when it’s lightest. Additionally, having minimal extra rope to pull through the bolt is important. Rig the rope so its end barely touches the ground.

Lastly, avoid this technique above 15-20 feet off the ground unless it’s an emergency scenario because the rope weight will cause more friction when pulling. And of course, this can all be avoided with a stick clip.

If the bolts are close enough together, it’s often easier to just down-aid the route. The steeper the route, the less I’ll tension the rope. In a full roof with close enough bolts, I won’t tension the rope at all. There’s no need for bulky aiders though. I like to keep a 120cm sling coiled up on a lightweight carabiner permanently on my harness, using draws as a PAS.

If the route involves a full roof, it’s usually best to just down-lead/back-aid the route. This is the standard method with the massive roof cracks in Canyonlands. If you can bump cams then there’s no need to tension the rope.

However, this article is about sport climbing. What do you do in a pure roof with spaced out bolts? After running into this scenario many times, I find it easier to do a mix of toprope to clean and jugging the rope. I leave my Grigri on the rope, while the belayer belays with their Grigri, sometimes taking in slack, and sometimes acting as a meat anchor. A Black Diamond grappling hook on the bolt hanger allows me to easily unhook the bolt and take the swing.

Speaking of getting creative, let’s wrap up with what will likely be one of the more controversial takes in this article. I’ve often found the fastest, safest (if you pay attention), and easiest (overall for the team) method is for the climber and belayer to both clean simultaneously. I can’t outright recommend it, unless you have a great attention to detail and years of experience with all of the previous techniques, as well as a variety of other techniques. But I’m including it for those climbing veterans who want to help their partners clean faster and easier.

I generally jump to this method if cleaning the lower part of the route on the way down would be a nuisance or dangerous, but cleaning the top on lower is no-problem. First, I tie off the brake strand on my Grigri. Then, in order to lower the climber, I will climb up the route. I go up, they go down. In doing so, they cancel out much of my weight. If the climbing is hard, I’ll just jug up to the first bolt, clip in, and lower them to their next bolt. We both clean our respective bolts at our leisure. The only communication needed is for the climber to call the standard, “lower” and “hold.”

The climber should stop cleaning one bolt before (above) entering any dangerous swing potential. Once they’ve hit that point, they just hang in space as the belayer continues to ascend with a mix of free, aid, and ascending as they clean. Of course, the belayer should tie backup knots when appropriate.

Once the belayer reaches that final draw left on the wall, they should double check that they are in fact above the danger swing potential and take the swing. If the belayer is still in the dangerous swing potential zone, they can use their brake strand to perform a lower-out. Then the belayer can lower themselves, and then the climber, to the ground. This method is fast and very efficient. It’s also nice for the tired climber who doesn’t want to clean the whole route. Just go about it very carefully and thoughtfully.

See? Cleaning steep routes doesn’t have to be a huge nuisance. If these techniques are all too much, just remember your safety comes first. Play it safe and leave a carabiner or a draw anytime. Yes, it’s not ideal, but carabiners are much cheaper than broken legs.
