Meet Grigoriy Volgin, a Developer Relations specialist at inDrive based in Cyprus. In this installment of our Meet the Developer Advocate series, we explore DevRel philosophy, internal community growth, and the true meaning of developer experience. Grigoriy shares how inDrive—a global mobility platform that allows passengers and drivers to negotiate fair prices directly—cultivates a thriving, 600-strong internal engineering ecosystem.

I am Grigoriy Volgin, and I work in Developer Relations at inDrive, currently based in Cyprus. My role sits precisely at the intersection of engineering, communication, developer experience, and community building.

On a day-to-day basis, I help our engineers share their knowledge and build robust internal technical communities. This involves organizing conferences and hackathons, but it also means bringing our complex engineering stories to a global audience.

Another incredibly important part of my work is focused on improving the developer experience. It is essential to understand exactly what slows engineers down so we can help create an environment where they can focus purely on meaningful work.

inDrive is a global mobility and urban services platform. Unlike traditional ride-hailing platforms, inDrive allows passengers and drivers to agree on a fair price directly. Behind this relatively simple idea is a highly complex engineering ecosystem that operates across vastly different markets, cultures, and technological environments.

What I particularly enjoy about DevRel in this context is the ability to connect people, technology, and business. I strongly believe that a solid engineering culture is built not only through writing great code, but also through consistent knowledge sharing, deep trust, and cross-team collaboration.

For me, DevRel is a fluid mix of engineering, marketing, product, and community management. I would never limit it to just one of these areas. I think a highly effective DevRel specialist must fundamentally understand what engineers do on a daily basis, what specific problems they face, and what the company as a whole wants to achieve.

You do not necessarily need to be the absolute best engineer in the room, but you absolutely must speak the same language. Furthermore, I believe that authentic DevRel starts inside the company.

It is remarkably difficult to build a strong external developer community if you do not already have an active, engaged engineering community operating internally.

For me, this discipline is not just about organizing flashy events, publishing articles, or counting page views and social media likes. It is deeply rooted in building genuine relationships with engineers, understanding their core needs, and helping them solve real-world problems. Sometimes, something as simple as organizing a small internal meetup can bring far more tangible value to the team than sponsoring a massive external conference.

When it comes to acting as a bridge between the company and the engineers, I think everything comes down to trust. Developers should always feel entirely comfortable sharing their honest feedback with you, even when it is not something the company necessarily wants to hear.

For example, when we work on improving the Developer Experience at inDrive, I try to make sure that we remain focused on the actual problems our engineers face, rather than simply following our initial top-down assumptions.

Sometimes, this process requires actively questioning our own approach or respectfully suggesting different priorities based directly on the developer feedback we have gathered.

Of course, there are always strategic business priorities, strictly limited resources, and differing opinions across management. Not every single developer request can be implemented, and that is a completely normal part of software development.

For me, the most important thing is to maintain an open conversation flowing in both directions. You need to understand the overarching business goals, but you also need to be able to effectively translate and explain why certain technical or cultural things matter deeply to the engineering team.

At inDrive, we actively work with both internal and external engineering communities. Internally, our engineering community brings together around 600 people, including backend, mobile, frontend, QA, and platform engineers, as well as engineering managers operating from several different countries.

We maintain this ecosystem through regular technical meetups, dedicated sub-communities for different engineering disciplines, an internal Techleads Club, and regular hackathons.

Externally, we share our ongoing engineering experience with the broader industry through technical articles, conference speaking engagements, and public meetups. I think consistency is one of the main reasons our community has grown so successfully. You simply cannot organize one big event and expect to have a self-sustaining, active community immediately after that. It takes time.

Another crucial factor is giving our engineers more ownership over the community itself. We constantly encourage them to suggest their own topics, organize their own specialized meetups, and share their unique experiences.

As a DevRel specialist, my job is to act as a facilitator to support these initiatives, not to manage everything myself. In fact, some of our absolute best community activities started with a simple, direct message from an engineer who just wanted to share something interesting with their colleagues.

One of the projects I am most proud of is our recent internal hackathon at inDrive. It was our biggest hackathon so far, successfully bringing together people from different countries, disparate teams, and varying professional backgrounds into a single collaborative event.

We explicitly wanted to make it more than just a standard engineering competition. We structured it so that people could work on real business problems, experiment with new technologies, and develop creative ideas that they simply would not normally have the time to explore in their daily work.

Organizing a global hackathon is quite a logistical challenge. You have to navigate different time zones, coordinate across multiple office locations, manage online participants, and take care of a massive number of small operational details.

One of the biggest lessons I took away from this experience was that the work absolutely does not end when you announce the winners. It is equally important to help the participants continue to develop their ideas after the event concludes and to actively connect them with the right stakeholders.

For me, the ultimate best result of a hackathon is when people are so inspired that they want to continue working on their projects even after the formal competition is over.

At inDrive, we generally do not build software products specifically designed for external developers to consume, so my day-to-day experience is a bit different from a traditional SaaS DevRel role. However, we do maintain extensive internal platforms and tools where the exact same DevRel principles apply.

A fantastic recent example of this is our push for internal AI adoption. We realized early on that simply giving our engineers access to new AI tools was nowhere near enough. We needed to actively help them understand exactly how to incorporate these tools into their specific daily workflows.

To achieve this, we organized hands-on workshops, launched targeted educational programs, and highly encouraged our engineers to share their own personal experiences and practical use cases with the rest of the team.

The results were excellent. Between July and October 2026, the total number of AI tool users inside the company grew by almost 40 percent. Of course, this impressive growth was the result of several overlapping initiatives, not just DevRel.

But it proved to me that providing access to a piece of technology is only the first step. You also need to invest the time to help people understand what they can actually accomplish with it in practice.

When it comes to the content our team produces, one of my favorite recent articles is How We Scaled to 530 Aurora Clusters With a 4.5-Person DBA Team by Elizaveta Maznaya, our DBA team lead at inDrive.

It is a highly engaging story about how a remarkably small team managed to double its entire database infrastructure while simultaneously reducing the number of critical incidents from around 50 down to just eight in a six-month period.

What I really appreciate about this particular article is Liza's authentic writing style. She talks completely openly about her team's mistakes, their unsuccessful technical experiments, and the specific things she would do differently today.

The piece is packed with real production numbers, deep technical details, and even some well-placed humor. I think this exact combination is why this type of content works so well. It is clearly written by an engineer, specifically for other engineers, without trying to sell them anything.

As a DevRel specialist, my primary goal is to help our engineers maintain their own unique voice when we collaborate on technical content. I would much rather publish an article with a bit of genuine personality than a perfectly polished corporate success story.

Measuring the impact of DevRel can be tricky, but we track several distinct groups of metrics at inDrive to ensure we are moving in the right direction.

One of my absolute favorite metrics to watch is the number of unique engineers involved in internal knowledge sharing. It is incredibly important for me to see new speakers and first-time authors stepping forward, rather than just working with the same five people all the time.

I also make a point to look closely at what happens long after an activity concludes. Did a promising hackathon project actually move forward into production? Did engineers genuinely start using a new internal tool after attending a workshop?

I do not ignore basic metrics like likes, impressions, or event registrations, but I absolutely do not want to optimize our daily work for these numbers alone. They are useful indicators, but they do not always tell us how much actual value we have brought to the engineers.

When we sit down to prepare a technical article or draft a conference talk, I consistently encourage our engineers to include real-world examples, actual code snippets, detailed architecture diagrams, and hard numbers wherever possible.

It is also incredibly important to talk openly about our mistakes and transparently explain exactly why certain difficult decisions were made along the way.

The exact same principle applies heavily to our internal community efforts. For example, when we organize learning sessions about new AI tools, I heavily prefer live demos where engineers show exactly how they actually use these tools in their daily work, rather than presentations about the theoretical potential of AI.

Of course, not absolutely everything needs to include code. Sometimes, simply sharing an unsuccessful experiment or breaking down a difficult engineering decision can be just as valuable. My job in DevRel is to help our engineers share their experience, not to turn it into a marketing story.

There is a well-known saying that developers are allergic to marketing. I do not entirely agree with that premise. I think developers are not allergic to marketing itself; they just naturally reject marketing that brings them no value.

When we work on technical content at inDrive, I try to keep the final product as close as possible to the engineer's original story. We help with the structure, editing, and distribution, but the technical expertise and opinions should always come from the author.

I also firmly believe that engineers are the absolute best brand ambassadors a technology company could ever ask for. A developer authentically sharing their experience at a local meetup can sometimes do more for your tech brand than an expensive marketing campaign.

Another critically important aspect is choosing the right communication channels. You need to deeply understand where your audience spends their time, what they read, and what specific problems they are interested in solving.

Kathy Sierra argues that the primary goal is to make users awesome, rather than just making the product look awesome. That idea is foundational to how I approach my work.

I see Developer Relations as a specialized way to help our engineers grow professionally. We are here to help them become confident public speakers, to guide them as they publish their first technical articles, and to support them in building their own communities.

The exact same philosophy applies to Developer Experience. It is not about promoting internal tools, but about helping engineers do their complex jobs better and with less friction.

For me, a truly great result is when someone who once needed our DevRel support eventually steps up and starts actively helping other engineers grow alongside them.

Building a thriving engineering culture requires more than just deploying new tools; it requires deep trust, authentic communication, and a relentless focus on solving real developer problems. By prioritizing internal community building and practical knowledge sharing, Grigoriy Volgin and the team at inDrive prove that the most effective Developer Relations strategies start from the inside out.
