The first fall frosts bring a dramatic and abrupt curtain call to flower borders. And for any dahlias that might have been bursting with dinner-plate blooms just days earlier, it can come as a bit of a shock when freezing temperatures leave them limp and shriveled. For new dahlia growers, this sudden collapse can be devastating. Yet while the stems and leaves may be wilting and the flowers may be done, the tubers remain very much alive and capable of producing decades of future blooms.

So take heart, because you can enjoy those gorgeous displays again, as long as you take the right steps now. The days following first frosts are critical if these tubers are to push new growth out next year. These Mexican and Central American natives need care in order to see out the next few months. If these tubers are subjected to hard-frozen subsoil or waterlogged winter mud, they might easily rot and perish.

To keep your favorite cultivars thriving year after year, you need to decide how to overwinter dahlias, either by lifting the tubers for indoor storage or by applying the right protection and insulation in milder regions. The right approach depends on your regional hardiness zone, local soil drainage, and weather. While it might be a little daunting at first, getting your dahlias ready for winter is a relatively simple process. Whether you lift or leave in the ground, here’s how to prime your dahlias for winter.

Timing is key if you want your dahlia tubers to survive winter, and it’s vital to pay heed to your USDA hardiness zone to help your dahlia tubers to survive winter. Never dig your dahlias before the initial frost. That frost sends a biochemical signal through the plant, letting it know that the growing season has ended. In response, the plant channels its remaining energy down into the ground, plumping up the tubers in preparation for dormancy. So allowing the blackened stems to rest untouched for a week or so after this frost ensures the tubers reach peak maturity.

Your overwintering choices will depend on where you live. If you are in zones 6 and colder, it’s best to lift tubers every fall. Winter ground freezes in these latitudes can be lethal for unprotected tubers. Zone 7 gardens are borderline: lifting is still advised, but tubers in sandy, well-draining soil can survive beneath heavy insulation. In mild southern and coastal regions (zones 8-11), many dahlia varieties can remain safely in the ground, provided winter soil drains freely and the tubers have some protection.

You don’t have to remove the tubers right after the first frost. Let the foliage die back naturally. Any green foliage is still collecting energy to store in the tubers. You don’t want to disrupt that process prematurely. However, if you are in a very cold climate where the ground is going to freeze before the foliage has died, don’t wait. Keep an eye on the weather forecast to decide when to dig and lift your tubers. The goal is to let them mature without freezing them. In warmer climates, you have more flexibility.

Before you start, check the soil with a soil meter like the Raintrip 4-in-1 Soil Meter from Amazon. This will help you determine when the ground is nearing that freezing threshold ahead of lifting or mulching. Dahlia type and variety matter, too. Massive dinner plate varieties (such as Café au Lait) produce heavy tuber clumps that require longer drying times, whereas compact varieties (like the Gallery series) form tight root balls that dry quickly. Whichever area and variety you have, these steps will help you to ensure your dahlias return with a bang next summer…

The first step is to cut back stems and foliage. Once the foliage has blackened and you have allowed the root system a week to absorb its final starches, you’re ready. Using sharp bypass pruners, sever the main stalks down to 4-6 inches (10-15cm). If you are after a pair of bypass pruners, the Corona BP Dual Link Bypass Pruners from Amazon are perfect for slicing through the thick stalks in a single pass without crushing vital tissue.

Leaving short, sturdy stems is vital, as it gives you a safe gripping point for lifting the heavy tuber clusters and reduces pressure on delicate tuber necks. You can also use them to attach identifying labels. Just make sure that you cut the stalks just before lifting. Dahlia stalks are hollow, so if you cut stems down and leave them through days of heavy rain, water will pool around the tuber and trigger rot. Cut on a dry morning and plan to lift tubers the same day to keep water from causing problems.

Now, you’re ready to lift your tubers, but do it slowly and carefully. The tuberous roots and the narrow neck that connects them to the crown can break easily. If these necks snap, twist, or crack during digging, the tuber becomes useless. Use a garden fork to dig several inches around the plant. Get under the roots with the fork, inserting the tines 10-12 inches (25-30cm) away from the main stem. Rock the fork handle backward to loosen the soil, working in a full circle around the plant.

Gently lift the clump, using the shortened stems to help, but don’t yank hard on the stalk. Once lifted, gently shake away loose clods of earth. Inspect the roots, and snip away any soft, mushy, or damaged tubers. However, resist dividing the tubers now. Those cuts can leave them vulnerable to rot or infections in winter storage. You can add a little sulfur power such as Bonide Sulfur Plant Fungicide Dust from Walmart to stems to prevent storage rots. Alternatively, dust stem cuts with a little cinnamon.

Your tubers will need a little time to dry before being packed away. Place them upside down on slatted wooden trays, wire screens, or newspaper in a shaded, frost-free, well-ventilated space at 55-65°F (13-18°C). Let the clumps dry for 2-3 days until the skin feels dry and leathery. But don’t leave them so long that the tubers begin to wrinkle or shrivel.

Pack the dry tubers in breathable containers, such as cardboard boxes, wooden apple crates, or plastic storage totes with the lids cracked open. Nestle the tubers in a sterile, moisture-buffering medium, such as dry vermiculite, horticultural perlite, coarse wood shavings, or peat-free coir. You can buy Miracle-Gro Perlite from The Home Depot to help cushion dormant tubers while absorbing excess humidity to prevent winter mold. Make sure individual tubers do not touch.

Move your container into a dark, unheated room, such as a cellar, basement corner, or frost-free garage. Make sure it holds steady at 40-50°F (4-10°C). Stored tubers need a balance of moisture and dryness. Check on the tubers a few times over winter. If they are too moist, repack in drier material. If too dry, give them a light spritz of water.

If you are in zones 8-11 (or a sheltered microclimate in zone 7), you can overwinter your dahlia tubers in the ground. However, success depends on 3 factors: mild winters, excellent winter soil drainage, and insulation. Heavy clay soil that stays saturated in winter will rot tubers in the ground, regardless of ambient air warmth. Wait for the first frost to blacken the leaves, then cut the stalks down to 2-3 inches (5-8cm). Immediately cap the hollow stem ends with small inverted plastic cups, foil wraps, or a dab of horticultural wax to prevent rain from channeling into the crowns.

After cutting back dead stems, spread a generous 4-6-inch (10-15cm) blanket of loose, organic mulch over the surface. Try team favorite Back to the Roots Organic Premium Mulch from Amazon for a lush bark mulch that insulates from freeze-thaw cycles and suppresses weeds. You can also use clean agricultural straw or chopped dry leaves for protection against cold snaps, while helping to maintain healthy soil aeration.

Container-grown dahlias are vulnerable to freeze-thaw shocks. Left on an exposed patio, tubers can turn to ice in hours, while ceramic and terracotta planters are prone to cracking. It’s wise to move the container to a sheltered location for winter. Just be sure the soil will stay dry and the storage area won’t freeze. Once frost kills foliage, cut the stalks back to 4 inches (10cm), cease watering, and allow the potting mix to dry out. In colder regions, move the pot to an unheated garage, shed, or basement where temperatures are 35-45°F (2-7°C). If indoor space is unavailable in borderline zones, cluster containers tightly against a sheltered, south-facing foundation wall.

Wrap the containers in heavy layers of burlap or thermal bubble wrap, and mound loose straw over the top of the soil as a light mulch layer. Sta-Green Burlap Blankets from Lowe’s are a great breathable option for insulating grouped containers against winter winds and chills. Alternatively, you can lift and store tubers, just as with in-ground dahlias. Unpot the root ball, gently tease away the potting mix, and store the bare tubers in crates of vermiculite, just as you would for in-ground tubers.

Just because this season is over, that doesn’t mean you can’t enjoy your dahlias again. A little extra effort now allows you to enjoy these gorgeous blooms year after year. Caring for your dahlias after the first fall frosts doesn’t require special techniques or time-intensive labor. A half hour is all you need to cut back frost-nipped stalks, lever tuber clumps from the earth, and pack them into cozy quarters, or protect them if you leave them where they are. If you do tuck your tubers away this month, you just need to check in on them occasionally over winter Next year, your tubers will be in great shape and fully primed to deliver your most breathtaking blooms yet.
