Archaeologists discovered hundreds of stone hammers at a Spanish copper mine that likely traded its metal with Scandinavia

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Excavations at Las Minillas in Badajoz found 735 grooved stone hammers and 17 radiocarbon dates, and 11 Nordic bronzes fit its copper.

Digging at Las Minillas, a Bronze Age copper mine just south of Granja de Torrehermosa in Badajoz province, Spain, started in 2020 as a joint effort of the University of Seville and Gothenburg’s Maritime Encounters project. Field campaigns in 2020, 2021 and 2023 logged 892 stone tools, and 735 were grooved stone hammers.

Then came the lead isotope results. Slag and tiny copper droplets from the mine gave lead isotope values that fit 11 bronzes from hoards and other sites in Denmark and Sweden.

The paper, by Juan Latorre-Ruiz of Durham University and nine co-authors, went online on October 26, 2026. It calls those bronzes consistent with Las Minillas copper, not proven to come from it. It also says the mine produced less copper than Mitterberg in the Austrian Alps.

The outcrop is about 985 feet (300 meters) long. Claude Domergue put the old workings in his 1987 catalogue of ancient mines and smelters of the Iberian Peninsula. Mark Hunt Ortiz of the University of Seville, now a co-author, published a surface survey of the mine in 2017.

The work began as a forerunner of Maritime Encounters, a University of Gothenburg program led by archaeology professor Johan Ling. In 2021 Sweden’s Riksbankens Jubileumsfond gave that program 47 million Swedish kronor.

Field crews ran magnetometer and ground-penetrating radar surveys and then opened 11 trenches. A Rolatec RL-36-L rotary rig drilled into the old workings, and drill hole S3, near trench 8, brought up charcoal from 27 feet (8.2 meters) down.

Bronze Age miners had cut one long open trench along the vein, 6.5 to 13 feet (2 to 4 meters) wide. It is now packed with rubble, some of it probably dumped during underground mining in the early 20th century.

Trench 12, first called “trench pond” in the supplementary report, is the only place where the dig reached the bottom of the old cut, and no ore was left there.

Burnt and mineralized wood at the base suggests fire-setting, where miners lit fires against the rock so that it would crack.

In trench 3 the team found postholes, fireplaces and the remains of a round hut, with handmade burnished pottery of a style known from southwestern Iberia. The supplementary report reads it as a short-lived hut, probably part of a seasonal mining camp.

Marta Díaz-Guardamino of Durham photographed the hammers. They look like the hammers from Chinflón, the first prehistoric mine studied in Iberia, in the 1970s.

Based on the tools, the dates and the size of the workings, the team calls Las Minillas the largest securely dated Late Bronze Age copper mine excavated in Iberia so far.

Until this project, 11 radiocarbon dates from five sites backed Late Bronze Age copper mining in Iberia. Las Minillas added 17, run on charcoal from strawberry tree, oak, mastic and other plants.

Nine samples came from the mining trench and eight from the hut. They fall between about 1350 and 750 BC, and the authors place copper production at the site from about 1300 to 800 or 700 BC.

Copper mining in Iberia goes back much further. A high cave in the eastern Pyrenees has given evidence of copper mining going back 7,000 years.

The Las Minillas dates line up with a gap elsewhere in Europe. Great Orme in Wales was in decline by 1400 BC, and the Mitterberg mines in Austria followed around 1300 to 1200 BC, according to the paper.

Zofia Stos-Gale of the University of Gothenburg drew up the isotope plots. The team thanks the SGIker isotope facility of the University of the Basque Country for technical support.

Each ore body carries its own mix of lead isotopes, and bronze made from it keeps that mix. The team checked Las Minillas against published data on more than 300 Late Bronze Age bronzes from Scandinavia.

Eleven objects matched in isotopes and in their silver and antimony levels. A rod and a pair of tweezers from Tillinge in Uppland, the oldest of the 11, date to between 1300 and 900 BC.

Two hanging bowls came from Danish hoards, one on Funen and one near Aalborg, and a spearhead came from Orust in western Sweden. A socketed axe and a disc-headed pin came from a scrap hoard of 47 objects in Uppland.

Two twisted neck rings from a hoard at Antvorskov, near Slagelse, date to 700 to 500 BC, after the mine shut. Seven of the objects date to 900 to 700 BC, the same years as the last work at Las Minillas.

Iberian copper has also been traced in bronze figurines from Sardinia and in ingots from the Salcombe metal cargoes off Devon, in southern England.

A 2025 chapter by Hunt Ortiz and colleagues put the mine’s copper at about 217 tons (197 metric tons) as a cautious figure, with a range from 54 to 814 tons (49 to 738 metric tons). Great Orme is thought to have produced 223 to 833 tons (202 to 756 metric tons) between 1600 and 1400 BC.

Mitterberg produced about 25,000 tons (23,000 metric tons) between about 1700 and 1300 BC. Surveys have listed 84 sites in southwestern Iberia with hammers like the Las Minillas ones, and the paper cites more than 100 possible prehistoric mines across southern Iberia.

Las Minillas first came up in an earlier look at how Spanish metal reached Scandinavia, which followed a survey from February 9 to 16, 2026, around Cabeza del Buey, also in Badajoz.

Ling’s group ran it with the University of Seville and the provincial archaeology museum and found six unregistered Bronze Age mines, one with about 80 grooved stone tools. The new paper adds the excavation record and the 11 matching bronzes.

Ling said the discovery of the new mines in Extremadura “represents only the tip of the iceberg,” in a University of Gothenburg release. He put the number of prehistoric mines still undocumented in Extremadura and Andalusia at up to 150.

Trenches 6 and 7 held copper droplets and a partly preserved round structure of stone and mud brick. In 2022 the crew collected all the slag in trench 6, which weighed about 660 pounds (300 kilograms).

Rodents, ploughing and the removal of mine waste for ballast have mixed up the soil there. Five charcoal samples from the area dated to the Roman period or to between AD 1023 and 1158.

A reddened clay fragment from the structure was dated by luminescence to about 700 BC, plus or minus 270 years. The structure could be a pottery kiln rather than a smelting furnace, the supplementary report says.

The paper says the droplets and slag indicate smelting took place, though the round structure may not have been a furnace.

Only six mines in southern Iberia are firmly dated to the Late Bronze Age, so most of the mines behind the regional picture still have no dates.

A full site report will compare Las Minillas metal with bronzes from Britain, Poland, Spain and Portugal. The 735 hammers stay at the center of the case for large-scale mining at Granja de Torrehermosa.

The full study was published in the journal Antiquity.
