A new species of millipede that loves moisture has been discovered living in a surprisingly dry part of South Australia

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

A post-fire survey on Kangaroo Island turned up a millipede first collected in 1936 and left unnamed for 90 years.

In Australia, entomologists had only ever found millipedes from the moisture-loving family Dalodesmidae in the wetter east and southwest. So a male collected in 1936 on a dry stretch of South Australia’s coast didn’t fit.

Ninety years later, that millipede finally has a name, Colobothrix deckeri. It’s the first of its family known from such a dry part of the country.

Robert Mesibov, a millipede specialist in Ulverstone, Tasmania, described the new genus and species after finding fresh specimens on nearby Kangaroo Island.

Until now, scientists had not described any dalodesmid anywhere in a stretch of more than 1,240 miles (2,000 kilometers) between western Victoria and southwest Western Australia.

Most of the southern half of the country inside that gap gets less than 16 inches (400 millimeters) of rain a year.

The entomologist Herbert Womersley collected the millipede on the lower Yorke Peninsula in April 1936, during a little-known scientific expedition based at Marion Bay.

The specimen went into the South Australian Museum as an unidentified millipede.

The millipede stayed unidentified until 2015. That year, the German millipede researcher Peter Decker borrowed many of the museum’s specimens for his Ph.D. work on a different millipede family.

He was then at the Senckenberg Museum of Natural History in Görlitz. Among the loaned specimens were fragments of Womersley’s male.

Decker recognized the male as a dalodesmid and sent microscope images to Mesibov, who specializes in that family. The new species carries Decker’s name for that reason.

A 2012 study used the term shelf life for the wait between collecting a new species and describing it. For Colobothrix deckeri, Mesibov wrote, that wait was 90 years.

In the southern winters of 2016 and 2017, Mesibov searched forest, woodland and scrub within about 12 miles (20 kilometers) of Marion Bay. He didn’t find the millipede, though the trips did produce a different new species.

By then, entomologists had already collected the animal somewhere else. In 2010, arachnologists Michael Rix and Danilo Harms were collecting spiders and other small animals on Kangaroo Island, about 31 miles (50 kilometers) south of Marion Bay.

At one site in the island’s relatively wet northwest corner, they found a new species of assassin spider.

Rix and Harms also collected two male and seven female millipedes there. The animals went to the Western Australian Museum. There Catherine Car, a specialist in millipedes and centipedes, identified them as dalodesmids.

They weren’t matched to the 1936 specimen for years.

Bushfires swept Kangaroo Island in 2020 and burned out the 2010 site. The assassin spider, Zephyrarchea austini, had been described from that single spot, and there were fears the fires had left it close to extinction.

In 2021, arachnologist Jessica Marsh ran a targeted survey for the spider. She found two more on private land about 2.5 miles (4 kilometers) west of the original site, and the small animals she collected along the way included the millipede.

This year, while searching online for dalodesmid records in southern Australia, Mesibov found the Rix and Harms specimens in the Western Australian Museum’s online records.

Curator Mark Harvey sent close-up photos of one male, and its body and reproductive structures matched Womersley’s specimen.

Mesibov then traveled to Kangaroo Island. He couldn’t find the millipede at the 2010 site. He did identify it among the animals Marsh had collected.

“Third time lucky!” said Mesibov. “I found the new millipede alive and well this year at relatively wet sites not far from its 2010 and 2021 localities.”

An adult male Colobothrix deckeri is about 0.75 inches (19 millimeters) long, close to the width of a U.S. penny. Its yellowish-brown body is speckled with reddish brown. The feature that sets it apart is on the legs.

Mature males in this family, and in some other millipedes, carry tiny, ball-shaped structures called sphaerotrichomes. Normally, a long, pointed hair sticks out of each ball. In the new species, that hair is only a short, blunt rod, roughly a quarter the length of the ball.

So Mesibov named the genus Colobothrix, from Greek words meaning stunted hair. What the balls do in this group of millipedes is still unknown, he wrote.

Neither living nor preserved specimens gave off a strong smell. In Mesibov’s view, that suggests any defensive chemical the species makes releases only a little cyanide.

On Kangaroo Island, the millipede is known from five sites inside an area of about 23 square miles (60 square kilometers).

They’re in Flinders Chase National Park, in the Western River Wilderness Area and on private land along Snug Cove Road, and all of them have native woodland.

At the Flinders Chase site, modeled rainfall is about 32 inches (810 millimeters) a year.

Mesibov suggested that the species may once have lived across more of the island’s wetter western half. Farm clearing and frequent bushfires have removed or damaged much of the native woodland over the past two centuries, he wrote.

In the remaining patches near farms, introduced European millipedes now make up nearly all of the millipedes he has seen.

Mesibov’s paper also describes a second new species from the 2010 site, Notonesocambala adoceta. It’s a dark gray millipede up to about 2 inches (50 millimeters) long.

The biggest open question is whether Colobothrix deckeri still lives on the Yorke Peninsula.

Near Marion Bay, modeled rainfall is about 18 inches (450 millimeters) a year, well below the 24 to 31 inches (600 to 800 millimeters) modeled for northwestern Kangaroo Island.

“The 1936 locality is underlain by limestone,” said Mesibov, “and the rock is broken where it is exposed at the ground surface.”

He offered one possibility. “I wonder if the species survives in small voids in this bedrock, as a relict from wetter periods in South Australia’s past,” he said.

“That might explain why I couldn’t find it in 2016 and 2017, and how it could persist in such a dry climate.”

Researchers would have to find the millipede inside the rock to test that idea. Mesibov kept some of the new specimens for future genetic work.

He also wrote that other dalodesmids may still live on the nearby Fleurieu Peninsula.

He pointed to forest remnants in its wetter middle and to deep gullies in Deep Creek Conservation Park.

The full study was published in the journal ZooKeys.
