Family lore of an Indigenous ancestor is common in Quebec, but the DNA from the first French colonists suggests otherwise

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Ancient DNA from 45 people buried in colonial Montreal showed no Indigenous ancestry.

Plenty of families in Quebec pass down the story of an Indigenous ancestor somewhere back in the family tree. History seems to back this. French Canada, in the usual telling, was founded by colonists and Indigenous people who lived and married together.

But researchers have now tested the bones of 45 people from an early Montreal cemetery, and none carried any recent Indigenous or African ancestry.

All 45 descended from French Europeans. The researchers read that as a sign of who was kept apart from the colonial town, not as proof that such marriages never happened.

Diane Martin-Moya, a biological anthropologist at the Université de Montréal and the Université du Québec à Trois-Rivières (UQTR), led the work. Geneticists at McGill University and the University of Copenhagen, where the ancient DNA was extracted, were part of the team.

In an interview with Earth.com, Martin-Moya was careful about how far the result reaches. “Our sample is small and comes from a single cemetery,” she said. “This does not show that such unions never happened.”

The Notre-Dame cemetery opened in 1683 beside Montreal’s first Notre-Dame church, in what is now Old Montreal. It took burials until 1796. The city later grew over it. Archaeologists recovered the human remains during excavations between 2006 and 2012.

The team sampled 60 petrous bones, the dense bone behind the ear, and 33 teeth. The researchers removed samples with too much modern contamination or too little DNA. This left 45 people with enough genetic data to compare to other populations.

Earlier studies of the same skeletons had come to different conclusions. One, based on the shape of the teeth, and another, based on the chemistry of the bones, had suggested that some people in the cemetery were not European.

A third study, which measured the skulls in three dimensions, had found no such sign.

The researchers compared the 45 genomes with 900 from Europe, 751 from West Africa, and 673 from Indigenous groups across the Americas. All 45 grouped with the Europeans.

With the genomes narrowed to 647 from Western Europeans, the comparison placed them closest to people from France.

The researchers then tested whether French and Indigenous ancestry, or French and West African ancestry, mixed to explain any individual. Neither mix fit any of the 45. Every Y chromosome, which passes from father to son, was European as well.

Two people did carry an unusual maternal lineage, passed from mother to child. One was a type most common in Africa, the other a type typical of Indigenous Americans. Both also turn up in Western Europe, in under 1 percent of people.

Since everything else in those two genomes was European, the researchers read them as old traces of gene flow into Europe, long before Montreal existed.

The lore has roots in real policy. When French colonists first arrived, the workforce was mainly male and there were raids by the Haudenosaunee. The crown encouraged colonists to marry women from allied Indigenous nations by offering dowries.

When that failed, the Crown sent roughly 800 French women, known as the King’s Daughters, between 1663 and 1673. From there the population of French colonists grew fast while Indigenous populations kept shrinking, through disease, missions, warfare, and the fur trade.

Montreal also became the largest slave-holding center in the St. Lawrence Valley, and colonial records from 1632 to 1760 list more than 4,000 enslaved people in New France, most of them Indigenous.

Martin-Moya said the absence of Indigenous people in the cemetery fits their growing separation from the colonial town. Land was taken, and missions were moved away from the urban center.

It also fits what genealogists and geneticists have found in living descendants: Indigenous ancestry among French Canadians today is under 1 percent.

During the project, Indigenous stakeholders told the team they were worried about how the results might be used. They warned that genetic data from old bones could become one more “truth” used to undermine Indigenous rights.

The authors wrote that claims of Indigenous identity built on a single distant ancestor have risen in Quebec over the past few decades, sometimes to gain rights tied to land.

Asked by Earth.com what readers should take from the result, Martin-Moya said an Indigenous ancestor establishes at most a genealogical connection in the Western sense, based on blood ties. That holds, she said, whether the ancestor is documented or simply passed down as family lore.

“It does not by itself establish belonging to an Indigenous nation,” she said. “Indigeneity is political, cultural and relational, rooted in kinship, community and relationships with land.”

She pointed to the work of Darryl Leroux, who has documented how people use distant or claimed ancestors to build new Indigenous identities, sometimes to claim rights tied to land.

The ancient DNA also showed who was related to whom. The team found a maternal uncle and his nephew buried near each other, and a grandparent and grandchild on the father’s side. More distant relatives connected them into five extended families.

Martin-Moya told Earth.com the family trees were what surprised her most. “These pedigrees remain hypotheses, but they reveal connections that skeletons alone cannot show,” she said.

Earlier archaeologists suggested that people may have buried French families together. Instead, the relatives turned up in different sectors and burial phases of the cemetery, and new graves cut into older ones as the town grew.

People with money or clerical rank received orderly, single graves inside the church, while everyone else went into overlapping group burials outside. When workers demolished the church, they left those outside in place, and construction workers later disturbed them.

The team also found no sign that the small colony had been marrying within families. No one carried the long stretches of matching DNA that a child inherits when the parents are close relatives.

“Despite the small size of the early colony, this was a genetically diverse population,” she said.

The 45 people are a small share of everyone who lived in colonial Montreal, and they all came from one cemetery. The researchers read most of the genomes at low depth, which is why the family trees stay hypotheses, and the raw genetic data isn’t public yet.

The cemetery can show who was buried in it. It can’t show where the town’s Indigenous and African-descended people were buried, or how they lived.

Martin-Moya said the history of colonial Montreal has long centered on French colonists, while people of African descent have stayed mostly absent from it.

She also said archaeology of human remains in Quebec is now done mostly by private firms ahead of construction, under tight deadlines and budgets.

“As Indigenous communities have long reminded us, the dead are not just data or obstacles,” she said.

Her next step relies less on bones. She is working toward sedimentary ancient DNA, the genetic traces that survive in soil, and on linking it with archives and oral traditions.

“The goal is not only to produce new data, but to find different ways of telling the past, alongside the people whose histories they are,” she said.

For now, these 45 genomes show who was buried in one churchyard, not who was kept out of it.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
