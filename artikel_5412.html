Fake Black Friday deals: where the savings come from framing

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Fake Black Friday deals can look like bargains while saving less than promised, from inflated original prices to add-on warranties, padded bundles and minimum-spend shipping

A discount isn't a price. It's a comparison. A sale tag only means something next to a reference number, and the retailer usually gets to pick that number. It might be a list price, a "compare at" figure or a price the item carried for a few days back in October. Swap the reference and the same checkout total can pass for a small markdown or a steep one.

Black Friday lets retailers play that game at scale. The day itself falls after U.S. Thanksgiving, which is the fourth Thursday of November, but it stopped being a single day years ago. Promotions now run for weeks, online and in stores, and "early Black Friday" sales often start well before the date.

A fake Black Friday deal is one where the savings come from how the price is presented, not from the price itself. Some lean on a reference price the product almost never sold at. Others push scarcity, swap in a cheaper version of a familiar product or pad the offer with add-ons that aren't worth much. A few just move the cost somewhere else: a monthly installment, a protection plan, a shipping minimum.

The Federal Trade Commission has rules against deceptive former-price claims in the U.S. Shoppers don't have to rely on enforcement, though. Price-history trackers, model numbers and the fine print on a promotion usually reveal what a discount is actually worth.

The 10 practices below aren't ranked. Each one is a different way a Black Friday price tag can overpromise, covering price framing, limited stock, lower-spec products, bundles, extended warranties, gift card offers, buy-now-pay-later plans, prices that were already low, last-generation electronics and minimum-spend shipping. For each, we explain how it works, how to spot it and what it can cost you.

Plenty of Black Friday discounts are real, and some are big. The point is to tell the difference between a deal that lowers what you pay and one that just dresses up the same price.

A Black Friday discount is only as real as the original price it is measured against. When the crossed-out figure is set high, or the item rarely sold at it, a large percentage off can describe a modest saving or none at all.

This comparison is known as reference pricing. The struck-through number beside a sale price is the reference point a shopper uses to judge the offer. It might be a list price, a manufacturer's suggested retail price or a regular price from the store's own records.

Those labels do not mean the same thing. The manufacturer's suggested retail price, or MSRP, is a recommendation from the brand. Many products sell below it for much of their time on the market. A discount measured against MSRP can look generous while matching what other stores charge in an ordinary week.

Phrases such as "compare at" and "valued at" work in a similar way. They point to a price at another seller, or to a notional value, rather than a price the shopper would have paid at that store. The tag does not say which seller charged that amount, when it did so or whether anyone bought at it.

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission publishes guidance on former-price comparisons. Its general principle is that an advertised reduction should start from a price the seller genuinely offered. A claim printed on a price tag is still not proof that the original price was real.

Shoppers can check a reference price with price-history tools. Camelcamelcamel and Keepa track prices on Amazon $AMZN listings and display them as charts. A chart shows whether the higher price held for months or appeared only briefly. Both services also offer browser extensions and price-drop alerts.

For items sold outside Amazon, a useful test is the current price of the identical model at several other retailers. If competitors list the same product near the advertised sale price, the percentage off mostly reflects the tag one store chose to display.

The number that decides whether an offer is a bargain is the final price paid, not the size of the markdown. A price below what other sellers charge is a saving whatever percentage appears beside it. A steep percentage off a price few people paid is a framing choice rather than a lower cost.

A doorbuster is a heavily advertised price that a retailer may stock in very small quantities. Its main job is to bring shoppers into the store or onto the site, where they may buy other items at regular prices.

The strategy behind it is the loss leader. A store accepts a thin margin, or even a loss, on one featured product. It expects to make that back on everything else in the basket. A discounted television can draw in a shopper who then picks up a soundbar, cables, a wall mount and a few unrelated gifts. The advertised price does its work whether or not that shopper ever gets the television.

The limits usually sit in the fine print of the circular or the product page. Phrases such as "while supplies last," "limited quantities" and "no rain checks" signal that the store has made no promise to meet demand. Some ads state a minimum number of units per location. A minimum tells shoppers the store will carry at least that many. It says nothing about how many people will be waiting for them.

Timing adds another restriction. Many doorbusters apply only during a set window, such as the first hours after a store opens or a scheduled online release. Once that window closes or the stock runs out, the same shelf may hold a similar product at a higher price.

That substitution is where the real cost shows up. A shopper who planned a trip around one price may buy the nearest alternative rather than leave empty-handed. The alternative may be a different brand, a larger size or a model that was never discounted at all.

Before going after a doorbuster, read the quantity and time wording in the ad. Decide in advance what to buy, if anything, if the item sells out. Look up the regular price of a fallback model at other retailers. That reference makes it easier to judge whether the in-store substitute is a real deal or simply the next product on the shelf.

Some TVs, laptops, and other electronics sold for Black Friday are built specifically for the holiday sales rush. They often resemble a brand's regular models but use cheaper components. The low price can reflect a lesser product rather than a real markdown.

These products are sometimes called derivative models. A manufacturer produces a variant of an existing design for a particular retailer or sales event. The outer shell, the brand name and much of the marketing copy can match the familiar version. The differences tend to sit inside.

In TVs, the cuts can show up in the panel and the connections. A made-for-sale set may have fewer HDMI ports, a lower refresh rate or a dimmer, simpler display panel. It may lack features such as local dimming that appear on similar-looking sets from the same brand. Those gaps matter most to buyers who play video games, watch in bright rooms or plan to connect several devices.

Laptops follow a similar pattern. A holiday version may ship with less memory, a smaller or slower storage drive, a lower-tier processor or a lower-resolution screen. The chassis may use more plastic, and battery capacity can differ. Less memory and storage leave less headroom as software becomes more demanding over the years a laptop is kept.

One common warning sign is an unfamiliar model number. A product that appears at only one retailer, or only during the sales season, may have no regular-price history to compare against. That makes any "was" price hard to judge. It also means reviews of a brand's mainstream model may not describe the product on the shelf.

A shopper can check by copying the exact model number from the listing. The next step is searching for it on the manufacturer's own website and reading the full specification sheet. Comparing that sheet line by line with the brand's standard model in the same size or range shows where the parts differ. A model number that returns no manufacturer page at all is itself useful information.

A made-for-sale product is not automatically a poor buy. A cheaper TV with fewer ports can work in a spare bedroom where only one device is connected. The relevant question is whether the price is low for the product actually in the box, not for the better model it resembles.

A Black Friday bundle saves money only if the shopper would have bought every item in it anyway. When the package includes extras with little use or resale value, the real discount on the main product can shrink to very little.

Retailers and manufacturers build bundles by pairing a headline product with add-ons. A game console might come with a game or a second controller. A camera might ship with a memory card, a bag and a basic tripod. A laptop might include a software subscription, a sleeve or a wireless mouse.

The advertised bundle value is often the sum of each component's individual list price. That figure can look large on a sign or a product page. It does not show what the shopper would actually pay for those items separately, or whether the shopper wants them at all.

Low-cost accessories are common padding. Cases, screen protectors, charging cables and generic earbuds are widely sold at low prices outside of any sale. Counting them at full list price inflates the stated value of the package.

Bundled games and software need a separate look. A console bundle may include a title the buyer has no interest in, or a download code for an older release. A software subscription may cover only a trial period. Some trials renew at full price unless the buyer cancels.

Bundles also make prices harder to compare. A single product has one price that a shopper can check against other stores. A package of four or five items has no direct equivalent elsewhere. That hides the effective price of the main item.

The practical check is to price the core product on its own at several retailers. Then add only the accessories the buyer would have purchased regardless, at their usual selling prices. If that total is close to or below the bundle price, the bundle offers little extra.

Return terms are worth reading before checkout. Policies for bundles vary by retailer. Some stores may require the entire package back, including every opened accessory, rather than accepting a single item.

A bundle can still be a reasonable purchase. When every piece fills a real need, buying them together can save time and sometimes money. The test is whether the savings hold up once the unwanted extras are set aside.

An extended warranty or protection plan sold at checkout often covers problems the shopper is already protected against. Its price can also cancel out much of the discount that made the item look like a bargain.

Most new electronics and appliances come with a manufacturer's warranty. That coverage typically handles defects in materials and workmanship for a set period at no extra cost. A protection plan bought on the same day usually runs alongside it. The early months of paid coverage can duplicate what the manufacturer already provides.

Legal protections add another layer. In the U.S., most states recognize an implied warranty of merchantability, which requires that a product work for its ordinary purpose. Sellers can disclaim it in some states, so the terms of sale matter. In the U.K., the Consumer Rights Act 2015 gives buyers rights to a repair, replacement or refund when goods are faulty. Neither protection requires paying for a separate plan.

Some credit cards also extend the manufacturer's warranty on purchases made with the card. These benefits vary by issuer and by card, and some issuers have reduced or dropped them. A shopper can check the current benefits guide for a specific card before assuming the coverage exists.

The terms of a paid plan set its real value. Many plans exclude accidental damage unless it is listed. They also commonly exclude cosmetic damage, normal wear and misuse. Plans that do cover accidental damage may charge a deductible or service fee for each claim.

The claims process matters as much as the coverage. Some plans require the owner to ship the device away for repair, leaving them without it for a period. Others may replace a failed product with a refurbished unit rather than a new one. Plans are often run by a third-party administrator rather than by the store itself.

Timing is part of the pitch. A protection plan offered at the register arrives when a shopper has already committed to the purchase and has little time to compare options. Some retailers and manufacturers let buyers add coverage for a period after the sale. The decision does not always have to be made at the counter.

The math takes one step. Subtract the cost of the plan from the advertised saving. The remaining figure is the real discount on the purchase.

A spend-and-get gift card promotion does not lower the price a shopper pays at the register. The bonus card has value only if the shopper returns to the same retailer, spends it before any deadline and follows its terms.

These Black Friday offers usually follow a simple pattern. A shopper buys a qualifying item or spends past a set amount, and the store hands over a gift card or store credit. The receipt still shows the full sale price. The reward sits on a card that works only at that chain.

That difference matters when comparing two offers. A direct discount leaves money in a shopper's pocket at checkout. A gift card with the same face value keeps that money inside the retailer's own system. The shopper must make a second purchase to collect any benefit at all.

That second purchase often costs more than the card covers. A gift card rarely matches the exact price of the next item a shopper wants. The shopper pays the difference out of pocket. That extra spending also goes to the same retailer.

Promotional cards can also carry tighter rules than gift cards bought with cash, including earlier expiration dates. Some bonus cards work only during a set redemption window, which may not open until well after the original purchase. Others exclude certain brands, categories or items already on sale.

Returns add another complication. A retailer may reduce the refund on a returned item by the value of the bonus card, or ask for the unused card back. A shopper can check the return policy before buying rather than after.

Any balance left unspent stays with the retailer. The industry term for gift card value that never gets redeemed is breakage. That unredeemed value counts in the store's favor, not the shopper's.

Before picking a gift card offer over a cash discount elsewhere, a shopper can weigh three questions. Would they shop at this store again soon anyway? Can they meet the redemption dates? Does the fine print exclude what they plan to buy next?

Splitting a Black Friday purchase into installments through Klarna, Affirm or Afterpay changes when a shopper pays, not how much. A pay-later plan spreads a cost over time. It is not a discount, and in some cases it raises the total a shopper pays.

Retailers often display installment options right next to the sale price. A product listing or checkout page may show a small per-payment figure in large type. The full price may appear nearby in smaller print. That framing can make an expensive television or laptop look cheaper than it is. The total owed stays the same, or grows.

Many short-term plans split a purchase into a handful of equal payments over several weeks. Some of these charge no interest when every payment arrives on time. Longer plans for larger purchases work more like a conventional loan. Some of them charge interest, and the rate depends on the lender, the plan and the borrower.

Missed payments carry their own costs. Depending on the provider and the plan, a late payment can bring a fee. Some lenders also run credit checks or report payment history to credit bureaus. A shopper should read the specific terms before agreeing, rather than assume every pay-later plan works the same way.

Stacking plans is a separate risk. Each plan can look manageable on its own. Several plans opened at different stores over one shopping weekend can overlap, though. Those payments may come due in the same weeks and draw on the same bank account or card. One provider's app does not show plans held with other lenders, so the shopper has to track the combined total.

Returns add another step. A refund has to move through the lender as well as the retailer. A shopper who sends an item back should confirm with the provider whether scheduled payments pause while the return is processed.

One check cuts through the framing. Add up the total paid under the plan and compare it with the upfront price. If the plan adds fees or interest, the Black Friday discount shrinks by that amount. An installment schedule changes the timing of the payments, not what the item costs.

A Black Friday price is not always a new low. Many advertised Black Friday prices match what the same product cost earlier in the fall. The November tag can simply repeat an earlier offer.

Retailers often attach the Black Friday name to promotions that start early in November. When the same discount carries through to the Friday itself, the date adds a label rather than a lower price. An item at its holiday price in the first week of the month can sit at that figure until the weekend.

Other sales events can set an equal or lower mark. Amazon $AMZN's Prime Day, back-to-school promotions and Labor Day sales in the U.S. all cut prices on electronics, appliances and home goods. A price reached at one of those events can match or beat the Black Friday offer on the same item.

A price-history chart shows whether the Black Friday figure has appeared before. For products sold on Amazon, Camelcamelcamel and Keepa chart how a listing's price has moved over time. If the same figure shows up several times earlier in the year, it is a recurring price, not a one-time cut.

This check answers a different question from whether a store's "was" price is genuine. A product can have an honest original price and still hit its holiday sale price several times a year. The useful comparison is with the lowest recent price, not the list price printed beside the discount.

Store policies can also reduce the pressure to buy on a single day. Some retailers match a competitor's lower price. Some refund the difference if their own price drops within a set period after purchase. Terms differ from store to store, and holiday periods can carry separate rules. The current policy is worth reading before checkout.

Prices can shift again after the Friday. Cyber Monday, the Monday after Thanksgiving, brings another round of online discounts. December sales can bring others.

A repeated price is not a bad price. It does mean the Black Friday date adds little urgency. A shopper who misses it may see the same figure at the next major sale event.

Steep Black Friday markdowns on phones, laptops, tablets and game consoles often apply to models that have already been replaced or are about to be. The price cut reflects the product's age in its release cycle, not a one-off act of generosity from the retailer.

Electronics makers refresh many product lines on a fairly regular schedule. Apple $AAPL has introduced most of its new iPhone lineups at events in September. That leaves the previous year's models on shelves in November, beside newer versions that launched only weeks earlier.

Retailers and carriers have a reason to clear older inventory before the holiday shopping season ends. A Black Friday promotion is one way to move it.

The listing may not spell any of this out. A product page can describe an item as new, which is accurate in the sense that it is unopened and unused. That word says nothing about when the model first went on sale. A shopper who sees a large discount on a laptop may not realize its processor belongs to a chip generation that has since been superseded.

A few checks can establish a model's age. The manufacturer's website usually lists the current lineup, and a model missing from it has likely been discontinued. Product reviews and manufacturers' press pages give the date a model first went on sale.

The trade-offs go beyond raw speed. Phones and tablets depend on software updates for security fixes and new features. Every device eventually stops receiving those updates. An older model reaches that cutoff sooner than a newer one bought on the same day.

Many manufacturers publish update policies for their recent devices, and those pages show how long support is promised. Older hardware may also lack newer ports, wireless standards or camera features that later models added.

A last-generation device can still suit a buyer who wants a capable phone or laptop at a lower price and does not need the newest features. The useful comparison is between the discounted older model and the current one. That means weighing the gap in price against the gap in specs and the years of software support each has left.

A free shipping offer that applies only above a minimum order total can cost more than it saves. If reaching the threshold means adding items a shopper did not plan to buy, the extra spending can exceed the delivery fee it was meant to avoid.

The mechanics are straightforward. A retailer sets an order value at which shipping charges drop away. A shopper whose cart sits just below that line faces a choice. They can pay for delivery, or they can add something to cross the line. The threshold works for the retailer when shoppers take the second path.

The added item is the part to examine. Online stores often show low-cost products near the cart or checkout page, such as socks, phone chargers, batteries or small household goods. These fillers make it easy to close the gap. Whether that counts as a saving depends on whether the shopper needed the item.

A quick comparison settles the question. Set the shipping fee against the price of the filler item. If the filler costs more than the fee and serves no real need, paying for delivery is the cheaper outcome. If the filler was already on the shopping list, crossing the threshold can make sense.

Several alternatives can remove the threshold from the decision. Many large chains let shoppers buy online and pick up in store, generally without a delivery charge. Some retailers sell paid memberships that include free shipping without an order minimum. That membership fee pays off only for shoppers who order often enough to offset it. A retailer's threshold during a holiday promotion may also differ from its usual one.

Returns deserve a look before checkout. Free outbound shipping does not guarantee free return shipping. Some retailers deduct a return shipping fee or a restocking charge from refunds. Those policies can vary by product category. A filler item that ends up being sent back may cost the shopper money twice.

The threshold itself is a pricing tool rather than a discount. It encourages larger orders. Each delivery then carries more merchandise, which spreads the retailer's shipping cost across a bigger sale.
