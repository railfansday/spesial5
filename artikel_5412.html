Will the Anthropic IPO supercharge San Francisco’s already-hot housing market?

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Anthropic’s $1.5T IPO adds a new source of liquidity to San Francisco, where the median single-family price rose 29% year over year to $2.39M and 86% of homes sold above asking. With fewer than 700 listings, additional demand could intensify competition, even as tech shares remain volatile.  AI Summary

On Nov. 19, Anthropic went public on Nasdaq at a valuation of approximately $1.5 trillion, creating perhaps 5000 millionaires, many of whom reside in the city of San Francisco, where the company is based.

The question people keep asking me is: what happens to the real estate market in San Francisco?

San Francisco’s housing market was already the hottest market in the country before Anthropic’s IPO came to town. While the rest of the country wrestled with mortgage rates near multi-decade highs, San Francisco faced bidding wars and intense competition. The median single-family home sale price rose 29% year over year to $2.39 million in October, the largest gain since February 2018. Some 86% of homes sold above asking price, up from 77% a year earlier.

Among 552 U.S. counties with at least 300 single-family home sales from August through October, San Francisco was the only one where prices rose more than 10%. The typical county saw prices rise just 1.9%, 149 counties recorded declines, and San Francisco homes were more than four times as likely as the typical U.S. home to sell above asking.

In past decades, a single or even a series of IPOs have not notably been a driver of the San Francisco housing market. Macro market cycles have mattered more. Notably, San Francisco home prices tend to follow the peaks and valleys of the entire Nasdaq Composite by about 9-12 months. This makes sense since any one IPO might impact the wealth of a few hundred people, where the Nasdaq represents an entire universe of tech related wealth and economic dynamism.

Compass market analyst Janel Evans-Anderson looked at 19 major tech IPOs over the last 20+ years and notes that IPOs from companies like Google, Facebook and Uber might spur some home sales, but much more important are the big trends in tech and the economy.

But the scale of the Anthropic IPO, combined with San Francisco’s current housing market conditions, creates a different set of circumstances from previous technology IPOs.

The economic effects of Anthropic’s IPO may have already hit San Francisco real estate, even before employees can access the full value of their shares.

In the past, the IPO was a starting point for growth and wealth creation. Now the IPO may be the exit. For example, NVIDIA went public in 1999, but the massive wealth created by the company happened nearly 20 years later. Anthropic’s IPO valuation is 100x or 1000x the old thresholds.

Employees of Anthropic and other highly-valued tech firms have access to capital in ways that were not available to tech workers 10 or 20 years ago. In the last decade, a robust secondary market for private company stock has developed. Many executives at hot AI companies (not just Anthropic) have been able to sell their shares into the secondary market for years prior to the IPO.

Equity-rich tech workers can also borrow against their shares. This financial instrument lets people access the cash, pay cash for a house, and not have to pay taxes on the sale of the stock. In decades past, when a big IPO was $1 billion, perhaps one or two execs at the startup would have access to this kind of borrowing. When the IPO is $1 trillion, suddenly hundreds or thousands of employees have access to capital prior to the IPO.

Anthropic’s IPO is introducing an enormous pool of newly liquid wealth into a housing market where prices are already rising rapidly and available homes are scarce. And because Anthropic employees face lockup restrictions, with sales limited initially to 10% of holdings and more shares unlocking through 2027, that wealth could keep reaching the housing market well into 2027.

San Francisco County was already the hottest housing market in the nation before the IPO. Among 552 U.S. counties with at least 300 single-family home sales from August through October, it was the only one where prices rose more than 10%. The typical county saw prices rise just 1.9%, while 149 counties recorded declines. Homes in San Francisco County were over four times more likely to sell for above their asking price than the typical U.S. home.

It’s not just that the IPOs are bigger now. This cash tsunami floods into a housing market that is much smaller than it used to be. There are fewer than 700 single family homes and condos for sale in the city as of the end of November 2026. That’s about 20% fewer than normal for the 2010s and is 40% fewer than in 2012 when Facebook IPO’d.

When new purchasing power enters a market with limited inventory, competition intensifies and pushes prices higher. The imbalance is particularly striking in 2026: we’ve counted over 250 sales in the city this year that closed at least $1 million over asking.

The days since the IPO have been dramatically volatile for Anthropic shares and for the entire market. Valuations of many public tech companies have been hit by market turmoil in recent weeks. It could be that by next spring the AI bubble has popped and much of this wealth has evaporated.

Looking back on the dot-com bubble burst in 2000, that led to a sharp change in the housing market in the city. Annual home price appreciation plummeted from nearly 30% per year at the peak of the bubble to 0%. At the time, a handful of executives that had borrowed against their shares had to sell the home to pay back the loan. If the Anthropic IPO is 1000x bigger than previous events, what does a bubble burst look like this time?
