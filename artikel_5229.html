MSCI Confirms Strategy and Metaplanet Removal at November’s Rebalance

TL;DR: MSCI has confirmed that Strategy and Metaplanet will leave relevant equity indexes after the November 30 close. The change redirects benchmark-tracking funds’ shareholdings; it does not itself require either company to sell Bitcoin.

The uncertainty over whether MSCI would remove two prominent Bitcoin treasury companies has become a dated implementation event. Its November review places Strategy among US standard-index deletions and Metaplanet among Japanese small-cap deletions.

That gives investors a specific question to assess: how will demand for these companies’ shares change when funds following the affected benchmarks rebalance? It is a different question from whether the companies remain able or willing to hold Bitcoin.

MSCI’s standard-index changes name Strategy A in the US deletions column. Its small-cap changes list Metaplanet for removal in Japan. The provider’s review announcement, released November 11, says changes take effect after trading closes on November 30.

Investors therefore have a timetable rather than a consultation proposal. The distinction matters because a possible future exclusion and an announced rebalance can produce different positioning decisions, even if market participants have been preparing for the outcome.

For a fund whose mandate closely follows an affected index, holding an excluded stock may create a mismatch with that mandate. Other shareholders can make different decisions based on valuation and company strategy. That difference is why the rebalance identifies a channel of potential selling pressure without measuring the final market impact.

In its final methodology announcement, MSCI describes a first screen for substantial operating assets, followed by five financial ratios for companies that fail that initial test. Four flagged ratios can make a security ineligible, with timing rules intended to limit unnecessary turnover.

This is an index eligibility judgment. It should not be read as a determination that Bitcoin ownership is unlawful, or as a regulatory order to unwind a treasury. A benchmark provider decides what belongs in a particular investment universe; that decision can still carry significant commercial consequences for an excluded company.

Strategy’s consultation response argues that its Bitcoin activity is an operating business and criticizes MSCI’s approach. The company also says MSCI-linked funds held roughly 13 million of its shares, or 3.1% of basic shares outstanding, at the time of its response.

Those are Strategy’s estimates and arguments, not an independent forecast of the eventual selling. Current holdings, trading ahead of the rebalance and the methods individual funds use will affect how the change reaches the market. A holding estimate should not be presented as a guaranteed dollar outflow.

The potential connection runs through financing. If a treasury company can issue shares on attractive terms, it may use the proceeds to acquire Bitcoin. Less demand for those shares could make that route more expensive or less appealing. The outcome depends on valuation, alternative financing and management’s allocation decisions.

NewsBTC’s coverage of Strategy’s latest Bitcoin purchase and preferred-share repurchases shows why those choices need to be examined together. Raising capital does not mean every dollar automatically becomes a Bitcoin purchase.

The distinction also helps separate this event from redemptions in spot Bitcoin ETFs, which concern funds holding the asset itself. Selling a company’s stock changes its shareholder register; it does not directly transfer coins out of its treasury.

The next useful evidence will be the rebalance’s execution and subsequent financing disclosures. Until then, claims that the deletion guarantees a Bitcoin liquidation, or has no possible economic effect, both reach beyond what the announcement establishes.
