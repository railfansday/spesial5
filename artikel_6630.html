Skyler Gisondo, Such a Nice Young (Leading) Man

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

It’s a wet, quasi-Floridian November afternoon in Carroll Gardens, Brooklyn; the sidewalks are plastered with layers of translucent brown and gold leaves. We’ve just met at a cafe for a bite; our plan is to walk to Brooklyn Bridge Park. But the second we step outside, we’re pelted with rain. Gisondo is dressed more for a sunny Southern California boardwalk than these soggy Brooklyn streets: white vintage-ish T-shirt (“Norwalk Seaport’s Oyster School”), canvas pants, brown boots, and the usual Rolex Submariner on his wrist. No hat to cover his thick auburn hair, no jacket, and, crucially, no umbrella.

We quickly unite in a single mission: to locate and purchase one. “I have a good feeling about Park Natural,” the actor says optimistically, gesturing to the health food store across the way. We splash over to check, and an employee redirects us to a CVS down the street, prompting a nine-block journey under my single umbrella. Gisondo is a supportive partner on the scavenger hunt. He is patient as I lead us the wrong way, carefully holding the umbrella over us both while I look at my phone to course-correct, and takes the lead only with my permission. He matches each of my questions with his own, seemingly determined to maintain the cadence of a conversation, rather than that of an interview. As we walk, the rain gets even heavier, saturating the cuffs of our pants. Gisondo draws a few excited looks of recognition from people on the street, before two passersby attempt to recruit him to their church. “Oh, that’s all right—thank you,” he responds politely.

By the time we finally arrive at the CVS, I’m worried the trek was a mistake—not the type of thing you subject a Hollywood actor to, no matter how easygoing they may seem. But when I offer to buy Gisondo a $20 drugstore umbrella and charge it to GQ, his pale-blue eyes light up and a big smile breaks across his freckled face. “Hoo-ley smokes,” he says, his voice rising in astonishment. “Thank you.”

“Heck yeah,” he continues as I navigate through self-checkout. “You know what’s funny? The real ‘pinch me’ moments are this stuff.” He explains with complete earnestness how he grew up reading GQ. “Being a boy, being a young man, I was like, ‘I want to look like these guys.’ It’s almost like Stranger Than Fiction, where you hear the narration in your head or something.”

When we step outside, it’s stopped raining almost entirely. Still, Gisondo thanks me again. “Spanking brand-new umbrella,” he declares with childlike awe, as if he’s holding the key to the city.

This is how Gisondo moves through the world: present, content, unflappable, well-mannered. An all-around good sport. It’s why, in a 4/20-themed Jimmy John’s campaign transparently angling for virality, he seemed not just willing to play ball but really, truly stoked to be eating a Jimmy John’s sandwich. It’s why the persistent memes that imagine Gisondo as a ’90s kid with a peanut allergy who got “hyper as fuck at recess” feel spiritually, if not always factually, accurate. And it’s those same wholesome qualities—and his ability to wield them in increasingly dark scenarios—that have turned the steady, subtle drumbeat of his acting career into an infectious stomp-clap over the course of a year.

In 2026 thus far Gisondo has appeared as a deadbeat but emotionally literate surfer bro boyfriend in the Apple TV series Brothers, made a charming cameo in a Phoebe Bridgers music video as one of its titular adventure-seeking “Lost Boys,” and received a seven-minute standing ovation at the Venice Film Festival for his absorbing role as a sex offender decoy in Primetime. The day before Thanksgiving he’ll complete his sprint with Focker-In-Law, the latest Meet the Parents sequel, in which Gisondo plays Greg Focker’s well-intentioned but avoidant grown-up son. Next up, he’s got two lead roles in projects tentatively slated for next year: a return to the DC universe of James Gunn’s 2025 Superman, as the unwitting ladies’ man and journalist Jimmy Olsen in the true-crime mockumentary The People vs. Gorilla Grodd, and an A24 comedy called Goblin, where he stars opposite a talking puppet, in a film whose script Gisondo calls “probably the most batshit crazy thing I’ve legitimately ever read.”

Though these projects take place in wildly different worlds, Gisondo’s characters have one thing in common: They’re nice guys in fraught situations. In a cultural moment where dark, morally slippery antiheroes dominate entertainment as well as real life, Gisondo has carved out a lane as a sincere, scrupulous bright spot. “He reminds me of Michael J. Fox in Back to the Future,” says Danny McBride, who cast Gisondo as aspiring stuntman Gideon, the grounded eldest grandchild of the dysfunctional evangelical family empire in The Righteous Gemstones. “There’s something about him that hearkens back to a simpler time and an innocent time, and it makes you root for him.”

For a guy with an IMDB credit page almost as long as the CVS receipt that came with his umbrella, the blitz of red-carpet events, late-night TV spots, and awards season buzz for his role in Primetime has been especially surreal. “For the first time, at 30, I’m engaging with the part of this job I haven’t before, in a heightened way,” he says.

Now, with his first real shot at an Academy Award nomination, People magazine calling him the “internet’s boyfriend,” and a 2027 stacked with high-profile parts, Gisondo appears poised to prove that, even in the darkest of timelines, nice guys don’t necessarily finish last. And since his role in Primetime, he’s never been more locked in. “It lit a fire under me that I didn’t know I had before,” he says. “I heard actors say stuff like that in the past, ‘I want to do stuff that scares me,’ and I never got it. And now, light bulb. That’s it. That’s when you feel alive. That’s the point.”

Up until about age 12, Gisondo wanted to be one of three things when he grew up: an actor, a professional hockey player, and/or a professional basketball player. “I was imagining playing both seasons somehow,” he tells me with a grin at Mister Cheeks, the cafe where we first meet. “I held onto that dream for too long.”

He recalls really “tearing it up” in the 9-to-10-year-old hockey league—until he moved up to the next age bracket, which widened to include older kids, ages 11 to 14. “That’s the first league where full-contact was allowed,” he says. “I was 11 and the size of an eight-year-old, and there were dudes who were shaving.” This mismatch in size and ability culminated in a wake-up call on ice: “All I remember is getting hit, and then the next thing I’m on my back, opening my eyes with my parents over me. The game had stopped and that was the day that dream died,” he says—before adding, with a laugh: “Thankfully, the acting thing kept working out.”

The acting thing included fewer instances where one might be bodychecked by a pubescent teenager, at least in the literal sense. And by that point, Gisondo was already pretty experienced in the craft. Growing up in Palm Beach, Florida, he was “obsessed with movies,” watching West Side Story on repeat until he’d memorized the entire script. Gisondo got it in his head that he could be an actor. His parents, who are process-oriented oceanic engineers, were skeptical. But when a work project brought his family to the Los Angeles area, his mother finally acquiesced and took him to an audition: a hundred-child call for a national Pizza Hut commercial. Gisondo, a precocious blue-eyed cutie pie with bangs and an underbite smile, landed the part. At the time, his dad was convinced his family was the victim of some kind of scam. In actuality, Gisondo had secured his first professional acting gig—and a Screen Actors Guild card—at the tender age of six, a whole 17 years earlier than his character Dan Plum in Primetime. Fans have since dug up and posted evidence of his acting debut, delighting over “TINY GISONDO.”

Thus began a string of small roles—as “Chris,” “Ryan,” or “Boy #1”—on a handful of family-friendly TV sitcoms. Gisondo’s parents relocated to Southern California and began homeschooling him to support his budding career. As Gisondo tells it, his mother specifically forbade any Disney and Nickelodeon auditions, in order to keep him far away from child stardom. “You get one childhood, so I think she wanted to preserve as normal of a childhood as she could for me,” he says. “And I’m really grateful that she did that.”

That freedom to be a kid helped him play one so well that he has since become a folkloric avatar for carefree adolescence—and the subject of one of the modern media landscape’s few truly benevolent memes. Captioning an old photo of Gisondo (from the press cycle for 2015’s Vacation), fans have imagined him enjoying all manner of nostalgic ’90s-kid activities—doing crazy tricks on trampolines, playing with Nerf guns, buying a Guinness Book of World Records at a book fair.

“Some of them I’m like, Oh my God. This is uncanny,” Gisondo says excitedly. “These people tap into something.” Gisondo and his friends did, in fact, shoot Nerf guns at each other “point-blank” during sleepovers. As a kid he was “ecstatic” to be on a trampoline. And he keeps a copy of The Guinness Book of World Records on his coffee table to this day.

“At a certain point, with the memes,” he says, “I feel like they transcended and it became about something else, like it’s not about me anymore. It’s become a vessel for something.”

Nevertheless, these posts have been passed around long enough that even his girlfriend—rumored to be Sterling Point’s Ella Rubin—seems to have internalized them. “There’s one that’s like, I just know his ass has a peanut allergy or something,” he says. “I don’t have a peanut allergy, but I’ve been with my girlfriend three years, okay? We’ll be at dinner. I’ll go to take a bite from her plate and she’ll very seriously go, ‘Oh no, peanuts.’ And then she’ll go, ‘Why do I always think you have a peanut allergy?’ That’s been happening for years.”

To Rachel Brosnahan, who starred alongside Gisondo as Jimmy Olsen’s Daily Planet colleague Lois Lane in Superman, the memes speak to a larger appetite for Gisondo’s wholesome qualities. “It feels so in line with something that we were pleasantly surprised and excited by when the first Superman movie came out, which is that being earnest and caring a lot and loving really hard can also be cool,” she says. “It’s been awesome to watch a world that feels sometimes increasingly cynical and apathetic embrace that. And it’s not surprising to me that Skyler has become the face of that.”

Even if Gisondo managed to have plenty of good old-fashioned fun as a kid, being homeschooled for nine years left him with a lasting identity as an outsider. He’d show up to camp each summer, trying to figure out what kids who’d spent the year in school together were into. “And I remember that feeling of exclusion,” he says; the older he got, the more apparent it was. “I remember being 15 and getting auditions for high school kids, and they were at a house party. And I was like”—here Gisondo shifts into a blissfully clueless impression of 15-year-old him— “‘I don’t know what the fuck this is about.’”

That was around the time Gisondo enrolled in traditional high school, in pursuit of some quintessential experiences. But by then the kind of parts he was booking made even the most prototypical occasions extraordinary. He attended his senior prom—and brought a date he’d secured by filming a prom-posal video on the set of Night at the Museum: The Secret of the Tomb with the help of his costars Ben Stiller and Robin Williams. (Stiller even paid for his limo the night of, as a “wrap gift.”)

Gisondo would chase his dream of a normal life all the way to University of Southern California’s film school, where he pledged a frat for all of three weeks before dropping out. He managed to attend at least one real-life college party before finally accepting his fate as a full-time actor. “It was not my vibe per se,” he says, as we stroll through a dank tree-lined block of brownstones. “It’s like Lord of the Flies.”

You could argue that only tip-toeing past these young-adult rites of passage has helped Gisondo preserve a certain old-world aura—untouched by keg stands, gravity bongs, or double-digit daily screen time. “Something about Skyler reminds me of actors from the 30s or 40s, who had a real personality and a kind of street smarts about them,” Stiller says via email. “I could see him in a movie playing one of the Dead End Kids in the 30s, like these kind of New York street kids.”

Gisondo did manage to collect enough real-life memories to call upon in future standout supporting roles like the rich-kid hypebeast nerd in Booksmart or Cooper Hoffman’s smarmy child-actor nemesis in Licorice Pizza. He now measures his life milestones by the types of roles he’s being called to audition for. “I remember when I got sent Fockers, I think it was my first time being a fiancé, and I was like, Oh my God, I’m a kid,” he says. “And then it hit me like, Wait a minute, most of my friends are engaged. I’m not a kid. I’m not a child bride. This is a completely appropriate age to be doing this.”

Those years as an outsider have made Gisondo especially attuned to social dynamics. He says he senses when someone is quiet in a group, and will be the one to go talk to the person standing alone in the corner of the party. “A lot of my friends and people I respond to are people who are, I don’t know, weirdos in some way, who maybe do have a more difficult time,” he says. ‘It’s funny, there’ll be moments with my girlfriend where I say something. And she’ll be like, ‘Oh, my God, I forget you were homeschooled for nine years.’”

But after turning 30 this past July, he says that he feels more self-assured than ever. “I think you spend a lot of your 20s posturing, trying to find your place, find your lane. And I almost feel like I sprung into consciousness two years ago. At 30, I feel very much like myself for the first time. You know what you like, you know who you are. It becomes almost harder to fake it. You become less interested in faking it. I guess I did always feel that, but there’s an immediacy to that feeling now.”

That self-assuredness has only grown with time, notes Beanie Feldstein, who worked with Gisondo on Booksmart and then again, seven years later, on Focker-In-Law. “Skyler has always been very pure and truthful and rooted and that has never changed, but of course we are older and have lived more life,” she says via email. “He feels like a more settled-in version of the same person I’ve always known.”

That Gisondo has always felt more at home with the out crowd has made him all the more surprised by his current buzzy moment—much of which can be credited to Primetime, and what he calls “the most challenging” role of his life. The propulsive film fictionalizes the lurid early-aughts reality series To Catch a Predator, hosted by former Dateline NBC correspondent Chris Hansen. Gisondo plays one of the decoys Hansen and his team used to lure sex offenders inside a sting house, an impressionable musical theater fan named Dan Plum—a.k.a. “Decoy Dan”—who’s so eager to kickstart his acting career that he’s initially oblivious to the toxic workplace and mission he’s joined.

Unlike Plum, of course, Gisondo was already a known quantity when he auditioned for Primetime, but he desperately wanted the part. As he tells it, Lance Oppenheim offered it to him within the first 10 minutes of their introductory call. “I was used to singing for my supper,” he says. “But he was so certain. And I remember asking him, ‘What have you seen of mine that makes you so certain?’ Oppenheim, who says he “bookmarked” Gisondo when he saw Licorice Pizza, was drawn to what he describes as “this particular combination of sweetness, humor, and slightly awkward, boyish earnestness.” The director also liked that Gisondo had experience as a child actor, but from the perspective of an older, wiser adult. “I was drawn to the idea that Skyler had spent so much of his life navigating the strange machinery of the entertainment industry himself, and what he might bring to a character who was also trying to find his place within that world,” he told me via email.

After he signed on to the film, Gisondo recalls trying to explain the part to his father, the oceanic engineer. “It was the most exciting opportunity of my career by far,” he says. “I remember telling my dad all of these things: So, it’s an A24 movie, starring Robert Pattinson, this guy Lance Oppenheim. And I would play a guy who’s an aspiring actor who’s used as bait to catch pedophiles in the decoy operations. And I’ll never forget, my dad was kind of quiet and he goes, ‘So this is something you’d be excited about?’”

As Dan, Gisondo parades through rented homes in puka shells and tie-dye T-shirts, directing unknowing predators toward a variety of snack foods with a high-pitched, exuberant naivete that manages to both brighten the film’s depraved subject matter and make it feel that much more sinister. (These scenes, too, were GIF’d and memed by the Gisondo hive.) But as we watch Hansen (Robert Pattinson) and his producer Andie Bender (Merritt Wever) plunge deeper into a shameless pursuit of higher ratings, Dan recoils, gradually becoming the closest thing this story has to an ethical center.

“He plays it absolutely honestly, absolutely straight-faced with absolute sincerity,” says Wever, who calls Gisondo’s performance “perfect and undeniable.” “So when there’s comedy to be had—even if it’s uncomfortable or squirmy comedy or cringey comedy or comedy that is uncomfortable because you’re laughing because you can see the heartbreak inside somebody, the true ouch of the vulnerability and the unknowingness—it’s there because he’s not winking at the audience. He’s really inside that part. It has so many colors because he’s playing it so truthfully. That’s a sign of a very smart actor.”

I point out to Gisondo that this isn’t the first time he’s played a character who becomes the sneaky moral compass of the story. He had something similar going as Gideon in The Righteous Gemstones, where he served as a relief to the over-the-top antics of a completely insane evangelical empire. Considered alongside all those memes about his imagined childhood, a single through-line becomes obvious—in the parts he plays, but maybe also on a human level, he seems to remind people that good still exists in the world.

“What’s funny is hearing you say that—it’s my first time bumping up against perception, in a way. Being perceived on that scale,” he says, slowing down to sort through his words for the first time in our otherwise free-flowing conversation, his eyes widening a little. “I think all any of us want is to be seen. I think to be loved is to be seen, in a way. People thinking that about me is the most gratifying possible thing. To me, it communicates I’m doing something right in my life.”

But even as he’s astonished by the public feedback loop about his persona, he also can’t help but feel a little unnerved by it. “Usually, I feel like, only at your funeral, are you meant to see so many people talking about what they think of you.”

By this point Gisondo and I have made our way down through Brooklyn Bridge Park, looking out at the Manhattan skyline from a waterlogged pier and then wandering along a few wooded paths to arrive on the edge of Dumbo, where we’re idling as our conversation winds down. Behind him, the Brooklyn Bridge is enveloped in rain clouds. I remind him that people are saying good things.

“Everybody wants to read nice things about them, whether it’s a review, whether it’s a meme,” he says. “There’s no doubt it feels good. I’m not sure I want to feed the part of me that likes how good that feels. That’s the gremlin in all of us.”

With that, we say our goodbyes and I turn to make my way to the subway. “Thanks for my umbrella,” he says. I have no question he really, truly, earnestly means it. ✦
