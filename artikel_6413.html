All the Gaps We See in Protoplanetary Disks Aren't From Planets

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

When astronomers look out into the Milky Way at very young stars, they find rings in their protoplanetary disks. It's pretty clear that these rings are where planets are forming. They're carving gaps and rings in the protoplanetary disks, the reservoirs of material around stars that planets form from.

ALMA, the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array, has captured images of many of these disks and rings. Those images are part of the effort to understand how planets form, one of the primary questions in contemporary astronomy. But it's not clear if every ring is because of a planet. Protoplanetary disks are complex, and there are many forces at work in them. Some rings could be planets, and some could be caused by other phenomena.

V1094 Sco has a protoplanetary disk that extends out to 300 astronomical units, with multiple ring-gap pairs at great distances from the star.

Keplerian rotation means that objects further from the star orbit slower than those nearer the star.

Young stars with protoplanetary disks are T-Tauri stars, and among that group, V1094 Sco is known for having a giant disk with multiple rings, making it a rarity. It's the subject of a lot of research and observations. It was first recognized for its extended ring with multiple gaps and rings in a 2018 paper. That paper said, "In this work, the nature of V1094 Sco as a giant, multi-ringed protoplanetary disk is identified for the first time, based on data from the Lupus survey of protoplanetary disks." (The Lupus survey was an ALMA survey of disks in the Lupus star-forming complex.)

Ever since then, astronomers have been trying to understand V1094 and its unusual disk. There's not much doubt that still-forming planets are responsible for some of its features, but can they explain them all?

In an interesting twist, the researchers didn't use new ALMA observing time to study V1094 Sco. They relied on existing data and applied a new open-source super-resolution imaging technique called PRIISM (Python module for Radio Interferometry Imaging with Sparse Modeling.) It was built specifically to reconstruct sharper images with existing ALMA data, and replaces a previous technique called CLEAN. PRIISM proved its value in a 2025 study of 78 disks in the Ophiuchus star-forming region, where it found previously undetectable features in disks, only a few hundred thousand years after the stars formed.

Secular gravitational instability intrinsically produces evenly spaced rings, while exoplanets don't.

"V1094 Sco therefore supports a hybrid pathway in which weak turbulence in an extended disk allows secular gravitational instability to assemble long-lived midplane dust concentrations that can cradle planet formation beyond â¼100 au, alongside planet-driven substructures at intermediate radii," the authors write.

These results don't mean that planets won't eventually form in these features that are at great distances from the star. It just means that there are no planets forming there right now. "In this framework, secular GI does not compete with planet formation; rather, it concentrates solids and establishes disk-wide conditions favorable for subsequent planet growth," the authors write in their conclusion.

ALMA doesn't image planets; that was never its job. But if exoplanets are in V1094 Sco's disk, other telescopes might find them.
