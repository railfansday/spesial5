The Numbers Behind Your Stocks' Wild Swings

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

If your stocks feel far jumpier than the market lately, you're not imagining it.

By The Premium Team – Nov 25, 2026 at 11:30AM EST

If your stocks feel far jumpier than the market lately, you're not imagining it.

This past Friday, Nov. 20, Wall Street's best-known fear gauge, the VIX, closed at 35.89. That's high, but it's nowhere near a record. A lesser-known gauge that tracks the stocks inside the S&P 500 (^GSPC -2.60%) told a different story. It closed at 57.91, well above its long-run average of about 32.

On this month's Epic Opportunities podcast, Team Hidden Gems' Tyler Crowe called that gap "off the charts." Then he explained why it's happening.

The VIX tracks expected swings in the S&P 500 as a whole. The second gauge, the CBOE S&P 500 Constituent Volatility Index, or VIXEQ, does the same for the individual stocks in the index.

Single stocks always swing more than the index, so the VIXEQ normally sits above the VIX. But stocks are also moving out of step with each other far more than usual. When one soars and another sinks, those moves cancel out. So the index looks calmer than the stocks inside it.

You've probably seen this in your own portfolio. Tyler pointed out that stocks are jumping 10% to 15% on a single earnings report. And on Monday, Nvidia (NVDA -8.64%), a company worth trillions of dollars, "moved 30%" in a single day, as Tyler put it. "These things don't happen a lot," he said.

Tyler pointed to three forces that, together, make big moves in individual stocks more likely.

Put them together, Tyler said, and you get amplified trades in a shrinking pool of shares. The result is massive daily moves, even in the world's largest companies.

Big daily moves can feel alarming. But a 15% drop after an earnings report, or a 30% jump on a headline, may say more about how stocks trade today than about the business behind them.

That's one reason we suggest owning 50 or more stocks. With a broad mix, many of those swings cancel each other out in your portfolio, just as they do in the index. Keeping each position to a sensible size helps too.

And when a stock you own makes a big move, ask one question before you act: Has anything changed in the business itself, or just the share price?
