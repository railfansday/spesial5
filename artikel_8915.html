⁠Before You Spend on Gravel Suspension, Try Wider Tubeless Tires for a Simpler, Smoother Ride

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Wider tires can add comfort and traction on chunky gravel without the weight of suspension parts.

Just the other day a friend getting into gravel was asking me about suspension parts for his relatively new gravel bike. He was considering different suspension components as a means to gain comfort while he rides—especially on chunkier gravel. He mentioned his frame isn’t compatible with a suspension fork, but was looking at parts like a suspension stem and suspension seatpost. If this sounds like you read this before hitting add to cart on those parts.

When I ran a shop I installed plenty of suspension parts on bikes for people who felt it was the only way they could be more comfortable on the bike. Certain riders fit the bill for a suspension seat post or suspension stem. A few customers had prostate issues and a suspension seat post helped them stay on the bike longer. Others had wrist or hand issues and they benefited from a suspension stem. But even those riders could get additional comfort from a simple change.

After trying all the suspension components I could add to my own bike (as well as trying a full-suspension gravel bike), I have realized that throwing suspension at a gravel bike doesn’t necessarily fix the issue of being more comfortable on the ride. In the shop, customers would ask about installing a suspension fork on a hybrid or gravel bike, but not all frames are even compatible with a suspension fork.

Adding suspension parts to the bike can change the overall feel of the bike, not to mention the weight those parts typically add. When riders are looking for more comfort on their gravel bike the component swap I pick first is simple—wider tires. Here we’ll talk about the benefits of wider tires and why going tubeless gives your ride that extra bit of cush.

Step one of getting more comfortable on your gravel bike is setting up your tires as tubeless if they aren’t already set up that way. Tubeless systems allow you to run your pressure lower compared to running tubes, which in combination with a wider tire offers more comfort. Running tubes with a lower tire pressure leaves you susceptible to pinch flats, but tubeless systems remove the tubes from the equation.

One easy way to check if your tires are set up as tubeless is to loosen the nut at the bottom of your valve and gently press it into the rim. If you press it in and air starts to come out, then tighten the nut back up—congratulations, your tires are set up as tubeless!

If they aren’t tubeless, then it’s time to check compatibility. Check the specs of your bike to see if your rim and tire are tubeless-compatible. If they aren’t you’ll have to explore new wheel and tire options, but if they’re compatible you can convert them to tubeless. Some bikes come stock with tubeless-compatible rims and tires, but don’t come set up tubeless out of the box. Tubeless systems require specific rim tape or rim strips to properly set them up. I’ve seen plenty of bikes come with tubes installed, but with rims taped with tubeless tape. Again, check your individual bike’s specs to see if this includes your bike.

If you need to pull the tubes out of your tubeless-compatible wheels and tires, then you’ll need a few things. First is a good set of tubeless valves. My favorite are Muc-Off Tubeless Valves. They come with a valve core remover tool on the valve cap, come in different lengths depending on your rim depth, and there are a myriad of colors to add a little pop to your bike. You’ll also need tubeless sealant. I’ve tested more sealants than I can remember, but the tried-and-true sealant I go back to is Stan’s Sealant. A bike rarely comes stock with the widest tire that fits in the bike. For example, the Trek Checkpoint SL5 is equipped with 700x42mm, but can fit up to 700x50mm tires when there are no fenders on the bike. Always check the technical specifications for your specific make and model to see how wide you can go. Over the last few years, bike manufacturers have been pushing the envelope to allow for wider and wider tires on bikes. If you have a previous model-year bike that doesn’t mean you can bank on the current model’s tire clearance fitting your bike. Be sure to check the correct generation for your specific make and model. If you go with a tire wider than the maximum width for your bike, you risk damaging the fork and frame.

On my own gravel rig, I recently bumped from 700x40mm tires to 700x45mm tires and noticed the difference immediately. I stuck with the same tire make and model—the Pirelli Cinturato H. The extra five millimeters of width immediately gave more comfort and traction on chunkier gravel and some local singletrack; even with the same tire tread. Five millimeters might not sound like a lot, but it added significant comfort while putting out rides longer than one hundred miles.

Wider tires are the right choice for many—even if you aren’t pushing your mileage into the triple digits. Recently I bumped up the tire width on my dad’s gravel bike. Adding wider tires to his bike (as well as a slightly thicker handlebar tape) leveled up his comfort level while riding. Whether you are taking your gravel bike on singletrack, chunky gravel, hundred-mile rides, or just want a little more comfort on your local lunch loop, go tubeless and bump up your tire width.
