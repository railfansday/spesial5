U.S. companies from Nvidia to Apple face a growing China dilemma

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

BEIJING — How does the U.S. stay ahead of China in the artificial intelligence race? Keep advanced chips out of Beijing's reach, or sell weaker versions to keep Chinese companies reliant on American technology?

The consequences for U.S. businesses came into sharper focus after Jensen Huang's resignation as Nvidia CEO, ahead of U.S. President Donald Trump's meeting with Chinese leader Xi Jinping this week.

Huang, who resigned last week amid reports he'd had an affair with a woman U.S. intelligence suspects of being a Chinese spy, had argued that keeping U.S. technology available in China could help the U.S. stay ahead in the tech race.

Others want tougher restrictions on chip sales: The Trump administration tightened restrictions on Chinese companies' remote access to advanced U.S. chips on Monday, seeking to prevent them from bypassing export controls by using data centers overseas.

The question facing Nvidia is part of a broader dilemma for U.S. tech companies: how far to remain invested in China as tensions between the two countries put their access to customers and supply chains at risk.

On Sunday, nine countries, including Singapore and the U.K., signed the U.S. State Department's "San Francisco Declaration" to safeguard sensitive technologies.

"Companies operating in China face competing pressures from Washington and Beijing," a client brief from New York-based consulting firm Strategy Risks said following the Nvidia scandal.

"Actions that satisfy one government can create serious problems with the other, putting market access, investments, and corporate reputation at risk," the brief said.

"Executives need to understand and price out these trade-offs before making consequential decisions."

China's economic slowdown and escalating bilateral tensions have made it harder for American businesses there. Few expect Trump and XI to significantly expand on a fragile trade truce achieved last fall.

Huang was among the tech CEOs at the Xi-Trump summits in September and May, but U.S. officials said Trump does not plan to raise the alleged Nvidia spy case during Thursday's meeting. Elon Musk is travelling with the president, who is otherwise not bringing a business delegation as he did in May.

"American tech companies have been diversifying away from China," Kyle Chan, a fellow at The Brookings Institution, told CNBC, but Nvidia and Apple are "in some ways stark outliers against this trend."

Analysts are watching how Apple CEO John Ternus, who took over from Tim Cook in October, navigates bilateral tensions.

"Ternus may accelerate a process of diversification away from China that Tim Cook started," Chan said. "But it's hard to imagine that China will not play an essential role in Apple's future under Ternus, given the depth and breadth of China's manufacturing supply chains."

Apple still manufactures most of its iPhones in China, and Greater China, which Apple defines as mainland China, Hong Kong and Taiwan, accounts for around 15% of sales.

"The most defensible path is managed decoupling—not the rolling deferrals and hedged announcements that defined Cook's tenure—but a genuine strategic commitment to building a company that can absorb the reductions of China access without losing the ability to manufacture at the cutting edge," Strategy Risks CEO Isaac Stone Fish wrote in American Affairs earlier this year.

China's economic clout, however, also increases the fallout from any retaliation Beijing decides to enact in response to tighter U.S. policy.

Companies, especially U.S. multinationals, pursued the China market after it began re-opening to foreign businesses in the 1980s. Consumer demand boomed, while local factories produced so much they reached 40% of global container exports this summer. The U.S. AI data center buildout relied on China-made parts.

China has amassed control of the supply chain for critical minerals. Beijing has used its grip on rare earths as leverage in trade talks with the U.S. and the EU.

Despite some buildout of the rare earths supply outside China, "it's just not at the scale that the world needs to continue to build products and for industry to continue to evolve," said Cameron Johnson, a Shanghai-based senior partner at business consultancy Tidalwave Solutions.

He cited looming shortages of yttrium, which would limit the production and repair of airplane engines.

"There's just no road map. That's really one of the biggest issues," Johnson said. "Companies have no idea what they're ordering now, whether they will have access to it again."
