How Two Bored Belgians Broke the Backyard Ultra World Record

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Trail Runner contributing photographer Jacob Zocherman drove 15 hours from his home in Sweden to document the Belgian team at the 2026 Backyard Ultra World Team Championship in Retie, Belgium. Unlike a typical race with a predetermined finish and a predictable narrative arc within it, backyard ultras only end when there is one runner left. Zocherman spent the five days and four hours of what ended up being a world-record-breaking run on the course, sleeping in a camper van when he could. He spoke with managing editor Abby Levene during and shortly after the race.

Ivo Steyaert and Merijn Geerts had been running for 98 hours at the 2026 Backyard Ultra World Team Championship, and they were bored. Never mind they had each run over 400 miles on the same 4.167-mile course. Never mind they hadn’t gotten more than a few minutes of sleep at once in over four days. They looked fresh, the nights were long, and blah blah blah, on-the-ground photographer Jacob Zocherman reports. But with the 100th lap on the horizon, something clicked.

“You could see something happen to them, like a spring flower waiting through winter,” Zocherman tells Trail Runner. “Suddenly the sun comes and nothing happens, nothing happens, and then in a specific moment everything happens and the flower comes out in bloom. That’s what I saw in them—suddenly they were happy to be there, cheering with the audience, laughing with each other and joking.”

It was like colleagues going out to the bar on a Friday afternoon: They’ve found their happy place, Zocherman says. Except in this case, their happy place was a park in Retie, Belgium, aka the middle of nowhere.

“It’s very touching to see, because they are so humble and so easy to like,” Zocherman says. “At that point, I felt like the race would go on forever. But it also feels very fragile—when there are only two runners left, there is no room for error.”

Per backyard ultra rules, runners must run the 4.167-mile lap on the hour, every hour, until only one runner remains and is declared the winner. In case that isn’t nutty enough, the Backyard Ultra World Team Championship is an even nuttier spinoff of this format: Each participating country (of which this year there were 67) simultaneously conducts its own backyard race with 15 participants, and the country with the most cumulative laps wins. This backyard team world championship now alternates every other year with the individual championship, held on the original backyard ultra course in mastermind Gary “Lazarus Lake” Cantrell’s backyard in Bell Buckle, Tennessee.

Team USA would go on to win the team world title, with Mark Dowdle leading the stars and stripes to a collective 1,120 laps, also known as “yards,” for its second world title in six years. Australia took second, 51 yards behind, and Belgium took third, 11 yards off of them. While the team competition inherently shaped the dynamic of the race, with runners sacrificing their own best interests to slow down and pull teammates along, Steyaert and Geerts had several additional goals on their minds.

They’d each run over 100 laps multiple times—four times for Steyaert and six for Geerts. But neither had ever run through a fifth night. Belgium’s fourth-place runner Kenneth Vanthuyne was long gone after hallucinating that he was at work when Steyaert and Geerts put on their headlamps for the fifth time.

“That was the critical moment,” Zocherman says. “It was the one thing they had always wanted to do.”

As the sun rose over the dewy fields surrounding the dirt road of the race course, they both looked unscathed.

“I was like, ‘OK, nothing can stop them,” Zocherman says. They turned their attention to the Belgium national backyard ultra record of 115 laps, held by Jan Vandekerckhove. They checked that off and reset the goal to the world record of 119 laps, set by Australia’s Phil Gore at Dead Cow Gully in Queensland last year. That, too, proved to be seemingly no problem.

At 5:00 p.m. on Thursday, October 22—over five days after they started running—Zocherman heard rumors that Steyaert and Geerts were setting out on their final lap.

“I think they could have continued. They looked fresh, they looked really good,” Zocherman says. “But I also know they wanted to finish together. And the longer you go, the smaller the margins are that something will happen and one will finish over the other. And they wanted to avoid that. But they wanted to finish together.”

Sure enough, when the pair returned to the start/finish arch at 5:57 p.m., they did not go back out when the clock reached the top of the hour. Instead, the two friends, standing side-by-side in matching red shirts, grinned to the crowd of cheering onlookers, each raising a hand and joining them together over their heads in the shape of a heart.

Together, they had accomplished what no one had done before: Run 124 laps of a 4.167-mile course every hour, on the hour, for a total of 516.7 miles and a new backyard ultra individual world record.

“They are like brothers and have run many times as the two remaining runners,” Zochermans says. “So to them, it is not a competition that late in the race. They are doing it together. And they want it to remain like that. It’s kind of beautiful. It’s poetry.”

From the start at 2 p.m. local time on Saturday, October 17, until Steyaert and Geerts concluded their record-breaking run five days and four hours later, Zocherman was on the ground to document it all. It’s a stroke of good fortune that of the 67 backyard ultra team competitions happening around the world, Zocherman—who lives in his native Sweden—chose to go to Belgium. It’s also not a coincidence.

In fact, Zocherman almost didn’t make the 15 hour drive to Retie after his mother was unexpectedly diagnosed with an aggressive form of cancer two weeks before the race. The only reason he did go, Zocherman tells Trail Runner, is because that random campground in a foreign country is like home. Zocherman had been at the same course in April for the Legends Backyard Ultra, and the mini-universe environment of these events bring people together in a special way that’s hard to describe.

“It was an emotional rollercoaster and a logistical battle,” Zocherman says. “I wouldn’t have gone if it wasn’t this backyard ultra. It was a personal reason to be among people I know and like. I didn’t think it would be the crazy result that it was.”

Zocherman made the trek with his dog Balou, who he says keeps him grounded at these events with no predetermined end, or even rhythm. (She’s now witnessed three world records and could not be less impressed.) They did two laps of the course every day and got a decent amount of sleep for the first few nights before squeezing in 20 minutes at a time between loops by the end.

“I also know it will be a very long race and I have to drive home for 15 hours after so I can’t be crazy wasted,” he says. “Although you become crazy wasted anyway.”
