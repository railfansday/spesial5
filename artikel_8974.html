Large Glass Openings at Work in a Production Facility

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Integrating truck access into a glazed facade requires coordinating panel dimensions, opening mechanisms, and the space needed for vehicles to maneuver. These decisions affect both the composition of the facade and how materials and products move in and out of the building. Glass thus becomes part of the daily workflow: when closed, it encloses the space and admits natural light; when open, it allows loading and unloading to take place.

At Vitrocsa's new headquarters in Yverdon-les-Bains, Switzerland, two guillotine window systems, each six meters wide and nine meters tall, allow trucks to enter the warehouse. Together with a six-meter-tall pivot door, they incorporate the company's minimalist window systems into the daily operation of its own facilities.

The inauguration of Vitrocsa's new headquarters marks the arrival of its production, administration, and storage teams under one roof. The building replaces the company's former premises in Saint-Aubin-Sauges, which no longer met its manufacturing and logistics needs, and it was completed after 18 months of construction at the Y-Parc technology park in Yverdon-les-Bains. This location was chosen for its proximity to most employees and the opportunity to consolidate activities on a single site, making design, production, and delivery easier to coordinate.

Incorporated into the facade and interior spaces, these systems turn the building into a working showroom, where visitors can experience their operation in everyday use. The building's infrastructure includes geothermal energy piles and rooftop photovoltaic panels.

At Vitrocsa's warehouse, each of the two large openings consists of four glass panels. The guillotine mechanism moves the panels vertically, allowing trucks to enter without a door leaf swinging into the circulation area. This brings loading and unloading operations closer to the storage areas while incorporating vehicle access into the glazed facade. When closed, the windows admit natural light into the warehouse.

Integrating windows and doors at this scale requires coordination among the architect, structural engineer, and manufacturer. The weight of the components, their supports, the mechanisms that move them, and access for maintenance must be considered alongside circulation and the arrangement of interior spaces. At the headquarters, these decisions can be seen in use: the warehouse openings accommodate the movement of trucks and goods, while the pivot door brings visitors into a building that demonstrates its systems through everyday operation.
