Even slightly warmer summers are linked to a higher death risk for people with heart disease

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

A UK study links extreme heat and repeated heatwaves to higher death risks among people with heart disease.

An average summer temperature of about 62°F (16.5°C) sounds mild. But even slightly warmer summers were linked to a higher risk of death among people with cardiovascular disease.

In a large UK study, researchers found that for every 1.8°F (1°C) increase in average summer temperature, patients had a 15% higher risk of dying from any cause.

The risk was also higher among patients who experienced more heat waves. The odds of death rose on the hottest days and after sharp jumps in temperature from one day to the next.

Taken together, the findings indicate that heat may influence survival across entire seasons in heart patients. An influence during brief hot spells is suggested as well.

The research was conducted by Ka Yan Lai of the University of Hong Kong alongside colleagues there. Further collaborators were based at the University of Oxford.

Using health records, the investigators followed nearly 64,000 UK patients for periods of up to 16 years.

Lai said the health effects of extreme heat have “rarely been studied among people with pre-existing conditions.”

All of the patients had been enrolled in UK Biobank, a long-running British health project. Half a million volunteers aged between 37 and 73 were recruited to it from 2006 to 2010.

From this pool, 63,860 volunteers were selected by the team for analysis.

All received their first hospital diagnosis of cardiovascular disease between 2006 and 2022.

Cardiovascular disease, or CVD, includes conditions that affect the heart and blood vessels.

Four of those disorders were examined on their own as well. Atrial fibrillation, an irregular heartbeat, was the first of them.

The second was ischemic heart disease, in which narrowed arteries can’t deliver enough blood to the heart. Heart failure and stroke made up the remaining two.

At diagnosis, the patients had reached an average age of about 69 years. Deaths from any cause had been recorded for 12,237 of them by the end of 2022.

That figure corresponds to roughly one patient in five.

Temperature data were taken from the daily records kept by the Met Office. Those records are mapped onto squares measuring about 0.6 miles (1 kilometer) across.

For every summer, defined as June through August, each home address was matched to its square.

Considerable variation in average summer temperature was evident across that map. The coldest squares averaged about 43°F (6.1°C), whereas the warmest reached about 66°F (18.7°C).

The central result relates to the average summer temperature at each patient’s place of residence. A 15% higher risk of death was associated with every additional 1.8°F (1°C).

Several other influences had been accounted for before that estimate was produced. Among them were age, sex, smoking, alcohol use, diabetes and neighborhood deprivation.

For patients with ischemic heart disease, each added degree Celsius corresponded to a 19% higher risk. A smaller increase of 10% was found among those with atrial fibrillation.

The results for heart failure and stroke were less certain. In both groups, the link with average summer temperature was too weak to exclude chance.

Adults aged 65 and older in New England had been examined in an earlier US study.

A 1% increase in deaths was reported there for each 1.8°F (1°C) rise in long-term average summer temperature. Older adults in general were covered by that work, not only people with heart disease.

Heat waves were also counted by the researchers. A heat wave was defined as two or more consecutive days hotter than 90% of local summer days.

Exposure to four or more heat waves a year was experienced by some patients. Their risk of death exceeded by 24% that of patients exposed to two or fewer.

Among patients with atrial fibrillation, the corresponding increase in risk was 29%. It stood at 28% among those with heart failure.

A second analysis was restricted to patients whose deaths occurred during a summer.

For each such patient, temperatures on the day of death and the five preceding days were examined. These were then compared with other days in the same month that fell on the same weekday.

Because every patient served as their own comparison, fixed traits can’t account for the results. Sex and schooling are examples of such traits.

On the hottest 1% of summer days, the odds of death among the patients as a group were 70% higher. The reference point was the temperature at which deaths were lowest.

Temperature changes from one day to the next were measured as well. The largest one-day rises were taken to be those in the top 1% of such changes.

After rises of that size, the odds of death were roughly double those seen when temperature held constant.

Roughly threefold odds were found in patients with ischemic heart disease or heart failure.

Considerable uncertainty surrounds the estimates for sharp rises. In heart failure, the plausible range extended from 36% higher odds to more than seven times as high.

No clear link with deaths was detected for sudden drops in temperature.

Events inside the patients’ bodies were not measured at any point in the study. A plausible explanation was instead drawn by the authors from earlier research on extreme heat.

In hot weather, warm blood is pumped toward the skin so that heat can be shed by the body. As much as 5 gallons (20 liters) of blood may be pumped by the heart each minute.

Sweating can meanwhile strip water and salt from the body. Blood that holds less water becomes thicker, and thicker blood may clot more readily.

In a person with heart disease, the extra pumping may require more oxygen than the heart can obtain.

Among patients lacking a college or university degree, warmer summers were tied more strongly to death. A weaker link was seen in patients who held such a degree.

A similar contrast emerged between neighborhoods with moderate green space and the greenest neighborhoods, where the link was weaker.

Among heart failure patients, the link was stronger in the most deprived neighborhoods.

Several possible reasons were put forward by the authors. Deprived neighborhoods tend to be overcrowded and to suffer more air pollution and traffic noise, they wrote.

Physically demanding work in extreme weather is also more common among people with less money.

Green spaces, meanwhile, have been shown in earlier studies to cool their surroundings.

According to Lai, protection will have to be delivered at several levels at once. City planning was named as one of those levels.

Cooling strategies form another, as do personal decisions about where to live. Lai said such protection is needed most by the most vulnerable groups.

Extreme heat is projected to grow more frequent and more intense as populations age. That observation came from study co-author Chinmoy Sarkar of the University of Hong Kong.

Together, he said, those trends will make protecting frail people with chronic diseases a top public health priority.

A link between heat and death can be demonstrated by a study of this kind. Proof that heat caused the deaths lies beyond its reach.

Because temperatures were assigned by home address, the patients’ actual whereabouts each day remain unrecorded. Nor was any information available on air-conditioning use or on changes in medication.

Smoking and drinking habits were recorded only once, when volunteers joined UK Biobank.

Those habits may well have changed afterward. Since cases were identified through hospital admissions, milder disease that never led to admission was missed.

UK Biobank volunteers may also differ from the wider population. About 96% of the patients in this study were white. The authors wrote that the volunteers may not represent everyone in the UK with cardiovascular disease.

None of the patients was given a cooler home or a greener street during the study. How many deaths such changes might prevent therefore can’t be read from the records.

Air-conditioning use could be accounted for in future research, as could travel to places with different temperatures.

The full study was published in the journal PLOS Climate.
