Giant crane bones reveal a surprising case of mistaken identity

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Ancient DNA suggests a giant crane thought extinct for 150 years was a common crane, a bird still living in Europe that used to be bigger.

For more than 150 years, unusually large crane bones found across Europe were often credited to an extinct giant called Grus primigenia.

Ancient DNA from a sample of those bones now points to the common crane, a bird that still lives in Europe and used to grow bigger than it does today.

So the giant may never have been a species of its own.

John Stewart, a professor of evolutionary paleoecology at Bournemouth University in England, led the work.

His team read DNA from 18 crane bones found in England, Wales, France, Germany and Spain’s Balearic Islands.

The original bone used to describe the proposed species wasn’t tested.

Alphonse Milne-Edwards, a French biologist, described the crane in the 19th century.

His key specimen was the lower end of a leg bone from a cave at Les Eyzies, in southern France. It was wider from side to side than the same bone in a sarus crane, a large crane from Asia.

Experts argued about the bones for more than a century. Some accepted the giant as a real, extinct bird.

Others thought the bones belonged to sarus cranes or Siberian white cranes, both still living in Asia, or to extra-large common cranes.

Among the extinct prehistoric birds of Europe and northern Asia, only the great auk has drawn more debate, the authors wrote.

“Previous studies had merely used the size and shape of the bones to attempt to understand these large birds. The use of DNA analysis has given us a more accurate picture of what they represent,” Stewart said.

The DNA work was done at the Centre for Palaeogenetics in Stockholm, Sweden. Researchers drilled about 0.002 ounces (50 milligrams) of powder from each bone.

They analyzed mitochondrial DNA, which is passed down from mothers, and compared it with sequences from living cranes.

All 18 bones matched the common crane, oversized ones included. The sarus crane and the Siberian white crane turned out to be distant relatives, which ruled both of them out.

Some researchers had wondered whether Britain once had its own large type of common crane, but the samples gave no sign of such a crane.

The British birds’ DNA was mixed in with that of birds from mainland Europe, and the team has posted the raw data in the European Nucleotide Archive, a public database.

“We suspected that these large cranes were not extinct and instead represented larger, modern European common cranes,” Stewart said.

“We believe that the reason for the mistaken identity was that cranes have large males and smaller females and we think this contributed to previous speculation that there were two different species – the living, common crane we see today and the extinct, larger species.”

In birds, males carry two copies of a sex chromosome called Z, while females carry one Z and one W.

Relative to DNA from other chromosomes, females have about half as much Z chromosome DNA as males.

The team used this difference to identify the sex of most specimens. Nearly every bone from a male was as big as a modern male’s or bigger.

The female bones mostly fell between today’s female and male sizes, and none had measurements that matched only modern females. Female cranes in the past were larger, too.

One thigh bone was found at Le Morin, a rock shelter in southwestern France.

In both measurements the team could take, it exceeded the modern common crane bones used for comparison. DNA analysis showed that it belonged to a female.

Earlier work on the bone found cut marks and a type of tissue suggesting people butchered her in the breeding season.

The youngest large cranes the team knows of come from Flixborough, an Anglo-Saxon settlement in northern England.

People lived there from the late 600s to the early 1000s, and excavations uncovered 229 crane remains.

Common cranes got smaller after that, within roughly the last thousand years.

That timing is unusual: size changes in other animals more often date to the end of the last ice age.

The mute swan also appears to have shrunk over the past 11,700 years, the authors noted.

A 2023 study estimated that roughly 1,300 to 1,500 bird species have been lost since the late ice age.

By that estimate, more than half of those bird extinctions haven’t been discovered. Stewart’s team wrote that such claims need careful studies of single species like this one.

Many extinct birds from Europe and northern Asia were named mostly from size and shape, and confirming them may take the same kind of ancient DNA work.

“While it is true that many species of birds are vulnerable to human actions today, it may be that we have overestimated the number of extinctions in the past. Some of these may instead represent size changes to living species,” he said.

Milne-Edwards’ original bone, kept at the Muséum national d’Histoire naturelle in Paris, wasn’t sampled because testing would damage it.

The French cave bones in the study come from the same late ice age period, about 15,000 to 17,500 years ago. So the authors expect the Paris bone to be a common crane too, though a small doubt remains until it’s tested.

A bigger open question is why the cranes shrank.

The authors listed climate change, wetland drainage and other changes in how people use land as possible causes, but called each one speculation for now.

“The challenge going forwards is to investigate the reason for the cranes’ decrease in size,” Stewart said.

“Wetland drainage and human land use changes are what we suspect, but more work is needed to resolve that.”

The full study was published in the journal Royal Society Open Science.
