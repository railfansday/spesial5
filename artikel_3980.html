I'm not saying Sleeping Dogs is the world's best life of crime sim, but no other game has replicated the importance of pork buns to whole manhood as deftly

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

For absolutely no reason whatsoever, I’ve recently fired Sleeping Dogs up again. The United Front Games-developed, Square Enix-published open-world crime romp is still as good as it’s always been, and still occupies its own corner in the dingy bar where the GTAs and GTA-likes hang out. Back in 2015, RPS co-founderman Alec Meer (RPS in peace) referred to it as “the halfway house between GTA's sneering arrogance and Saints Row's chaotic nonchalance”. Fair enough, I’ve never been that big a Saints Row guy, but I see slumbering canines a bit differently.

Tonally and in terms of setting, Sleeping Dogs feels a lot closer to GTA 4’s gritty urban gangland drama than Rockstar’s follow-up did. GTA 5 is an exotic and sun-kissed heist movie, a mould that GTA 6’s tale of gun-toting love in Vice City very much looks to follow. That leaves Sleeping Dogs protagonist Wei Shen standing next to Niko Bellic. One might be kicking faces in Hong Kong and the other might be getting into shootouts in New York, but both are fresh off the boat in places both familiar and alien. They find themselves caught between two lives.

For Wei, it’s a balancing act between his day-to-day life surviving among the triads, and keeping his hands clean while securing evidence against them as an undercover cop. For Niko, it’s a balancing act between trying to settle old scores and start a fresh life in post 9/11 America. One can stop at a street vendor to buy a pork bun to chow down on. One can stop at a street vendor to buy a hotdog to chow down on. Both of their games are beloved for delivering tales which get the player invested in Wei and Niko specifically, that excel in putting you into the emotional shoes of these two underworld operators whose worlds are tearing at them from multiple angles as they claw their way to the top of the food chain.

Despite my headline gag, they do this with a comparably minimal amount of in-depth life-simmy details, like those that’re baked into Red Dead Redemption 2 (and GTA 6, to a degree) as some of the main selling points. Wei can buy a pork bun, the vendor loudly informs him that by doing so he’s avoiding the possibility of never becoming a whole man, but there’s no ‘whole manliness’ meter which demands to be satiated with regular pork bun munching, lest Wei become a less than whole man. All it does is give him a temporary health regeneration boost, because as we all know, the first thing the doctors do if you’re admitted to hospital with grievous wounds after a fight is serve you a scrumptious meal that miraculously closes up your cuts in minutes.

Sleeping Dogs dedicates all of its life simming effort to making you feel the weight of Wei’s torment as he grapples with an unsustainable double life of sinning and saintly acts. Like GTA 4, it’s not got the time, tech, or budget to adorn itself with animations or systems that replicate every aspect of Wei’s day-to-day existence in excruciating detail. It’s simply assumed, rather than demonstrated, that some of the back alleys he wanders down to bust drug dealers probably smell a bit unpleasant. He doesn’t have over 60 species of bird to catalogue as he commutes from North Point to the businessman-infested Central district, then on to the houseboat-heavy Aberdeen and gated communities of Kennedy Town. Though, he does keep nicking jade statues, in order to persuade his kung fu instructor to teach him some fun new ways to knee baddies into submission.

He can sing his lungs out to nail I Fought the Law at one of Hong Kong’s few karaoke booths or lose all of his cash playing mahjong, but he doesn’t have an endless laundry list of optional hobbies long enough to suck players who aren’t even particularly trying to get distracted in for hundreds of hours.

Then again, if he wanders into his Central apartment, Wei can take a piss. In truth, I’d forgotten about this prior to my recent revisit, but it is a wonderfully simple feature that really emphasises how things’ve changed for games like these in the years since 2012. I press one button when the prompt pops up near the loo, and Wei unzips before dribbling a rather loud trickle into the bowl. Then, he’s done. I can get him to wash his hands if I wish, but no bladder meter has gone down and no dirtiness meter is being satiated by my scrubbing. If Wei doesn’t take regular whizzes, no NPCs comment that he’s looking a bit pained, and his walking animation doesn’t dynamically adjust to huddle his legs closer together.

You could ask what was even the point of animating Wei’s pissing if it’s a pointless and totally optional activity, outside of arguably doing a tiny bit of work in helping Shen be convincingly human (and it unquestionably being quite funny the first time you stumble across it). By looping what would once have been one-note button presses into systems that help them play a more active role in fleshing out the world and the routines of its inhabitants, uber-detailed games like GTA 6 - at their best - add an extra layer of definition to the tales and adventures they depict.

They often feel like the sort of game Sleeping Dogs might’ve been if it’d had their tech and its budget was the GDP of real world Hong Kong. The question is, when limitations like that don’t apply to the degree they once did, where the line lies between extra layers which tangibly deepen the player’s experience and overindulgent bloat?

Sleeping Dogs, despite working in its own takes on elements of GTA, Saints Row, and I’d argue RGG Studio’s earlier Like A Dragon/Yakuza entries, is an unquestionably lean and focused game. Less isn’t necessarily more, especially if you were to ask slumbering canines-loving teenage me, who would’ve bitten your hand off for Hong Kong to boast even a third of the extra features and activities GTA 6 and Red Dead 2 pack into their life of crime sims.

The latter two are games that wouldn’t have been possible to make, at least in a format the masses would be able to play, back in the era when Sleeping Dogs and GTA 4 represented what an open-world crime game typically looked like. They’ve eaten up what used to be the domain of arcade racers like Midnight Club and hunting sims to step closer to being something reminiscent of Better Than Life, the addictive VR everything game from the Red Dwarfverse, which takes over players’ lives by simulating a world where everything they want can play out in reality-mimicking detail.

They aim to be far, far more than what defined their predecessors, and to use that to appeal to an ever-expanding audience. What I’m intrigued to see, whenever I get my hands on Rockstar’s latest work, is whether those like me who come to it having recently played a game like Sleeping Dogs will find a game that feels like it’s being pulled in a million different directions, to the point that it loses something of what historically has been at the centre of its fundamental appeal.

Or, to put it another way: is there a point at which you’ve eaten so many pork buns in one sitting that you begin to stray away from the self-actualisation which steady consumption of them is known to facilitate?
