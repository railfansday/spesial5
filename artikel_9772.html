When Does Semaglutide Form Fibrils? What Circular Dichroism Can Tell You

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

In one solution, semaglutide formed long, twisted fibers. In another, the same peptide formed small, round clusters called micelles. What caught my attention in Valeria Castelletto and colleagues’ semaglutide study was that you could see a change in the acidic sample before you ever looked at a microscope image. If I saw that change in a formulation screen, which sample would I send for imaging, and when?

Semaglutide was back in the news on November 14, when Novo Nordisk announced its REDEFINE 9 results for CagriSema, its investigational combination of cagrilintide and semaglutide. The fibril finding comes from a separate March 31 paper. That team studied research-grade semaglutide in water. Its conclusions apply to those preparations, and the stability of the trial formulation and marketed products was outside the scope of the work.

I’d start with Figure 2b. The researchers used an Applied Photophysics Chirascan to measure far-ultraviolet circular dichroism (CD). The shape of a CD spectrum tells you about the way the peptide backbone folds, averaged across the molecules in the sample.

Look at the fresh semaglutide trifluoroacetate solution at 1% by weight and pH 2.4. Its spectrum had two dips, one at 208 nm and another at 222 nm, characteristic of an alpha helix. After 40 days at 4 °C, the curve had one broad dip near 216 nm, consistent with a shift toward beta-sheet structure. That answers one part of the timing question. The peptide had changed by day 40. To find out how early that change was detectable, I’d want the samples from the weeks in between.

One detail I’d borrow from their experimental setup is the choice of quartz cells with light paths of 0.01 or 0.1 mm. A shorter light path lets more far-UV light through a concentrated solution. That matters here because semaglutide’s assembly depends on concentration. Diluting a sample to get a cleaner spectrum could change the very thing you’re trying to measure.

Take a look at Figure 1c and the close-up in Figure 1d. The cryogenic transmission electron microscopy images show long, narrow fibers in the acidic sample. In the close-up, you can see the twist. Small-angle X-ray scattering gave the team a second check on shape across the solution. A long-cylinder model fit the acidic sample, while the data for the base form of semaglutide at pH 8 supported micelles.

There’s a reason I’d keep imaging in the plan even if two spectra look similar. In an earlier semaglutide study, the aged sample still had a helical CD signature, yet the researchers found a small population of short fibrils alongside the micelles. CD is good at showing when the overall structure has shifted. If a small population of fibers would change your formulation decision, it’s worth looking for those fibers directly.

A study of the GLP-1-like peptide G48 took that next step. The researchers tested whether adding polysorbate 80 would help G48 keep its structure under conditions that caused it to aggregate. Their Chirascan Plus spectra showed that G48 retained its helical shape with the additive at all three pH values tested.

The result I’d want beside those spectra is the 72-hour comparison in Figure 7. Without a stabilizer, the team found no soluble monomer left at any of the three pH values. With polysorbate 80, they measured little to no loss of soluble monomer over the same period. That gives a formulation scientist two reasons to advance that condition for G48. The peptide kept its helical structure, and it stayed in solution as individual molecules.

Suppose two formulations start with the same spectrum, but only one develops a broad dip near 216 nm during storage. I’d repeat that comparison with independently prepared samples, the same peptide concentration and a matching blank for each formulation. I’d also check for cloudiness because light scattered by particles can distort a CD spectrum.

This is where I see a place for Chirascan in a formulation lab. Three formulations, four storage time points and two independent preparations already give you 24 samples, each with a buffer to account for. For a panel that size, Applied Photophysics reports that its automated Chirascan Q100 can measure 48 buffer–sample pairs in 24 hours. That makes it practical to keep the intermediate time points that a fresh-versus-aged comparison misses.

I’d use Chirascan to choose a pair of samples for the microscope: the first time point with a reproducible change and the one immediately before it. I’d include the matched formulation that held its original spectrum. Seeing fibers at day 40 tells you where the sample ended up. Knowing whether they were already present when the CD curves first diverged tells you where to focus the next round of formulation work.
