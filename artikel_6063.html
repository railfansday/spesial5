Restored to Calder’s plan, the clouds are spinning again

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Sen. Alex Padilla, D-Calif., speaks during a ceremony marking the restoration of Alexander Calder’s Mountains and Clouds sculpture in the Hart Senate Office Building atrium on Wednesday. (Bill Clark/CQ Roll Call)

The day after Alexander Calder presented his plan for a public art display in the Hart Building’s nine-story atrium to the Architect of the Capitol, he died.

Fifty years later, Alexander S.C. Rower felt a sense of awe as he stared up at his grandfather’s work, finally restored to what he had envisioned.

“It’s totally phenomenal,” he said, pointing up at the now-moving clouds as he spoke, spinning his finger around. “It’s not entertainment, but it’s trying to emulate naturalness, trying to emulate what nature would do.”

It was a long time coming for Rower, who remembers traveling to the Capitol in his 20s for the original dedication of the Mountains and Clouds sculpture in 1987. The mobile portion stopped rotating as Calder intended in the 1990s, and it was ultimately removed in 2014 after being deemed unsafe by the Architect of the Capitol. Millions of dollars in private philanthropy later, Calder’s clouds are spinning again.

Rower was back on the Hill on Wednesday for an event celebrating the restoration, joining Architect of the Capitol Thomas Austin, Sens. Alex Padilla, D-Calif., Angus King, I-Maine, and James Lankford, R-Okla., and the project’s donors. As the speakers addressed the crowd, the clouds began to speed up slightly. Onlookers gathered on the balconies overlooking the atrium and peeked through office windows. Attendees left with tote bags emblazoned with images of Calder’s art.

According to Rower, who leads the Calder Foundation dedicated to preserving and interpreting his grandfather’s art, the mobile is rotating on a 15-hour cycle that is “totally variable.”

“It can go a little faster; it can go a little slower,” he said. “I told the team today that during lunchtime, I want it to speed up just a little bit because everyone’s going to lunch. There’ll be … a lot of activity passing through, and then right at 2:30, well, let’s make it go quieter.”

It was a labor of love for those involved, and not an inexpensive one. Jon and Kim Shirley funded the $12 million restoration. Because the pieces were so large, they were transported on an extra-wide load truck and driven at a speed of 35 miles per hour from Michigan.

“We had to call every senator or governor that we knew on that route, which went through seven states, to get permissions,” he said. “That was a huge thing to overcome, and it all happened over a weekend. So my wife was actually on the phone with a governor’s wife on Saturday evening. The next day, he called up his Department of Transportation and got a permit.”

Christian Quilici, who helped manage the project on behalf of the Shirleys and said he serves as a liaison between the foundation and Congress, said the hardest part of the project was the size of it.

“We had to tear the wall down on C Street, which nobody really thought of in the beginning,” he said. “Nobody got how big this was until it showed up on their doorstep.”

Quilici said he spent years educating at least 30 different senators about the quest to reunite the mobile with the stationary mountains left behind. Some of the biggest champions were seated in the front row at Wednesday’s event, including Senate Democratic Whip Richard J. Durbin. Absent was Sen. Christopher S. Murphy, D-Conn., who has been away from the Capitol while recovering from a stroke but has been a supporter of the sculpture’s restoration for years, pressing former acting Architect of the Capitol Joseph DiPietro on it at a 2024 Senate Legislative Branch Appropriations budget hearing.

“Is there any chance that before I leave the Senate, or at least before I die, we can get the clouds back up there?” Murphy said at the time.

Padilla read a letter on Wednesday from Murphy, whose state was Calder’s home for much of his life.

“This statue is actually a statement on our democracy, that we have big pieces of monumental art right in the middle of our most important and most iconic government buildings. To get this piece of art restored to how it was designed by the artist, well, that’s good for Connecticut. But I also like to think … it’s good for democracy too,” Murphy’s statement said.

The restoration was funded entirely by donations from the Shirleys, who are prominent Calder collectors, according to Rower.

“It’s very emotional to see the work completed after 10 years of us working so hard with the Architect of the Capitol and Calder Foundation. It’s a very overpowering, overwhelming emotion,” said Kim Shirley.

In addition to helping fund the project, she’s a former Senate staffer who worked in Hart before Calder’s work was installed. Years after her time in the Senate was over, her husband Jon wanted to show her the sculpture, now filling the space she used to walk through every day.

But when the two arrived, the clouds weren’t hanging from the ceiling like they were supposed to be. He called up Rower, who was a friend of his, and said he wanted to get them back to how Calder intended. On Wednesday, the three of them stood together in Hart, looking up.

Technological and engineering advancements allowed for newer, lighter clouds to be built and be safe to hang from the ceiling.

“Before it was like a bulb, it looked like a hoagie sandwich, and now it looks just really sharp, like a razor cutting through space, which was Calder’s intention,” Rower said.

According to Rower, it involved laser-measuring the original model of Mountains and Clouds sitting in a glass case nearby in the Hart atrium. The new clouds, he said, are made of carbon fiber.

“These are giant things up in the air, and they’re just so gentle and subtle,” he said.

Younger Hill staffers don’t know a time with clouds. But for those who have spent decades in the building, it was a special moment to see them spin, as Lankford told the crowd.

“I can’t tell you how many tour groups that I’ve walked through this building and I pointed to this, and I said it’s only half there, and would take them over to the little model that’s right over here, and say this is what it’s supposed to look like,” said the senator, a former Hill staffer himself.

Standing under his grandfather’s sculpture, Rower found himself quoting the man responsible for it all.

“I find that everything I do, if it is made for a particular spot, is more successful,” he said, gazing up at it.
