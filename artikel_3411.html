From ‘Disclosure Day’ to ‘Devil Wears Prada’: Emily Blunt Breaks Down 10 of Her Greatest Movie Moments

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Jimmy Stewart once said that with movies, it’s key moments that people remember.

Emily Blunt, who’s on the circuit these days talking about her standout role as a language-spouting meteorologist in Steven Spielberg’s “Disclosure Day,” gave me some memorable scenes that she will never forget from her wide-ranging filmography. (We spoke on Zoom.)

Clearly, Blunt can deliver in any genre: romance, comedy, musical, sci-fi, action, thriller, drama. So far, Blunt has notched one Oscar nomination, for Supporting Actress in Christopher Nolan’s bio-drama “Oppenheimer.” Sci-fi thriller “Disclosure Day” could score Blunt’s first Best Actress nod. 1. “My Summer of Love” (2005, Pawel Pawlikowski) was Blunt’s first movie role. “I remember the wildness of that Edith Piaf dance [‘La Foule’], where I have the cigarette dangling out of my mouth, and she’s trying to romanticize this girl from the wrong side of town and sophisticate her. I remember the scene with Paddy Considine where she tries to seduce him and then she laughs at him. I will never forget the energy in that room and how Paddy was in that scene. I have taken what Pawel taught me about ambiguity and reality on camera, and I have used it throughout my career. I am an untrained actor. I don’t have anything technical to fall back on, but the aliveness that he created on ‘My Summer of Love’ was informative for how I moved forward: what lies beneath the character. You know what the front foot is, and what is the shadow of that person? What’s really going on?”

2. “The Devil Wears Prada“ (2006, David Frankel) marked Blunt’s Hollywood breakout as Miranda Priestley’s beleaguered first assistant Emily Charlton. After she gets hit by a taxi, second assistant Andy Sachs (Anne Hathaway) visits her hospital room. Charlton is angry and tearful at everything she is missing. “The hospital scene makes me laugh because that’s really who she is: this completely broken down, vulnerable, insecure person.”

3. “The Young Victoria” (2010, Jean-Marc Vallée) gave Blunt a queen to embody. “I remember the scene where she asks [Prince] Albert to marry her, because he was not allowed to ask her. She is the monarch, and had to be the one to ask. That scene was so vulnerable and beautifully twinkly, how Jean-Marc shot that.”

4. “The Adjustment Bureau,” a romantic sci-fi thriller, co-starred Matt Damon. “I remember never being able to get through a scene with Matt Damon without laughing. But I did love the scene in the bathroom where they meet. That scene had to sparkle because otherwise you don’t care about them, and you don’t care about them using their magical fedoras to get back to each other. Matt Damon doesn’t know how to be bad. That’s the truth. He doesn’t know how to play a false note. There is something about what Matt does, and he’s so dexterous in his humanity, but I don’t feel it’s technical with Matt. He’s like a laser beam of truth, and I feel myself, anyone who watches him, they ease back and they go, ‘Oh, thank God, Matt Damon’s here.’ He just leads with the truth. He’s like Josh Brolin. They only know how to be honest, and so they have such humanity to them. That’s why you can’t take your eyes off them. John [Krasinski] and I were so proud of what Matt did [with “The Odyssey”]. It’s obviously the most Herculean undertaking, but he grounded everything with that performance.”

5. “Edge of Tomorrow” (2014, Doug Liman) co-starred Tom Cruise and thrust Blunt into action mode for the first time. Her moment was a war scene set on a beach. “It was the beach set, and we started calling it ‘the bitch’ because it was such a nightmare. That set was always flooded. You were always exposed to terrible elements of the weather, freezing, and we shot that beach scene for a month. Tom Cruise gave me a lot of flowers on that one. He was so good to me, and he revealed halfway through the shoot that this was the deep end of action for him— this was the most challenging action movie that he had ever done, and it was my first action movie, so I was flung in at the deep end of action. But we held each other through that. I loved athletics when I was a kid, and then couldn’t be bothered to work out, and then thought I knew what working out was, and then I realized I didn’t know what working out was until I started training for ‘Edge of Tomorrow,’ and that was a transformational thing to my body. She needed to look lethal, absolutely lethal. And so it helped me a lot, doing all of that physicality.”

6. “Into the Woods” (2014, Rob Marshall), adapted from Stephen Sondheim’s stage musical, boasted a huge ensemble cast; Blunt played the baker’s wife opposite James Corden. “‘Moments in the Woods,’ that big song she has right before she gets squished by the giant. She’s weighing up the duality of life, whether she goes for this poetic interlude in her prosaic life or if she goes back to the prosaic life. That kind of agony that she’s going through in the song was cool. I had I’d never sung in front of anyone before, really, other than one small TV thing years ago. I didn’t even want to audition for it, and Rob Marshall said, ‘Look, I’m not looking for singers who can kind of act. I’m looking for actors who can kind of sing,’ and that was great advice. I said it to my 12-year-old this morning as she went off to her musical audition at school. I said, ‘Don’t worry about the notes; just tell us a story. That’s it. That’s what the song should be, and that’s what will make it sound amazing.'”

7. “Sicario” (2015, Denis Villeneuve) plops Blunt’s straight-arrow FBI agent into a violent Mexican border scenario. “The scene that stands out for me is a spellbinding one, when Benicio del Toro comes to my apartment, and he puts the gun to my face and forces me to sign the contract. It’s right at the end of the movie, and I think that scene holds a special place for me because we were not sure about the scene that was written by Taylor Sheridan. It didn’t feel like it belonged in what we were trying to achieve between the characters. We wanted to change it a little bit. It was an idea that Benicio had. We were racking our brains as to how to finish this scene and this moment between the two characters. He always has so many crazy ideas, and he will have some absolute bangers in there. And he called me at about 11 o’clock the night before, and he goes, ‘I think I have it. What about this?’ And I said, ‘That is a great idea, because you’ve got this very idealistic FBI agent who’s pulled into this incoherent, amoral world. And she’s being forced to sign a contract that says everything they did was aboveboard, and that’s like a death to a character like that.’ We rehearsed that scene for two hours. Denis loved it, and then we all turned to [cinematographer] Roger Deakins: “Roger, what do you think?” And he gave his slow nod of approval, and then we shot it, and it was so.”

8. “A Quiet Place” (2018, John Krasinski) was Blunt’s first experience being directed by her husband. The memorable moment: giving birth in the bathtub as a killer monster hovers nearby, listening for any noise. “We shot that whole sequence from the water breaking in the bathtub in order, which I was grateful to John for. It took four days. We did two takes of that bathtub scene because he shot it mainly as a oner. It starts as the blood’s trickling out, and it comes up, and you see the creature, and then it goes into that scream, and then the fireworks go off, but yeah, it’s a wild scene. It’s really smart to have a baby in a silent world. Every woman I’ve spoken to about that scene, they go: ‘Oh my God, that scene made me cross my legs.” And I was: ‘Okay, all right.'”

9. “Oppenheimer” (2023, Christopher Nolan) offered Blunt a constrained role as Kitty, the recessive wife of the great physicist (Cillian Murphy). Blunt agreed that the scene that earned her the Oscar nomination was in the courtroom where she is badgered by the formidable Jason Clarke. “I knew that was the monster scene. When I read the script, I thought, ‘What an extraordinary scene, that you have this person staggering in, looking like a liability,’ and Chris had written that she almost chokes, and you think she’s going to blow it. When I talked to him about it after I read the script, I said, ‘What a reclamation that scene is of that extraordinary brain coming back to life.’ The brain that had gone crazy at the ironing board, probably meant for bigger things than being someone’s wife and someone’s mummy, and I had a great deal of empathy for her and a lot of women who went crazy at the ironing board. She owns Jason Clarke in a way, and you realize she could probably run circles around him. He played such a wonderful bully in that film, but we saw what Meryl [Streep] did with Miranda Priestley: There’s such power in quiet, great, great power and great dignity.”

10. “The Devil Wears Prada 2” (2026, David Frankel) allowed Blunt to indulge Charlton’s excesses. “We all had scenery stuck in our teeth on that one. It was so fun to go back to playing that lunatic. My favorite scene was the funeral scene where she comes in and rants about Dolce and Gabbana. It was such a ludicrous outfit to be wearing to a funeral. Anne Hathaway is the most unstoppable force. And what a wide range of roles she has access to. I feel this moment in her life. She’s in this full wingspan of being able to harness everything she can do and everything she has to offer, and I can’t believe she had the year she just did, and she was pregnant!”

“Disclosure Day” (2026, Steven Spielberg) leaned on Blunt to carry the sci-fi thriller. “The four-and a-half minute oner in the beginning when she arrives at the news station. I’ve seen what Steven Spielberg can do visually, his visual prowess is rather unparalleled. So to be in a oner that he conjures up on the day, that happened spontaneously — he was planning on covering it. And I said, ‘what would help me so much is if you could let me whirlwind out of control in one take from the moment I pull up, going into the makeup room, coming out speaking Korean, she’s in this incoherent tailspin, and then you see her disintegrate over the course of it. And she gets up there to deliver the weather, and she starts to speak in the alien language. Can we just try it as a oner, just for me to see where I’ve got to get to?’ And he watched me do it. And by the time I got up there to do the weather, my legs were shaking. I was out of breath, beside myself, and he loved it. And he goes, ‘We’re going to do it as a oner.’ And we were done by 2 pm. because he planned to shoot it all day. It was Steven in his element of going, ‘Oh, I see it’ and not only was he intentional about creating the environment for me to be able to do that so he didn’t overcover it, he saw a cinematic advantage.”

Blunt plays several characters in one. “All the stuff in the childhood home was deeply moving because that also dealt with that moment where the character goes from crisis to serenity and the ability to then deploy these powers rather than be puppeteered by them. So I loved that shape-shift in the character, like playing two people throughout the course of this film. And the other moment that felt so human to me was the panic attack in the box car after the train sequence, and she’s at max capacity, bubbling over, overwhelmed, and a few people, including myself on set, had experienced panic attacks, and that felt like a real reaction to someone who was utterly overwhelmed. That also felt so Spielbergian: no one’s a slick elite action hero in Steven’s films. Everyone’s afraid. Everyone, including Indiana Jones, everyone’s scared. That hand that’s shaking on the way to Omaha Beach, that is trauma. He can relate to your vulnerabilities. Everyone’s a bit over their head, and in a different action movie, me and Josh O’Connor would have dropped in the boxcar and gone, ‘Whoa, that was crazy.’ And instead, she has a panic attack. I got them a lot in my early twenties, and Steven’s not immune, neither is Josh. All of us have experienced that. That moment where he takes my hands and he puts them on the piano and he goes, ‘This is real. This is real. Feel this. I’m here. You’re here. We’re here.’ I’ve done that. So it just was like, ‘Oh my God!’ You get to sublimate what you’ve experienced into this creative art form sometimes. That’s the great gift often on movies.”

The languages were right in Blunt’s wheelhouse. She has always loved languages. She learned them two to three months before the start of the shoot. “I learned those scenes. The first thing I learned was the Russian and the Korean, because I wanted it to be spoken at such a velocity that it sounded as conversational as it does as me speaking to you right now, and she doesn’t know she’s speaking Russian,” she said. “She thinks she’s speaking English. You have someone who is white-knuckling their way through most of this film, and these forms of mind control are unconscious to her in the beginning, it’s compulsive.”

Next up: “Walk the Blue Fields” directed by John Crowley and co-starring Andrew Scott as another hot priest, Father Joe, presiding at a wedding. “It’s a short story that I bought the rights to about six years ago by Claire Keegan. If you’ve ever read any of her short stories, they’re like laser beams. They’re so wonderful. My sister Felicity is a book agent. She represents Claire, and she sent it to me. Story A is a wedding day, and story B is what led up to that wedding and why everything is starting to unravel on the wedding day. And it plays like a thriller,” she said. “She’s coming back to Ireland to try to build a relationship with her Irish father [Ciaran Hinds] after they’ve been estranged for years, and it’s a story about identity and religion and love. Andrew Scott has to be one of the most beautiful actors I’ve ever shared a scene with.”

And, of course, John Krasinski’s upcoming “Quiet Place 3,” which co-stars Jack O’Connell, Cillian Murphy, Jason Clarke and Noah Jupe, is shrouded in secrecy. “I don’t want to blow John’s cover here,” she said. And “Sicario 3” is also moving forward with the original cast, a script by James Bond writer Steven Knight and director Padraic McKinley.
