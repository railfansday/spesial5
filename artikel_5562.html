10 facts on why raw cookie dough is risky during holiday baking

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Flour is closer to a farm product than a finished food. Wheat grows outdoors, exposed to rain, irrigation water and soil. Birds, deer and rodents move through the same fields. If droppings from those animals reach the crop, bacteria such as E. coli can settle on the grain before harvest.

The milling process is built to separate the kernel, not to sanitize it. Grain is cleaned and often tempered with water, then ground and sifted to split the starchy endosperm from the bran and germ. Whole wheat flour keeps the bran and germ, but it goes through no added safety step. Ordinary flour sold for home baking typically has no heat treatment or other kill step.

The label on the bag does not change this. Bleached or unbleached, all-purpose or whole wheat, bread flour or cake flour — each one is still a raw ingredient meant to be cooked before eating.

Dry storage does not make flour safe. Bacteria cannot multiply in a low-moisture food, but some can survive in it for long periods. A bag bought for last year's holiday baking can still carry whatever it left the mill with.

The strains of concern are Shiga toxin-producing E. coli, often shortened to STEC. They differ from the many harmless strains of E. coli that live in the human gut. Several U.S. outbreaks of STEC illness have been traced to flour rather than to eggs.

Contamination can happen in two ways. Salmonella can sit on the outside of the shell, picked up from the hen's environment. It can also be inside the egg. The strain Salmonella Enteritidis can infect a hen's ovaries and reach the egg before the shell forms. That means an egg can look clean, smell normal and still carry the germ.

Cracking an egg into a bowl gives no visual warning. A contaminated yolk looks the same as a safe one. Freshness and a clean shell offer no guarantee against bacteria inside the egg.

Commercial eggs sold in the U.S. are washed before they reach stores. Washing removes the thin natural coating on the shell. That is why eggs sold in the U.S. need to stay refrigerated. The FDA advises keeping eggs in a refrigerator set at 40 degrees Fahrenheit or below. Cold storage slows bacterial growth but does not kill Salmonella already present.

The FDA and USDA also advise throwing out eggs with cracked or dirty shells. A crack gives bacteria on the surface a path into the egg.

Pasteurized eggs are the main fix for recipes eaten raw. Pasteurized in-shell eggs are heated in their shells to a temperature that kills bacteria without cooking the egg. They are sold in many U.S. grocery stores and are marked as pasteurized on the carton. Liquid egg products sold in cartons are pasteurized as well. Either option can stand in for regular eggs in a dough recipe.

Switching eggs does not make raw dough fully safe. Flour carries its own separate risk, which pasteurized eggs do not address.

Flour made by General Mills $GIS was linked to a 2016 outbreak of Shiga toxin-producing E. coli O121 and O26, according to the CDC. The agency reported that 63 people in 24 states became sick, and 17 of them were hospitalized.

The investigation showed that flour alone could carry a dangerous strain of E. coli. No egg was needed for raw dough to make someone sick.

The case started with patients, not with a product. Public health laboratories compare the genetic fingerprints of bacteria taken from sick people. When samples from people in different states match, investigators treat them as one outbreak and look for a shared source.

Interviews came next. Health officials asked patients what they had eaten and handled in the days before they fell ill. Several reported eating or handling raw homemade dough or batter. That pattern pointed toward an ingredient found in many home kitchens rather than a single restaurant meal.

The trail led to flour produced at a General Mills facility in Kansas City, Missouri. Laboratory testing of flour samples found the outbreak bacteria. That result gave investigators physical evidence to go with the interview data.

General Mills recalled flour sold under its Gold Medal and Gold Medal Wondra brands, along with Signature Kitchens flour. The recall widened in July as more test results and illnesses came in.

Flour recalls depend on shoppers checking the bag itself. A recall notice identifies affected products by details printed on the package, such as the product name, size and dates. A household that bought flour before the notice went out has to compare its own bag against that list. Holiday baking often draws on flour bought earlier in the year, when a recall may have been missed.

The outbreak also showed that a sealed bag from a major brand is no guarantee against contamination. The bacteria in this case did not come from a dirty kitchen or careless storage at home. The flour was contaminated before it reached store shelves.

Investigators then had to work out which ingredient carried the bacteria. E. coli O157:H7 is more commonly associated with undercooked ground beef and fresh produce than with sweets. The eggs in the dough were pasteurized. That made eggs an unlikely source. Investigators considered flour the likely source, although they did not confirm it as the definitive cause.

The investigation widened the conversation about raw dough. Warnings about tasting dough had long focused on eggs. After 2009, public-health officials also pointed to flour as a raw ingredient with its own risk.

An outbreak of E. coli O26 traced to flour sickened 21 people in nine states in 2019, according to the CDC. The outbreak added to a record of illnesses linked to raw flour.

E. coli O26 belongs to a group called Shiga toxin-producing E. coli, often shortened to STEC. Most E. coli strains live harmlessly in the intestines of people and animals. STEC strains are different. They release a toxin that can damage the lining of the gut.

O157 is the best-known STEC serogroup in the U.S. O26 is one of several other serogroups that public health laboratories also track. The 2019 outbreak was not the first flour investigation to involve a serogroup other than O157.

Linking illnesses to flour takes detective work. The CDC coordinates PulseNet, a national network of public health laboratories. The network compares the DNA fingerprints of bacteria taken from sick patients. When samples from different states match closely, investigators treat the cases as a possible outbreak with a shared source.

Next come interviews. Health officials ask patients what they ate in the days before they got sick. They then look for foods that come up more often than expected among healthy people.

The process takes time. Several weeks can pass between the day someone falls ill and the day that case is formally tied to an outbreak.

Recalled flour can stay in home kitchens long after an announcement. Flour recall notices typically list the affected lot codes or best-by dates. Checking a bag against those details shows whether it is part of the recall. The CDC advises people to throw away recalled flour rather than use it. It also advises washing any container where that flour was stored.

That advice matters during the holidays, when bakers reach for bags opened earlier in the year. A recall notice from the spring can still apply to flour used in December.

Severe stomach cramps, diarrhea that is often bloody and vomiting are the main signs of a Shiga toxin-producing E. coli infection, according to the CDC. These symptoms typically begin three to four days after a person swallows the bacteria.

The E. coli strains tied to raw flour and dough outbreaks in the U.S., including O157:H7, O121 and O26, all belong to this Shiga toxin-producing group. The toxin damages cells that line the intestine. That damage is the reason blood can appear in the stool.

Fever is possible, but the CDC notes it is usually not very high. Most people recover within five to seven days. Some infections are mild. Others become severe or life-threatening.

The most serious complication is hemolytic uremic syndrome, known as HUS. The condition destroys red blood cells and can lead to kidney failure. The CDC reports that HUS usually develops about a week after symptoms first appear. It often starts as the diarrhea is improving, which can make a patient seem to be getting better.

Warning signs of HUS include urinating less often, feeling very tired and losing pink color in the cheeks and inside the lower eyelids. People who develop HUS are usually treated in a hospital. Most recover within a few weeks, but some are left with permanent damage.

The CDC advises contacting a health care provider for diarrhea that lasts more than three days. The same advice applies to diarrhea with a fever above 102 degrees Fahrenheit, to bloody diarrhea and to vomiting so frequent that liquids will not stay down. Signs of dehydration call for a doctor too. Those include passing very little urine, a dry mouth and throat, and dizziness when standing up.

Antibiotics are not recommended for suspected Shiga toxin-producing E. coli infections, according to the CDC. Diagnosis depends on laboratory testing of a stool sample. Mentioning raw dough or raw flour gives a clinician useful information about a possible exposure.

Fever is one of the clearest differences between salmonellosis and Shiga toxin-producing E. coli infection. With that type of E. coli, the CDC notes, any fever is usually not very high. Some people with Salmonella also have nausea, vomiting or a headache. Most people recover without specific treatment, the CDC reports. Antibiotics are generally reserved for people who are severely ill or at risk of complications.

Recovery is not always quick. The CDC reports that some people's bowel habits do not return to normal for a few months. A smaller group develops reactive arthritis, which causes joint pain that can last months or years. In rare cases, the infection spreads from the intestines into the bloodstream and other parts of the body.

Diarrhea and fever together can drain the body's fluids fast. The CDC advises contacting a doctor for diarrhea with a fever above 102°F or diarrhea lasting more than three days without improvement. Bloody stool, vomiting that prevents keeping liquids down and signs of dehydration also warrant a call. Those signs include very little urination, a dry mouth and dizziness when standing up.

A doctor cannot confirm Salmonella from symptoms alone. Diagnosis requires a laboratory test, usually of a stool sample. Confirmed cases are reported to public health officials. Those reports are how clusters of illness linked to a single food come to light.

Children under five, adults 65 and older, pregnant people and people with weakened immune systems face the highest risk from raw dough. The CDC and FDA name these four groups as most likely to become seriously ill from foodborne germs. For them, the bacteria in raw flour and raw eggs can do more harm than they would to a healthy adult.

Young children face that risk for more than one reason. Their immune systems are still developing. Their small bodies also lose fluid quickly through diarrhea and vomiting. Dehydration can set in faster than it does in an older child or adult.

The CDC reports that hemolytic uremic syndrome, the kidney complication linked to Shiga toxin-producing E. coli, is most common in children under five. That is the same family of bacteria behind several U.S. outbreaks traced to flour and raw dough. In many households, children are also the ones handed the mixing spoon or the beaters.

Adults 65 and older face a different set of problems. The FDA explains that the immune system and organs become less able to recognize and fight off harmful bacteria with age. The FDA also notes that stomach acid declines in older adults. Stomach acid is one of the body's first defenses against germs in food. Many people in this age group also manage chronic conditions such as diabetes or kidney disease. Those conditions can make recovery harder.

Pregnancy changes the immune system as well. The FDA's food safety guidance for pregnant people treats both the pregnant person and the developing baby as at higher risk from foodborne illness.

People with weakened immune systems make up a broad group. The CDC includes people living with cancer, diabetes, liver or kidney disease, HIV or an organ transplant. It also includes people taking medicines that suppress the immune system, such as chemotherapy drugs or anti-rejection drugs after a transplant.

Many people in these groups look healthy, and a host may not know a guest's medical history. Holiday kitchens often hold grandparents, toddlers, expecting parents and friends undergoing treatment at the same time. A bowl of dough set out for tasting reaches all of them at once.

No-bake recipes raise the same issue. Truffles, bars and fillings that fold in raw flour never reach the oven. Without commercially heat-treated flour, those treats never get a kill step.

Holiday baking adds small moments when raw dough gets eaten without much thought. A finger run through cake batter counts. So does a pinch of pie crust scrap or a bite of gingerbread dough left over after cutting out shapes. Each of those bites skips the one step that makes the dough safe.

The edible label is what separates these products from the refrigerated dough sold in logs, tubs or pre-cut squares. A package that lists oven directions is meant to be baked. Its contents should be handled as raw dough. That holds even when the flavor and branding match an edible version on the next shelf.

Homemade no-bake recipes are a separate question. Some call for heating flour in an oven or microwave before mixing it with butter and sugar. Home heat-treating is not a proven way to make flour safe to eat raw. A kitchen version should not be treated as equal to a commercial product sold as edible.

Cleanup is the other half of safer holiday baking. Raw flour and raw egg can contaminate anything they touch. Flour dust also spreads easily and can settle on counters beyond the mixing bowl. Wash hands with soap and water after handling dough, flour or eggshells. Bowls, spoons, beaters, measuring cups and countertops that touched raw dough need washing too.

Children need the same rule in two forms. They should not eat raw dough, and they should not play with it. That includes homemade play dough and salt dough ornaments made with uncooked flour. Hands that have shaped raw dough should be washed before a child touches food or puts fingers in their mouth.
