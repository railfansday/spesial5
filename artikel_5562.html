Massachusetts voters narrowly reject minimum lot size measure

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Massachusetts voters narrowly rejected Question 7, a first of its kind ballot initiative that would have limited local zoning barriers for qualifying single family lots. Supporters say the results highlight voter confusion and potential targets for future zoning reform, including Boston.  AI Summary

Massachusetts voters narrowly rejected a ballot measure last week that would have forced towns and cities to allow single-family homes on smaller home sites than most local zoning rules currently permit.

The vote on the first-of-its-kind ballot initiative nationwide was so close that the Associated Press called the race only on Friday, nearly three days after the election. It was the last ballot question decided because the gap, roughly 32,000 votes, was the smallest margin among the nine questions on the ballot.

Local government organizations fought the measure, arguing it was a top-down approach that limited local control over zoning. Municipalities have made that argument against any reform that includes preemption, winning some fights and losing others.

In a statement after the AP projection, Massachusetts Municipal Association Executive Director and CEO Adam Chapdelaine thanked voters for “recognizing that Question 7 was not an effective or workable approach to creating entry-level homes in the Commonwealth.”

Still, supporters are treating the close result as a win.

“If you told me six years ago that we would have a statewide ballot to change zoning statewide across the entire Commonwealth, and it would barely lose within one percentage point, I would have thought you were crazy,” Jonathan Berk, board chair of six-year-old Abundant Housing Massachusetts, told HousingWire TBD.

The measure would have required “that cities and towns allow single-family homes on residentially zoned lots that meet minimum standards of at least 5,000 square feet in area, at least 50 feet of frontage on a street or public way, and access to public sewer and water services.” In effect, the state law would preempt local zoning rules that require single-family home lots to be larger than 5,000 square feet, which is about 0.12 acres. Cities and towns could still have set reasonable rules on setbacks, height, bulk, and short-term rentals.

Supporters said the change would spur starter-home construction and make ownership more attainable for working- and middle-class families since many localities currently zone for a minimum single-family lot area of one-half to two acres.

While the result disappointed proponents, it gave them valuable information on where to press for smaller lot sizes.

Voters in Boston overwhelmingly supported smaller lot sizes even though the city was exempt from the measure. Rural communities on well water and septic systems were also outside the measure’s reach but voted against it.

Berk said the outcome points to confusion among voters in both groups. Rural voters did not understand that the measure did not apply to them.

“I would bet that a lot of those voters thought they were voting for something that applied to Boston,” he said.

Berk said the city is missing an opportunity, adding that Mayor Michelle Wu has been “a bit gun shy” about large-scale zoning reform. He said Boston’s zoning code is the longest in the country, about four times the length of New York City’s, and that 99% of the city’s residential land is “existing non-conforming.”

Boston is one of the least affordable big cities in the country. HousingWire Intelligence shows median monthly rent at $3,900, on par with San Francisco and New York City. A median home list price of $890,000 puts Boston in competition with California cities.

A knock against Boston is that building in the city is expensive because of zoning code challenges.

“It’s hard to develop here. It should be,” Boston Planning Chief Kairos Shen said during an October inclusionary zoning symposium on housing costs sponsored by the MIT Center for Real Estate.

“You guys want us to lower our standards,” Shen added. “Part of my job is to hold those standards and try to still make them work.”

The forum included representatives from developers The Davis Companies and Oxford Properties.

Policies requiring income-restricted units in market-rate apartment projects have drawn broad criticism. That is one factor in stalling Boston multifamily production. Higher construction costs and interest rates have also played a role.

Rental construction is only part of the picture. The ownership side is where the ballot question and Boston’s zoning code also collide.

“I do hope that this gives some cover to Mayor Wu in Boston to actually pursue some more dramatic zoning reform in the city,” Berk said, citing single-family lot sizes and modest middle housing such as townhomes and duplexes.
