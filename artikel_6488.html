Scientists Discover a Hidden Magnetic Effect That Produces Signals 100 Times Stronger

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Antiferromagnets could pave the way for faster, more energy-efficient electronics, but accurately measuring their properties remains a major challenge.

A nearly invisible layer of magnetic material can overwhelm scientific measurements, producing signals more than 100 times stronger than those of the material researchers are trying to study. Scientists at Martin Luther University Halle-Wittenberg (MLU) and the Max Planck Institute for Microstructure Physics discovered this problem while investigating a promising material for ultrafast electronics. Their findings, published in Advanced Materials, also show how to prevent the interference.

The research focuses on antiferromagnets, materials in which neighboring atomic magnetic moments cancel one another out, leaving little or no overall magnetization. Unlike conventional ferromagnets, they are resistant to electromagnetic interference and can switch magnetic states up to 1,000 times faster. These properties could help engineers develop faster electronic components that consume less energy.

The researchers investigated Mn₃Sn, a compound made of manganese and tin that belongs to a special group called chiral antiferromagnets. Its magnetic moments are arranged at 120-degree angles rather than pointing in opposite directions. This arrangement has a property called chirality, meaning it cannot be superimposed on its mirror image simply by rotating or moving it.

“Chiral antiferromagnets combine the advantages of both classes of magnets while minimizing their drawbacks. However, they are often difficult to control,” explains Professor Georg Woltersdorf from the Institute of Physics at MLU and spokesperson for the Cluster of Excellence “Center for Chiral Electronics”.

Researchers in the cluster are exploring ways to use chirality in spintronics, an approach to electronics that exploits the spin of electrons as well as their electrical charge. Spin is an intrinsic form of angular momentum that gives electrons magnetic properties. Controlling it could enable electronic devices that operate at extremely high speeds while using less energy.

To examine Mn₃Sn, the team fabricated layers just a few nanometers thick and combined them with either platinum or a nickel-iron alloy. They then exposed the samples to ultrashort laser pulses and measured the resulting electrical voltages on picosecond timescales, equivalent to trillionths of a second. These measurements allowed the researchers to investigate rapid changes in magnetic order.

However, the samples containing platinum produced unexpected results. The researchers traced the problem to a heating step used during fabrication to repair defects in the crystal structure. During this process, atoms at the interface between the materials reacted to form an unintended ferromagnetic compound.

This thin interfacial layer generated magnetic signals more than 100 times stronger than those produced by Mn₃Sn itself. As a result, the measurements were dominated by the newly formed compound rather than the antiferromagnet the scientists intended to investigate.

“It was only when we modified the process and allowed the Mn₃Sn layer to be cure fully before depositing the platinum that the material remained unchanged. Before that, we had essentially only been measuring a layer that was an unintended result of the sample preparation process,” explains the study’s first author Paul Marschall from the Institute of Physics at MLU.

By completing the heating treatment before adding platinum, the researchers avoided forming the unwanted magnetic compound. Properly prepared samples then allowed them to investigate the actual properties of Mn₃Sn, including how efficiently it converts spin currents into measurable electrical voltages.

When the scientists directed a spin current from the nickel-iron layer into Mn₃Sn, they found that the conversion efficiency was approximately 10% of that typically achieved with platinum. This provided a more reliable assessment of the material’s behavior without the interfering magnetic layer.

“Interfaces are not just a peripheral aspect of spintronics, they are often the actual component. If we want to harness chiral antiferromagnets for technological applications, we must first understand and be able to control what happens within these few atomic layers,” concludes Woltersdorf.

Reference: “Spin Transport Across Interfaces With the Non-Collinear Antiferromagnet Mn3Sn” by Paul A. Marschall, Wolfgang Hoppe, Prajwal Rigvedi, Börge Göbel, Franziska Ziolkowski, Samir Lounis, Banabir Pal, Stuart S. P. Parkin and Georg Woltersdorf, 20 September 2026, Advanced Materials. DOI: 10.1002/adma.75024

The study was funded by the German Research Foundation (DFG).
