China dominates battery graphite, but this startup could break the monopoly

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Electric vehicle sales in the U.S. may have leveled off, but the market for batteries inside them isn’t slowing down, quintupling in the last five years. Many of those are made in the U.S., but they’re largely dependent on China for key ingredients, including graphite.

Graphite is a key ingredient in lithium-ion batteries, where it forms the anode, the part that stores the battery’s charge. Chinese companies control about three-quarters of the graphite anode supply chain and 97% of synthetic (human-made) graphite anode production, according to Benchmark Mineral Intelligence. One five-year-old startup, Molten Industries, is working to break that dominance.

Molten Industries has started operating a demonstration plant in Oakland meant to prove the technology works, the company exclusively told TechCrunch. The plant is will pave the way for U.S.-made graphite to win a share of the market.

“The new plant will be producing graphite that’s at cost parity with China plus tariffs,” Caleb Boyd, co-founder and CTO of Molten Industries, told TechCrunch. In other words, once U.S. import taxes are added to Chinese graphite, Molten’s price would match it. He said future plants should match China’s cost even without tariffs, which he called a built-in advantage of the technology.

Molten’s Oakland plant will make about 300 metric tons of graphite annually, which is enough for 300 megawatt-hours of batteries. That’s enough for about 4,300 EVs. The bulk of the output will go to undisclosed customers that are testing it in their battery cells.

The next facility, which Molten has yet to announce, will take the same template used in the Oakland plant and replicate it several times over, said Boyd.

The startup’s technology began in a Silicon Valley garage. It uses electrical resistive heating, which passes electricity through a material to heat it, to split methane (the main ingredient in natural gas) into pure carbon and hydrogen. “You can think of it almost like a massive toaster,” Boyd said. The company says the system uses three times less electricity than existing methods, and it runs on natural gas, which is cheap and plentiful in the U.S.

“We think it’s very hard to compete with China if you’re just copying their processes and trying to compete on cost,” said Kevin Bush, who co-founded Molten Industries with Boyd and is the company’s CEO. “So we are using a resource in the United States that’s abundant in natural gas, and we’ve brought some innovation to actually be able to drive down the cost of graphite production globally.”

Here’s how the chemistry works: When methane (CH4) is heated without oxygen, each molecule breaks into one carbon atom and four hydrogen atoms. The hydrogen atoms pair up into hydrogen gas (H2), which Molten sells at market prices to a customer next door. The carbon is then processed to meet battery makers’ requirements.

The basic idea of using heat to separate methane has been around for over a century, but early attempts to commercialize it ran into trouble. Carbon built up and clogged the reactor, and Boyd said no company before Molten had been able to produce high-quality graphite this way.

Solving that problem changed the company’s direction. Boyd and Bush — both materials science PhDs from Stanford — originally set out to make clean hydrogen from natural gas. Most hydrogen today comes from steam reforming, a cheap but polluting process. The two wanted to reap the benefits of a low-cost feedstock like natural gas without the pollution, and they expected to sell the leftover carbon as a byproduct.

But as they experimented with ways to keep the reactor clean, “we actually started making this really high quality graphite,” Boyd said, which “changed the entire economics of what we were doing.” Molten went from a hydrogen company that happened to sell graphite to a battery-grade graphite company that happens to sell hydrogen.

The company says both businesses could bring in billions of dollars. If Molten supplied the entire U.S. battery market, it could also provide about a third of the hydrogen used in the country, the company claims. Other uses for high-quality graphite, like thermal batteries that store energy as heat, could expand both markets further.

The new factory, Boyd said, “is really a milestone, not just for the company, we think, but globally.”
