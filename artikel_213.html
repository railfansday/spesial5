Ars Live recap: Don’t expect military attacks on data centers to stop

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Militaries have many reasons to target data centers and the cloud in wartime.

Amazon data centers came under military attack for the first time ever in the Middle East this year, and Russia and Ukraine exchanged an unprecedented flurry of drone strikes on each other’s data centers. To examine what such attacks on data centers mean for the future of cloud services that all modern societies depend upon, Ars hosted a live discussion.

In a conversation with Ars Technica Tech Reporter Jeremy Hsu, Joseph Jarnecki at the Royal United Services Institute first talked about why public cloud data centers have increasingly become military targets during the 2026 Iran war and the ongoing full-scale Russian invasion of Ukraine:

We see an increasing acknowledgement and fact of dual use in particularly the public cloud, but also just across the board in private cloud data centers as well. The dual use that I refer to is the utilization of compute functions within these data centers—within cloud services—by militaries, for example, to run battlefield management systems, to do their HR, to do their resource management. Once a military uses a piece of civilian infrastructure, at least under international law or in terms of the principles of targeting that are taken forward by militaries, it can sometimes become a military object and is therefore of interest to an adversary, and there’s an incentive to target it.

Jarnecki also highlighted the fact that relatively cheap kamikaze drones can inflict disproportionate economic costs and create widespread cloud disruptions when striking data centers:

The second reason that we’ve seen targeting particularly in Russia’s largest data centers or some of Russia’s largest public cloud data centers like Yandex, as well as American hyperscalers that are based in the Gulf, is to impose economic cost in a way that would be disadvantageous for an adversary and would otherwise be quite difficult to impose in other ways. So it’s relatively low cost of munition to high cost of disruption. If you think about the amount of investment that’s been put forward by AWS and other companies in the Gulf to build out these data center installations relative to the cost of the amount of Shahed drones that are manufactured, produced, and utilized by Iran to target these data centers, I would put it very, very conservatively at 100X or even more. The cost ratio is really very lucrative for the adversary, and it provides the opportunity to really degrade the economic outcomes of who you are targeting.

Much of the discussion also focused on cloud resilience lessons for both governments and hyperscalers, with the latter including large cloud and data center operators such as Amazon, Google, Microsoft, and Meta. Jarnecki highlighted the example of how Ukraine took steps to safeguard its cloud infrastructure when Russian President Vladimir Putin ordered Russian military forces to launch the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022:

I think that Ukraine anticipated this problem set very effectively. They anticipated and understood that Russia would not be discriminating necessarily in its targeting of civilian digital infrastructure particularly, and therefore conducted a large-scale cloud migration at the onset of the full-scale war in part in reaction to the anticipated targeting but then also actual targeting that took place through kinetic means.

Such lessons are important because 2026 is unlikely to be the last year in which data centers come under substantial military attack, Jarnecki said:

Unfortunately, I think that this really does indicate a continuation of this kind of targeting, not just because data centers are, despite efforts to harden some of them, relatively squishy, relatively easy to find, and have the potential, because of their complexity, to induce cascading risks within the facility or more widely if they are hit particularly by kinetic munitions. So at present, I would say it’s a dangerous trend that is likely to crop up again.

Throughout the event, Jarnecki also addressed viewer questions about whether data centers can realistically be hardened or built underground to withstand physical attacks, and even how orbital data centers or low-Earth orbit satellite constellations such as Starlink could provide backup options from space.
