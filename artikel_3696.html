Little Shop of Horrors: An Oral History

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Like an unassuming plant from outer space, Little Shop of Horrors had humble beginnings—but managed to blossom at a surprising rate. The musical is performed numerous times each year across the globe; the current Off Broadway production has been running for seven years straight. But none of that would have happened without the 1986 film version of Little Shop, a cult classic that Warner Bros. executives once characterized as “the highest-budget musical they’d ever done,” according to its director, Frank Oz. “I don’t know if it’s true or if it’s hyperbole, but that’s what they told me to pressure me into doing a good job.”

“I love it. Audiences love it,” says composer Alan Menken. “Anytime I watch Little Shop,” though, “every other second I’ll be thinking about my late collaborator”—lyricist, book writer, and original stage director Howard Ashman, whom he worked with on both the original stage show and the film. “I’ll look at his work and how amazing it is,” Menken says. “Then people come running up to me, and I go, ‘It’s Howard.’”

Moranis as Seymour Krelborn and Ellen Greene as Audrey in Frank Oz's 1986 film adaptation.

It’s been nearly 40 years since Audrey II began its cinematic rampage. To commemorate the movie—which is getting a brand-new 4K restoration and rerelease on Blu-ray—Vanity Fair spoke with Oz, Menken, producer David Geffen, stars Rick Moranis, Ellen Greene, Tisha Campbell, Tichina Arnold, Michelle Weeks, and more about assembling an A-list cast including Steve Martin and Bill Murray; the film’s long, difficult production; why Moranis had to film several of his scenes as though he was moving in slow motion; and the movie’s elaborate, expensive, utterly depressing original ending.

Back in 1959, legendary low-budget filmmaker Roger Corman had a challenge: to shoot an entire film in two days. He enlisted Charles B. Griffith to write a film using preexisting sets—including a florist shop and a dental office. The original Little Shop of Horrors was born: a scrappy but ambitious B movie featuring a brief cameo by a young Jack Nicholson.

The film became a staple of late-night television, entrancing viewers like a teenage Howard Ashman. As an adult, Ashman knew it’d make a great musical. His agent watched the Corman film and told Ashman he was out of his mind.

Poster for the TV adaptation of The Little Shop of Horrors, 1960.

Ashman then approached jingle writer Alan Menken about doing the musical with him at the WPA Theatre, of which Ashman was a cofounder.

ALAN MENKEN (COMPOSER): I thought it was absolutely amazing. This was one of those so-bad-it’s-good movies. When we first took a crack [at writing the songs], it was actually very close to the Corman film…. And then Howard said, “You know, I think we should do this as the dark side of Grease.”

You think about those beach blanket horror movies from the early ’60s or late ’50s, and there is both a tackiness and an incredible exuberance about them at the same time. And then you think about the Phil Spector Wall of Sound and his work with the Ronettes. So the score became a whole homage to all those early rock influences, and it just became a perfect tonal bed for the storytelling.

ELLEN GREENE (“AUDREY,” ORIGINAL OFF BROADWAY AND THE 1986 FILM): I got to watch Howard direct several sessions with Alan playing on the piano. They counterbalanced each other; Howard was very serious, and Alan is just a sweetheart. Although Howard was also a sweetheart.

MENKEN: What makes for a good collaboration, first of all, is to get your fucking egos out of the room. Bring your talent into the room and always be ready to throw something out…. Don’t ever allow yourself to fall in love with your own work. You are driving this particular thing called a musical. Howard was the best I ever experienced at doing that…. Howard knew exactly what he wanted.

GREENE: I remember walking down the street [listening to a demo of the song “Skid Row” and thinking], I don’t want to do a camp musical. [Then I] got too unemployed, and I'm listening to “Somewhere That’s Green.” I started quietly singing “Green” to myself, and it made me cry. And I knew right then and there I had an Audrey inside of me that was waiting to be born…. When I went into the audition, I did the scenes, and the speaking voice just came out of me.

Michelle Weeks, Tisha Campbell, and Tichina Arnold as Chiffon, Crystal, and Ronette, respectively, in the 1986 adaptation.

LEE WILKOF (“SEYMOUR,” ORIGINAL OFF BROADWAY): I got a call from a casting director that I knew in New York. [She said], “A theater in New York is doing a musical based on the movie Little Shop of Horrors…. Would you come in and read for the part of the dentist? I walked into the audition…and Howard Ashman looked at me and he went, “You’re not a dentist. Would you mind going out and looking at the role of Seymour?” And I did, and I got a callback. Apparently, it was between me and another actor. His name’s Nathan Lane. And the assistant director apparently said that she thought that I would work better as a Seymour. Her name’s Connie Grappo, and we’ve been married 42 years.

GREENE: I was a brunette at the time. I said, “Howie, meet me at 14th Street.”… And I tried on a long blonde wig with bangs, and he went, “I don’t know what you’re doing.” I said, “Just let me finish.” Paul Holmes, the stage manager, helped me cut the wig, and Howard still wasn’t sure. Then I completed the look [with a black cocktail dress], and he loved it.

The show opened at the WPA in 1982 for what was supposed to be a two-week “showcase,” with the actors all making a mere $50 a week in exchange for being reviewed by major theater critics. It was an immediate success.

WILKOF: It was impossible to get a ticket. It went from being this obscure little show. The word on the street apparently grew.

MENKEN: We had lines of producers around the block.

DAVID GEFFEN (PRODUCER): I don’t quite remember how I first got involved, but I think Cameron Mackintosh and I were approached…. I thought it was terrific.

Director of photography Robert Paynter on set, 1986.

MENKEN: We knew there were people who wanted to bring Little Shop to Broadway, and Howard said absolutely not.

GEFFEN: The [Off Broadway] show was very successful, highly thought of. Then we opened it up in Los Angeles. It was natural that we’d make it as a movie.

The creative team began planning a feature film, which Geffen would produce. Tom Cruise, per Menken, wanted to play Seymour (although his reps have claimed he wasn’t interested in the role); he was immediately vetoed by Ashman. Geffen had his eyes set instead on a comedic actor from Canada.

RICK MORANIS (“SEYMOUR”): I first started hearing about the film being made early on, like ’82 or ’83. I ran into David Geffen randomly—I had never met him before—and he stopped me and said, “I want you in my movie.” I said, “Which one?” And he told me, and I said, “Well, okay!” And then for the next three years, I would occasionally hear about it or read about it in the hands of one director or another, and nothing ever happened.

At one point, Steven Spielberg was slated to produce the film, with Martin Scorsese directing. John Landis was also attached to the project for a time. Perhaps the name that made the most sense on paper was Frank Oz, best known as the man who brought to life childhood staples like Miss Piggy, Fozzie Bear, and Yoda.

FRANK OZ (DIRECTOR): I saw [the show] and thought it was amazing. But I didn’t have a take on it. And when I read the script for the movie, I couldn’t get my hands around it. It was so big, and I didn’t have a clear vision of it. So, through my agents, I said, “Thank you, but I can’t do it.” Two weeks later, something clicked in me regarding the three girl-group singers. I realized that when they entered onstage, all they could do was enter from the left or from the right…. But in a movie, it’s 360 degrees; you can have them enter from anywhere. So that’s what kind of changed my idea. I called back and I said, “You know, I may have a way to do this,” and then David asked me to do a rewrite…. Then I went to London, and I spent a month on the rewrite, and I felt really good about it. I knew if I said yes [to directing it], that’d mean two years of my life would be on this project. But I was committed and excited about it.

GREENE: [Frank] has to see the vision. Until he sees the vision in his mind’s eye, in his head, he will not take something on.

Greene, Steve Martin as Orin Scrivello, and Moranis, 1986.

MORANIS: [It’s] the intersection of so many skills and qualities that he has: a remarkable sense of humor, a real affinity for the darker arts and all the puppeteering, and such a keen eye for design.

OZ: I had not known Rick, who is now one of my closest friends after all these years. I never knew Rick, never heard of him because I was in London shooting for five years, and Rick was doing SCTV, and that was not shown in London. But I saw some of his work and I thought it was brilliant.

MORANIS: I knew this was an extraordinary opportunity to do a musical, and shoot it in London, and work with Frank and that cast. [Film] musicals don’t come along too often for people who get to call themselves actors.

OZ: David understandably wanted a star, but…I couldn’t see an Audrey with the depth and the heart that was given to the character by Ellen. I couldn’t see that being done by anyone else. So I asked him to please let me do a screen test with Ellen, and he did. And that’s when David said yes to Ellen.

MORANIS: Ellen had created the character of Audrey for the Off Broadway show many years before we did the movie.  She wasn’t just acting the part of Audrey; she was Audrey. I had just come from years of comedic character acting on SCTV and in films like Strange Brew and Ghostbusters. So we were both approaching our performances for Little Shop in similar ways. I think that’s why we had such a strong onscreen chemistry.

GREENE: Frank saw the character a little more heroine-like [than she was onstage]. He wasn’t sure about the “Audrey-isms,” and so that’s why the line “You don’t meet nice boys when you live on Skid Row, Mr. Mushnik” is not in the film…. Freddy Cooper was a cameraman…and he’s the reason I got to do the squeak in the film. Frank and Rick weren’t sure, and he spoke up and said, “Sir, I think it’s funny.”

OZ: David also suggested Steve Martin, and Steve I had worked with a little bit prior to that. Steve and I talked, and at the time there was a very popular character called Fonzie, and Steve said he just didn’t want to do Fonzie. I said, “Absolutely, that’s fine.” He wanted it more like Elvis. I said, “Fantastic.” As far as I was concerned, Steve could do anything.

The film’s glue wasn’t any of its big names; it was the three girls, all under 18, who made up its ’60s-girl-group-esque Greek chorus.

TISHA CAMPBELL (“CHIFFON”): I had just auditioned to play Regina King’s best friend in 227, and I didn’t get it. So I quit. I was 16 years old. I wanted to go to camp with my friends. And while I was there, my mother called me. She said, “Your agent keeps bugging me. They won’t stop calling me.” I said, “Did you tell them I quit?” And she said, “I told them and said you just wanted to be a regular kid, and they were like, ‘Please, just this one audition. Then you can go to camp.’ Can you just do it and I’ll send you right back to camp? No harm, no foul.” And I was like, “Okay, fine.”

MICHELLE WEEKS (“RONETTE”): At the time, I was on Broadway doing Tap Dance Kid, and I had a management company submit me. When I went for the audition…I was like, Yeah, they’re not gonna pick me. I’m too big. But then when we went for the callbacks, I was like, Oh, I have a chance!

Steve Ross, Courtney Sale, Steven Spielberg, and David Geffen at an off-Broadway performance of Little Shop of Horrors, 1982.

TICHINA ARNOLD (“CRYSTAL”): [I was] starting off in the theater [as] a little kid with wide eyes and ready to get it cracking. I just so happened to know Michelle Weeks because she and I went to school together. We had the same friends, and then Tisha and I knew each other from just auditioning.

CAMPBELL: They auditioned girls from everywhere…. And it just so happened that [the three girls cast were] from the tristate area. Michelle was from Brooklyn, Tichina was from Queens, and I was from New Jersey.

ARNOLD: And Frank Oz, we asked him, “How did you choose us?” And he was just like, “You girls had a synergy.”

Little Shop presented a real technical challenge: how to capture chemistry between its lead, Rick Moranis, and the man-eating plant. For the voice of the plant—named Audrey II by Seymour—the creative team turned to Motown singer Levi Stubbs. But actually bringing the creature to life would be the real struggle.

OZ: There’s only two words necessary, and that’s [puppeteer] Lyle Conway. Lyle passed recently, sadly, but Lyle is the one responsible for the plant. He was a pure artist, and he did an incredible job.

BRIAN HENSON (PRINCIPAL PUPPETEER): I think [Lyle] brought almost everybody that was on Return to Oz, and then a lot more because it was a much bigger build…. My dad [Jim Henson] came and visited the shoot a couple times, for sure. And then my little sister, Heather, was in it. She’s in the dentist scene.

BRAN FERREN (SPECIAL VISUAL EFFECTS): Lyle Conway was brilliant, his vision for how to create—because there wasn’t one plant; there were a series of plants that were used to show its various stages of growth.

OZ: I needed pure lip sync, really good lip sync, not just a mouth opening and closing. Because you’re on film, they can see in a tight shot that it’s just a puppet if it’s just opening and closing.

HENSON: This was definitely the biggest and the most complex [thing] that had ever been done in animatronics. It was also before the Creature Shop–type people were using motors, so it was almost all cable-driven.

OZ: All the performers had to rehearse the song over and over and over to make sure they hit every single syllable.

HENSON: There’s no eyes on that character, so it was always going to be the physical presence of the plant combined with this mouth and this voice. So Levi’s recordings were very important to Frank. [But] we started rehearsing before he even had Levi’s recordings.

MORANIS: They were looking for ways to make vowels and consonants so that they could form words, and then form phrases, and then be able to do that to music. And it was only possible to do at a slower motion than 24 frames a second.

There are 24 still frames in every second of standard film. But during rehearsals, it was discovered that the plant could not believably lip-synch at that speed. The production’s solution was to slow down Audrey II’s motions—operating the plant at 12 frames per second, then speeding the film up later so that it would look normal. This meant that anytime Moranis was in the same shot as the plant, he also had to move, act, and sing in slow motion.

MORANIS: The trick was to keep my butt tight. Because we keep our butt tight when we’re walking quickly. And when you try and walk slowly, it’s very easy to let your butt relax. If you were to look at that at 24 frames, you would look like you were doing some sort of weird walk. So by keeping the butt tight, I managed to pull it off, I think.

GREENE: [Frank] taught me how to act at 12 frames per second. He put me in a room; he had a camera. He said, “Ellen, walk across the floor.” He set it up to the regular speed. He said, “Now look at this,” and it looked like I was rocking back and forth. He showed me how you have to walk only forward, and ballet sort of helped. I worked on acting, talking, and moving in 12 frames a second.

John Candy as Wink Wilkinson and Moranis as Seymour Krelborn, 1986.

FERREN: How do you put emotion into a plant? I mean, news flash: It wasn’t a real man-eating plant! And my suspicion is that not many people watching the movie said, “Oh my God, they found an actual man-eating plant.” If you get it right, they shouldn’t be thinking about that.

HENSON: When we started rehearsing, we just had a bit of skin for the lips. The head was bald, and it was quite lightweight. Every week they’d build a little bit more, and it would just get heavier and heavier and heavier. By the end, it weighed more than 150 pounds.

With the cast—and plant—in place, filming began on October 21, 1985, at Pinewood Studios on the 007 Stage. Per Frank Oz’s vision, it had to be done entirely on a soundstage, as the world they were creating couldn’t come to life on location.

OZ: Roy Walker, our brilliant production designer, and I walked through the whole set. I told him where I felt things needed to be for each particular number, because I had planned the numbers in my head months before I even got to London.

GREENE: [As] someone who first was on this tiny, tiny stage, [I walked] into Pinewood Studios…and there in front of me is the land that is built, that I only saw in my mind's eye because I was on the stage—and it’s even better than I ever imagined.

OZ: My goal was to make it a movie sensibility instead of only a theatrical sensibility. It’s really a mixture of it, and my real goal was to make sure it reflected the gritty atmosphere of an Off Broadway play, and not a musical. I didn’t want to make it into Hello, Dolly! or Mame.

MENKEN: Little Shop is meant to be this tacky show in a hole-in-the-wall…and they wanted the film to have that same quality.

OZ: The opening number is packed with information. You have the three girls dancing in the streets. I needed people to know that they were not regular human beings, so I had them sing in the rain—and they were not affected by the rain.

ARNOLD: Frank Oz explained to us, “It’s gonna be raining around you, but you won’t get wet.” And I was like, “How is that gonna happen?” He showed us the pipes and stuff that they had in the ceiling and said, “Okay, so choreography-wise, you’re gonna follow this path [to ensure you don’t get wet].”

CAMPBELL: Man, it was raining on our heads! The first time we filmed that scene, it was raining so hard that you could tell on our faces. They had to cut. They were like, “You can’t show that it’s raining on y’all!”

WEEKS: We had to be up early in the morning, and us being teenagers, we were going places we weren’t supposed to go, like the club. We used to go to the Hippodrome and get out at like two or three in the morning, and have to be at the studio at six. But it was so much fun!

OZ: I loved shooting “Suddenly Seymour.” When Roy showed me the set—which is beautiful—I saw that staircase that he made. I thought, Okay, it’s got to be a Romeo-and-Juliet section where she’s up there, and he’s down there, and he runs up there. So it was all built up to that moment. I had him run up the stairs 30 to 40 times.

GREENE: Fifty-three takes we had to go up those stairs. [The “Suddenly Seymour”] set was so beautiful, with grass growing through the cracks and bricks and cobblestones. If any set was ever called a work of art, I think this would be applicable.

CAMPBELL: [Steve Martin] was very quiet off camera. But when that camera rolled, I was just in awe of how he would light up and become this character, and just be very big and broad.

OZ: When David suggested Billy [Murray, who has a cameo in the dentist scene]—and I knew Billy before that—I called him. I said, “Hey, Billy, do you want to do this?” And Bill said, “Do I have to do the words?” And I said, “No. I don’t care what words you use as long as you’re the masochist and he’s the sadist. That’s all I care about.”

As technical as the film itself was, nothing could compare to the plant.

OZ: People tend to think, Oh, puppets, that’s no problem. They are the hardest things in the world to shoot, to do right…. Just “Feed Me” alone took five weeks to shoot.

HENSON: Nowadays, you think of a film unit shooting 30 to 40 setups a day. Little Shop of Horrors was way down at three to five, five to seven if you were shooting easier stuff. It was slow, meticulous shooting. And Frank is a very meticulous director.

OZ: We’d usually go about 25 to 30 takes to make it perfect.

MORANIS: [For] “Feed Me,” Brian was inside the head of the plant, and then all the other puppeteers were connected to the plant. The actual plant store that we shot in was constructed on the second floor, and the first floor is where the puppeteers set up camp and where their cables would go down to. They would have monitors, and they could watch themselves operate the plant.

HENSON: There were vines that were being worked by one or two people on each vine. It [took] maybe five of us to work the head…. With “Mean Green Mother”…there’d be puppeteers all the way around the outside of the set, doing cables, and on the roof of the set was another maybe 10 puppeteers. And the set was also on top of a tank, so they could get to the underside of the pot for the roots.

MORANIS: It was so enjoyable to do [“Feed Me”]. I don’t think I had a moment when I didn’t enjoy shooting it. I do remember one aspect, just because it went on for so long: Every day I would come in and put on the same clothes.[Just] put on the same clothes every day for five weeks, or whatever it was.

HENSON: [During “Feed Me”], I’m right there. I said to Lyle, “You know, I just can’t stay hidden all the time.”… I couldn’t hide completely behind the stem. And Lyle said, “Okay, this is what we’re going to do. You’re going to wear a skintight green Lycra suit, and I’m gonna glue leaves to it.” There’s bits of me in the shots, but you don’t know it’s me.

The stage version of Little Shop ends with the plant eating Audrey, Seymour, and, finally, the audience. The filmmakers were determined to capture that same spirit, and did so in a $5 million epic ending that saw Audrey II taking over New York—ending on top of the Statue of Liberty.

OZ: Before I even went to London, I was at David Geffen’s office, and David said to us, “You know, you can’t end it the way the play ends it, because you’re killing your leads.” And Howard and I both said, “Well, we want to be true to the ending from Off Broadway, in part because we just like being true to things, but also because it was a deal with the devil. He deserved to die because he killed people, even though he didn’t do it maliciously.”

GREENE: I gravitate to the dark underpinnings of the Faustian tale. Even though there's a magical world full of whimsy and silliness and goofiness, it has a moral with dark underpinnings.

OZ: Richard Conway had worked about nine months on the models of the destruction at the end. It was no CGI, no opticals. Richard had built all this stuff, and then built the small plant that would be on the bridge, and he did an incredible job having the plants like Godzilla raging through the city.

MORANIS: I remember that, when I saw the ending of the play, something was off with it, but it didn’t matter because the vines came out of the ceiling. And I thought, This is brilliant! They’re distracting you from the tragic ending by creating this fun experience of stuff flying out of the ceiling.You’re part of the action. The shock and surprise, and all the tactile fuzziness of it, distracted from whatever was lacking in a fulfilling happy ending. It didn’t occur to me that that would become a problem in the film.

This is Audrey II in Little Shop of Horrors, 1986.

GREENE: In the play, I say a line—“When I die, which should be very shortly…”—just to lighten the moment, and it’s silly. It releases some of the energy in the audience. But when I say it in the film, it’s very intimate, and it’s to Seymour. It’s weighted differently…. I’m very proud of that scene.

MENKEN: When we did [the first] preview…I'm sitting there going, “Wow! Look at this!” I look at David Geffen, and he said, “Yeah, wow, but wait a second—the cards, Alan!”

Those would be the reaction cards on which that era’s preview audiences would answer questions regarding how much they liked a film. Typically, producers were looking for 55% of a screening’s viewers to say they’d “recommend” the film. Little Shop’s first screening in San Jose got 13%. Oz asked Geffen to screen it again in LA to make sure this wasn’t an anomaly. The results were the same.

GEFFEN: People were hostile to the end of the film. They were hostile to the characters getting killed. We thought we were going to have a disaster.

OZ: The audience was against me because I killed people they loved, and it was a great lesson to learn. Onstage, they also die, but the actors come out for a bow. In movies, you don’t come out for a bow. You just die.

GREENE: [Onstage], my death is not as heartbreaking. It's a mixture. Just when you’re about to laugh, I’ll make you cry. Just when you’re about to cry, I’ll make you laugh.

GEFFEN: I think what the audience didn’t get was that this was a send-up of monster movies in the ’50s. They took it quite seriously. We made it so realistic that they were horrified by the end of the movie.

FERREN: The idea was, I want to go out smiling and happy. Now, I guess, it shows my personality. I went out smiling and happy, watching the world end.

OZ: They wouldn’t even release the movie if I didn’t change the ending…. The hardest of all was the terrible, terrible, terrible phone call I had to make after Richard Conway and his amazing crew made all those miniatures in that whole ending. I remember I had to call London from Los Angeles and say, “Richard, we’re not going to use any of it.”

What I also heard at the time [after the failed test screenings] was that there was a thought to try and get Rodney Dangerfield to do the voice of the plant, I assume to make it funny. My intent for the plant was to have it [be] a strong antagonist. If it was funny, there would have been no threat to Seymour or Audrey.

The team had to figure out a new finale that would stay true to the story and give Seymour and Audrey a happy ending. The solution? Seymour would electrocute the plant during their final showdown, and he and Audrey would finally get to move somewhere that’s green. But sitting outside of their dream home would be a small Audrey II, smiling at the camera.

OZ: The idea [came together] very fast. Howard and I knew exactly what had to happen. That part where the little plant smiles at the end was because I couldn’t make it a happy ending. It had to have some danger to it…. We went back [to London] for a month to reshoot.

Made on a budget of $25 million, including $5 million that wound up on the cutting-room floor, the film was unleashed on the public on December 19, 1986. It wound up doubling its budget at the box office, earning $54 million globally. A modest success, it didn’t necessarily set the world on fire—at least, not right away.

ARNOLD: Tisha and I had a really interesting moment. We decided to go see the movie in Times Square. We sat behind these two girls that were watching it, and we were like, “Okay, let’s go in when it’s dark so nobody recognizes us.”

CAMPBELL: There were these three girls sitting behind us. And [during the movie], they were like, “I’m so sick of these girls popping up out of nowhere!” [Laughs] So our first experience being able to watch our film, there were some haters behind us.

ARNOLD: Needless to say, I don’t watch myself anymore.

MENKEN: “Mean Green Mother” was nominated for the Academy Award, which was just amazing. I remember Levi Stubbs came out and sang “Mean Green Mother.” And I'm thinking, Wow! Well, I have a speech in my pocket. Who knows? Howard just goes, “Alan, you can relax. You’re not giving any speeches tonight.”

OZ: [When it came to home video], that’s when everything kind of exploded.

GREENE: [While currently touring with the movie], I get to see their love of Little Shop, their love of Audrey, even their love of me. I just hug everybody because I know they want it.

WEEKS: I went to see [Ellen on the tour recently]. It brought me to tears a couple of times…. They introduced me, and Ellen was surprised to see me. I was so happy to see her. We hugged and everything, and we talked a bit later on. But the warm reception that I got kind of brought me to tears because they were like, “Oh my gosh, it’s Ronette!” It just made me feel seen.

ARNOLD: I always said Little Shop of Horrors was a movie done before its time. But it stood the test of time and is still standing.

MENKEN: It is a true musical, and it’s driven by high-energy pop and rock, and it’s not about, “Oh, look how hip it is.”… It’s a thrill ride that really moves fast.

GEFFEN: I thought it was a terrific movie when we made it. I was very unhappy that we changed the ending…. I always wanted to restore it. And, as you know, we did. [In 2017 a version of the movie with the original ending played in theaters.]

OZ: I finally got to call Richard before he retired, saying, “Richard, finally they’re going to see your work.”

MORANIS: It’s become a point of discussion for people, whether they prefer one to the other, or if they’ve seen both of them. It’s prompted a couple of extra releases of different versions of the film.

OZ: I think [it resonates] because when you look at the movie, you know it’s not CGI…. It’s nice to show people what could be done when a lot of human beings get together and make something.
