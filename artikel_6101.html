Delta CEO’s Cope: Ed Bastian Says Doesn’t Need Starlink, Current Wi-Fi Good Enough

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Delta Air Lines CEO Ed Bastian says “customers are not choosing airlines based on the Wi-Fi.” That’s a remarkable claim from an airline that’s spent years promoting free internet as a reason to choose its premium experience. Speaking at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum in Detroit on Monday, Bastian defended passing on Starlink and waiting for Amazon Leo which is still unproven and whose timetable is now in question. He downplayed competitors’ progress, argued Delta’s current Viasat service comes close enough, and expressed confidence that Amazon would match or beat Starlink. It was pure cope. Delta doesn’t plan to begin installing Amazon until 2028 and that’s if the service works and delivers on schedule. United and Southwest both plan to finish their Starlink installations in 2027, and American is adding the service as well (so are Alaska and even Frontier). Delta’s Wifi Was Fine When Fewer People Used It Delta’s Viasat internet was fine when a few people on each flight were paying to use it. Make it free, get a much larger share of the plane online, and the experience can become unusable. I wrote about this last December after flying Delta to Las Vegas. The system repeatedly bogged down until nothing would load. I’d rather pay for internet that works than get free internet that doesn’t. Bandwidth has to be shared. Removing the price doesn’t create more capacity, but it gives a lot more people a reason to connect. A connection that works well with light use just isn’t the same thing – and Starlink’s low earth orbit satellites simply give the signal less distance to travel, delivering almost no latency. Bastian points to two new Viasat satellites. ViaSat-3 F3 entered service in August, and F2 entered service in September. But Delta’s own current wifi explanation acknowledges that performance varies with customer demand and satellite capacity, with less consistent service in high-demand or capacity-constrained areas. The airline knows this problem exists, which is why they’re touting Amazon’s service as a future improvement. United And Southwest Are Already Flying Starlink Bastian suggests most airlines promoting Starlink haven’t started yet. He doesn’t identify which airlines he’s counting. Here’s what the competitors actually say: United Airlines: More than half its daily flights already have Starlink. It expects nearly 64% by year-end and completion of installations by the end of 2027. Southwest Airlines: Its latest quarterly filing confirms equipped aircraft are already in service, with the full fleet expected to be finished in fall 2027. Last week it publicized gaming and livestreaming aboard a flight from Austin to San Diego. Alaska and Hawaiian: The group’s October 26 installation update shows all 93 Embraer 175s, Hawaiian’s 18 Airbus A321neos and 24 Airbus A330s equipped, along with 16 Boeing 737 MAX aircraft. Even Southwest expects to have Starlink throughout its fleet before Delta starts putting Amazon on its first 500 aircraft. That’s an awkward comparison for the airline charging a premium when it’ll be universal on the only U.S. airline without first class even. Wifi Doesn’t Have To Decide Every Ticket To Cost Delta Money I explained last month that I’ve avoided United and Southwest when I couldn’t count on functional internet. Starlink changes that calculation. I’ll pay more to work onboard because losing several productive hours costs more than the airfare savings. Recon Analytics finds wifi ranks among the top five business-trip airline selection factors for 32.6% of flyers, and first for 6.5%. Even among leisure travelers, 3.8% rank it first. Delta doesn’t need to lose everyone. It can lose a few expensive trips from customers who fly often, buy premium seats and carry airline credit cards. That’s precisely the business it wants. United is already using wifi to recruit those customers. It says its wifi-focused status match campaign produced a tenfold increase in daily applications. Amazon Still Has To Deliver. Bastian says that when Delta gets Amazon Leo, “we’ll be at least as good as Starlink if not better.” He hasn’t demonstrated that. Amazon needs enough satellites in the right orbits, working aircraft equipment, certification, and an airline installation program. It then has to deliver consistently with actual passengers using it. Delta’s own materials make the 2028 start subject to technical, certification and deployment milestones. And 2028 is the start of installations on an initial 500 aircraft. As I covered yesterday, Sunday’s failed Vulcan launch lost 40 Amazon satellites. We don’t yet know whether that pushes back Delta’s rollout. But the risk extends beyond replacing those satellites: Amazon contracted for 38 Vulcan missions to launch more than 1,500 of them. Amazon’s prelaunch photo shows the satellites stacked for that mission. Bastian’s account of testing Starlink is consistent with reporting from 2022. Delta had an early look at the product. It chose different commercial terms. I’ve covered the dispute over Delta’s branded portal. And last month Bastian said the branding disagreement out loud: “(Elon) thought it should be called Starlink on the planes. I said ‘No, it’s gotta be Delta.’” Amazon could offer a broader deal involving cloud computing and entertainment. Delta also gets to keep directing passengers through its own platform, where it promotes partners and sells them things. But a cheaper contract has to save enough to cover the business lost while passengers wait. If someone moves expensive flights to United, earns status there and starts spending on a United credit card, Delta may have to buy that loyalty back long after Amazon finally arrives. What customers consider to be “premium” is an ever changing thing. What exactly is so premium about Delta as compared to others? On time performance? It’s surely not the food. FAs are as much of a mixed bag as at UA/AA.

Even if DL wifi is usable, it’s certainly not thought of as premium. Starlink is simply excellent and is thought of as premium. Therefore, it would make sense for a brand that wants to be associated with the word premium to actually associate themselves with a brand that stands for premium and excellence.

The “it can’t also say Starlink” argument is absurd bunk. Maybe @TD will try to argue again that it doesn’t even say Airbus or Boeing on DL’s website (spoiler alert, it does).

The fact that DL did not choose Starlink is completely indefensible. Full stop. We know this to be the case because (other than B6, which barely counts) literally every other major airline in the world has signed on with Starlink. I think it’s around 50 of them at this point?

So DL is going to be an outlier… why? Because it’s CEO didn’t like having to bend the knee to Elon Musk? I mean, sure, personally, that’s a hard ask. But as a business matter? Not a close call.

So DL’s plan is to wait a few more years for Amazon to come online? Amazon may be many things, but it is not thought of as premium. It’s utilitarian. So even once that happens, they will have associated themselves with a non-premium brand. Why?

If DL wants folks to think of them as utilitarian, and wants to switch places with AA, that’s up to them I guess. But one would think the folks at Amex would be ringing alarm bells over this, and that someone at Amex is making some well placed phone calls to try and stop this madness.

Now, @TD will tell us that there’s no data anyone can point to, blah blah blah. What that has to do with brand perception is beyond me. And brand perception that translates into credit card spend is literally the entire ballgame!

@TD will also say that DL has more wifi throughout its fleet today or whatever and UA still doesn’t have Starlink on the widebodies, blah blah blah. How you can criticize a company for rapidly installing an excellent service throughout its fleet is beyond me. UA will be done installing Starlink in 2027, well before DL installs anything relating to Amazon – and does anyone seriously believe 2028 is achievable for more than a handful of installations just to say that they install something in 2028?

@Gary is 100% correct that Starlink will absolutely drive some consumer behavior here. And corporate behavior – shockingly corporations want their employees to have access at all times to internet that is high speed and works! DL’s actions here are borderline insane.

Is DL suddenly going to be unprofitable because of this? Of course not. Inertia is a powerful thing. But why DL would surrender an inch of premium association is beyond me. And that ultimately may have an impact on the bottom line – maybe not tomorrow, but in the weeks, months and years to come.

Finally, spare me the morality play. Competition is a good thing of course, and it’s good that Amazon is trying to be a viable competitor. But corporations have a responsibility to make sensible business decisions that enhance their profits, not to ensure competition. Amazon is simply not a competitor today. The airlines do everything possible to reduce competition at their hubs, they know the playbooks well. DL gets exactly zero moral brownie points for going with Amazon.

It’s not as though Delta’s wifi is going to be bad, it just won’t be anywhere near as good as its competitors and that’s where the VERY artificial delta premium brand starts to fall apart as customers start realizing how much is normal or worse at Delta vs competitors.

Delta still needs to work on replacing Gogo before they can even think about how great their ViaSat is. They’re way behind the industry in still even offering Gogo as a wifi product.

But trying to make the claim that ViaSat is as good as Starlink is just idiotic and even Delta knows that or they wouldn’t be upgrading already. ViaSat was much better than United’ satellite Panasonic when it rolled out and now Starlink is much better than Viasat. This isn’t that tough but a certain someone will probably lose his mind over this.

I’m aboard a Virgin transatlantic flight this moment using Starlink. Delta owns 49 % of Virgin and has no problem with it.
