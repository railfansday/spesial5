However space has lots of energy travelling through it.

This is the key part of the equasion. An astronaut floating in space facing the sun will have hundreds of degrees of heat pummelling him on the side of the sun, and almost no heat at all on the other side. His face will melt and his arse will freeze. This is of course ignoring thermal transfer through the astronauts body itself.

It needs to expel lots of energy to avoid it melting into slag. Unfortunately if its in the sun, it means it will struggle radiating heat because the temperatures from direct energy transfering into it is so much higher than what it wants to radiate. It can only radiate heat, and its best placed to do that shielded from the sun.

In other words it needs its solar panels in the sun where its super hot and its radiator panels in darkness where its super cold. True, but we're talking about Earth orbit, which isn't anywhere near a perfect vacuum. At 500 km the atmosphere is around a thousand Kelvin and any solar wind particles you run into are in the tens of thousands up to a million or so.

What you have for instance are photons from the Cosmic Background radiation, whose temperature is 2.7 K. But you also have cosmic rays, whose temperature can go into billions of degrees (Fahrenheit, Kelvin, Celsius, does not matter in this case). And because they don't interact with each other, they don't give you an average temperature.

That means that Space is essentially without any temperature, but that means in turn that you can't lose heat by heat exchange, because for that, you would need particle interaction. All that's left is Black Body radiation, and at temperatures around 300 K, you get a radiation of around 450 Watts per square meter of surface. And be aware that none of the surfaces should face each other and especially, all have to face away from the Sun.
