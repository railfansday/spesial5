Leonard Clark: Explorer, Spy, Liar

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Born in 1907, Leonard Francis Clark was an explorer, war hero, and writer who made daring expeditions all over the world before dying mysteriously in Venezuela in 1957.

Today, he isn’t well known, but has this lack of scrutiny been a blessing in disguise for this forgotten explorer’s legacy? His books, filled with adventure and excitement, read like fiction. The more one digs into the forgotten reports and old newspaper articles, the stranger his story, and the more complicated the man.

A Tibetan expedition under Leonard Clark in 1949. Photo: The Marching Wind

The main sources on Leonard Clark are all, well, Leonard Clark. This isn’t necessarily damning, but it is something he shares with most good con artists or cult leaders. The following is a short biography based only on official records, such as draft cards and census data. The first rule of reading historical accounts is that every historical figure is a liar.

Leonard Francis Clark was born in British Columbia, Canada, in January 1907 to two Americans, Maud and Francis Clark. In 1909, they moved back to Maud’s birth state of California, and Francis promptly died.

There are conflicting statements online about which university he attended, but census data shows him living only a few kilometers from the University of California (now the University of California, Berkeley) in 1930, so I’d put my money on that one. He began traveling right out of college, spending three years in Borneo on a hunting trip with his brother, Laurence. By the late 1930s, he was on a National Geographic-funded expedition to the South China Sea.

His article, cryptically titled Among the Big Knot Lois of Hainan, was published in the 1938 September edition. In it, he recounts how he and a small expeditionary force landed in the midst of a deadly cholera epidemic. Relying on hired porters to carry their supplies, Clark and Nicol Smith, another future travel writer, set off into the interior. Soon, malaria and infection laid low most of the team, including, per Clark’s article, Smith.

Again, according to Clark, Smith urged him to take the remaining five porters, the minimum of equipment, and keep going.

Much of the article is dedicated to ethnographic observations, with the typical exoticism and patronizing tone of the era. At one point, Clark noted that the Li people of Hainan reminded him of indigenous Amazonian people and “the men were tall, many six feet.”

A recent study showed that the average height for men in Hainan today is 168.7cm, around 5’6″, and the same study found that average heights had increased over the past century. This is a small nitpick, but he’ll repeat this unlikely claim again in the Amazon. It is worth noting that the “six-foot-tall Indian warrior” was a stock character in contemporary pulp adventure fiction.

There is an interesting contrast between Clark’s account and some of the newspaper reports, especially one from a Virginia paper, which relied on Smith for the story. This article implies Smith did much more than lay around his tent with malaria. Clark’s article claims he was the first white man to enter 47 different villages, while the newspaper says that Smith was the first Westerner to penetrate inland.

There is a third source: the 37-minute silent film compiled from the expedition’s footage and photographs. In it, we briefly see a white man lying under a mosquito net as if ill. But whether this is Smith, Clark — who admits himself to experiencing malaria symptoms on the expedition — or the third westerner on the expedition, one Jack Munroe, the film doesn’t say.

His previous experience in the region led him to return a few years later, this time on behalf of the U.S. Office of Strategic Services (OSS), a precursor to the CIA. His work during the war centered around the China-Burma-India corridor, particularly the Yellow River valley. He was involved in organizing guerrilla activities, building intelligence networks, and navigating the Japanese surrender of Taiwan.

In a later press statement, Clark explained how he had worked with General Dai Li, and that “the Reds had it in for him,” due to the infamously repressive actions Dai Li took against Chinese Communists. Dai Li, whom historian Barbara Tuchman called “China’s combination of Himmler and J. Edgar Hoover,” was killed in a 1946 plane crash. Wide speculation places the blame for this crash on either the Chinese Communist Party or the OSS. This would make things awkward for Clark a few years later.

He continued his government work in the area after the Second World War, sliding right into the developing Cold War. In 1949, Clark led a large, well-funded expedition into Tibet.

It would have gotten him in the history books if Leonard Clark identified a peak higher than Everest. Unfortunately for him, he didn’t. But he certainly proposed that he had, when he claimed that he’d surveyed Amne Machin and found it to be 9,040m high.

Amne Machin is not taller than Everest. Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Clark published his account of the expedition years later, titled The Marching Wind. In it, he wrote with his typical style: daring escapes, stereotypically aggressive natives, and breezy adventure writing. What it did not contain was the data to support his claims.

As soon as he released his first report and measurements, geographers, geologists, and mountaineers pored over them. Bradford Washburn, a cartographer, photographer, and explorer, published a thorough examination of the surveying techniques and questionable results from Clark’s expedition.

A contemporary review in the Alpine Journal, commenting on his photographic evidence, wrote that “anything more unconvincing could hardly be imagined — yet, ostensibly…the mountain was the object of the expedition.” More on that “ostensibly” later. Suffice it to say, Clark’s estimate that Amne Machin could be almost 1,000m taller than Everest was not adopted by geographers at large.

The review concluded that “it can hardly be wondered if mountaineers, whom Mr. Clark rather despises, cannot accept his results.”

Clark blithely remarked that he had “some rather bulky complaints by mountain and other specialists,” but admitted subpar instruments made his results “indecisive.”

A surveying party including Clark, far right, taking observations of Amne Machin. Photo: The Marching Wind

If he doesn’t seem too broken up, it may be because Amne Machin’s height wasn’t the main purpose of his trip. Clark was supported logistically and went on behalf of Ma Bufang, the fiercely anti-communist warlord ruling Qinghai, on the border of Tibet. Anticipating that he could not long hold out against the People’s Liberation Army, Clark proposed that his expedition would map a path of retreat. This would help Ma Bufang’s army cross into Tibet and continue their anti-Communist campaign from the mountains.

Clark envisioned Ma Bufang’s Qinghai, through Tibet, becoming “the bulwark against Red invasion of India.” Clark went on the expedition and did his mapping, but the PLA took Qinghai only months later, and Ma Bufang fled to Hong Kong.

In his introduction to his book, Clark claims that the United States was unaware of his “very slight aid” to Ma Bufang and local anti-Communist guerrilla activities, and he involved himself with the Chinese Nationalist army “as a private American citizen.” If I were to say that I was skeptical of this claim, I would not be the first to do so. But that falls under speculation, so I will say only that I sent a Freedom of Information Act request to the CIA regarding Clark’s work in Tibet. However, the records were not forthcoming.

After returning from the mountains, Clark went to a wedding anniversary celebration in Canton, surrounded by his fellow Allied intelligence members and their set. Things got a little out of hand, and Clark soon found himself in a Cantonese jail with a bullet wound and two manslaughter charges.

Chinese Nationalist soldiers on Clark’s Tibetan expedition. Photo: The Marching Wind

The other two men involved were Harold Harris, an Englishman who died immediately, and Willard Freeman from Massachusetts, who died later from his injuries. Clark also sustained a gunshot wound, but had recovered sufficiently by late November to be moved from hospital to prison.

The three had been drinking together in Freeman’s home when, at about 4 am, servants reported hearing gunshots. Mrs. Maya Freeman entered the room at 10 am to find an odd and grisly scene. Harris, fully clothed, lay dead on Freeman’s bed with three gunshot wounds. Freeman, completely nude, was also lying on the bed, alive with two shots to the abdomen. Clark, in his underwear, was on a second bed, with a single gunshot to the chest. Furniture was overturned, indicating a fight, and all three were covered in scratches. The only weapon found was Clark’s .32 caliber pistol.

Later that month, the Canton District Court denied Clark’s bail request. The court, still trying to determine what exactly happened, focused on the widowed Maya Freeman. Interviewing her for hours, they attempted to make her confess to an affair with Clark. Both of them repeatedly denied it. Clark further denied that he had fired any of the six shots, though he didn’t name the shooter.

Clark’s brother contacted former OSS head General William Donovan, who rallied the State Department. Clark reportedly was concerned that if he wasn’t released before the city fell to the Communists, he would be tortured for information regarding Dai Li and OSS activities. The State Department investigated and, thanks to its intervention, Clark was released.

Clark’s legal troubles were splashed across newspapers worldwide. Photo: The Journal (Caldwell, Ohio) November 24, 1949

What exactly happened in Freeman’s house that night? Was the dispute between the Allied Intelligence men personal, or professional? If the world were ever to find out, it wouldn’t be from Clark. Once freed, Clark returned to California and moved on to his next adventure. It was an ambitious one: Leonard Clark was going to discover the lost city of El Dorado.

He had an old parchment map to the lost city, he claimed, which showed it lay in the upper reaches of Peru. Never mind that it had been pretty well established for over a century that El Dorado was a myth, following the efforts of explorers Alexander von Humboldt and Robert Schomburgk.

Clark’s resulting account, The Rivers Ran East, contains all the genre tropes you would expect from a fictional work in the same setting. Native Amazonians are ferocious cannibals who capture Clark and keep him in a poison snake pit, crocodiles can and must be lassoed (there are no crocodiles in Peru, only caimans, a type of alligator), and there is a beautiful lady explorer, Inez Pokorny, who is secretly part of a London-based international El Dorado-seeking organization. If this organization existed, I have found no trace of it outside of this book.

If I were to list every blatant impossibility in his account, we would be here for days, so I’ll just continue on one particular thread: Leonard Clark seems to have a very loose grasp of which animals are in Peru and how big they are. At one point, he is blinded by a venom-spitting snake. There are three venom-spitting snake species, and none of them are in South America.

His 500-pound (227kg) boars must be peccaries, pig-like animals native to the Americas. The Amazon native white-lipped peccary tops out around 40kg, while the unconfirmed giant peccary still has only been estimated at 50kg. Likewise, his 12m-long pythons must be anacondas, which have a maximum recorded length of eight meters.

Clark claims to have found El Dorado. What he found was a group of Chicham people panning for gold, who traded some of it to Clark and Pokorny. According to his book, Clark traveled under the pretense of investigating botanical medicines, while actually searching for El Dorado.

In fact, as the El Dorado adventures on the page grew more and more ridiculous, I began to wonder if it were not the other way around. Here I have fully entered the realm of speculation. American corporations were, and still are, extremely interested in extracting resources from South America, including pharmaceuticals. In fact, Clark also reported success in this area, claiming to have identified over 60 native medicinals with commercial possibility — as well as Amazonian oil.

In praise of Clark’s work, Baron Wolfenegg, Director of Colonization for the Orient in Peru, accidentally said the quiet part out loud, stating he’d “learned much from this book that will help my work of colonizing this vast jungle.”

On Peru’s Perené River. Photo: The Rivers Ran East

It is practically gauche for explorers to die in their beds, and Clark, who seemed to mold his life to the shape of adventure stories, was no exception. Clark was in Venezuela in 1957, looking, he claimed, for diamond mines. He entered the Amazon basin, never to emerge again.

One history of the OSS offhandedly mentions that Clark died with “two native arrows in his back.” I suspect the explorer’s penchant for sensationalism survived his death. Other contemporary reports describe Clark as having drowned when his canoe overturned.

As I pointed out earlier, most of the information on Clark’s expeditions came from Clark himself. Since he never returned, startlingly little information is available about his death at age 49.

The area around the Orinoco River in Venezuela is, in fact, rich in diamond deposits. Photo: Wikimedia Commons

The line between fact and fiction is notoriously vague in much of the adventure travel genre. Is Herman Melville telling the full truth about his time spent on the island of Nuku Hiva? Did anyone actually complete the “Long Walk” out of a Soviet detention camp, which Slavomir Rawicz falsely claimed as his own tale? How much artistic license and embellishment is acceptable before exploration accounts become outright hoaxes?

I didn’t write this article with the goal of proving Leonard Clark a charlatan. It can hardly be denied that he really was going to these places, at the very least. But there are enough discrepancies and inaccuracies in his accounts to conclude that they should be taken with a grain of salt, and his treasure-hunting pseudo-archaeological tendencies make his anthropological findings, well, questionable.

As a contemporary New Yorker book critic put it, Clark had “the same gift, or weakness, for grandiose verbiage that got Marco Polo in trouble with his fellow Venetians.”

The real story, and one which may never be fully disentangled, lies in why, exactly, Clark was doing his exploring. Somewhere between the romantic adventurer and the cynically colonialist spy using exploration as a guise for the extractive, violent enforcement of Western capitalist hegemony, lies the truth.

“Every explorer has two faces,” Clark wrote in the opening to The Rivers Ran East; “the secret one and the one he shows to the world.”
