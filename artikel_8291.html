On Climbing Mount Robson’s Emperor Face Alone

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

High on the Emperor Ridge of Mount Robson, enjoying lunch in the sun. (Photo: Anton Korsun)

There is a cinema up north. Beyond the rising northern star, beyond the Icefields Parkway, and far beyond the chaos and clamor of the Banff Mountain Film Festival—it is built in the image of a gothic cathedral and shrouded in storm. This cinema operates year round. It has no dress code, schedule, or rules on serving alcohol to minors. Inside, every visitor watches their own film: Some see triumph and elation. Others see despair, retreat, or a kaleidoscope of rock and ice before the front doors shut forever and the silver screen goes deathly black. Yet others simply stand alone in its great white hall, listening to the sound of silence and watching mountain ranges stretch to the horizon like limbs of a great white spider.

This is a film review of sorts. In September of 2026 I soloed Infinite Patience on the Emperor Face of Mount Robson. I’ve been asked to write about it. I’ve been asked why I haven’t posted anything on social media or pontificated my radness to the press. Frankly, it’s because I am a rather poor film critic.

There is no doubt in my mind that alpine climbing is more art than sport.

If I were to ask you—how did Usain Bolt run 100 meters in 9.58 seconds?

“Well, he moved his legs quite fast,” you might say.

But can you tell me how Voytek Kurtyka and Robert Schauer suffered up the Shining Wall of Gasherbrum IV (7,925m) in a 10-day sleep-and-food-deprived push? Or how Steve House and Marko Prezelj clawed their way to the summit of the North Twin with one missing boot and two coreshot ropes? Because I can’t.

I sit in awe of the performers on stage as they pull another rabbit from their hats or escape the icy chains of some far-flung north face yet again. The beauty of alpinism is similar to the beauty of theater, dance, and stage magic—an illusion of wizardry brought about by true mastery of the performers.

However, while similar, alpinism and theater are not the same. Climbing has no audience and no stage. Climbing media has both. In recent times it has become too easy to convince ourselves that perception equals participation—that by clicking that “follow” button or watching that film we can stand in the shoes of the artist. But we can’t. Because to believe otherwise would be to mistake climbing as performing art for climbing as performance art. The former is the way the ascent is portrayed in words, photographs, or film. The latter—what was seen, felt, thought and feared in the moment—is privy only to the performers themselves.

I often find myself wondering about how the way an ascent is communicated influences the audience’s perception of what actually happened. This can range from flying in a big-budget film crew, to more subtle cues, like how a photo was cropped for Instagram, or whether one was shared at all. These words themselves are guilty, although I try to segregate my act of climbing from my act of writing about it.

My reluctance to share much about the climb stems from a fear that this blade is sharp on both sides—that the audience’s media-driven perception can in turn influence the climber’s own perception of the experience. Much like how the behaviour of quantum particles changes when they’re observed, climbers too, change our behaviour. Our egos feed off validation. Our eye becomes drawn to the viewfinder or to the stopwatch. We feel the presence of the audience cut through the ridgetop wind. We hear their applause over the crash of séracs in the distance.

I watched The Alpinist on the big screen when it was first released. While I never met Marc-André Leclerc, it left me feeling that much more went unsaid—that the real movie of his life was seen through his eyes only. A movie no one can rewind.

But anything is possible in cinema. In his account of Infinite Patience, Marc-André describes being unable to take a photo of a beautiful sunrise because his camera batteries had failed—“the shadow of Mt. Robson extending forever into the horizon against a red sky.”

From my sleeping perch high on the Emperor Ridge, I awoke to the same scene. Waves of red and gold crashed against the east face of Whitehorn Mountain while Robson’s summit pyramid shadowed the valley thousands of feet below. Was this movie magic or something more? Feeling as if I was suddenly not alone, I pulled out my phone in disbelief and took that photo. We can’t rewind each other’s films, but sometimes life lets us watch them together.

Nonetheless, I’m saddened to be left alone with my experience. My words are a lousy companion and my photographs are only worth a thousand lousy words. All I know is that I watched my film on the summit of Mount Robson. Where are you watching yours?
