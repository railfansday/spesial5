Days 17–18: O Cebreiro, Fonfría, and a NERO

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

We could have eaten breakfast at the open café in La Faba as we walked past, but we decided to hold out for O Cebreiro. The steep climb up into town wasn’t as difficult as I remembered from 2017, but the village itself was clearly structured around tourist buses—and they appeared to have all left with their tourists. Cafés were shutting down, their tables piled with dirty dishes. “Go away!” one told us bluntly when we asked for a menu.

Trying every spot in sight, we finally landed back in the first one we had gone in, but now the kitchen was closed. We came back because it was the only open café that hadn’t locked its doors or shooed us out. Unfortunately, the power was off as an electrician was fiddling around in an open junction box. Our reward for the long morning climb was cold tuna empanada and cold bottled drinks—truly the saddest breakfast on the Camino. But we were happy for it, as the proprietor had also tried to send us away.

The beauty of the way into O Cebreiro was not matched by its hospitality

One bright spot was meeting Steve from Texas. I could tell by his distinct speech cadence that he was a practiced public speaker. It turned out he was a recently retired Church Administrator. Since I had served as both an executive pastor and senior pastor, we had plenty of shop to talk about. Steve had started way back in Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port, but after sustaining an injury, he was taking it slowly and relying on luggage forwarding to keep moving forward. He also spoke fluent Spanish and had intervened on our behalf when the lady was trying to tell us she couldn’t serve us because the power was out. “Isn’t there something cold they could have?”

The rest of the way, as they say, was downhill. We chose the older, traditional mountain path—the one I had walked back in 2017—rather than the newer route alongside the busy highway. It was serene and peaceful until it eventually rejoined the main road. Once we descended down O Cebreiro, The Way paralleled the pavement from a respectable half-mile distance, winding through quiet farm fields where the sound of traffic couldn’t reach us.

We reached Fonfría in late afternoon to stay at A Reboleira Hostel. Having arrived late, our private room was given away, but the hosts generously opened up an unused section of the dorm just for us, letting us pick our bunks. A few other pilgrims trickled in later making a total of 4 in a room built for 24, but it was so quiet we felt as though we had the entire place to ourselves.

Dinner was served in a traditional palloza—a round, thatched stone building identical to the ancient structures atop O Cebreiro. At least fifty pilgrims gathered on common tables. We sat with Charlie and Sarah, enjoying great conversation and getting to know them better, though sadly, our paths would diverge after that night.

The next day was a NERO—a “Near-Zero” rest day where we only had a short hour-and-a-half stroll on the trail, meaning there was no rush to get up and out early.

It was a wonderfully slow morning: breakfast, coffee, more toast, and more coffee. The Wi-Fi was fast and the café was warm, so why hurry? We rolled into O Biduedo by lunchtime and sat outside waiting for check-in at Casa Quiroga, watching the steady parade of pilgrims march past while I caught up on the sad state of world affairs via the New York Times on my phone.

Look carefully and you’ll see the cows coming home

As we sat there, local farmers herded cows down the narrow lane past us, only to return later, depositing fresh layers of manure across the pavement. In a way, it felt remarkably like reading the news, at least the manure part. I reminded myself of an old trail truth: It is good to bring the Camino home, but it is not good to invite home onto the Camino. Reading the news was precisely that—inviting the chaos of home into this sacred space. I hadn’t checked the headlines this whole trip, and I instantly regretted breaking the fast.

At Casa Quiroga, we checked into a lovely private room, ate, napped, and stepped out to explore the quiet hamlet. We spent the afternoon dodging fresh piles of cow manure and sneaking into an open, empty albergue to get our second stamp for the day. On the Camino, you are expected to collect two stamps daily in your credential, which can prove quite a logistical puzzle when resting in a tiny mountain village!

Back on pilgrim-watching duty outside, we met several friendly Australian couples who recounted stories of their cruises along the wild coast of Western Australia.

Later over dinner, Suzanne and I mapped out every relocation across our 40 years of married life. This Camino trek is our 40th-anniversary gift to each other, and with our house back home currently on the market as we contemplate another move, I wondered what wisdom we could extract from our past transitions.

We had never realized it until mapping it out on paper, but a clear pattern emerged: Suzanne was almost always the one who pulled us forward into a new chapter—leading us to Southwestern University, Ashesi University College in Ghana, back to Southwestern, and back to Ashesi again. Meanwhile, my role was recognizing when the Spirit was releasing us from a place or calling us to step down from an occupation. She provides the trajectory forward; I discern the moment to let go.

That’s a quiet blessing of the Camino, she longs to teach you things if you take the time to listen.
