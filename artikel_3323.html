Eight Hacks Every Tidal User Should Know

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

I recently switched from Apple Music to Tidal, and I'm happy I made the change. The main reason is AI-made music: Tidal has stricter AI filtering than any other major streamer. The second reason is audio quality. While all major music streaming services do basically the same thing, Tidal's focus has always been on streaming music at the highest possible quality. While other streaming services have more or less "caught up" in terms of pure sonic fidelity, Tidal still is better at getting that quality to your speakers, if you know a few tricks.

If you're switching from Apple Music or Spotify to Tidal, you probably want to bring your playlists with you. Tidal points new users toward third-party transfer tools like TuneMyMusic and Soundizz. They both have a free tier, but they only transfers a few hundred songs gratis. My playlists are way bigger than that. The easy solution is to grumble and sign up for a month, then cancel when your tracks are transferred, but that didn't sit well with me. A playlist is just a collection of song names, so why should I have to pay anything to transfer it? To solve the problem, I came up with a simple script that transfers playlists from Apple Music and Spotify to Tidal. I will not be charging a fee. Here's how it works:

Download your playlist as a .txt file. You can do this directly from Apple Music under File>Library>Export Playlist. You can't from Spotify, but the free site Exportify will download any of your playlists as a spreadsheet file you can save.

Install Python on your system. Double click "install certificates" in the folder that opens after installation.

Download this script. Save it in the same file as your playlist.

Run the script: (For Mac, open Terminal. For Windows, go to the Command prompt. Type python3 on a Mac or py on Windows.) Drag the script into the terminal window and press enter. It should ask you to drag your downloaded playlist into the window next.

Sign in to Tidal. Hit "Approve" to give permission for Tidal to run the script.

Have a banana. The script is going to search for all the songs on your playlist and match them to Tidal's library, so it can take a little while. I tested it with a playlist from Apple Music with 2,397 songs. It took about 20 minutes and found 2,362 of my tracks, a 99% success rate.

Fix the leftovers: The program creates two separate text files for "orphans." One is songs it couldn't find and one is songs it might be worth double-checking manually.

If everything goes well, your large playlist should be available on Tidal.

Note: The script uses a community-created library to talk to Tidal, so it could stop working if Tidal changes things up.

Like most streaming services, Tidal has a "heart" button called "add to my collection." You can click on any track, album, or playlist to "like" and saved it. But Tidal also has a separate, somewhat mysterious "Save for Later" option that puts songs, albums, and playlists into a separate collection. There are a couple reasons for this. First, it keeps a running list of new-to-you music waiting for you on a rainy day, and secondly, it (presumably) affects your recommendation algorithm differently. While Tidal isn't open about every detail of its recommendation algorithm, I assume "save for later" has less weight (if any) on your future recommendations than "add to my collection."

On Tidal, tracks that are 100% AI-generated get an "AI" badge, earn no royalties, and are never placed on a Tidal-curated playlist. Users can flip a switch in Settings to either allow or disallow AI content, too. Simple, but there's a bit of nuance that goes beyond the on/off switch. You have to set the AI-toggle separately on each device you use Tidal on, even if it's the same account. I guess this is so I can listen to AI music in my car but not at my desk? Sadly, AI detection is not a science, so some AI-made, and partly AI-made, tracks may still get through.

If you're streaming music from a music app through Bluetooth, the output is automatically compressed to a lower quality, and your phone stays in charge, so a phone call or a notification can interrupt your music. But Tidal's Tidal Connect feature skips the middleman. Music streams directly from your wifi to your speaker and your phone becomes a remote control, so you can take a call, go for a walk, and even run out of phone battery and the music will keep playing.

Have an external device with Tidal Connect compatibility.

Make sure your speaker or other output device and phone or computer are on the same wifi network.

Click the icon again to switch back to your phone.

Plenty of wifi speakers don’t support Tidal Connect, so check before you buy. Tidal keeps the full list of supported devices on its site. There's everything there from WiiM Mini—a $99 small box that adds Tidal Connect to speakers you already own—to dCS components that cost more than a car.

Tidal Connect isn't the only way to get the highest quality audio from Tidal. You can go for an old school, wired path like I have. I play audio from my desktop and route it directly to a mixer via USB. Here's the set-up:

Settings > Streaming > Audio Quality: Max.

Sound Output: choose your mixer as the output device instead of your computer’s speakers.

Exclusive Mode: On. It’s under Sound Output > More Settings. Tidal sends audio straight to the mixer at each track’s native sample rate, without your computer resampling it or mixing in system sounds.

Force Volume. It's in the desktop app next to Exclusive Mode, under Settings > Streaming > Sound Output > More Settings. Force Volume means Tidal will always output at the highest volume. You'll be controlling the actual volume from your mixer.

To check what your mixer can handle, look at its maximum sample rate:

Mac: Open Audio MIDI Setup (in Applications > Utilities), click your mixer, and open the Format menu. The highest number listed is your ceiling.

Windows: Go to Settings > System > Sound, click your mixer, and look under Output settings > Format.

My cheap-ass mixer tops out a 16-bit/48kHz which is CD quality, but not Tidal's top of 24-bit/192kHz, but at least I can control the EQ. Speaking of...

It's much cooler to turn dials than mess with computer settings. Tidal doesn’t have an equalizer, so if you’re using Tidal Connect or a wired connection, you'll need something to shape the sound. Here are some options:

Your computer: System-wide EQ apps, like Equalizer APO on Windows or SoundSource on Mac, can adjust Tidal’s output. They don’t work with Tidal Connect though, which bypasses your computer’s audio processing.

Your speaker’s app: If you use a Tidal Connect speaker or streamer, it probably has a companion app for EQ.

A mixer or audio interface: Channel EQ knobs let you adjust bass, mids, and treble by hand, in real time.

Your speakers: Many powered speakers and studio monitors have low and high controls on the back.

A stereo receiver or amp: Old-school bass and treble knobs still work in 2026.

Most of Tidal's catalog streams as "better-than-CD quality" FLAC files at every subscription tier, so you might be inclined to click "play music at highest quality on everything." but settings are only as good as the gear you're using, and there are times when lower quality is the right choice. Here's when to turn down the settings:

When you're in your car: CarPlay and Bluetooth cut audio down to CD quality or less before it reaches your speaker, and with road noises, it usually doesn't matter anyway, so turn the streaming down to "High" instead of "Max." Or download your playlist before you leave, if you must have "as good as possible" audio.

You have a data cap and are off wifi. Top quality audio requires huge file transfer, so set your mobile data to High or Low and save Max for wifi.

Your connection is spotty: To avoid buffering, a lower setting keeps the music playing.

This isn't a universal hack, but some Android phones have microSD card slots, which can save you a lot of hassle if you're downloading a lot of audio files—an album at high quality might top 1GB, so having an external source will save you a ton of hassle. If your phone has a microSD card slot, go to Tidal's settings, find "download destination" and choose the memory card.
