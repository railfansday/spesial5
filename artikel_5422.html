Zeno Power makes progress on an underwater power system that will eventually go nuclear

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

by Alan Boyle on Nov 19, 2026 at 7:00 amNovember 19, 2026 at 8:01 am

Zeno Power has hit a major milestone in its push to build nuclear batteries for underwater use.

The startup said today that it successfully demonstrated an electrically heated Stirling generator in a controlled maritime setting in Washington state. The two-week demonstration produced electrical power outputs in excess of 20 watts, said Lindsey Boles, Zeno’s chief product officer.

“It’s a really big step for us — just solidifying our expertise in how to design these, and then confirming our ability to translate that into operational hardware that meets or exceeds what our customers need,” Boles told GeekWire.

Zeno is developing the power system with support from the Department of Defense as a follow-up to an initial $7.5 million contract awarded in 2023. The generator uses a Stirling engine to convert heat into electricity by cycling gas through heating and cooling phases in a closed cylinder.

For the recent demonstration, the prototype device relied on an electric heating element. Future devices will instead rely on heat generated by the radioactive decay of strontium-90, a radioisotope that can be extracted from spent nuclear fuel.

Nuclear batteries have been in use for decades, primarily for space missions ranging from the Apollo moon shots to NASA’s Voyager spacecraft and Mars rovers. But the batteries being developed by Zeno Power promise to be at least four times more efficient than traditional radioisotope thermoelectric generators, Boles said.

Boles said the non-radiological test exceeded expectations. “There’s a difference between a design that works on paper and hardware that works in the field,” she said in a news release. “This is the moment our team has been building toward: proof that this technology works outside the lab.”

Long-lasting nuclear batteries could be used to power undersea sensor networks or recharge autonomous underwater vehicles. “There are a lot of applications, especially on the maritime front, that we really want electricity for,” Boles said. “And we really don’t need a ton of it. But we need it to be really reliable, and we need it to be long endurance, and we need it to be self-managed. … I think of it like a slow Tesla supercharger on the seabed.”

Retired Navy Adm. John Richardson, former chief of naval operations and a member of Zeno’s board of directors, called the recent test an “important step toward undersea superiority.”

“Protecting maritime infrastructure and powering distributed systems at sea requires energy; energy that lasts for years, not days or months,” Richardson said in a written statement. “As competition intensifies in contested domains, powered subsea systems will help keep us ahead. Zeno Power has steadily reduced risk, proven its technology, built a supply chain, and prepared to scale production.”

Next year, Zeno Power plans to install a radioisotope heat source in its generator and test the system in a water tank under controlled laboratory conditions. If those tests are successful, Zeno will move forward with the first operational seabed deployment of its radioisotope-fueled maritime system later in 2027.

The maritime battery isn’t Zeno’s only product. The company is also working on a nuclear-powered heater system for spacecraft, with a demonstration on the lunar surface planned as early as 2028. Zeno’s broader vision is to provide long-lasting, reliable power on the moon and Mars, and in extreme environments on Earth.

Since its founding in 2018, Zeno has secured $105 million in private capital and has brought in $60 million in contracts with the Pentagon and NASA. The company has more than 95 employees at locations in Seattle, California and Washington, D.C., with the largest contingent working at its engineering headquarters in Seattle’s South Lake Union district.
