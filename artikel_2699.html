How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Wolves

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Game 7 of the Western Conference Semifinals against the Sacramento Kings. My dad was in the family room, having what looked to be some kind of religious experience. 13-year-old me walked in, alarmed and… concerned? He had always been a bit of a curmudgeon, and now here he was, dancing in front of the TV, on the verge of bursting into a full-blown "Sister Act" number. I had never seen this side of him before. Whatever had my dad so enraptured seemed like something I'd like to be a part of myself.

"I'll have what he's having," essentially.

In that game, the Big Ticket, Kevin Garnett, would put up a godly stat line of 32 points, 21 boards, five blocks, and four steals, leading the Minnesota Timberwolves over the Kings with a final score of 83-80. It was pandemonium in the Dooley household.

May 19th, 2004, was the date that I was baptized and purified in the waters of Wolves basketball.

They'd go on to play the Los Angeles Lakers in the Western Conference Finals, and all I really remember from that series was Game 6, with the Wolves down 2-3. My dad and I drove over to my grandma's house off Lake Nokomis in South Minneapolis for a Monday visit and to anxiously watch the most important game of the season.

In my grandma's living room, I sank helplessly in her old reclining sofa chair as Kareem "not Abdul-Jabbar" Rush came off the bench and unconsciously fired away haymaker after haymaker from deep. The man they called K-Rush would hit his first six threes before finally missing late in the fourth when the damage had already been done.

In just six games, the Wolves were sent packing by a relative no-name on a roster full of future Hall of Famers. As someone new to the sport, even I knew that this was almost certainly not on the scouting report. Little did I know at the time, though, that the K-Rushes of the league would turn out to be some of my favorite players. But that night at least, I found K-Rush to be a pretty big jerk.

After learning what it felt like to be punched in the gut by sports – and I must say, it didn't feel great – I was now impatiently waiting for the next season to kick off.

Actually, I was more than ready. The Wolves would be ready as well. I always loved a good story, and the Wolves' was a vengeance quest. They would return. And this time around, they would end up hoisting the trophy that, two weeks later, after the Detroit Pistons shellacked those no-good-dirty-rotten Lakers, I learned belonged to some dude named Larry O'Brien, former NBA commissioner and politician who, according to his Wikipedia page, "learned about politics at a young age."

That hardware was on my mind for the rest of the summer. "Hey, Patrick, wanna go to Sebastian Joe's with me and Sam?" Sorry, can't. Too busy thinking about the Larry O'Brien Trophy.

A female classmate of mine asked me if I wanted to be her boyfriend. After I had just learned about this huge summer event called the NBA draft? Sorry, Ali, I gotta study.

Before I knew it, summer had ended, fall was upon us, and the 2004-05 season was just around the corner. My first full season as a fan of the Minnesota Timberwolves.

The Minnesota Timberwolves join terrifying kids commercials as some of my worst nightmare fuel.

Your teenage years are hard enough as it is. I didn't make it any easier for myself.

I tuned into every game that '04-05 season, waiting for the good vibes to come, but they never did. The January 19th game in Los Angeles against the antagonistic Lakers was an especially nauseating experience. I lay in bed trying to multitask between half-sleeping and listening to Chad Hartman on the radio call. The lights remained on deep into the night. The game was a back-and-forth tilt, with neither team able to get any real separation.

In the end, though, my AM radio would taunt me with Hartman's defeated utterances underneath the static scratches, of missed shots on one end and made shots on the other. Neither were from the right team. It felt unjust. What did I do to deserve this?

Despite running it back, the Wolves just couldn't get over the hump that year, and by proxy, I felt like I couldn't either. This thing called "Wolves fandom" had me feeling sluggish and languid. Like that gut punch from Game 6 of the WCF, I was learning in real time what prolonged, sustained depression feels like. Every game had me on pins and needles. I'm pretty sure this was the year I developed insomnia. I started having frequent sleep paralysis episodes, and also some of the most bizarre nightmares you could imagine.

My dad and I attended the Wolves' final regular-season game that year against the San Antonio Spurs, a team that two months later would be crowned NBA champions. In perhaps their best showing of the season, the Wolves dominated, winning by 22 points.

Former Wolves project Ndudi Ebi had what, to this day, is still the worst airball I have ever seen on a three-point "attempt". He also brought the roof down with a ferocious dunk that had the whole backboard shaking. That was the joy of guys like that.

We were sitting in the lower level right behind the hoop, equal parts in awe and also concerned that we were about to bear witness to a Shaq-shattering-the-backboard moment. (Un)fortunately, that moment never came, and after the game had ended, we left the Target Center wondering what could have been. If they had just played like that all season, maybe things could've gone differently.

Trendy pre-season favorites to win it all, the Wolves missed the playoffs that year, only managing to go 44-38. The late great coach Flip Saunders was fired 51 games into the season, replaced by General Manager and very tall man Kevin McHale.

Little did I know that, over the next 14 years, when my age would double and my stubble would fully grow into a lumberjack beard, at least record-wise, 2005 would be the summit.

The years that followed were a fast, rocky descent down a black diamond, and I was a kid with two left skis, held together with duct tape and blind hope.

Wavering optimism in the circles of Hell, or the life of a Wolves fan.

Sometimes I felt I had made a terrible mistake.

I had unknowingly chained a handcuff I didn't even know I was wearing to a ball dribbled by jesters, malcontents, and egocentrics: Ricky Davis, Marko Jarić, future podcaster Rashad McCants, and some guy with two first names whose scoring was inexplicably halved (20.3 to 10.1) because, as I was starting to realize, the Wolves were a team that could never have nice things.

Dwane Casey was hired as head coach for the 2005-06 season, and then was disposed of a season and a half later. This team was a steaming pile of dung, and the joke was on me for locking myself in the barn, waiting for the heap to turn to roses.

Fast-forward to the summer of '08: "The Dark Knight" had just come out. I went to the Southdale theater to watch the blockbuster film my peers were touting as "the best movie ever", a performance by the late Heath Ledger that critics were lauding as "Oscar-worthy". They weren't wrong. It was quite transformative. I thought to myself, "This was the same guy who played that cowboy?"

Now, I never contemplated becoming the Joker. If it had been released a year earlier, however, I may have considered it.

The previous summer, Garnett had finally had enough and rather unceremoniously parted ways with the Wolves in a big "blockbuster" trade. Minnesota's return really only had two bright spots to note, the first obviously being Al "Big Al" Jefferson.

Defending dunk contest champion Gerald Green.

Under the tutelage of perpetually confused Randy Wittman, the 2007-08 Wolves would go 22-60. Make no mistake, they were god-awful. But during that season's all-star game festivities, something finally clicked for me.

At New Orleans Arena, Gerald Green participated in the Saturday night dunk contest to defend his title, ultimately going toe-to-toe with Dwight Howard, AKA "Superman". Green broke out all sorts of crazy dunks: the alley-oop windmill, a behind-the-backboard under-the-legs slam. But there was one dunk that I will never, ever forget.

The cupcake dunk had never been pulled off, let alone even attempted. It has still not been replicated, nor has anyone, to my knowledge, dared even try.

Though Green would go on to relinquish his title to Howard, that dunk was the talk of the night. And I truly believe the only reason he didn't get a perfect score on it was because the judges didn't see what we all saw.

And it got me thinking. On a rudderless team, both managed and saddled by an inept franchise, what else is there to see? What can I look for?

To start, I accepted the truth: this team wasn't winning anything anytime soon.

Now that actual expectations had been tabled, I could watch the Wolves without worrying about superfluous things like "the outcome of a game" or "where the Wolves sit in the standings". If they lost, well, that's okay. I expected them to. If they won, I was amused. I had even perfected my dad's patented half-smile.

I also became a more well-rounded basketball fan, always following a second team to supplement my thirst for promising development. After the Seattle SuperSonics moved to Oklahoma City in 2008, I started to follow the young upstart Thunder. Making the leap from 23 wins to 50 in just one year. As a Wolves fan, I could've felt envy, but instead I was like a proud stepfather. I was the fan that stepped up.

In 2012, when they made it all the way to the Finals only to lose to that villainous Miami Heat superteam, I took it personally. But it felt good to care. The stakes had never been higher. I could never feel that way about the Wolves, who were in a league of their own.

And I tried to see it all for what it was: just basketball. Like the Actor in Albert Camus' "The Myth of Sisyphus", I invested myself in the Wolves for two-ish hours every game night, and once the contest was over, it was back to business as usual. It was back to life as usual. I wouldn't let the Wolves define me. They wouldn't determine how I felt moving forward. Sometimes a game would end in heartbreaking fashion, and I would feel that tiny hammer starting to peck away at that thing in my chest. No. I would relent. I refuse to let it hurt.

Well, if I was built like this guy, this would have been way easier.

But eventually I had to interrogate something deep: Why did I start following this team in the first place?

I had chosen to attach myself to this team during some of the most formative years of my life. And despite the Wolves at times almost insisting that I leave them be, I could never.

My father has lived in Minneapolis his entire life. As have I. I guess you could say we are predisposed to Minnesota sports fandom. It's in our bloodstream. When I watched my father react so fervently to that epic Game 7 all those years ago, it felt like something I would like to share with him. Up to that point, we hadn't shared all that much.

That shared fandom extends far beyond the father-son bond. It's a communal thing. Minneapolis is Wolves territory. To have pride in them is just one of the ways I choose to have pride in my community, where I'm from and where I stay to this day.

In 2013, having just graduated from the University of St. Thomas, I discovered Canis Hoopus, another communal outlet for me to share my allegiance to Wolves basketball. That became a way for me to lean into the real truth I had been avoiding. Like it or not, the Wolves were now an integral part of me. And to ignore that part, to detach myself from it and let myself become jaded would, as Camus so bluntly put it, be a defeat of the human soul.

So, whether I was using Canis to spam the game threads with Free Dieng! or as an excuse to play around with iMovie, I was no longer afraid to let it all out. The Wolves were like an alley-oop, and I was Gerald Green, or Zach LaVine – take your pick; if there's one thing the Wolves have, it's dunk champs – and the team was all of us, together.

Not too long after Garnett was traded, another movie came out: "Into the Wild", adapted from the novel of the same name and the real-life story of Christopher McCandless, a naive, pretentious young college graduate who, left to his own devices, went on a solo hike across North America and into the Alaskan wilderness. Along the way, he made many friends, but ultimately always left them behind to continue his trek alone.

Eventually, he ate a berry that turned out to be poisonous. He would soon pass away in an abandoned bus in the wild, but not before writing in his journal a revelatory "Happiness is only real when shared."

While I find that a bit reductive, I believe that happiness is best when shared. And it's not just limited to happiness. As my former neighbor Dave Pirner once sang, "Misery loves company."

The highs and the lows, the celebratory and the funereal. All of it is best, and can be eased, by a shared camaraderie among people.

And that's how I learned to truly embrace the Wolves, by finding community in the struggle, and by finding myself in that communal openness where all mirrors are welcome, where the subjective woe can turn into a collective joy, and I am forever grateful for that.
