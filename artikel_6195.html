5 Reasons A Passenger Can End Up On The US No-Fly List

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Air travel is one of the safest ways to travel, with airlines, airports, and government agencies working together to identify potential security threats before passengers board an aircraft. In the US, an important part of this process is the federal No-Fly List, a government database designed to prevent individuals considered serious terrorism-related threats from accessing commercial aviation. Although the list is often associated with suspected terrorists, its inclusion criteria are more specific than many travelers realize.

Being placed on the No-Fly List is not simply a matter of attracting attention at an airport or having a disagreement with an airline. An individual must first meet the reasonable suspicion standard for inclusion on the broader terrorist watchlist and then satisfy at least one additional criterion relating to terrorism and national security. From threats against aircraft to concerns about domestic terrorism, several circumstances can result in a passenger being prohibited from flying.

One of the most straightforward reasons an individual can be placed on the US No-Fly List is posing a suspected threat to an aircraft or aviation security. The list's primary purpose is to prevent individuals who may carry out terrorist activities from accessing commercial flights, making threats against civil aviation particularly significant. This includes concerns about someone intending to hijack an aircraft, carry out an attack during a flight, or otherwise compromise passenger and crew safety.

The government considers threats against aviation particularly serious because of the potential consequences for everyone onboard. A commercial aircraft, whether it is a Boeing 737 operated by Southwest Airlines or an Airbus A350 operated by Delta Air Lines, carries hundreds of people who depend on security procedures for their protection. An individual reasonably suspected of planning a terrorist attack involving an aircraft could therefore meet the additional criteria required for No-Fly List designation.

However, the existence of a threat does not necessarily mean someone must have attempted to board an aircraft with a weapon or previously carried out an attack. The government can consider information suggesting that an individual intends to participate in terrorist activity, including conduct undertaken in preparation for an attack. This is why intelligence gathering and information sharing between federal agencies play such an important role in aviation security.

Importantly, an individual must also meet the underlying reasonable suspicion standard to be included on the terrorist watchlist. A passenger making an isolated, inappropriate comment at an airport would not automatically qualify for the No-Fly List. Authorities must consider whether available information establishes the required connection to terrorism and whether the additional aviation-related threat criterion is satisfied.

Another reason a passenger may end up on the No-Fly List is suspected involvement in domestic terrorism. Unlike international terrorism, which involves activities connected to foreign organizations or overseas interests, domestic terrorism concerns terrorist activities directed at targets within the US. The government considers this a significant national security concern, particularly when intelligence suggests that an individual may be planning or preparing a violent attack.

Domestic terrorism can involve threats against public buildings, government facilities, infrastructure, or groups of people within the country. An individual suspected of participating in preparations for such an attack may attract attention from federal authorities, particularly when evidence suggests an intention to use violence to achieve political, ideological, or other objectives. Where the reasonable suspicion standard and additional No-Fly List criteria are met, the individual can be prevented from boarding commercial aircraft.

The aviation connection is not always immediately obvious. Someone suspected of planning an attack on a location far away from an airport may still represent a concern to national security officials. Restricting access to commercial aviation can reduce the ability of a suspected terrorist to travel quickly, reach potential targets, or move between locations while authorities assess available intelligence. The restrictions are therefore not limited to people suspected of attacking aircraft themselves.

This distinction matters because the No-Fly List also addresses broader threats to the homeland. Someone suspected of planning domestic terrorist violence could qualify even if there is no specific allegation that they intend to hijack a flight or attack an airport. The government must still establish the relevant legal threshold rather than relying solely on an individual's political opinions, beliefs, or lawful activities.

The FAA has proposed adding a regulation to Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations, creating a prohibited area necessary for the president.

The third reason concerns individuals suspected of threatening US government facilities located outside the country. American embassies, consulates, and other government installations overseas can face security threats from terrorist organizations and individuals operating internationally. Protecting these locations and the personnel working inside them is an important part of US national security policy, and suspected terrorist activity directed against them can affect an individual's ability to travel.

A person suspected of planning an attack against a US diplomatic facility abroad may satisfy one of the additional criteria for inclusion on the No-Fly List. Such facilities represent US diplomatic and government interests in countries where security conditions can be complicated. An attack could endanger government employees, local staff, visitors, and members of the public, while creating wider diplomatic and international security consequences.

The concern does not necessarily involve an individual physically traveling to an overseas location to carry out an attack. Intelligence indicating that someone is helping organize, support, or prepare for terrorist activities can be relevant to the broader watchlisting process. Authorities may consider information gathered by intelligence agencies, law enforcement organizations, and other government partners when determining whether an individual meets the applicable requirements.

For airlines, the consequences of a designation can be significant. A passenger placed on the No-Fly List cannot simply choose another carrier to complete their journey through US airspace. The restriction applies to flights arriving in, departing from, or operating over US airspace, rather than being limited to a particular airline. Consequently, changing from an aircraft operated by United Airlines to one operated by American Airlines, for example, would not solve the restriction. The designation concerns the passenger, not the airline.

A fourth reason involves an individual suspected of intending to carry out a violent terrorist act while also possessing the operational capability to do so. This criterion considers both the seriousness of an individual's suspected intentions and whether they have the practical ability to act on them. The government is not simply assessing whether someone has expressed extremist views; it is considering whether the person represents a credible potential threat.

Operational capability can involve different circumstances depending on the information available to investigators. For example, authorities may consider an individual's connections, resources, access to support, and apparent involvement in preparations for terrorist activity. These factors can help establish whether a suspected threat is more than an expression of intent and whether the individual could realistically participate in a violent attack.

This criterion also demonstrates why the reasonable suspicion standard is only one part of the No-Fly List framework. Someone may be reasonably suspected of having connections to terrorism and qualify for the broader terrorist watchlist without necessarily meeting the additional requirements for the No-Fly List. The government must establish further threat-related criteria before applying the aviation ban.

Out-of-control travelers brought the flight to a halt before it could even depart.

Although the four preceding reasons describe the formal threat-related criteria, another important issue can affect passengers: mistaken identity or an outdated watchlist designation. Not every traveler who experiences problems boarding an aircraft is actually on the No-Fly List. Sometimes, a passenger shares a name or other identifying information with someone in a government security database, resulting in additional screening or travel complications.

The US Government Accountability Office (GAO) highlighted this issue in its 2025 report, Terrorist Watchlist: Nomination and Redress Processes for US Persons. The report explained that individuals can be incorrectly identified as someone on the watchlist or remain listed when their inclusion is no longer warranted. This can create difficulties when printing boarding passes, completing airport security procedures, or traveling internationally. Importantly, additional screening does not automatically mean someone has been placed on the No-Fly List.

The scale of the wider watchlisting system also helps explain why identification problems matter. The terrorist watchlist contained more than one million names in 2024, although this figure covered the broader database rather than the No-Fly List alone. The GAO also examined approximately 20,000 redress inquiries submitted between December 2021 and September 2023. Only 1.5% were related to the terrorist watchlist, with many other complaints involving passengers selected for additional screening through other security procedures.

Passengers who believe they have been incorrectly identified can seek assistance through the Department of Homeland Security Traveler Redress Inquiry Program. This process allows affected travelers to request a review of their circumstances and seek correction of identification problems. While these requests do not guarantee removal, they provide an established route for challenging an apparent error or a designation that may no longer be justified.
