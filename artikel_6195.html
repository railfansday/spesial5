Cardiovascular disease has long been linked to risk factors like high blood pressure and cholesterol — but a new study suggests that these factors, combined with proteins in the blood, could help forecast people's risk of heart attack and stroke decades in advance.

The new study, published Monday (Nov. 23) in the journal JAMA Network Open, took a broad view of the factors that spike the risk of stroke or heart attack. The researchers found that warning signs for these conditions can be seen in patients' health data up to 30 years before they happen.

The researchers say their work shows that some understudied biomarkers — measurable signals in the blood — could be useful tools in anticipating later conditions.

"We did know that there are some well-established risk factors that are related to heart disease, for example, blood pressure, cholesterol," said study co-author Dorien Kimenai, an epidemiologist at the University of Edinburgh. But "nobody really has looked at the patterns of these risk factors prior to when these people are getting cardiovascular events — not systematically."

For the new study, the scientists tracked data patterns from a diverse group of nearly 114,000 healthy people over 34 years. Their data spanned four long-term health studies conducted in the U.S., U.K., and Norway. At the end of the study period, the researchers divided data from participants who were still healthy at that juncture from those who had experienced heart attacks or strokes and either died or survived the events.

But even at the study's outset, these two groups' blood pressure and cholesterol data showed a clear divergence.

"We did see that, even three decades before, already, that these patterns were different," said Kimenai. That suggests, she added, that the difference between the two groups likely began even earlier than that.

The team also divided their participants by sex and age, finding that men and women experienced different trajectories in their cardiovascular disease risk over time. Women, on average, started with lower blood pressure than men in middle age, but that switched later in life.

"It shows that the age effect is as important as the sex differences," Kimenai said. In short, both factors need to be taken into account.

Dr. Sintip Pattanakuhar, a clinician scientist at the University of Strathclyde in Scotland who was not involved in the study, said that while these risk factors can't be modified in the way blood pressure or cholesterol can, age and sex could be incorporated into personalized screening programs for cardiovascular risk.

During a stroke, the brain's blood supply is blocked or a blood vessel bleeds inside the organ. This reduces the blood and oxygen supply in certain parts of the brain, driving tissue damage and death.

The team also looked at the trajectories of biomarker proteins that circulate in the bloodstream and are linked to cardiovascular disease. These include cardiac troponin and N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), which are released by damaged heart muscle tissue and used to diagnose a heart attack. They also included C-reactive protein, a liver-borne molecule that signals rising inflammation in the body.

Differences in cardiac troponin and C-reactive protein were visible in people who would go on to have a heart attack or stroke up to 30 years before they happened. For NT-proBNP, the differences emerged about 10 years before.

That said, Kimenai cautioned that the biomarker data came from fewer participants than the full study cohort, so these results bear further confirmation.

Dr. Deepak Bhatt, director of the Mount Sinai Fuster Heart Hospital in New York, said the study's blood pressure and cholesterol data confirmed what cardiologists had long suspected about these risk factors: that these modifiable factors predict cardiovascular disease. Bhatt, who was not involved in the study, added that the biomarker data in this study built on other studies that have also shown that cardiovascular events can be predicted years before they happen.

"What this adds is that it's actually predicting events decades before," he said.

The study's clearest message was that people should get their cardiovascular risk factors checked early, Kimenai said.

"When you're 40 years old, get your risk assessments and your blood pressure measured," she said. "Because these are the things you can change. If you've then been identified as having hypertension, then you can get those drugs that are very effective and safe and efficient to alter the pattern."

However, while the value of checking blood pressure and cholesterol is well established, Kimenai and Bhatt agreed that the clinical value of the biomarker data was less clear at this stage. Bhatt said that he might advise a patient with high blood pressure to start medication and cut out salt, for example, but the way forward for someone with an elevated biomarker reading is less clear for now.

"Ideally, more work will be done" to see whether panels of biomarkers, perhaps combined with tools that measure genetic risk, could flag patients at very high risk of having heart attacks and strokes and should be treated differently than a patient with lower risk, Bhatt suggested. "That's sort of the missing piece of the Holy Grail of prevention."
