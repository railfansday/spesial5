When Is Susan Smith’s Parole Hearing? Mom to Learn Fate After Killing 2 Sons

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Susan Smith is set to learn whether she’ll get a chance at freedom more than three decades after she drowned her two young sons and spun a national lie about a carjacking to cover it up.

The convicted child killer applied for parole for the second time after her first bid in 2024 was unanimously denied by the South Carolina Probation and Parole Board. Now, Smith is preparing to make her case again as the hearing date approaches, and her ex-husband, the father of the boys she killed, is preparing to fight her once more.

Here’s everything to know about Smith’s upcoming parole hearing, the crime that put her behind bars and where she is today.

Smith’s second parole hearing is scheduled for November 19, 2026. If the South Carolina Probation and Parole Board denies her bid, she will have to wait until 2028 to file a third application.

Smith was first eligible for parole in November 2024 after serving 30 years of a life sentence for the 1994 murders of her sons, Michael, 3, and Alexander, 14 months. The board rejected her unanimously at that hearing, and she has remained in custody since.

Only six of the 471 letters and emails the parole board received ahead of the 2024 hearing supported Smith’s release.

At her first parole hearing in 2024, Smith addressed the board directly and apologized for killing Michael and Alex.

“I want to say how very sorry I am,” she said. “I know that what I did was horrible, and I would give anything if I could go back and change it.”

Her attorney, Tommy Thomas, argued during that hearing that Smith’s actions were the direct result of untreated mental illness stemming from her father’s suicide when she was only 6 years old.

The board was unmoved. One of the loudest voices against her release came from inside her own family.

Susan’s ex-husband and the father of Michael and Alex, David Smith, challenged her 2024 parole bid and has said she needs to die behind bars.

“God gave us free choice. She made free choice that night to end their lives,” David said. “She changed my life for the rest of my life that night.”

In November 2026, David told Us that he once again planned to fight against Susan’s release as the hearing approaches. “I’m going to tell them, don’t keep her incarcerated because of what she’s done to me. Keep her incarcerated because of what she did to Michael and Alex,” David said of his message to the parole board. “Her behavior through the years [in prison] has shown it’s still all about Susan. In my heart of hearts, I don’t think she’s remorseful, and I don’t think she really cares what she did.”

On October 25, 1994, Susan told police that a Black man had carjacked her and driven off with her two sons still strapped into the back seat. She went on local television news for nine days straight, pleading tearfully for her sons’ safe return.

The story captured national attention — until it fell apart.

On November 3, 1994, nine days after Michael and Alex went missing, Susan confessed that there had been no carjacking. She had driven her car to the boat ramp at John D. Long Lake in Union, South Carolina, and let it roll into the water with her sons still inside. Their bodies were recovered from the lake on November 4, 1994.

Prosecutors argued that Susan killed her children because she was pursuing a secret relationship with a local businessman, Tom Findlay, who had told her he did not want children. Her defense maintained that she had planned the drownings as a murder-suicide and intended to die alongside her sons that night, telling investigators her body willed itself out of the vehicle.

Susan’s murder trial began in July 1995 after she publicly admitted to killing her sons. The prosecution argued she was motivated by her relationship with Findlay and pushed for the death penalty.

The defense countered that Susan was mentally ill and had intended to take her own life alongside her children on the night of the drownings.

The trial lasted less than a week. On July 22, 1995, a jury in Union, South Carolina, found Susan guilty on two counts of first-degree murder. The jurors declined to impose the death penalty, and on July 28, 1995, she was sentenced to life in prison with the possibility of parole after 30 years.

Since beginning her sentence, Susan has been anything but a model inmate. She was first booked into the Camille Griffin Graham Correctional Institution in Columbia, South Carolina, where she racked up two infractions for having sex with prison guards.

The first incident involved prison guard Houston Cagle, who had sex with Susan at least four times. Cagle pleaded guilty, was sentenced to three months in jail and was fired. Susan was disciplined for the encounters.

Prison captain Alfred Rowe was arrested in September 2000 for having sex with Susan. He later pleaded guilty and received five years’ probation.

Following the sexual misconduct incidents, Susan was transferred to the Leath Correctional Institution, where prison guards have taken special precautions due to her past. Her disciplinary problems continued there. Jailhouse records show she was disciplined at least five times between 2010 and 2017 for infractions including self-mutilation, drug use and possession of narcotics or marijuana.

She also was penalized for using another inmate’s pin number to contact the outside world. Most recently, Susan was disciplined on August 26, 2024, for speaking to the producers of a documentary film about her case.

Susan remains incarcerated at the Leath Correctional Institution in South Carolina as she awaits her November 2026 parole hearing. Her legal team is expected to again argue that she has served enough time, that she suffered untreated mental illness at the time of the killings and that she deserves a second chance.

If the parole board agrees, Susan could walk free more than 32 years after she rolled her car into John D. Long Lake with Michael and Alex strapped inside. If the board again denies her, she will remain behind bars until at least 2028, when she can apply once more.
