Booze, Tobacco Together in Pregnancy May Worsen Baby's Facial Abnormalities

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

— Smaller face size, reduced growth, and dysmorphism worse with combined exposure, study shows

Prenatal exposure to both alcohol and tobacco smoke impacted the baby's facial development more than either alone, a longitudinal study from South Africa showed.

Three-dimensional facial scans at 1 month and 1 year of age showed the greatest differences in facial shape, size, and growth with co-exposure from early pregnancy compared with none or single-substance exposure, Michael Suttie, PhD, of the University of Oxford in England, and colleagues reported in Pediatrics.

For girls, tobacco plus alcohol exposure was associated with greater reductions in facial growth and measurements around the eyes. Craniofacial growth didn't catch up by age 1 for girls exposed continuously to both substances in utero. For boys, dual prenatal exposure was linked to reduced nasal growth, resulting in smaller noses at 1 year.

"Findings highlight tobacco's potentiating effect on alcohol-related craniofacial harm," the researchers wrote. "These findings highlight the importance of interventions targeting alcohol and smoking cessation during pregnancy to reduce teratogenic risk."

An estimated 5.4% of U.S. women smoke during pregnancy, which carries known risk of craniofacial developmental issues for the baby, including craniosynostosis and orofacial clefts. Some 9.8% of pregnant women globally consume alcohol, which can lead to the baby having facial characteristics such as nasal shortening, midline differences, midfacial hypoplasia, ocular differences, and mandibular anomalies aside from some of the most identifiable signs of fetal alcohol syndrome: a smooth philtrum above a short upper lip and shortened width from the inner to outer corner of the eye.

"Both teratogens are known to disrupt neural crest cell migration, survival, and differentiation, processes essential for the formation of midline facial structures, ocular features, and nasal morphology," the researchers noted. "Nicotine exposure potentially compounds this disruption [by alcohol] through oxidative stress, hypoxia, and nicotine-induced vasoconstriction, which may impair oxygen and nutrient delivery during critical periods of facial morphogenesis."

To look for evidence that the impact of these substances might be additive, Suttie's group examined the Safe Passage Study, a prospective cohort of more than 7,000 pregnancies in low socioeconomic communities in Cape Town, South Africa, which has one of the world's highest rates of alcohol use in pregnancy. The cohort largely comprised women of mixed ancestry, known as South African Colored.

Of the more than 7,000 participants in the study, the analysis homed in on the subset of 634 participants who had 3D facial imaging of their infants at age 1 month and 1 year and antenatal substance exposure data. Exclusion criteria included illicit drug use by the mother (including marijuana) and genetic diagnoses in infants.

Alcohol and smoking exposures had been assessed via interview at each prenatal visit, with frequency and quantity both captured. Continuous exposure was defined as exposure in the first trimester and any subsequent trimester. There were 152 infants categorized as without exposure, 48 with continuous alcohol-only exposure, 149 with continuous smoking-only exposure, 150 with continuous co-exposure, and 135 with continuous smoking and any alcohol use.

Continuous prenatal co-exposure to smoking and alcohol was associated with reduced facial size compared with both controls and single-exposure groups (P<0.05), driven by differences among girls. The single-exposure groups showed no differences compared with controls.

"Our findings of sex-specific dysmorphism are consistent with prior work suggesting differential vulnerability to prenatal alcohol exposure by sex," Suttie and colleagues noted.

Continuous co-exposure was associated with greater facial dysmorphism overall, with the biggest difference at 1 month (P<0.001) but still significant at 1 year (P<0.05). Consistent midline displacement was seen with co-exposure at both time points. More outward lateral movement of the inner canthi of the eye was also seen with co-exposure at both time points (P<0.05).

Limitations included the need to scan infants while sleeping, potential modification by maternal nutritional deficiencies, and unknown generalizability to populations with different genetic profiles.

"Our results contribute to a growing body of evidence on the harms of co-exposures, and we encourage clinicians and healthcare authorities to emphasize the potential harm of concurrent smoking and alcohol use during pregnancy," the researchers concluded.
