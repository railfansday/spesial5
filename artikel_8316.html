One More Test

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

As I get ever closer to finishing my hike, I must take on the northern Pennsylvania section notorious for its endless rocks.

A problem for future me. That is what I thought to myself as I finished my hike up and over, and then up and over again, Mount Moosilauke. I had finished the Whites, that meant the rest of the Appalachian Trail would be a cake walk. There was just one problem, northern Pennsylvania. Consistently rated the worst part of the AT not because of steep climbs, but because of the rocks. Throughout my time on the AT, I’d heard tales of this section, and some rather tall ones at that. I pushed the thought out of my mind. It would take me weeks to get there. Including New Hampshire, it was six states away. Well, now I am officially starting northern Pennsylvania. The problem for future me has become the problem for current me. To make matters worse, there was rain in the forecast overnight into tomorrow. It wasn’t just any old rocks I’d be contending with, it would be wet rocks. Lucky me.

I originally planned to drive across the Delaware River to the Dunnfield Creek Trailhead where I’d turned around yesterday. Since it was a lighter mileage day I decided to keep my van where it was and walk north to the trailhead, turn around and continue past the van southbound. This would add some miles to today, but trim them from tomorrow. It would also save a bit of toll expense since hikers aren’t charged for crossing the bridge. I made my way onto the bridge, briefly crossing back into New Jersey and along River Road. I was back at the van before 9:00AM and ready to tackle the climb up Mount Minsi.

On the bridge through the Delaware Water Gap.

Briefly back into New Jersey before leaving for the last time.

The trail passed the small Lenape Lake before ascending the south side of the Delaware Water Gap. Eureka Creek flowed across the Appalachian Trail which continued climbing up Mount Minsi until it reached a few rocky outcrops presenting hikers with a view high above the Delaware Water Gap. I had a perfectly clear view of Mount Tammany, with its rock slides corkscrewing upward. The trail turned along the ridge, relieving me of any more vertical steps. A dirt road made for easy hiking while passing by great views of the valley to the southeast. Soon the trail turned off the dirt road and I had my first encounter with the rocks of northern Pennsylvania. While small, they stretched on for far longer than any other rocky patch on the AT. I couldn’t hike at my usual pace, I would have twisted my ankle in no time. I paid extra close attention to navigating through the rocky maze, moving left and right to find the best spots to put my feet. The constant back and forth made it a chore to keep my momentum up. Still, it was more of an annoyance than a dreadful experience, at first.

The trail follows a gravel road briefly after Lenape Lake.

Looking across the Delaware Water Gap at Mount Tammany.

Following the ridge in northern Pennsylvania.

Crossing the road at Wolf Gap took a bit of courage as there is no line of sight down either side of the crest. I waited for the passing cars to clear and then listened for any new traffic approaching before jogging across the roadway. I was now within striking distance of Wold Rocks. As is customary, I stayed on the AT over Wolf Rocks, even though there is a bypass in case the weather is rainy and windy. That might be the case tomorrow, but all I had today were blue skies. This was the first scramble of the day, which only lasted a minute or so. What followed was a sloped rocky ridge with views to the northwest. Honestly, if it weren’t for the reputation of this section of the trail, I wouldn’t have thought the rocks were that bad, and it made sense for the trail to come up here for the view. I am sure more challenging terrain lay ahead.

Sure enough, this was about where things got more trying. The trail became something of a dense field of jagged rocks sticking up from the earth. The trail went on like this for mile after mile with only brief reprieves to be found. I was managing, but I cringed when thinking of what it would be like tomorrow in wet conditions. I fought my way to Wind Gap, a gap in the ridge that was thankfully not all that windy today. I checked in on a few more viewpoints before continuing the trek about 2 miles past the Leroy A. Smith shelter. Knowing there was finite daylight remaining I turned around to investigate the shelter.

Following the ridge in northern Pennsylvania.

I was struggling to decide where to spend the night. I was going to reach the Leroy A. Smith shelter with some daylight left. If the shelter was empty it might be a good idea to stay in it. I’d avoid a wet tent, but I’d have a longer hike back to the Delaware water gap. I also have had a lingering feeling ever since I passed on staying at the secret shelter that aid be able to get a much earlier start heading out from a shelter than my tent. Packing up camp requires light and warmth. It’s possible to pack up with just my headlamp, but there are a lot of things that could be accidentally left behind like tent stakes. It would be much easier with everything in plain sight in a shelter. If the goal was to finish hiking as early as possible, it might make more sense to stay in the shelter rather than hike a few miles up the trail and camp.

In the end, the decision was made for me. Someone had set up in the shelter already. Two dog bowls suggested that they either had a dog or unconventional eating habits. I dropped my backpack at the shelter and headed down to the spring. I encountered a man and a young dog at the spring. Both seemed friendly enough. The dog reminded me a lot of the dogs I used to foster when I lived in Maryland. It looked tough on the outside, but after a few good ear scratches it melted into a pup as gentle as they come.

I drank a liter of water at the spring and collected two more to get me through the morning. The pipe spring gushed water like a champ. It was quite a hike up from the spring. I noticed that water is rarely on the trail in this section because the mountain is so narrow. I’ll need to expect fewer streams right in front of shelters and more steep treks for water over the next week or so.

View from the trail near the Leroy A. Smith Shelter.

Back on the trail I had limited time to find a camping spot before light ran out. It seemed cruel that now that I was finally back in a state that allows camping along the trail with few restrictions, the areas near the trail rarely seemed fit for tenting. The trail was rocky, and so was all the flat ground near the trail. I was only sure of one spot about 2.5 miles back, I wasn’t going to make it there unless I wanted to retrieve my headlamp from my pack, which I was starting to consider. Before I could, I spotted what logged to be a suitable spot. I turned off the trail and proceeded to walk over the area, checking for sticks and rocks. It felt decent, so I rolled out my tent and felt the ground with my hands to see if there would be any lumps where my tent floor would be. I removed a few more rocks and sticks but was soon satisfied that the spot would work. I finished pitching the tent while my water filtered in the background. It wasn’t easy to get the stakes into the ground. A couple were more stuck between rocks than buried in the ground, but they seemed like they would hold. I went to bed hoping that the precipitation superintendent would cancel the rain tomorrow.

Still rocky, going to be hard to find a spot before it gets dark…
