Sustainable cement production via a liquid-phase pathway

Nature (2026) Cite this article

Slowing climate change requires overcoming inertia in political, technological and geophysical systems1,2,3. Portland cement manufacturing is among the most challenging industrial processes to decarbonize, yet it remains indispensable for building the infrastructure underpinning the low-carbon transition4,5. Here, building beyond the two-century-old conventional cement process6, we report a route that creates sustainable cement, by accelerating its reaction kinetics through a microwave carbon-plasma method, reducing the standard processing time from hours to seconds. Our technique generates localized temperatures up to 2,400 °C, sufficient to liquefy the raw materials. These liquid-phase reactions promote elemental mixing and solidification, producing reactive clinker phases. Our in situ transmission electron microscopy captures the early-stage cement hydration process in real time, revealing that surface disorder accelerates clinker reactivity by facilitating the dynamic nucleation and growth of hydration clusters. Beyond typical limestone and clay feedstocks, we show that hydrated cement paste from waste concrete can also be processed into clinkers, meeting ordinary Portland cement specifications. By coupling circular resource use with ultrafast electrified processing, our approach enables sustainable, low-cost cement production at scale.

Phase diagram of the binary system CaO–SiO2, adapted from Day and Shepherd16, illustrating the composition, possible secondary phases, and transition and melting temperatures for both C2S and C3S. In cement chemist notation: C = CaO, S = SiO2, F = Fe2O3, and A = Al2O3. Trd represents tridymite. Tricalcium silicate (3CaO·SiO2, C3S), dicalcium silicate (2CaO·SiO2, C2S), tricalcium aluminate (3CaO·Al2O3, C3A), and calcium alumino-ferrite (4CaO·Al2O3·Fe2O3, C4AF), with impurities, are commonly referred to as alite, belite, aluminate, and ferrite, respectively.

(a) Characterization of carbon fibres. SEM images of the carbon fibres used in MCP heating experiment, showing a fibre diameter of approximately 7.5 μm. (b) Chemical mapping of cement raw meal (RM) with a particle size of D50 = 4.6 μm. The primary components of the raw meal are limestone (CaCO3) and clay minerals (SiO2, Al2O3), as identified in the Ca, Si, and Al elemental maps. The milling process induces the formation of fine, intermixed aggregates, where individual particles become closely attached with each other. This intimate mixing enhances the homogeneity of the raw meal. The formation of these aggregates is particularly beneficial in facilitating efficient fusion and clinker formation, as the localized heating effect promotes uniform thermal decomposition and phase transformation within the RM particles.

(a) Temperature profiles of gas (purple) and raw meal (blue) in a commercial rotary kiln system compared to our method (orange). We note that temperature non-uniformity can still be significant in conventional cement kiln, with local temperature differences on the order of several hundred degrees Celsius (up to ~600 °C) between the raw meal and gas flame, which contributes to inefficient heat transfer and over-burning/under-burning risks in the clinker bed. (b) Diagram of raw meal transitioning to clinker in a conventional kiln over time. Temperature data in (a) were collected from a 54-m-long and 3.5-m-diameter rotary kiln with a production capacity of 850 tonnes of clinker per day.

(a-d) Synthesis of single-phase cement minerals, including m-C3S (a), β-C2S (b), c-C3A (c), and o-C4AF (d), confirmed by PXRD, STEM-EDX mapping, and HRTEM imaging overlaid with the corresponding atomic structures (the unit cell of the mineral is outlined in green). The residual error, representing the difference between the experimental and fitted data, is displayed as gray curves in the PXRD profiles. The zone axis and lattice planes were indexed by analyzing the fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns of the HRTEM images and comparing them with TEM simulations. Additional details are provided in the Supplementary Materials. (e) Ternary plot of synthesized materials produced using MCP method. In cement chemist notation: C = CaO, S = SiO2, F = Fe2O3, and A = Al2O3. m-C3S (a), β-C2S (b), c-C3A (c) and o-C4AF (d) represent monoclinic tricalcium silicate (3CaO·SiO2, C3S), beta-dicalcium silicate (2CaO·SiO2, C2S), cubic tricalcium aluminate (3CaO·Al2O3, C3A), and orthorhombic calcium alumino-ferrite (4CaO·Al2O3·Fe2O3, C4AF), respectively. c-limestone, c-clay, and c-LDH refer to calcined limestone, calcined clay, and calcined layered double hydroxides, while LDHs indicate materials synthesized in liquids. Low-grade fly ash and slag were activated using MCP method.

(a) Flow spread as a function of polycarboxylate-ether (PCE) superplasticizer (SP) dosage, used to quantify flowability and superplasticizer demand. (b) Photographs of the NEW cement mortar at PCE dosages of 0–0.25% by mass of cement. The NEW cement (blue) achieved a slump extension of 205 ± 5 mm at 0.25 wt% MasterGlenium 7920, which was adopted as the target workability for self-compacting cementitious systems. Under identical mixture proportions and testing conditions, commercial ordinary Portland cement (OPC, orange) required a slightly higher PCE dosage (0.30 wt%) to reach the same target flowability, indicating that our NEW cement exhibits comparable workability relative to OPC. The error bars were calculated from multiple measurements.

(a) Particle size distribution; (b) Instantaneous heat release; (c) Tensile strength of carbon fibre-reinforced cement pastes at varying fibre dosages, by weight of cement, after curing for 28 days. Note that the carbon fibres show partial alignment during the sample preparation process. Raw meal (RM) and hydrated cement paste (HCP) were activated to produce new clinker (NEW) and recycled clinker (RCL), respectively. The error bars were calculated from measurements of multiple samples.

(a) Washing and agitation to separate cement fines from sand. (b) Cascade filtration of the resulting slurry in (a) to recover the solid cement waste fraction, with a processing capacity on the order of tonnes per hour. (c) Recovered solid cement waste fines.

(a) Magnetron and directional coupler connected to the MCP reaction chamber. (b,c) Reaction chamber with dimensions of approximately 1.6 × 0.7 × 1.2 m, accommodating batch sizes of up to 100 kg. A pumping unit can be added to regulate chamber pressure and gas residence time, extending the versatility of the MCP platform to materials synthesis requiring controlled reaction environments while the control system is used for regulating reaction time and monitoring temperature.

Real-time observation of the clinkering process of HCP at 1,400 °C using the DENSsolutions system inside a transmission electron microscope. Rapid melting followed by crystallization is clearly captured, demonstrating the formation of clinker phases.

STEM-EDX nanotomography revealing elemental segregation within a clinker grain. The distribution of key elements highlights compositional heterogeneity at the nanoscale, offering insights into phase development during clinkering.

Hydration process of alite under 2 torr water vapour at room temperature, recorded using the Protochips Atmosphere system inside a transmission electron microscope. Control experiment of alite inside a sealed SiNx cell without water vapour flow. No significant surface changes are observed, confirming the necessity of water vapour for hydration reactions to occur.
