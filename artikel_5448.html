Why the Supreme Court denies most petitions and what happens to the rest

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A guide to how the Supreme Court chooses which cases to hear, from certiorari petitions and the rule of four to circuit splits, the cert pool and unexplained denials

The U.S. Supreme Court sets its own agenda. A party that loses in a lower court can ask the justices for review, but it has no right to receive it. Most of those requests are turned away, usually through a single line on an order list with no reasons given. The cases that make it through become the ones that produce rulings on federal law for courts across the country.

That control over the docket comes from both legislation and the Court's own practices. Congress made most of the Court's appellate work discretionary with the Judiciary Act of 1925. A 1988 law later removed most of the mandatory appeals that remained. The justices filled in the details through written rules and internal routines that have built up over decades.

Knowing how the Supreme Court chooses which cases to hear explains why some legal questions get a national answer while others stay unsettled for years. It also explains why a refusal to hear a case is not a ruling on its merits. A denied petition leaves the lower court's decision in place, but the denial itself sets no precedent.

The selection process runs through several stages and a specialized vocabulary. Law clerks screen petitions for a writ of certiorari, many of them through a shared arrangement called the cert pool. The Chief Justice circulates a list of petitions for discussion, and the justices vote in a private conference. Four votes are enough to add a case to the docket. Conflicts among the federal appeals courts, known as circuit splits, are among the grounds the Court's own rules name for granting review. The Solicitor General, who represents the federal government before the Court, can also influence which cases advance, sometimes at the justices' invitation.

This list covers 10 of the steps and concepts that govern case selection. It is a selection, not a complete account of the Court's docket. The entries move from the mechanics of a cert petition through the rule of four, the other reasons listed in Rule 10 and the role of friend-of-the-court briefs. Later entries turn to denials, relists and dissents from denial. The final entry covers the routes that bypass ordinary certiorari, including disputes between states and emergency applications. Each entry stands on its own. Readers can start with whichever question brought them here.

Most cases reach the U.S. Supreme Court through a petition for a writ of certiorari, a formal request asking the justices to review a lower court's decision. The Court has no obligation to grant it. Review depends on the justices' discretion, not on a right held by the parties.

The petition comes from the side that lost below. That party, called the petitioner, argues that the case deserves the attention of the nation's highest court. The opposing party, the respondent, can file a brief in opposition urging the justices to leave the lower-court ruling in place.

The word itself is Latin. Certiorari is usually glossed as "to be more fully informed." The name reflects what the writ does. A higher court calls up the record of a case so it can examine what happened below. Lawyers and court watchers often shorten the term to "cert," and the Court speaks of granting or denying cert.

The Court's own rules spell out the discretionary nature of the process. Supreme Court Rule 10 states that review on a writ of certiorari is not a matter of right but of judicial discretion. A petitioner who believes the lower court decided the case wrongly has no guarantee of a hearing. The petition has to persuade the justices that the case warrants a place on their docket.

A petition follows a set format under the Court's rules. Rule 14 requires the questions presented for review to appear first, expressed concisely. Those questions frame what the justices would decide if they granted review. The petition must also include an appendix with the opinions and orders issued by the lower courts in the case.

Deadlines apply as well. Under Rule 13, a petition is generally due 90 days after the lower court enters its judgment. If a party asked the lower court for rehearing in time, the clock instead runs from the date that request is denied. A justice can extend the deadline by up to 60 days. The application for more time goes to the justice assigned to the circuit where the case arose.

Cost does not have to block a filing. Under Rule 39, a petitioner who cannot afford the Court's fees can ask to proceed in forma pauperis, a status that waives them. Many petitions from prisoners arrive this way.

Four of the nine Supreme Court justices must vote to grant a petition for review. A majority is not required. A minority of the bench can put a case on the Court's docket.

The rule of four is a practice the justices follow among themselves. It does not appear in the Constitution, and no federal statute imposes it. It governs the justices' internal votes rather than anything a litigant can invoke or enforce.

The threshold sits one vote below a majority of a full nine-member Court. In practice, a group of justices who think a legal question deserves an answer can move a case forward without persuading a fifth colleague. The case then gets a full hearing even if most of the Court would have passed on it.

The vote takes place in private. The justices do not usually announce who voted to grant review. When the Court accepts a case, the public record typically shows only that the petition was granted. The tally behind it stays out of view.

A grant moves the case onto the Court's merits docket. The parties file full briefs on the legal question, and the case is usually scheduled for oral argument. Deciding a case follows a different rule from accepting one. A ruling on the merits requires a majority of the justices who take part.

That difference has consequences. On a full bench, four justices can bring a case in, but four votes cannot decide it. If the participating justices split evenly, as can happen after a recusal, the lower court's judgment stands. A ruling by an equally divided Court sets no national precedent.

A grant also says nothing about the final outcome. A vote to hear a case reflects a judgment that the question merits the Court's attention. It is not a vote on who should win. Justices who voted to grant review can land on opposite sides of the decision. The Court sometimes affirms the very ruling it agreed to review.

The Court can also step back after granting. It occasionally dismisses a case as improvidently granted, a disposition often abbreviated as DIG. That can happen when briefing or argument exposes a problem that keeps the Court from reaching the question it meant to decide.

A circuit split happens when two or more federal courts of appeals answer the same question of federal law in different ways. Supreme Court Rule 10(a) names that kind of conflict among the appeals courts as a reason the justices may agree to hear a case.

The U.S. has 13 federal courts of appeals. Twelve of them are regional circuits. These are the First through Eleventh circuits, each covering a group of states or territories, plus the D.C. Circuit in the nation's capital. The 13th is the Federal Circuit. It does not cover a region. It hears appeals on specialized subjects, such as patent cases, from across the country.

Each regional circuit sets precedent for the federal trial courts inside its borders. A ruling from the Ninth Circuit, which includes California, binds federal district judges in that circuit. It does not bind judges in the Fifth Circuit, which includes Texas. Those judges follow their own circuit's precedent. The appeals courts are also free to disagree with one another.

That structure is where splits come from. When circuits split, the same federal statute or constitutional provision can mean one thing in one part of the country and something else in another. A person's rights, a company's legal obligations or the outcome of a criminal case can then depend on geography. Within the court system, only the Supreme Court can settle the disagreement nationwide. Its rulings on federal law bind every lower federal court.

Lawyers who file petitions often try to show that a split exists. A genuine conflict gives the justices a reason for review that their own rules recognize. Petitions and legal commentators also describe splits by their size. A shallow split may involve only a few circuits. A deep split involves several circuits lined up on each side of the question.

A split does not guarantee that the Court will take a case. The justices sometimes let a disagreement continue so that more appeals courts can weigh in before the question reaches them. Legal writers often call that process percolation.

Supreme Court Rule 10 names several grounds for review beyond conflicts among federal appeals courts. They include an important federal question the Court has not settled and a sharp departure from accepted judicial procedure. A lower-court ruling that conflicts with the Supreme Court's own decisions is another.

Rule 10 places all of these grounds under a single standard. The rule says a petition for a writ of certiorari will be granted only for "compelling reasons." The listed grounds are examples of reasons the justices may treat as compelling. They are not a checklist that guarantees a hearing.

The first ground concerns unsettled law. A state court of last resort or a federal court of appeals may decide an important question of federal law that the Supreme Court has never resolved. The rule describes this as a question that has not been, but should be, settled by the Court. The word "important" carries weight here. A novel question alone does not meet the standard.

The second ground concerns how a case was handled. A federal court of appeals may depart far from the accepted and usual course of judicial proceedings. It may also approve such a departure by a lower court. Rule 10 treats either situation as a reason the Court may step in. The rule ties this ground to the Court's supervisory power.

The third ground protects the Court's own precedent. A state court or federal appeals court may decide an important federal question in a way that conflicts with relevant Supreme Court decisions. Review may follow in that situation. Lower courts are bound to apply the Supreme Court's rulings. This ground gives the justices a way to address departures from them.

Rule 10 also states that its factors are neither controlling nor fully measuring the Court's discretion. The justices can grant review in a case that fits none of the listed grounds. They can also deny review in a case that appears to fit one.

The rule closes with a caution for litigants. A petition that claims only that a lower court made erroneous factual findings is rarely granted. The same is true of a petition that claims only that a lower court misapplied a properly stated rule of law.

The cert pool is a shared screening system, begun in 1972, in which participating justices' chambers divide the work of reviewing petitions. A law clerk writes one memo on each petition. The memo recommends that the Court grant review, deny it or ask for the Solicitor General's views.

Justice Lewis Powell proposed the arrangement, and Chief Justice Warren Burger supported it. Before the pool existed, each chamber read every petition on its own. Thousands of petitions reach the Court each year. Splitting the load meant a petition could be summarized once for the participating justices rather than separately in each chamber.

Inside the pool, petitions are assigned among the participating chambers. The clerk who draws a case prepares a single memo. That memo then circulates to every justice in the pool.

The memo lays out the facts of the dispute and the rulings in the lower courts. It also sets out the questions the petitioner wants the justices to answer. It then assesses whether the case is a suitable candidate for review.

Each memo closes with one of those three recommendations. A call for the Solicitor General's views asks the federal government to file a brief before the justices decide.

Law clerks are typically recent law school graduates. Most have already clerked for a judge on a lower federal court. Each associate justice can hire up to four clerks.

A pool memo is a starting point, not the final word. Justices often have their own clerks review the shared memo and add notes that reflect that chamber's priorities. The decision on whether to hear a case still belongs to the justices themselves.

Not every chamber takes part. Justice John Paul Stevens stayed out of the pool, and his clerks screened petitions for him. Justice Samuel Alito later withdrew from it. Justice Neil Gorsuch also does not participate. The Alito and Gorsuch chambers review petitions on their own. Petitions get a separate read in those chambers in addition to the pool memo.

A cert petition reaches the justices' private conference only if it appears on the discuss list, which the Chief Justice circulates. Any justice can add a petition to that list. Petitions that never make the list are denied without discussion.

The discuss list is a second filter on top of the earlier screening. By the time a conference arrives, the justices' chambers have already reviewed the pending petitions. The list narrows that pool to the ones at least one justice thinks the full Court should talk through.

The Chief Justice drafts the list but does not have the final say over what reaches the table. A justice who wants the Court to consider a petition can add it without anyone else's agreement.

Petitions left off the list never receive a conference vote. They are denied automatically. The petitioner gets the same brief denial as a party whose case was debated at length. The set of excluded petitions is often called the "dead list."

The conference itself is closed. Only the nine justices attend. No clerks, secretaries or other staff are in the room. The most junior justice takes notes and answers the door if a message arrives. Discussion moves in order of seniority, starting with the Chief Justice.

The calendar shapes the workload. Petitions keep arriving during the Court's summer recess, and they build up. The justices take up that backlog at the "long conference," held in late September. It comes shortly before the new term opens on the first Monday in October. The long conference handles far more petitions than a typical conference during the term.

The results of each conference become public through order lists, which the Court releases after the justices meet. An order list records which petitions were granted and which were denied. It also carries other procedural orders in pending matters. The Court posts its order lists on its website, where lawyers, journalists and litigants check them for news about specific cases.

The Solicitor General represents the federal government before the Supreme Court. If the government is not a party but has a stake in a case, the justices can issue a call for the views of the Solicitor General, or CVSG.

The office sits inside the Department of Justice. Congress created it in 1870, in the same law that established the department. The president nominates the Solicitor General, and the Senate confirms the nominee. The Solicitor General also argues some of the government's cases before the justices in person.

The job includes a screening role on the government's side. When a federal agency loses in a lower court, the Solicitor General decides whether the government will ask the Supreme Court for review. As a result, the government does not petition on every loss. It brings only the cases its lawyers judge worth the Court's time.

Petitions the Solicitor General files on the government's behalf are granted at a much higher rate than other petitions. The office is sometimes called the "tenth justice." Lincoln Caplan used that phrase as the title of his 1987 book about the office.

A CVSG works differently. In these cases, the government stands outside the dispute. The parties might be two companies, or a private plaintiff and a state. The case may still turn on a federal statute or a federal regulation, or on a question that affects how federal agencies operate. The Court asks the Solicitor General to file a brief setting out the government's position.

That brief usually tells the justices whether the government thinks the petition should be granted or denied. It can also address the underlying legal question. The justices are not bound by the recommendation, but it carries weight in their decision.

A CVSG also adds time to the process. The Court generally holds the petition while it waits for the government's brief. A case that draws a CVSG stays undecided until that filing arrives.

Outside groups can weigh in before the Supreme Court decides whether to take a case. Under Supreme Court Rule 37, a friend-of-the-court brief may be filed either supporting or opposing a petition for a writ of certiorari. Its formal Latin name is an amicus curiae brief.

The filers are not parties to the lawsuit. They are organizations, governments or individuals with a stake in the legal question the petition raises. Common filers include states, trade associations, labor unions, advocacy groups and academics who study the area of law involved.

At the petition stage, an amicus brief does not argue the full merits of the dispute. Its job is narrower. It tries to show the justices that the question deserves a national answer, or that it does not.

A brief supporting review might describe how a lower-court ruling affects businesses, agencies or courts that had no part in the original case. It can point to people and institutions outside the courtroom who live under the ruling. A brief opposing review might argue that the issue is too narrow or too rare to need the Court's attention.

That focus matches what the Court says it wants from amicus filers. Rule 37 describes a helpful amicus brief as one that raises relevant matter the parties have not already brought to the Court's attention. The rule adds that a brief which does not serve that purpose burdens the Court. Such filings are not favored.

Rule 37 also sets a disclosure requirement. A brief must state whether counsel for a party wrote it in whole or in part. It must also identify anyone other than the amicus, its members or its lawyers who paid for its preparation or submission. That puts the brief's authorship and funding on the public record.

Amicus briefs carry no vote and bind no one. Review still depends on the justices' own decision to grant it, and an outside filing cannot stand in for that. What a cert-stage brief can add is context the petition alone may not supply. That context might include the range of institutions a ruling reaches or the other settings where the same question is arising.

When the Supreme Court denies a petition for certiorari, it almost never explains why. A denial also does not mean the justices agree with the lower court's ruling. It means only that the Court has chosen not to hear the case.

A denial leaves the lower-court ruling in force, and the parties remain bound by it. Other courts cannot treat the denial as an endorsement of the ruling below or as Supreme Court precedent.

Justice Felix Frankfurter made this point in Maryland v. Baltimore Radio Show in 1950. In a separate opinion, he wrote that a denial carries no expression of opinion on the merits of the case. Lawyers and judges still read denials that way.

A denial can rest on reasons that have nothing to do with the legal question. A case may have procedural problems, such as an issue that was not properly raised in the lower courts. Its facts may make it a poor vehicle for deciding a broader question. The justices may also prefer to let an issue develop further before stepping in. The Court rarely says which reason applied.

Relisting is a separate step. The Court sometimes relists a petition, which places it on the agenda for a later conference instead of resolving it at the first one. A relist can mean a justice wants more time to study the case or is drafting a separate writing. It is not a promise of a grant. Some relisted petitions are granted, and others are denied.

Justices who disagree with a denial can say so publicly. A dissent from denial of certiorari explains why a justice believes the Court should have taken the case. A statement respecting denial lets a justice comment without dissenting. Such statements sometimes flag a question the Court could address in a future case. Neither type of writing sets precedent. Both become part of the public record and can show where individual justices stand.

The Court also has a middle path between granting and denying. In a GVR — grant, vacate and remand — the Court grants the petition, vacates the lower-court judgment and sends the case back. The lower court then reconsiders the case, most often in light of a new Supreme Court decision. The Court often holds petitions that raise issues similar to a pending case, then disposes of them once it rules. A GVR does not decide who wins. The lower court can reach the same result after taking a fresh look.

Not every case reaches the Supreme Court through an ordinary cert petition. Original jurisdiction, mandatory direct appeals, certiorari before judgment and emergency applications each follow their own path to the justices.

Original jurisdiction comes from Article III of the Constitution. It covers cases affecting ambassadors, other public ministers and consuls, and cases in which a state is a party. In these matters, the Supreme Court acts as the trial court rather than as an appeals court.

Disputes between states form the core of this docket. Federal law makes the Court's jurisdiction over controversies between two or more states exclusive. No other court can hear them. Many of these suits involve state boundaries or the division of rivers and other shared water supplies.

A state cannot simply file such a suit and expect a trial. It must first ask the Court for leave to file a complaint, and the justices can refuse.

The justices do not take testimony themselves. In many original cases, the Court appoints a special master to gather evidence and hear arguments. The special master then writes a report with recommendations. The parties can file exceptions to that report. The justices make the final decision.

A small set of direct appeals remains mandatory. The best-known category involves rulings from three-judge district courts, which Congress requires in some redistricting challenges. The losing side can appeal straight to the Supreme Court. The Court must decide these appeals, though it can resolve some of them summarily without oral argument.

Supreme Court Rule 11 creates another shortcut, called certiorari before judgment. It lets the Court take a case while it is still pending in a federal appeals court. That skips the intermediate appellate ruling entirely. The rule allows it only when a case is of such imperative public importance that it justifies departing from normal appellate practice.

Emergency applications travel a separate track altogether. A party might ask the Court to stay a lower-court order or to pause a policy while litigation continues. Each application goes first to the justice assigned to the circuit where the case arose. That justice can act alone or refer the request to the full Court.

These emergency rulings often come without full briefing or oral argument. This part of the Court's work is often called the shadow docket.
