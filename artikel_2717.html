LeBron Log No. 6: A Rocky night, a shooting lesson and a fight over ‘Bron'

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

LeBron Log No. 6: A Rocky night, a shooting lesson and a fight over 'Bron' originally appeared on NBC Sports Philadelphia

It was another eventful week for LeBron James.

That's why we started the LeBron Log in the first place.

The King spent his latest week taking his teammates to the movies, fighting over his name in federal court and producing his best three-point shooting performance as a Sixer days after recording a podcast about the craft.

This week also marks 20 years since James faced the Sixers alongside Allen Iverson.

James found an appropriate choice for the Sixers' latest team outing.

He organized a private screening of I Play Rocky on Friday night for players, their families and members of the basketball operations staff. The film follows Sylvester Stallone's fight to get the original Rocky made while insisting that he play the title character.

"I mean, it's Rocky. We in Philly," James said. "I heard it was a great movie, and we had a night off. I've got a few connections, and I was able to bring that to my teammates and this organization for us to watch it. I thought it was a great movie. Unbelievably cast. Everyone looked like exactly who they were portraying. That is Hollywood for you. It was pretty cool."

The Sixers shared photos of James alongside Jaylen Brown, Kentavious Caldwell-Pope, Labaron Philon Jr., Jabari Walker, Tacko Fall and Adem Bona.

"Movie night, courtesy of LeBron," the team wrote.

The nationwide release of I Play Rocky came less than two weeks before Philadelphia celebrates the 50th anniversary of the original film on Dec. 3.

Nick Nurse could not attend because of a previous commitment, but he received positive reviews from his players.

"They went, they said it was very good, they loved it," Nurse said. "The big takeaway was how much Anthony actually looked like Rocky. Or Sylvester Stallone. One in the same."

James' first season in Philadelphia now includes a private Rocky screening. A trip up the Art Museum steps remains available for a future edition.

James and Steve Nash devoted the latest episode of their Mind the Game podcast to the art of shooting.

The two examined shooting form, confidence, repetitions, free throws and some of their favorite jumpers. James also revealed one detail that can affect what he does after catching the ball.

"I'm a guy who don't like shooting against the laces," James said. "So sometimes I'll come up on a quick stop and I'm about to raise and I can feel the laces not fixed. I'll turn that into a pump fake."

The episode was released Nov. 17, while James was experiencing an extended struggle from three-point range. It did not provide an instant fix.

James went 1 for 5 from three against Orlando and missed his lone attempt against Milwaukee. He entered Monday night's meeting with Portland shooting 21.7 percent from deep through his first 13 games.

James made all three of his first-half attempts from three. He ended the second quarter by pump-faking, stepping to his left and beating the buzzer with a corner three that tied the game at 69.

Nobody could see where the laces sat on the catch. The pump fake was clear.

James finished 4 for 5 from three and scored a season-high 23 points on 8-for-13 shooting. It was his third consecutive game with at least 20 points, following 22 against Orlando and 21 against Milwaukee.

The Sixers lost to Portland, 128-111, but James produced his best long-range performance since joining them.

Six days after discussing the art of shooting, he provided a brief demonstration.

James is also attempting to protect one of his most recognizable nicknames.

James and LBJ Trademarks LLC filed a federal lawsuit on Nov. 17 against PlayVision Inc. and its founders over a sports-betting research app initially called "Bron."

The complaint alleges that the company used an AI-generated version of James in an advertisement without his permission. The figure wore a No. 23 jersey with "Bron" across the back and resembled James through its voice, build and tattoos.

It also scrolled through wagers involving NBA players.

The lawsuit alleges that the advertisement falsely created the impression that James endorsed the app and suggested that he considered betting on NBA games. The complaint said the video received roughly 1 million views before it was removed.

"The integrity of NBA competition, and the public's confidence that players are not betting on the games they play, is of paramount importance to the league, its players and its fans," the complaint said. "Any suggestion that an active NBA player is betting on NBA games is therefore far from a trivial matter, and it should not be used for clicks and views."

The app offered odds comparisons, betting trends and tracking tools but did not directly accept wagers, according to its developer.

PlayVision renamed it "Evie" on Nov. 18, one day after James filed the lawsuit. James is seeking an injunction and unspecified damages. The case remains active in the Southern District of New York.

James spent Thanksgiving weekend in 2006 on the other side of the Sixers-Cavaliers matchup.

On Nov. 25, 2006, a 21-year-old James recorded 25 points, 11 assists, four rebounds and one block in Cleveland's 108-95 win over Philadelphia.

Donning the Cavs' old-school orange threads, he played nearly 41 minutes.

Iverson led the Sixers with 31 points. Andre Iguodala added 17 points and eight rebounds, while Kyle Korver made three three-pointers off the bench.

James recorded 11 assists that night. Nearly 20 years later, another 11-assist performance against Indiana moved him past Jason Kidd for third place in NBA history.

The 2006 meeting also came near the end of Iverson's original run with Philadelphia. The Sixers traded him to Denver less than a month later.

James was 21. Iverson was nearing his departure.

Twenty years later, James is wearing a Sixers uniform and Iverson's No. 3 hangs above his home floor.
