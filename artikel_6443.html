Experimental Drug Wipes Out Aggressive Pancreatic Cancer in 75% of Mice and Triggers Lasting Immunity

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

An experimental drug helped mice resist cancers their immune systems had never encountered, raising the possibility that treating one tumor could someday help protect against others.

Pancreatic cancer remains one of the most difficult cancers to treat, partly because tumors can wall themselves off from the immune system. Many breast, colon, and lung cancers use the same defense, surrounding themselves with dense, scar-like tissue that keeps cancer-fighting immune cells out. This barrier is one reason immunotherapy, which helps the immune system attack cancer, works for only a minority of patients with solid tumors.

Researchers have now developed an experimental drug that breaks through this barrier in mice. The treatment eliminated aggressive pancreatic tumors in some animals, prevented breast cancer from spreading, and produced lasting immune protection against future cancers.

The study, published November 9, 2026, in Cancer Cell, was led by Seong-Jin Kim, PhD, and scientists at MedPacto, Inc., in Seoul, Korea.

Tumors build their defenses partly by releasing large amounts of a chemical messenger called TGF-β. This substance encourages nearby cells to produce fibrous tissue while weakening the immune cells that could attack the cancer. Blocking it has proven difficult because TGF-β also performs important functions in healthy tissue. Previous drugs have struggled to suppress enough of it inside tumors without causing unwanted effects elsewhere in the body.

To get around this problem, the team developed a drug called FEBP-sTβRII that carries a molecular tag directing it toward tumors. The tag attaches to a particular form of fibronectin, a protein abundant in many cancers but rarely found in healthy adult tissue. Researchers detected this target in mouse models of breast, colon, lung, liver, prostate, pancreatic, and brain cancers, as well as melanoma, and in many human tumor types.

The targeted drug accumulated inside tumors and remained there for days. Without the tag, the same blocker had little effect on tumors and altered the activity of 693 genes in the blood, compared with just 25 for the targeted treatment.

“This study shows that where you block TGF-β matters as much as whether you block it,” said senior author Kim, who is also affiliated with the GILO Institute.

With the protective barrier weakened, cancer-killing T cells could enter the tumors. They released a chemical signal called interferon-gamma, prompting nearby support cells and immune cells to display recognizable pieces of the cancer. This helped the immune system identify its target and sustain the attack. Researchers observed this response in pancreatic tumors and melanoma.

Unlike existing immunotherapies that primarily remove restrictions on T cells, the experimental drug also changes the environment surrounding the cancer, making it easier for immune cells to reach and recognize tumors.

“TGF-β helps build the defenses of many solid tumors, so this approach has the potential to reach well beyond a single cancer,” said Dr. Letterio. “By delivering the blocker directly to the tumor, we don’t just open the door to T cells. We appear to be teaching the immune system lessons it remembers long after the tumor is gone, and in some cases those lessons protect against other cancers.”

Study co-author John J. Letterio, MD, PhD, directs the Angie Fowler Adolescent and Young Adult Cancer Institute at University Hospitals Rainbow Babies & Children’s Hospital and the Developmental Therapeutics Program at Case Comprehensive Cancer Center, Case Western Reserve University.

The immune response observed in mice resembled patterns previously seen in patients who responded to immunotherapy for melanoma, gastric cancer, and pancreatic cancer. Measuring the drug’s fibronectin target in tumors might eventually help identify patients most likely to benefit.

A human version of the drug has entered safety testing, with clinical trials planned for 2027 to evaluate its safety and effectiveness in patients whose solid tumors have not responded to standard treatments.
