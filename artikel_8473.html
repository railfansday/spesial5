A property manager typically takes 8% to 12% of the rent it collects, plus a leasing fee for each new tenant, which can exceed a small rental’s profit. Divide its yearly cost by the hours it saves you, and hire one if your time is worth more, under a contract you can end on 30 days’ notice.

Next to what the rental earns you, that fee can look like money thrown away. Your hours belong in the math too, especially if you live far away or own several units.

Take an owner whose house rents for $2,000 a month and leaves him $200 a month after the mortgage and other costs. A manager charging 10% of the rent, the middle of the typical range according to the National Association of Residential Property Managers, would cost him $2,400 a year. If a new tenant moves in every two years, a leasing fee of half a month’s rent adds $500 a year. His $2,400 yearly profit becomes a $500 loss. On cash alone, managing it himself beats hiring by $2,900 a year, so the choice comes down to his time.

Say he lives 300 miles away and would spend about 100 hours a year on the house, trips and late-night calls included. The manager costs him $29 for each of those hours and spares him the cost of the trips. If an hour of his time is worth more than $29, hiring beats doing the work himself, provided he can cover the $500 yearly loss.

If your hours are worth more than the manager’s cost per hour, vet each management company before you sign.

Sign only a contract you can end on 30 days’ notice, with a dollar limit on repairs ordered without your approval.

Then add up a full year of each finalist’s fees at your rent, since leasing and renewal fees can make a lower monthly percentage cost more than a higher one.
