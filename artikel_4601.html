I’ve long been interested in the lives and fate of rescued dogs, the spirit of empathic kinship, the wonderful relationships that can developed between rescued dogs and their humans, why rescued dogs aren't any quirkier than other dogs, and why Psychology Today writer and dog expert stopped using the excuse, "Oh, she's a rescue dog." because her dog Bella didn't like to be touched by strangers and there is nothing wrong with this.

Writing about shelter and rescue dogs can be tricky business and I was very pleased to learn of a detailed and balanced view centering on the lives of these dogs who need all the care and love they can get. In her new highly acclaimed book Everydog: The Real Story of America's Shelter and Rescue Dogs ... and How You Can Help Them, the eclectic writer Eliza Rubenstein brings years of experience in different aspects of the world of dogs to the fore and offers "a compassionate, clear-eyed examination of the problem of homeless dogs in America."

Here's what Rubenstein had to say about these most complex issues while blending facts and research with interviews, stories, humor, and commentary.

Eliza Rubenstein: Every era of my life has been made better (okay, and a little messier) by dogs--living with them, training and showing them, writing about them, and working to find homes for the ones who need a place to go. A little over 30 years ago, my best friend and I wrote one of the earliest how-to books about dog adoption, and I've always thought that someday I'd write another book on the subject--not a how-to this time, but a deeper look into the fascinating world of sheltering and rescue for dog lovers who want to understand it better and become more effective allies.

ER: I wrote this book for everyone who loves and cares about dogs: those who have adopted (or been frustrated in their attempts to adopt) a dog, those who work in shelters or rescues, or those who simply find the subject interesting. It's conversational in tone and specifically designed to be more uplifting than it is depressing, since I know that's a concern for all of us with soft hearts!

MB: What are some of the topics you consider and what are some of your major messages?

ER: Everydog explores the nuance behind (and unpacks some of the confusion around) many of the hottest topics in sheltering. Why is it so hard to adopt when I keep hearing that there are too many dogs in shelters? What does "no-kill" really mean? Did people really return all their COVID adoptees to shelters after they went back to work? Can we really "save them all"?

There's a lot of incomplete and fragmented information on the internet about dog adoption, and a lot of it is wrapped up in emotion and anger, even to the point of misanthropy, so I'm interested in filling in the information gaps and helping to find some common ground among the various weary factions of people who care about them.

One of the constant themes in the book is that we can't help dogs if we alienate humans, because--as a very smart woman quoted in its pages says--"you need people to save animals."

MB: How does your work differ from others that are concerned with some of the same general topics?

ER: When I began to imagine what this book could be, I went searching for others on the subject; surely someone had already written a book for people who wanted to understand the world of dog sheltering, adoption, and homelessness. But nobody had.

There were (and are) great books on niche aspects of the field, and inspiring stories about particular dogs and people that offered important and insightful glimpses into it, yet I couldn't find anything quite like the one I was envisioning. And when you spend enough time in sheltering, you learn that there's no virtue in waiting around for someone else to do the work when you could be doing it yourself, so I started writing.

MB: Are you hopeful that as people learn more about the ins and outs and ups and downs of sheltering dogs they will come to appreciate what the dogs and humans go through to give the dogs the best lives possible?

ER: At least in my own life, I know that being the best possible ally and advocate means combining empathy with education. I hope Everydog will equip dog lovers with information they can use, while encouraging them to keep an open heart even on the days when that's difficult. But I also hope it will be a book they love reading! It's filled with stories of wonderful dogs, incredibly inspiring people, a lot of funny stuff, and plenty of my own "aha" moments and dog-driven learning curves.

There are sobering moments and difficult topics, but there are also so, so many reasons to be optimistic about the future of dogs in this country. It's my fondest hope that this book encourages readers to join that movement. Dogs are worth it.
