Australian government moves ahead with algorithm opt-out legislation

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Let Social Media Today's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The proposed bill has passed another Senate hurdle, according to Australia’s ABC News.

The Australian government is moving to the next stage of implementing laws that would force social media platforms to provide algorithm opt-outs for all users.

In September, the Australian government announced that it would seek to implement legislation to enable all social media users to opt out of engagement-based algorithms. The move would let users switch to a chronological post order by default, ensuring that they would only see content from profiles that they have explicitly chosen to follow.

The landmark initiative could provide a valuable test case on the impacts of algorithms, and the data gleaned from that could form the basis for similar laws and restrictions in other markets.

Though various groups have opposed the concept, including U.S. authorities, which have raised concerns about localized censorship of content and control over U.S.-owned businesses.

However, the Australian government has now gained support to press ahead with the next stage of the plan.

As per reporting from Australia’s ABC News, the government reached a deal with the Australian Greens, a left-leaning political party, to secure support for the proposed bill, though the legislation did have to include some amendments.

As per ABC News: “Under Labor’s deal with the Greens, the maximum penalty for breaching the duty would be raised to up to 5 per cent of a platform's global revenue, rather than Labor's initial proposal of up to $109.2 million. The amendments also grant the government rule-making powers to establish a digital complaints ombudsman in the future to handle small claims and resolve minor issues.”

The proposal will also now include more specific examinations of potentially addictive design features.

Some political commentators and opponents of the legislation have raised concerns about the potential impact on free speech as a result of these laws.

Conservative commentator Michael Shellenberger recently suggested that the bill would give Australia’s eSafety Commissioner “sweeping powers over online speech, including the ability to operate fake social media accounts.”

The Australian government has amended the initial proposal to ensure that all content decisions are put to Parliament, and are not made by a single arbiter. Meanwhile, the government is also assessing a provision that allows researchers to create accounts to test whether platforms are adhering to the laws.

If approved, Australia's algorithm opt-out laws are projected to take effect from late 2027.
