It’s Not Too Late to Plant These 5 Perennials for a Gorgeous Fall Garden

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Add cheer to gray days with these late-season planting picks.

Consistent wind and rain might not exactly be “gardening weather,” but you can still add color to your garden this autumn. If you have a bare patch to fill, some established perennials can be planted now—as long as the ground isn't waterlogged or frozen.

As autumn deepens and the last of the summer-flowering plants begin to fade, our gardens can start to look a little tired. Add the persistent drizzle and gray skies, and it's easy to feel that the color has gone for the year.

The good news is that there's still time to brighten things up. Established autumn-flowering perennials are a quick and reliable way to bring color and seasonal interest to your garden, and a mild, wet spell can help newly planted roots settle in.

The key is to check the conditions first: avoid planting directly into waterlogged soil, which can suffocate plants, or when the ground is frozen. If the soil is too wet to work, choose pots with good drainage and keep them somewhere sheltered from strong winds.

As Charlotte McGrattan, hardy nursery stock buyer at Hillier Garden Centers, adds, newly planted perennials “require watering well while they establish.” Rain can help with that, but check the soil rather than assuming your plants have had enough: containers, in particular, may still need watering.

Here, McGrattan shares five autumn-flowering perennials you can plant now.

Heleniums are tough, hardy, and easy to grow. If your soil is very wet or compacted, it's best to plant them in a pot positioned in a sheltered spot away from the wind.

“Heleniums add an abundance of color, bursting from mid-summer through into autumn. There are hundreds of varieties to choose from, from hot yellows and reds to vibrant oranges,” McGrattan says.

In these mild temperatures, it's also not too late to plant heliopsis, also known as “false sunflower.” “They not only make impressive, colorful features in borders and beds, attracting an array of pollinators but can also make for lovely cut flowers,” says McGrattan.

“They grow in clumps with branching stems that give a bushy habit, and the flowering period is between six and eight weeks. To encourage buds to form, deadhead any spent flowers.”

While spring is generally the best time to plant penstemons—which are available in shades of purple, pink, white, and blue—you can still plant them in pots now.

“With some varieties being bi-colors combining two shades, they bring welcome color to the garden,” says McGrattan. “Penstemon thrives in well-drained soil and is very tolerant to drought conditions.”

Crocosmias are a great way to bring both height and vibrant color to your autumn borders, as long as you plant established plants with roots instead of growing them from corms (bulbs). “Originating from South Africa, they require fertile, moist, but well-drained soil,” McGrattan adds.

You can also still plant potted Japanese anemones in late autumn, provided the ground is neither frozen nor waterlogged. “Japanese anemones boast cup-shaped pink or white flowers on the top of tall stems and can spread rapidly, so can be a great means of filling borders,” says McGrattan.
