Shock anti-doping revelation: How Chris Lynn's positive cocaine test was kept under wraps

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Australia's Chris Lynn served a one-month ban for cocaine use and completed a rehabilitation programme.

His sample was taken on T20 Blast Finals Day, when Northamptonshire won the trophy.

His ban was reduced to one month, and he reportedly joins Melbourne Renegades.

A positive cocaine test that stayed out of the public domain for weeks has now come to light, after England's Cricket Regulator has revealed that Chris Lynn, the veteran Australian, served a one-month ban in August and has since gone through a rehabilitation programme.

Lynn is 36, and his name has been a fixture of franchise cricket around the world for well over a decade.

The trial began on 18 July, when Lynn went through a routine anti-doping check on T20 Blast Finals Day. He scored 39 and 4 that day as Northamptonshire won the trophy for the third time.

His urine sample was then sent to a laboratory in France, where the analysis found benzoylecgonine, a metabolite of cocaine. That finding set the entire disciplinary process in motion.

Lynn left for Sri Lanka almost straight after the Blast to play in the Lanka Premier League. He played only one game for Galle Gallants before being ruled out on 29 July with a leg injury.

Two days later, the Cricket Regulator informed him of the adverse analytical findings in his sample and placed him under a provisional suspension. Roughly three weeks after that, Lynn publicly admitted that he had taken the drug.

The statement he released explained when the use took place. He said it happened over a spell that stretched into the early hours of the following morning: "During a period of several hours across the evening of 15 July 2026 and into the early hours of 16 July 2026".

The timing adds a curious detail as earlier on that same evening of 15 July, Lynn had been forced to retire hurt in Northamptonshire's quarter-final win over Gloucestershire, after a low full-toss from Duan Jansen struck him on the foot.

The Regulator accepted Lynn's explanation that he used the drug out of competition and that it had nothing to do with improving his sporting performance.

That distinction matters a great deal under anti-doping rules, because it shapes how severe the sanction can be.

Lynn was first charged under two articles of the ECB's anti-doping rules. One of them was later withdrawn, since the amount of cocaine found in his sample fell below the minimum level that World Anti-Doping Agency rules require before a charge can be brought.

The Regulator had originally planned to keep Lynn out for three months. That was cut to one month once he provided proof that he had finished a "substance of abuse treatment programme". The programme was deemed complete on 30 August, which was a month after his provisional suspension had started.

A statement from the body said: "The Cricket Regulator has imposed a one-month suspension on former Northamptonshire Steelbacks cricketer Chris Lynn. The period of suspension has now been served. Mr Lynn produced an Adverse Analytical Finding (AAF) for the presence of cocaine's Metabolite, benzoylecgonine, related to an In Competition sample collected on the 18 July 2026. Mr Lynn has accepted the sanction. No further comment will be made on this matter."

Lynn has not played any professional cricket since the suspension began. He has not formally signed for the next Big Bash League season, the competition where he first made his name as a fearsome six-hitter. Code Sports reported this week, however, that he has agreed to join Melbourne Renegades.

The most uncomfortable part of this story is not the test result but the silence that surrounded it. A senior player was provisionally suspended in the middle of a major tournament, and the public heard nothing until the matter was settled.

That said, the outcome looks measured. The Regulator accepted that the drug use was out of competition and unrelated to performance, and it reduced the ban only after Lynn completed treatment. Rewarding that kind of effort sends a sensible message, because the aim should be to help players recover rather than simply to punish them.

Lynn also deserves credit for admitting what he did without dragging it out. A player of his stature could have fought the finding on technical grounds, and he chose not to.

Cricket needs to be clearer about how it handles such cases and when it informs the public. If Melbourne Renegades do sign him, the focus should return to his batting, but the game owes its followers a more open process the next time something like this happens.
