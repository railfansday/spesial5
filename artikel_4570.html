Zines Are a Great Tool for Learning

A zine (pronounced “zeen”) is a little booklet or mini-magazine you make yourself, combining elements of a scrapbook, journal, collage, or a handmade book. You can write, draw, add photos, stickers, quotes, stories, or anything else you want to express. Everything is customizable, from page size and arrangement to colors, materials, and overall look and feel. Zines have a long history of giving people excluded from mainstream publishing a way to share their ideas, experiences, art, and perspectives. Today, zine-making is a worldwide culture, with makers sharing and trading their work at fairs, festivals, libraries, bookstores, and community spaces. The zine has become a cultural icon with a distinct history, community, and spirit of creative independence.

Zine-making is a form of project-based learning and a student-centered way of learning through creating something meaningful. Project-based learning gives people an active role: they explore questions, solve problems, make choices, connect ideas, and apply what they are learning to real situations. Zines bring some especially useful qualities to that process. They give people many ways to work with an idea through writing, images, collage, humor, stories, symbols, personal experience, or cultural references, while also asking them to think about who will read or see the zine and how they want to communicate with that audience. The finished zine becomes something they can hold, share, discuss, and return to later. Across studies in classrooms and museums, zine-making has helped people connect course or exhibit content with their own experiences and interests, while giving them space to shape that learning into something distinctly their own.

Psychologically, making a zine brings together what a person notices, remembers, feels, experiences, questions, and connects while they are actively making something. As they revisit what they know, new information connects with prior experience and can reshape how they understand it. Putting ideas into words, images, and materials makes those ideas visible, giving the person something to reconsider and change. Thinking about an audience shapes how they communicate, while reflection can also turn inward toward identity and belief. Through this process, people build knowledge that feels meaningful and personal.

In this zine, the maker transforms a museum visit into a reflection on love, connection, memory, and being alive. Using photographs from the visit, handwritten thoughts, museum materials, collage, thread, and a three-dimensional heart labeled “my core,” they connect dinosaurs, animals, ancient history, memory, and emotion through themes of love and interconnectedness. Museum content becomes part of a personal interpretation, helping the maker explore what the visit means to them and communicate that meaning to others.

In this zine, a student transforms educational psychology concepts into a personal explanation of learning. They use gears to represent thinking, a bell for behavior and conditioning, drawings of memory and motivation, and a child connecting ideas across the page. They also develop their own argument that creativity is a form of intelligence, extending the course material with a personal claim. By choosing how to connect and visually represent these concepts, the student creates both a reflection on their learning and an expression of how they understand education.

Zine-making is accessible almost anywhere, from homes and classrooms to museums and community spaces, using simple materials like paper, pens, magazines, photos, and stickers.

Anyone can make a zine, so give it a try and see what emerges!
