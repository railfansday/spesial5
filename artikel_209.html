South Korea to Examine How K-Pop Industry Treats Underage Performers

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

The announcement came after a New York Times investigation about the restrictive contracts of the group NewJeans, signed when its members were 14 to 17.

South Korea’s government is planning to study how the K-pop industry treats underage performers, who are central to the industry, and to re-examine the standard template for contracts it released nearly two decades ago.

The Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism will start an industrywide survey sometime next year, a ministry official said in a telephone interview on Monday. The government already studies the K-pop industry, checking whether record companies follow its standard contract and how much money is invested in training young stars. The new effort will focus specifically on how underage artists are treated, but its methodology, scope and exact timeline have not been decided on.

The announcement came just over a week after The New York Times published an investigation into the record contracts signed by the members of NewJeans, one of K-pop’s biggest girl groups, when they were ages 14 to 17. The investigation found that the contracts removed language included in the government template that guarantees rights for minors, such as the rights to education and sleep.

It was a rare look into the business of K-pop, which has long been dogged by allegations of exploitation. Record companies recruit children who are aspiring stars, put them through years of intensive training and then often bill them for those costs. Several stars have died by suicide.

Two years ago, NewJeans tried to quit their label in a dispute that turned into one of K-pop’s most public fights, with the members saying they had been mistreated. A court later held them to the contracts they had signed, which run until 2029.

The Seoul Broadcasting System reported the ministry’s plan earlier.

In 2009, after a boy band sued its label over restrictive work agreements, the authorities created a standard contract. It outlined protections such as capping agreements at seven years, taking into consideration an artist’s physical and mental well-being and transferring trademark rights to the artist after the terms end.

The template has been amended several times over the years but still largely remains legally nonbinding. Record companies often alter clauses and add their own conditions. Some do not use the template at all, a government survey found.

Jin Yu Young is a reporter for The Times, based in Seoul, covering South Korea and international breaking news.

Sarah Hurtes is a Times reporter working on international investigations from Brussels.
