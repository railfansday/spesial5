In California’s Central Valley, Democrats hope to harvest fruits of Proposition 50

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

By Sean Campbell, Nicholas Corral, Jules Cox, Aden Max Juarez, Caitlin Roehmholdt, Jose Trejo and Maybeline Tucker

BAKERSFIELD, Calif. — In a final get-out-the-vote push Monday, California’s two U.S. senators joined Randy Villegas at a labor union headquarters in the Central Valley district that could be critical to Democrats’ attempts to win back control of the House.

The 22nd District, spanning farmland from north of Fresno to south of Bakersfield, was redrawn via Proposition 50 to weaken six-term Republican Rep. David Valadao’s chance of reelection.

Valadao, who lost his seat once in 2018 during a Democratic takeover in President Donald Trump’s first term, is facing an even tougher environment now thanks to redistricting, high prices and the war in Iran. Inside Elections lists the race as a Toss-up.

“This is still very much a battleground district for California and for the country,” Democratic Sen. Alex Padilla told reporters after the rally Monday morning. “That’s why you see the level of commitment from so many volunteers knocking on doors, making the phone calls, getting out the vote.”

Valadao has defied the odds before. He won the seat back in 2020 after losing by less than 1,000 votes in 2018 and has held it since. He is the only congressional Republican who voted to impeach Trump in 2021 who is still serving in the House. Trump won the counties in the 22nd District in 2024, but under the new lines, the district would only have gone for Trump by 1.5 percentage points, one reason why party and labor leaders made the trip Monday in a final push.

Villegas, a mechanic and progressive college professor, wore Converse and cheered as speaker after speaker proclaimed he would be the winner once votes are counted in California.

Introducing himself as the Robin to Padilla’s Batman, Democratic Sen. Adam B. Schiff said Californians “need a champion like Randy in Congress.” He egged on the crowd: “Are you so over David Valadao?”

Padilla, center, and Schiff, right, campaigned for Villegas on Monday. (USC Annenberg Media)

The senators are not the only big names to stump in the district. In September and October, Sen. Bernie Sanders, I-Vt., and Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, D-N.Y., as well as House Speaker Mike Johnson, R-La., appeared in the Central Valley. When asked, Villegas pushed back against the notion that his race was only competitive because of the favorably redrawn district.

“This is a district that we would’ve been competitive in regardless,” Villegas told reporters after the morning canvass kickoff. “Our message is one that has resonated across the political spectrum.”

Addressing the crowd in both English and Spanish, Villegas dropped a few curse words to punctuate his get-out-the-vote messaging. He told the labor activists gathered in the standing-room-only rally that when their sunscreen and sweat dripped in their eyes while canvassing later, they should remember, “That is the pain David Valadao and Republicans are going to be feeling.”

Cathy Abernathy, a Republican consultant and prominent GOP activist in Bakersfield, said Villegas is “too far” left for the Central Valley, but she stopped short of saying she was confident Valadao could win. She also criticized Prop 50 and the way Democrats redrew the lines to break up cities and counties and said she would support national reform of the redistricting process.

“David Valadao, he’s resilient. He’s a fighter. He has constantly gone against the odds,” said Matthew Martin, chair of the Kern County Young Republicans, taking a moment to chat with reporters after sending get-out-the-vote text messages in the Republican Party headquarters in Bakersfield. If it wasn’t for Prop 50 shifting the makeup of the district so dramatically, “I think this would be more of a slam dunk for David Valadao,” he said.

Martin predicted that Latinos age 45 and under would be the deciding factor in the election. The district is 74 percent Latino, according to 2020 Census data.

Prop 50, pushed by Democratic Gov. Gavin Newsom and approved by voters last year, 64 percent to 36 percent, redrew the 22nd to make it easier for a Democrat to win. Valadao’s own home base of Hanford, where his family owns a dairy farm, was carved out of his district.

Valadao posted a photo of himself on Facebook dropping his ballot in a drop box in Hanford. His campaign did not respond to multiple requests from reporters seeking to cover activity on the ground.

Instead, Valadao joined California Republican gubernatorial candidate Steve Hilton at a rally last Thursday. He also posted on his Facebook page about celebrating the birthday of the founder of the Sikh religion, Guru Nanak. At the event with Johnson last month, the congressman said, “We’ve got a lot on the line. We’ve got to keep fighting all the way towards the end.”

The district is at the epicenter of multiple nationwide fights: It saw early immigration enforcement raids during Trump’s second term, and changes to Medicaid through the “One Big Beautiful Bill Act” are expected to have a major impact. Roughly six in 10 residents in the district are enrolled in the state’s Medicaid program, known in California as Medi-Cal.

For Republican voter Kevin Caid, Trump was top of mind as he dropped off his ballot at California State University, Bakersfield. He lives in the neighboring district represented by Republican Rep. Vince Fong. Caid voted Republican down the ballot, saying his top issues were taxes and keeping a nuclear weapon out of Iranian hands.

“If gas is high, you know, that’s the price it takes,” Caid said.

Linda Fiddler, a canvasser for the Villegas campaign, is a long-time campaign volunteer in the district, having worked on Democratic campaigns since 2010. She described Villegas as a “game changer” compared to previous Democratic candidates.

“They never distinguished themselves from the Republican,” Fiddler said. She attributes Valadao’s previous success to voters lacking a strong reason to vote against him. She hopes Villegas’ messaging of “you deserve more” is that reason.

Jacob Brunell, 28, the field director for the Westside Democratic Headquarters, said he’s canvassed multiple times in Bakersfield, but this time he felt the excitement and noticed Republican voters crossing party lines.

“This is an area that has been consistently, throughout time, screwed over by land developers, by energy owners,” Brunell said. “I think [voters] are happy to see a Bernie Sanders-backed progressive taking on this fight and hopefully winning the election.”

Bakersfield Vice Mayor Manpreet Kaur said that in this land of farm workers, she has seen young people turning out to vote at the same level as voters aged 60 to 65, and said she believes it is to protect their immigrant families.

“Children of immigrants are becoming that protective barrier between Trump’s illegal operations being carried out and harming our families and our communities,” Kaur said. “The immigrant struggle is everyone’s struggle, whether they admit it or not.”

Jon Rodriguez, 26, a Latino voter from Bakersfield, said immigration was the biggest issue driving his vote after Immigration and Customs Enforcement activity increased in East Bakersfield. While he lives in the bordering 20th District, represented by Fong and packed with more Republicans by Prop 50, Rodriguez said he would have voted for Villegas if he could have.

“Whether they’re citizens or noncitizens, whether they have a visa here or they have full-time citizenship, they’re still scared because they know by the color of their skin they’re going to be targeted,” Rodriguez said after dropping off his ballot at a voting station at California State University, Bakersfield.

Andres Ramirez, 21, of Bakersfield, said he voted for Villegas. Because his parents are in the process of applying for U.S. citizenship, he said he hopes having Democrats in office will protect them from being deported.

“With Trump, he’s wanting to deport us,” Ramirez said. “But I’m legal here, so I make sure that he doesn’t want to, like, attack us.”

Democrats have focused their attack ads on Valadao’s vote for Trump’s “One Big Beautiful Bill Act,” which imposed a work requirement for Medicaid eligibility. Cecilia Torres, 30, cited that vote as she cast her ballot at CSU Bakersfield.

“I do kind of fear for my health care, and will it be affordable? Will I have it, even?” Torres said. “I really hope to see him removed from office and never again see him on the ballot.”

Dolores Huerta, a liberal labor icon who endorsed Villegas in the primary, died in Bakersfield last week at age 96. Chants of “Sí se puede,” a phrase coined by Huerta meaning, “Yes, we can,” broke out during Villegas’ Monday morning rally.

“It is everything,” Villegas said when asked what Huerta’s legacy meant to him. “One thing that I’ll always take to heart is her own words: that every moment is a potential organizing opportunity, every person is a potential activist, and every minute is a chance to change the world.”
