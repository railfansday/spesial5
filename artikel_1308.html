The curious case of Sebastien Pocognoli looks certain to have a grisly ending... the SFA have hired a dud

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

By CALUM CROWE, SCOTTISH DEPUTY CHIEF SPORTS WRITER

Published: 14:13 EST, 17 November 2026 | Updated: 14:26 EST, 17 November 2026

Of all the many stories written by F Scott Fitzgerald, one of the finest authors and wordsmiths of his time, The Great Gatsby will forever remain his masterpiece.

In some of his other work written as he travelled across Europe, Fitzgerald also had a line about Switzerland, describing it as a ‘country where very few things begin, but many things end.’

It is a curious quote and, like much of Fitzgerald’s work, is open to interpretation. As the man who also wrote The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, curiosity was a common theme.

As for the Curious Case of Sebastien Pocognoli, Switzerland may, indeed, be the country where everything came to an end.

What unfolded in St Gallen on Monday evening was a horror show. Scotland were battered 4-0 by Switzerland’s B team. There is no way to sugar-coat a result like that, no way to dress it up as anything other than an embarrassment. They played OK for half an hour and then fell apart.

Pocognoli claimed afterwards that there were positives to be taken and how some of Scotland’s football was the best they have produced across his six matches in charge.

Sebastien Pocognoli is facing serious questions after a miserable Nations League campaign

Utter nonsense. That was a manager clutching at straws at the end of a Nations League campaign which has been an unmitigated disaster pretty much from start to finish.

Let’s go back to the very start here. After Steve Clarke had pushed the Scottish Football Association (SFA) into awarding him a new four-year contract, only to then walk away after the World Cup, it was a chance for a fresh start.

On the day when Pocognoli was unveiled as the new head coach in August, Ian Maxwell and Craig Mulholland, the SFA’s chief executive and chief football officer, were beaming with positivity.

They told us all how data and AI had been used in the recruitment process, and how several clubs across Europe had been chasing Pocognoli.

They clearly viewed it as a coup to have landed the 39-year-old Belgian, who had won a league title in his homeland with Union Saint-Gilloise before a short stint with Monaco.

What would the data say now? Six games played, two wins, a draw and three defeats, five goals scored and ten conceded, seven points and a lousy third-placed finish in Group B1.

Both of those wins came against a North Macedonian side who lost all six of their games. They were a pub team – and Scotland still struggled to beat them at Hampden last Friday night.

Pocognoli was unable to inspire any resistance as his side succumbed to defeat in St Gallen

Scotland lost 7-0 on aggregate to Switzerland over the two games. Against Slovenia, a 0-0 draw on the opening day in Ljubljana was then followed with a woeful 2-1 defeat at Hampden.

All of which begs a very simple question. Where, exactly, are the grounds for optimism here? How is this getting any better?

When Pocognoli arrived, he spoke about trying to change the style of play, get Scotland playing a more exciting brand of football, whilst bringing through a new generation of players.

It was not a rebuild. He inherited a group of players who were accustomed to success under Clarke and who qualified for three tournaments out of a possible four.

His remit was to help Scotland take the next step in major tournaments. With a more dynamic style of play, the job was to help the nation take the next step.

There has been no revolution thus far. No evidence whatsoever that this coach is capable of delivering results nor a better style of play.

There is no satisfaction to be taken in any of this. When Pocognoli was appointed, it was, in the eyes of this observer, an interesting and progressive move.

SFA chiefs Craig Mulholland and Ian Maxwell proudly unveil new boss Pocognoli at Hampden

Over the past 48 hours or so, there has been a lot of revisionism from some folk about Clarke. ‘Aye, the grass isn’t always greener...’

That’s garbage. Clarke’s time was up with Scotland. He should never have been handed a new four-year deal in the first place. At least he did the right thing in resigning after the World Cup.

The whole thing was finished. The problem is that the SFA look like they have appointed a total dud as his successor. Both of those things can be true at the same time.

It is also worth remembering that the only reason we arrived at Pocognoli is because Clarke walked away. The SFA did not instigate any change.

The hiring of a new head coach was not a proactive change of direction, but a reactive move from an executive body who became accepting of Clarke’s limitations and allowed him to position them over a barrel.

Listen, there is nothing inherently wrong with using data and metrics in football. It’s the modern way. Using data to inform decisions and recruitment processes is smart business.

But the data in itself is no guarantee of success. There are some intangibles in football, things which are harder to quantify. Like a manager who blindly sticks to a system which is patently ill-suited to the players at his disposal.

Things like leadership and communication skills. We can cut Pocognoli some slack in that English is not his first language. He comes across as a thoroughly decent and likeable chap. He’s doing his best. He wants to embrace the culture and makes an effort to sing the National Anthem.

Scotland's defeat arose from individual mistakes, a common theme during the Belgian's reign

All of that is undoubtedly to be welcomed. Good on him. But would anyone describe him as an inspirational figure?

When you listen to him speak, have you ever felt slightly confused in terms of the message he’s actually trying to get across?

How must that sound in the dressing room? When Andy Robertson spoke post-match on Monday night, there was frustration in his voice.

The captain insisted there is simply no time or patience with these things. Scotland need to deliver results now.

To be fair, there actually would be a degree of patience shown by the Tartan Army towards Pocognoli if there was something to actually believe in.

There was a lot of goodwill towards him at the start. If there were signs across these last six games that he could deliver a more exciting team to watch, they would show patience.

But on the basis of what we’ve seen so far, there’s been absolutely nothing to suggest he can move this team forward. Ask yourself a very simple question – do Scotland pass the eye test?

Are we seeing a team who are playing on the front foot and creating chances? The answer, obviously, is no.

Steve Clarke celebrates Scotland's win over Denmark a year ago... how fans miss such highs

Many Scotland fans are now confronting the possibility that we might not even qualify for Euro 2028, a tournament which we are co-hosting.

By finishing third in the Nations League, we will now be in pot three for the Euro qualifiers and will face a more difficult group.

Beyond that, we would be relying on the back-door method of one of the best-placed home nations not to qualify directly.

Those Euro qualifiers will start in March of next year. Does Pocognoli even make it that far? Or should the SFA hold their hands up, admit they’ve made a mistake, and rectify it?

That feels unlikely. But on the basis of what we have seen over these first six games, it’s hard to see how this gets any better.

We are worse now than we were at the end under Clarke. Much, much worse. This is not teething problems. It’s a team who have regressed massively under a head coach who was supposed to move the whole thing forward.
