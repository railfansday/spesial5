Businesses are leaving Claude for free AI models that do the job almost as well

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

OpenAI and Anthropic have grown even as customers move work to cheaper models. Those labs may struggle to raise prices if users can leave any month

In June, Flo Crivello moved all of the AI work at his San Francisco startup, Lindy, off Anthropic's Claude and onto cheaper models from DeepSeek, a Chinese company that lets anyone download its software and run it. Lindy, a company of roughly 25 people, had been paying "unsustainable" AI bills for a long time, Crivello told CNBC. "It's a matter of survival for the business," he said. "That's all it is." After the switch, he said, "you could see that cost curve go down, like, crash to the ground." He expects the move to save Lindy millions of dollars within months, though the startup will still spend more on AI than on its payroll.

A Google $GOOGL engineer predicted moves like Crivello's in an internal memo that leaked in 2023. Someone posted the memo on a public Discord server, and on May 4, 2023, the research newsletter SemiAnalysis published it, declaring that Google and OpenAI no longer had a moat, or what keeps competitors from taking a company's customers. The memo argued that free models from outside developers were catching up fast, and that Google and OpenAI weren't positioned to win the race. The memo's author also predicted how customers would respond. "People will not pay for a restricted model when free, unrestricted alternatives are comparable in quality," he wrote.

Luke Sernau, the senior Google engineer who wrote the memo, argued that outside developers were matching Google's AI work for a tiny share of the cost. Within a month of Meta $META's LLaMA model leaking to the public in early 2023, people were running versions of it on a Google Pixel 6 phone, and anyone could tailor one to their own needs on a laptop in an evening. In his estimate, open-source developers were doing with $100 and a small model what Google struggled to do with $10 million and a model about 40 times bigger. For every dollar the open-source developers spent, Google spent $100,000.

Demis Hassabis, the CEO of Google DeepMind, told The Verge in July 2023 that he disagreed with the memo's conclusions. "I think that memo was real. I think engineers at Google often write various documents, and sometimes they get leaked and go viral," Hassabis said. Hassabis argued that Google's record of research breakthroughs would keep it ahead of the free models. "Look at the history of what Google and DeepMind have done in terms of coming up with new innovations and breakthroughs," he said. "I would bet on us." Hassabis was wagering that Google could keep building models good enough that customers would pay for them rather than settle for free ones.

In 2024, Google did what Sernau had urged. His memo told Google to lead the open-source community the way it had with Chrome and Android, and to publish small versions of its models for anyone to use, even if the company lost some control over them. "We cannot hope to both drive innovation and control it," he wrote. On Feb. 21, 2024, Google released Gemma, a family of free models that developers anywhere could download and run on a laptop. Google built Gemma from the research behind its Gemini models.

The paid labs still pull ahead each time one of them releases a major model, such as OpenAI's o1-preview or Google's Gemini 2.5 Pro, according to Stanford University's 2026 AI Index. But in March 2026, the best open model scored within about 3% of the best paid one in head-to-head votes by users. With ability no longer a clear way to tell the top models apart, the report's authors wrote, "competitive pressure is shifting toward cost, reliability, and real-world usefulness."

Intercom, which makes customer-service software, cut almost all of the cost of one of its busiest AI jobs by moving it to an open model. Before Fin, Intercom's AI customer-service agent, looks up an answer, a separate stage rewrites and summarizes the customer's question. Intercom ran the summarizing stage on OpenAI's GPT-4.1, and Fergal Reid, its chief AI officer, told the AI show Chain of Thought in February that it cost roughly a quarter of a million dollars a month, or about $3 million a year for a single step in one product, though he didn't remember the exact figure. Intercom replaced GPT-4.1 with Qwen 3, an open model it trained further for the job, and kept a top paid model only for the hardest work: writing the answer. By October, Intercom had switched again, to models from Nvidia $NVDA and Google. What matters, Reid wrote on LinkedIn, is the company's own process for training a model, "not any one base model."

Many companies now send routine work to cheap models and save paid ones for hard jobs. Vercel runs an AI gateway, a service that passes businesses' requests to models from many labs, and its September figures show customers splitting their work by cost. Vercel measures the work in tokens, the chunks of text an AI reads and writes. In September, open models handled more than seven of every 10 tokens that passed through the gateway but drew less than one of every four dollars spent there, because a token from an open model costs far less than one from a top lab. Open models' share of the money has still more than quadrupled since June, when it was about one dollar in 20, according to Vercel.

Development teams switch labs often. Almost one in three teams on the gateway changed the lab they used most in September, the highest share Vercel has recorded, and switching has risen for five months in a row. In August, Anthropic drew about 64 cents of every dollar spent on the gateway. Anthropic's take fell to 44 cents in September, the first month it drew less than half.

The memo got one thing wrong. Sernau wrote that "OpenAI doesn't matter" and that open models would eventually eclipse it unless it changed course, but paid labs have kept growing even as customers move work elsewhere. Anthropic's revenue rose from $41 million in 2023 to about $4.6 billion in 2025, more than 100 times as much, according to its prospectus. On Vercel's gateway, spending on the top labs' models is up about tenfold since last December, while spending on open models is up more than seventyfold.

Anthropic has bet that its sales will grow fast enough to pay for computing it has already ordered. The company has signed long-term deals for close to $900 billion in data center capacity, much of it difficult to unwind, even though in 2025 it spent about $1.60 on computing for every dollar of revenue it brought in. So far, Anthropic's sales have kept rising because companies are spending so much more on AI overall, even as Claude's share of the money shrinks. If total spending on AI stops rising faster than Claude's share falls, Anthropic's sales will stall while its data center bills keep coming due.

Crivello said he would bring Lindy back to Claude if Anthropic cut its prices. "I hope that they cut the costs again at some point but, until then, we've got options," he told CNBC. As long as the top AI models are about equally good, customers can leave any lab that raises its prices, and companies will have a hard time charging more. People and businesses will keep getting more out of AI than they pay for it, and the labs won't be able to raise prices enough to collect the difference. For Anthropic's new shareholders, winning back customers like Crivello means charging them less, while the company still owes close to $900 billion for computing.
