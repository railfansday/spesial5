Former Robinhood Engineer Pleads Guilty to Trading Crypto Listings Through Hyperliquid

A former Robinhood software engineer has pleaded guilty to using confidential information about forthcoming crypto listings to trade perpetual futures on Hyperliquid. The US Attorney’s Office for the Southern District of New York says Huaisong Xiang agreed to forfeit $125,523 in proceeds from the scheme. The case is a useful reminder that moving a trade to a decentralized venue does not erase legal duties tied to information obtained through an employer. It is also important to describe this as a guilty plea by one defendant, not a judgment against the entire platform or everyone named in earlier proceedings.

Federal prosecutors say Xiang had access to internal plans detailing which digital assets Robinhood would add and when public announcements were expected. Between 2025 and 2026, he used that information to take positions in perpetual futures linked to those assets before listings became public. Such instruments allow a trader to speculate on price changes without buying the underlying tokens, and they can be held without a fixed expiry if funding requirements are met. The information advantage came from alleged misuse of confidential business plans, not from a flaw in the exchange’s public data feed.

The Justice Department’s November 16 release says Xiang pleaded guilty to one count of wire fraud before US District Judge Colleen McMahon. He agreed to forfeit the stated proceeds, and sentencing is scheduled for February 25, 2027. The offense carries a statutory maximum of 20 years, but that is not a forecast of what sentence the judge will impose. This reporting describes the admitted conduct as set out by prosecutors and the plea. Any allegations against another individual should remain separate unless they are themselves resolved by court action.

Hyperliquid is a decentralized venue for perpetual derivatives, but the duties at the center of the case arose when confidential employer information was misappropriated. An employee’s ability to execute a trade without a conventional broker does not make the information public or remove obligations to protect it. On-chain settlement can also leave a detailed transaction trail, although linking wallets to specific people requires evidence. The case illustrates why compliance policies must cover external crypto platforms and derivatives, rather than only the trading accounts an employer officially monitors.

Companies planning token listings need clear access controls, internal approval records and policies that explain what staff may trade and when. Prosecutors’ earlier account described employees with access to nonpublic listing communications. Decentralized markets give informed actors new instruments, but do not change the underlying conflict between privileged information and fair market access. For traders, the case is not an argument that every price move before a listing is illicit; proof of confidential access, duties and misuse is the key distinction.

For compliance departments, the case illustrates why handling confidential listing plans resembles handling other market-sensitive corporate information, even when the resulting trade takes place on a decentralized venue. Firms may need clearer access controls, monitoring of employees' external accounts and written policies on information obtained through employment. Trading venues can publish transactions, but linking a wallet to the person who controls it often requires additional evidence. Regulators and prosecutors will continue to evaluate conduct under the applicable statutes rather than using blockchain transparency as an exemption.

The plea adds a concrete enforcement example to a debate often framed in abstract terms. It shows how traditional fraud theories can reach misconduct involving on-chain perpetuals while leaving the status of ordinary public-information trading to be assessed on its own facts.
