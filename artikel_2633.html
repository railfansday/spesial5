Cummins' Australia captaincy at a crossroads - Agnew

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Australia captain Pat Cummins, much like his fellow bowlers, is in the eye of the sandpapergate storm at the moment.

The reason is simple to me. The very notion that Cameron Bancroft - the most inexperienced batsman in the Australia team at the time – would use sandpaper on the ball against South Africa without the bowlers knowing has never made sense.

It has never seemed remotely plausible to me, as a former bowler, that the bowlers didn't know what was happening in Cape Town in 2018. It just doesn't seem possible.

Therefore, this day of reckoning was always going to come.

Let's be clear - as long as there has been cricket, there has been ball-tampering. There is no point hiding from the fact.

Back in the day it was about shining the ball, maybe using something like a sweet to get a sheen on one side.

Reverse swing was a ball-tampering game-changer. There was a requirement for a wearing process, roughing up and drying the ball.

But, to get one thing straight, whether a ball is being tampered with or not, it belongs to the bowlers. It is the bowling attack's weapon. As much as a bowler would not fiddle with a batsman's bat – sanding an edge or changing the grip - a batsman would not work on the ball without the knowledge of a bowler.

Some might wonder why revelations relating to the sandpaper controversy have not come to light before.

In response to that, I would say it would have been very difficult for a player in the Australia team to go public at the time. They would have been worried about their own futures, their careers.

Only when a player has retired, or believe their time as an international cricketer has come to an end, do they feel more comfortable to speak up.

I have also seen suggestions that Bancroft, Steve Smith and David Warner have had their guilt diminished by what has emerged in recent days, that they took bullets for their team-mates.

Smith was captain and should have been in control of his team. Warner cooked up the sandpaper idea. Bancroft executed it. If they did take bullets, they were bullets they deserved.

Now, the spotlight has fallen on Pat Cummins, whose agent has repeated his client's version of events in the last 24 hours.

The junior bowler at the time, Cummins has ascended to the captaincy, often joked to be the second-most important job in Australia, behind only the prime minister.

Cummins is not a typical Australian captain. He is not abrasive, or in your face. When he says he has worked hard to correct the image of his team, he is credible. Then again, he missed a trick to really prove Australia are no longer a win-at-all-costs outfit with the Jonny Bairstow stumping in the 2023 Ashes Test at Lord's.

After everything he said in public, and the way he has been backed by Cricket Australia (CA), there seems to be no obvious suggestion Cummins has considered resigning, or that he will be sacked.

He is due to lead Australia in the first Test against New Zealand in Perth on 9 December.

Looking ahead to that match, it is difficult to see how the build-up will be anything other than a circus, dominated by questions about sandpaper, who knew what and who said what to whom.

The litmus test for Cummins will be the public reaction. Remember, that Test is being played in Bancroft's home city, in the state of the newspaper where his 4,000-word testimony was first published.

If Cummins is still in his position by then, the response of the Perth crowd to the captain will be very interesting.

Even if Cummins does get through that, the Australian media will not let up. This is a huge story, one of the biggest in the history of Australia sport. The press will bang away until they feel they have got the truth.

And what about next summer's Ashes? If Cummins is tossing the coin at Trent Bridge on 18 June, then he is extraordinarily resilient.

Right now, I can't imagine that happening. He has said he never wants to talk about sandpaper again, which is ludicrous when you think what he will face on that tour: scrutiny from the media, constant baiting from the crowds.

Cummins will have to decide if he was the stomach for all of that, and whether he can see it through.

His way out could be to resign, without admitting guilt. To say the speculation has become a distraction for the team and the pressure is taking a toll on his family life – we have seen more pictures from Australia overnight of Cummins being confronted outside his home.

Cummins could perhaps continue in the ranks, just as Warner and Smith did. A resignation without an admission of guilt may also take the heat off the other bowlers – Mitchell Starc, Josh Hazlewood and Nathan Lyon.

Whether Cummins continues or not, questions will remain for others who were in Cape Town, Cricket Australia and the whole culture of the sport in that country.

Looking now at what happened in 2018, it appears there was an attitude in the Australia camp that they were fireproof, that they could get away with anything.

Credit goes to their media manager Kate Hutchison, who, according to Bancroft, made a stand about what was going on and knew what would happen if and when the Aussies got caught.

What about the others involved, coaches who were supposed to guide and protect young men? Darren Lehmann and his position at Northamptonshire, or David Saker, who has recently been working with England.

Cricket Australia has seemed so reluctant to get involved. If the big idea was to give individuals the opportunity to "tell their truth" behind closed doors, it is surely dead in the water.

What about telling THE truth? Nothing else is good enough.
