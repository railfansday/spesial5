Fed Is Modeling AI 'Super Intelligence' Scenarios, Including Job Losses and a Stock Market Slump

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh said Monday that central bank staff have started modeling how artificial intelligence could reshape the U.S. economy, including scenarios where the technology raises unemployment even as the economy grows.

Speaking at the Fed's Productivity and Jobs conference in San Francisco, Warsh described three possible paths: a productivity boom benefiting workers, a boom that leaves workers behind, and an AI investment bust that drags down stocks and economic growth.

“These are illustrative scenarios, not forecasts,” Warsh said, explaining that staff are using simplified economic models to test what might happen over the next several years. The Fed is preparing for different outcomes, not predicting which one will materialize.

Warsh called the technology “super intelligence,” or SI. That language is now official Fed terminology after an October change following a Donald Trump administration executive order, rather than a scientific determination that today's AI systems have already surpassed human intelligence.

In the most optimistic scenario, AI helps companies produce more with the same resources, raising the country's economic output above its historical growth rate. Workers share in those gains, and unemployment stays close to the level economists associate with a healthy job market.

In the second scenario, AI still makes the economy grow faster, but businesses automate jobs more quickly than displaced workers can find new ones. Unemployment rises despite the productivity gains.

The third scenario assumes AI investments fail to deliver the returns Wall Street expects. Companies spend heavily on infrastructure, stock prices rise on expectations of huge profits, and those expectations eventually prove too optimistic.

“Equity valuations could fall, leading to an economic slowdown,” Warsh said. In that scenario, unemployment rises more sharply, and the economy eventually recovers without the promised productivity boost.

Those risks are already receiving attention elsewhere at the Fed. Governor Michael Barr warned Monday that the AI construction boom is increasingly financed through complex arrangements, including long-term computing leases, energy contracts and financial guarantees.

The Fed estimates that the potential maximum value of these off-balance-sheet obligations now exceeds $3 trillion. Those commitments are not the same as debt already owed, but they show why disappointing AI revenues could create problems beyond the technology companies building data centers.

Warsh did not propose an interest-rate change in Monday's remarks. The Fed's next policy meeting is scheduled for December 8 and 9.
