Stop Cramming Bikes in Your Car. These 10 Editor-Tested Hitch Racks Make Hauling Easier and More Secure

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Our editors hauled bikes for thousands of miles to find the easiest, sturdiest hitch racks.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

Cramming your bike into your car or SUV gets old quickly. Removing wheels and getting mud or grease all over your interior is no fun, and transporting your bike inside your car also leaves less room for passengers, luggage, or gear. Hitch-mount racks are the easiest, most secure way to carry your bike to your riding destination without mess or hassle. They’re ideal for heading to your local mountain bike trails, taking a road trip with friends, or going for weekend rides with your kids.

Hitch racks mount to the receiver on your vehicle’s bumper, making hitch racks easier to load than roof racks—hoisting a heavy bike onto an SUV’s roof after a long ride is never fun. Plus, you don’t need to remove your front wheel. Unlike roof racks, you can also drive into parking garages and drive-thrus with bikes on a hitch rack. And importantly, in an era of sky-high fuel prices, hitch racks typically impact your gas mileage less than roof-mounted options.

While hitch racks usually cost more than simple, low-cost trunk-mount racks, they can last for many years with proper care and maintenance. Hitch-mount carriers can also carry heavier bikes, like electric bikes or full suspension rigs, than trunk racks and are more stable at highway speeds.

Our editors and test team evaluated dozens of hitch racks to see which ones perform best. Our staff has used some of these racks on their own vehicles for years, safely transporting bikes over tens of thousands of miles. Our picks for the best hitch-mounted bike racks vary in features like position, trunk access functionality, bike capacity, and price—but all are dependable options for your next cycling trip.

Based on price, weight capacity, and ease of use, Thule’s Outpace ranks as our current Best Overall pick. The Outpace is lighter and more compact than most hitch racks, which makes it easy to install and remove. At only 28 pounds, it’s light enough to carry across the garage and slide into the receiver tube. Setup takes less than a minute, so you can mount it on the car when you need to haul your bikes (rather than it semi-permanently living on your bumper).

The rack can accommodate a wider range of bike wheel sizes, making it great for families. The rack adapts easily to any size bicycle in either position. The tradeoff for this versatility is that it takes a bit longer to load than bulkier racks with cinch-style clamps. But for something that can carry all of our bikes, and is so quick to install or remove that it never has to be a permanent fixture, that’s a small price to pay.

Our tester noted the Outpace’s receiver arm is on the short side. It fit snugly against the bumper with about two inches to spare, but on another vehicle, it wouldn’t fit without a hitch extender.

The Trail Rider is a great, lower-cost alternative to premium racks. It holds two bikes in fixed wheel mounts, secured with a single ratcheting arm that clamps down over the frame. The upright beam (which holds the frame holders) folds back and out of the way for trunk access; it’s also handy for loading up two bikes. We could fold it down, set a bike on the rack closest to the car without lifting it up and over the arm, then lift the arm back into place and position the second bike.

The trade-off for the Trail Rider’s low price isn’t stability or strength, but adjustability: The bikes are positioned in a fixed location. You have limited ability to separate them if two bikes don’t fit well together. That said, you can stagger the wheel cradles so the bars and saddles don’t interfere.

The Swagman XTC2 is one of the most affordable 2-bike racks we recommend. This no-frills rack is a solid choice for budget-conscious riders. It fits both 1¼-inch and 2-inch receiver tubes, and it accommodates wheel sizes from 20 to 29 inches. However, with a total weight capacity of 70 pounds (35 pounds per bike), it’s not suitable for e-bikes.

As expected at this price, the rack has some trade-offs. The wheel hoops are adjustable, but not as smoothly or precisely as on more premium racks. The rack doesn’t tilt away from the vehicle when loaded, limiting access to tailgates or liftgates. Lastly, you must remove the center support bar to load a bike on the inside tray.

Yet, the XTC2 is lightweight, durable, and very reasonably priced. If you’re willing to trade a bit of convenience for significant savings, the Swagman XTC2 is a reliable and practical option.

The Piston Pro X is our runner-up for Best Overall hitch rack; its construction, features, and style make it a standout pick in the category. Aside from the high price, the Piston Pro X is almost the perfect hitch rack for most users.

With the Piston Pro X, Küat elevated the rack from something purely utilitarian to a desirable piece of gear that’s easier to use than other racks. The rack includes smart features (such as integrated brake and turn-signal lights) and can carry more wheel sizes (and more weight) than almost any other rack on the market.

The top-end Pro X model costs more than almost any other hitch-mount rack, but it has proven extremely durable and maintains its good looks. It's also available in 3- and 4-bike models. Küat also offers a slightly cheaper Piston Pro variant, which ditches the Kashima coating on the pistons and the integrated brake lights and turn signals.

If you like the Küat racks but $1,600 is out of your price range, the brand offers several other hitch-style racks that could meet your needs. One great option is the Sherpa 2.0 model.

While the Sherpa doesn’t have the features or carrying capacity of Küat’s more expensive racks, it has two big upsides—color options and low weight. The Sherpa weighs only 32 pounds (claimed) in the 2-inch hitch configuration (1.25-in. also available). Küat offers the Sherpa in three colors: black metallic (with a silver accent), grey metallic (with orange), and pearl white (with silver).

The Sherpa carries two bicycles weighing up to 40 pounds per tray. So it’s ideal for non-electric road, gravel, and mountain bikes. It has an integrated cable lock to secure your bikes during rest stops or meal breaks, and it features a foot-operated lever to lower the rack into position for loading bikes.

It’s often difficult to carry e-bikes that are equipped with racks and fenders on traditional hitch racks. That’s because many hitch racks secure the bike with extending arms that clamp one or both wheels. However, Thule’s EasyFold XT model holds your bike by its frame.

Another feature that makes the EasyFold ideal for e-bike owners is the included ramp. This makes loading or removing your bikes much easier; no more wrestling with heavy bikes or front wheels or handlebars flopping as you load them onto the rack. And the ramp attaches to the rack when you’re finished.

Thanks to the rack’s 45-pound weight, it’s easy to remove it from your vehicle when you’re not using it. It folds down and features casters (which make moving it around your garage easier). The EasyFold XT’s biggest drawback is that Thule only rates it to 65 pounds per bike. While that will accommodate many e-bikes, some fat tire models may weigh too much.

When Thule debuted the T2 over a decade ago, it raised the bar for what cyclists expect from a hitch-mounted bike rack. Over the years, Thule updated the rack to accommodate wider tires and different wheel sizes. It also improved the T2’s look and made it easier to operate. One of the best recent upgrades is a wide-trigger handle that allows you to raise and lower the rack (and dip it out of the way for access to rear hatches) with one hand.

Integrated cable locks help keep your bikes secure for quick stops, and each tray can hold a bicycle up to 60 pounds and tires up to 5 inches wide. This makes the T2 a good option for all but the heaviest e-bikes. The T2 Pro’s arms have wide, comfortable grips that slide easily to secure the front wheel. Instead of using a bolt, the rack secures to your hitch receiver with an expansion wedge that tightens with a locking knob. The system makes installing and removing the T2 easier (though it weighs 52 pounds), but it’s not foolproof.

Yakima’s StageTwo two-bike rack goes head-to-head with premium offerings from Küat and Thule at a lower price. In about 2,500 miles of on-car testing, the StageTwo impressed us with its ease of use and flex-free performance. This rack is only rated up to 35 pounds per tray, so it’s not made for transporting e-bikes. However, it’s one of only two racks on this list that accommodates wheels as small as 16 inches.

The StageTwo’s trays are offset, which means less chance of contact when carrying two similarly sized bikes (however, bikes sit more widely apart, so watch those tight parking spaces). The rack includes two SKS locking cables (they don’t auto-retract, so tuck them back in when not in use). The StageTwo also includes an integrated lock loop for extra security when parked or locking your rack off the vehicle.

The rack is offered in 1.25- and 2-inch sizes. The 2-inch variant can accommodate four bikes with an add-on kit. Also, check Yakima’s fit guide before buying—One of our testers experienced a hardware fitment issue.

If you travel to mountain resort towns (or have been around trailheads or bike parks lately), you’ve probably seen behemoth racks like these on the back of vans and trucks. Since modern mountain bikes are bulky, racks like Thule’s ReVert make sense if you’re hauling a full load with a single vehicle.

The ReVert holds six bikes, loads quickly, and sticks out no farther than a standard 2-bike horizontal rack. Its carrying capacity makes it ideal for large families, race teams, or ride groups.

At over 87 pounds, the ReVert is undeniably heavy. However, a few smart features make the rack manageable. Dual hydraulic dampers assist with lowering the rack when it’s fully loaded, so you don’t have to wrestle with it. Once in the lowered position, even shorter riders and kids can lift their bikes high enough to slot them into the baskets. Individual bikes are spaced 11 inches apart, giving you plenty of room to carry any combination—flat bar, drop bar, even cruisers.

1UP USA has built a cult-like following with its industrial-looking racks that take a simplistic approach to securing your bike. This model features two ratcheting arms that clamp down on your wheels—no hooks or straps necessary.

The Equip-D’s pivot mechanism is smooth and reliable even after years of abuse (although an occasional shot of WD-40 White Lithium Grease Spray goes a long way in keeping the release mechanism running smoothly). A T-handle release positioned at the end of the hitch bar means you don’t need to reach through the rack to fold it up. Tiered trays position bikes closer together, and the rack rides out of the way of most exhaust pipes. 1Up also offers the Equip in a single-bike version for a sleeker look.

Hitch racks are bicycle carriers that attach to your vehicle’s hitch receiver. Popular models carry one to four bikes, but some hanging-style hitch racks can haul up to six bikes.

A hitch is a standard feature on most trucks and many SUVs. However, if you have a car or wagon, a hitch can often be added. If your vehicle doesn’t already have a receiver, it’s easy to have one installed. (U-Haul is a reliable and affordable option for installation.)Receivers come in two sizes: 1¼-inch and 2-inch (measured across the inside of the square opening). Most trucks and SUVs use a 2-inch receiver. Many racks are available in both sizes, but heavier-duty racks—and racks that carry three or more bikes—often require the larger 2-inch receiver for added strength and stability. Racks rated to carry heavyweight e-bikes also typically require a 2-inch receiver.

If the rack will live on your vehicle year-round or see frequent use, invest in the most durable model your budget allows. While all racks listed here secure bikes effectively, higher-priced options tend to have sturdier moving parts—ratchets, straps, buckles, and locks—that stand up better to long-term use.

Not all hitch racks are rated for the extra weight of e-bikes. Those that aren’t suited for heavier bikes are clearly noted in our reviews.

Some racks are quite heavy, which contributes to their strength but can be inconvenient if you plan to remove and reinstall the rack frequently.

For most vehicles—aside from sedans—accessing the trunk or tailgate is restricted when the rack is in use or folded up. Many of our top picks offer tilt-down designs that allow you to open the back of your vehicle even with bikes loaded. For full access, swing-away adapters like the Küat Pivot v2 Swing-Away allow the entire rack to pivot 90 degrees, completely clearing the rear of your car or truck.

Need more clearance? Sometimes a simple hitch extender can do the trick. For added stability and peace of mind, we also recommend using an anti-rattle clamp with any rack you choose.

Pros: Hitch racks strike the perfect balance between the ease of access of a trunk rack and the security of a quality roof rack. They slot into your car’s hitch receiver, relying on no flimsy straps or sketchy hooks. They’re also positioned behind your car, which is great for gas mileage and roof-collision prevention (low standing height). Better yet, many are modular, allowing you to add extra trays, locks, and even bottle openers for more personalized functionality.

Cons: These racks are the most consistently expensive out there—quality materials and a confidence-inspiring construction unquestionably affect their price. Furthermore, they can be a pain to remove, as most options weigh well over 40 pounds and need tools to detach. Furthermore, with bikes on the rack, they add significant length to your vehicle, which could prove tricky if you need to parallel park. And most critically, not every car has a hitch, which means you’ll either need to install one yourself or forego this style entirely.

Our editors drove hundreds (and in some cases, thousands) of miles with their bikes on these racks. We hauled road bikes, mountain bikes, e-bikes, and city bikes. We carried bikes with disc brakes, big wheels, and fat tires. Our test team used these racks for months, in various environments and conditions. We tested them in the salty winters of Pennsylvania, the high mountains of Colorado, and the gritty Arizona desert.

These racks in this story stand out for their ease of installation and use, lack of play in key areas, manageable weight, and bike capacity. We also looked for sharp prices and deals.
