Socially responsible investing, also called ESG investing (short for environmental, social, and governance), means choosing investments by what companies do as well as what they earn. Since funds apply it in widely different ways, decide which issues matter to you first, then pick a broad, low-cost fund whose screening rules and top holdings match them.

ESG ratings, which score companies on those three areas, often disagree. In a 2019 MIT Sloan working paper, researchers found five major raters’ scores correlated at only 0.61 on average, where 1 means perfect agreement, against 0.99 for Moody’s and S&P credit ratings.

Fund methods vary, too. Some exclude whole industries, such as fossil fuels, weapons, or tobacco. Others favor each industry’s best-scoring companies, which, the SEC notes, can leave a fund holding a company in a high-carbon industry. Some vote their shares to push companies to change. So the label alone doesn’t tell you what a fund owns.

Impact investing, which aims for a measured social or environmental result, is a separate approach. So is banking through an app like GreenFi, which says customers’ deposits never fund fossil fuels.

To choose a fund, start with the two or three issues you care about most, such as climate, weapons, or how companies treat workers. A fund’s prospectus, its official description, says what it screens out and how it picks companies, and its holdings list shows what you’d own, largest first.

As of Sept. 30, 2026, Vanguard ESG U.S. Stock ETF held 1,209 stocks, and its 10 largest, led by Nvidia and Apple, were the same 10 companies as in Vanguard’s unscreened total-market fund. They made up 44.3% of the ESG fund and 36.1% of the unscreened one. That suits someone avoiding fossil fuels, which the ESG fund screens out, but not someone who objects to one of those 10 companies.

Compare costs, too. The ESG fund’s expense ratio, the yearly fee as a share of your balance, is 0.09%, or $9 a year on $10,000, against 0.03%, or $3, for the unscreened one. A 2021 review by NYU Stern and Rockefeller Asset Management found ESG investing matched or beat conventional investing in 59% of studies of investment returns and trailed in 14%. The rest were mixed.

A fund that puts a company you’d refuse to own in its top 10 isn’t screening for your issues, whatever its name says.
