What's your FI number? Calculate it in 60 seconds

Your net worth is the single most important number in personal finance. Not your salary. Not your credit score. Not how much you have in checking. Your net worth — the gap between everything you own and everything you owe — tells you exactly where you stand financially and whether you are moving in the right direction.

Net Worth = Total Assets - Total Liabilities

That is it. Add up everything you own that has monetary value. Subtract everything you owe. The number that remains — positive or negative — is your net worth.

In the FI community, net worth is not just a number. It is the scoreboard. Your FI number (25 times your annual spending) is a net worth target. Every dollar you save and invest moves you closer. Every dollar of debt you pay off does the same. Tracking your net worth turns the abstract concept of "financial independence" into a concrete, measurable goal.

Let's walk through exactly how to calculate yours, what counts as an asset, what counts as a liability, and how to use this number to accelerate your path to FI.

Calculating your net worth requires two lists: everything you own (assets) and everything you owe (liabilities). The difference between these two numbers is your net worth.

An asset is anything you own that has monetary value. Be honest and conservative — list items at what they would actually sell for today, not what you paid for them or what you hope they are worth.

A note on your home: The FI community debates whether to include your primary residence in net worth calculations. The pragmatic approach is to include it but recognize it is an illiquid asset that does not generate income. For FI planning purposes, many people track two numbers: total net worth (including home equity) and investable net worth (excluding home equity). Your investable net worth is what actually funds your financial independence.

A liability is any debt or financial obligation you owe to someone else.

A surgeon earning $400,000 per year with $600,000 in student loans, a $1.2 million mortgage, and $50,000 in car loans might have a lower net worth than a teacher earning $55,000 who has been investing 30% of her salary into index funds for 15 years. Income is what flows in. Net worth is what you keep. The FI community learned this lesson early: it is not about how much you earn — it is about the gap between earning and spending, compounded over time.

Let's walk through a real example. Meet Sarah, a 32-year-old software developer earning $95,000 per year. She has been saving and investing for 5 years since paying off her credit cards.

Sarah's net worth is $140,700. If her annual spending is $45,000, her FI number is $1,125,000 (25 x $45,000). She is 12.5% of the way there. More importantly, she is tracking her number every month and watching it grow — which means she can project when she will reach FI and make strategic decisions about how to accelerate the timeline.

Her investable net worth (excluding the car, which depreciates) is $155,200 - $30,500 = $124,700 in productive financial assets. This is the number that matters most for FI planning.

A negative net worth means you owe more than you own. This is completely normal — and far more common than people realize. According to Federal Reserve data, the median net worth for Americans under 35 is $39,000, but this is skewed by homeowners. For young renters with student loans, a negative net worth in the range of -$20,000 to -$80,000 is typical.

A negative net worth does not mean you are failing. It means you are early in the journey. If you graduated with $80,000 in student loans and have $5,000 in savings, your net worth is -$75,000. But if you are earning income, paying down debt, and starting to invest, your trajectory is positive — and trajectory is what matters.

The FI community is full of people who started with six-figure negative net worths and reached financial independence in 10-15 years. The key was tracking the number, seeing the progress, and letting that momentum fuel consistent action.

Calculating your net worth once is useful. Tracking it monthly is transformational. Monthly tracking turns financial independence from an abstract goal into a visible, measurable trajectory.

The FI community's favorite: Many FI practitioners use a simple spreadsheet. Create columns for each account, update balances on the first of every month, and chart the total over time. The visual of a steadily rising line graph is one of the most powerful motivators on the FI path. Some people prefer Empower for automated tracking — it links to your accounts and updates your net worth in real time.

Your net worth will not increase in a straight line. Market drops will temporarily reduce your investment balances. Large purchases (a car, a home down payment) will create short-term dips. The number to watch is the 12-month trend. If your net worth is higher than it was a year ago, you are moving in the right direction.

Early in the journey, debt payoff drives most of the growth. A $30,000 student loan shrinking to $18,000 adds $12,000 to your net worth just as effectively as saving $12,000 in new investments. Later, compound growth takes over and your net worth starts increasing faster than your contributions — that is the magic inflection point where your money starts working harder than you do.

Complete this in one sitting — it takes about 30 minutes.

Log into every bank account, retirement account, brokerage account, and HSA. Write down the current balance. For your home, use Zillow's estimate minus 10%. For vehicles, use Kelley Blue Book private party value.

Pro tip: Do not include personal belongings like furniture, clothing, or electronics unless a single item is worth more than $1,000 at resale.

Log into every loan servicer, credit card account, and any other creditor. Write down the exact payoff balance — not the monthly payment, but the total remaining balance including any accrued interest.

Total assets minus total liabilities equals your net worth. Whether this number is positive, negative, or zero, write it down. This is your starting point.

Multiply your annual spending by 25. This is how much investable net worth you need to be financially independent using the 4% safe withdrawal rate. Divide your current investable net worth by your FI number to see your progress percentage.

Pro tip: Use your actual spending, not your income. If you earn $80,000 but spend $50,000, your FI number is $1,250,000 — not $2,000,000.

Choose a tool — Empower, a spreadsheet, or a notebook — and schedule a recurring monthly reminder to update your net worth. The first of each month works well. Track the number, chart the trend, and watch your progress compound.

The most common habit among people who have reached financial independence is consistent net worth tracking. It takes 5 minutes per month and creates a feedback loop that keeps you motivated and accountable. When you see your net worth climb from $50,000 to $100,000 to $250,000, the progress becomes its own fuel. Start tracking today — your future self will thank you.

Calculating your net worth is the first step on the path to financial independence — and it takes less than 30 minutes. Add up everything you own, subtract everything you owe, and you have the single most important number in your financial life. Whether that number is positive, negative, or zero, it is your starting point. Track it monthly, watch it grow, and use it to measure your progress toward your FI number. The formula is simple: assets minus liabilities. The habit of tracking it is what separates people who talk about financial goals from people who achieve them.
