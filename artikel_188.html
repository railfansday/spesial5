Shabana Mahmood backs ban on police marching in pride events in uniform

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Home secretary says ‘routine two-tier policing’ does not exist, but public trust in impartial law enforcement must be restored

Shabana Mahmood has backed a ban on police officers marching in pride events in uniform as she said there had been a “few examples” of two-tier policing.

Although she said there was no systematic or routine two-tier policing, the home secretary said “a new impartiality standard” was necessary to reverse the fall in the public’s faith in law enforcement.

She said one example had been when police officers took part in a pride event in Northumbria last year, which a court found had breached a duty of impartiality. Mahmood said: “The judgment found that they got the balance wrong … and that marching … was not acceptable and not impartial.

“Today at those marches, you will often have campaigners, on gender identity … and those issues are now contested … the marching alongside looks like a political act.”

Nik Adams, the deputy commissioner of City of London police who is also the LGBTQ+ lead for police chiefs, said: “Using the language about LGBTQ issues being contested makes LGBTQ people in policing and wider society feel less safe, makes their lives feel less valued.

“LGBTQ is a protected characteristic in law. The rights are hard won.”

Wiltshire police faces a court challenge next week for setting up a stall at a pride event in Swindon after claims it had exposed a lack of neutrality amid disputes over transgender rights.

Asked if she was accepting there was two-tier policing, Mahmood said: “There’s no systematic, systemic, routine two-tier policing in this country. I reject that charge.

“What Reform and others will have you believe is every single day officers go out and they practise a thing called two-tier policing. That is utter, complete, ridiculous rubbish.

“But there have been examples of differential treatment, and those have had an impact on confidence in policing, and they’ve allowed that false perception of two-tier policing to take hold.

“I accept that there are few examples, and I know that actually the best way to deal with that is to make sure we have a new impartiality standard.”

One police leader told the Guardian this rhetoric was concerning as forces tried to navigate the culture wars buffeting Britain while serving all communities.

She said police had to improve its record on recruiting more black officers, noting there were barely any more than there had been 30 years ago.

Mahmood added: “We must also be wary of misguided attempts at course correction, where the pursuit of equality leads instead to differential treatment. I believe we see this in examples like the old police anti-racism commitment, which was a well-intentioned attempt to address racism that ended up achieving the opposite of its stated goal.

“It made statements that are clearly wrong: to the public, it was patently absurd to tell police officers that fair policing ‘does not mean treating everyone the same’. So was the document’s guidance that officers should not be ‘colour blind’.”

The home secretary also said officers should engage with the community to discuss local issues, but not with activist groups and campaigners who were pushing their own political agenda.

But one source said Mahmood’s soundbites did not take account of the complexities: “It’s an easy slogan, but dig into the details and it becomes more complicated.”

For instance some oppose the red poppy as part of Remembrance Day celebrations, believing it glorifies militarism, but these are worn by officers and chiefs.

Mahmood decried police talking to activists, but policing has been given money to boost the protection of Jewish people in Britain, while some groups that police may want to consult have been accused of extremism by pro-Palestinian groups.

Gavin Stephens, the chair of the National Police Chiefs Council, said: “Public trust depends on the police being seen to act fairly and impartially. Barriers to justice remain, discrimination still exists within our own ranks, and we rely on the trust and confidence of all communities to do our job. A diverse service is a stronger service.”
