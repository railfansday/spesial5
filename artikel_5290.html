GTA VI was built by hand. The man who ran Take-Two during GTA IV says AI could let a filmmaker make the next breakout game

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Grand Theft Auto VI, on track to be the biggest entertainment launch of any kind, was made the old-fashioned way: Take-Two Interactive CEO Strauss Zelnick said in February that Rockstar Games built the game without generative AI.

“With regards to GTA 6, generative AI has zero part in what Rockstar Games is building. Their worlds are handcrafted. That’s what differentiates them,” Zelnick told GamesIndustry.biz. “They’re built from the ground up, building by building, street by street, neighborhood by neighborhood.”

That’s not to say it didn’t take time and money. Analysts estimate Take-Two spent more than a billion dollars on the game, which came out more than 13 years after its predecessor (Zelnick never confirmed the amount but admitted the game was “expensive”). But the next blockbuster video game might come sooner, cheaper—and with an even more cinematic lens, according to Ben Feder, who ran Take-Two Interactive when Grand Theft Auto IV came out in 2008.

“Some great creative mind who would have otherwise gone to make a film will make a video game because it’s as simple as point and click,” Feder said in a long and winding conversation with Fortune about the state of the entertainment industry.

Feder, now managing partner of Tirta Ventures, said in a Fortune commentary this month that AI will change how big games get made. His firm invests in AI-native game projects. “We put our money where our mouth is,” he said of Tirta’s investments.

The next great video game maker might be someone who would otherwise have gone into movies, and that’s because AI would make it easier for a filmmaker without a technical background to get into video games. And Feder doesn’t expect that shift to hurt the franchise he once oversaw.

“I don’t think GTA is going anywhere. In fact, it probably goes from strength to strength,” Feder said. “But that’s not to say it’s going to get created in the exact same way that it got created here.”

Video games have long sat at the intersection of the software and entertainment businesses. Feder said AI will shrink the software side as writing code gets cheaper and easier.

“The argument would be that because AI is making software development commoditized, and software points to zero, it becomes much more about entertainment than it does about both entertainment and computer science,” he said.

Feder described the result as “somewhere between democratizing creativity—the cynic would say commoditizing creativity—but I don’t believe that creativity, true creativity, really gets commoditized unless you’re such a believer in AI that you think AI can also be creative.”

More games won’t mean more hits. Feder expects a flood of new titles, with only a small share breaking through, and pointed to YouTube.

“I don’t know how many billions of minutes of video have been on YouTube, but how many of them actually are good? How many of them actually get traction?” he said. “But the ones that do get traction and are good reach billions of people, literally, and can create enormous outcomes, both creatively and commercially.”

Grand Theft Auto VI shows how large those outcomes can be. Take-Two has not disclosed the game’s cost, though estimates put its development budget at $1 billion to $1.5 billion. Brokerage Oppenheimer projected about $1.85 billion in gross revenue in the game’s first three days, Reuters reported.

“Thirteen years of investment you make back in a day. Unbelievable,” Feder said. “But it only happens if you create the most awesome game ever.”

AI tools could change that math across the industry, he said. “It’s going to get harder to justify a $2 billion game in a world where the next best alternative is two orders of magnitude less.”

GTA VI, ironically, did make back all the money poured into it, and then some. Feder said that counterargument is a pretty good devil’s advocate. “What’s the argument for not spending another $2 billion on the next one and making it back in a day?” he said. “That’s a valid argument.”

At this point, the conversation focused on the lived experience of the entertainment industry at large. While most people think of film and cinema as making up the lion’s share of what people consider entertainment, video games have always been there, and they’ve been blockbusters of their own. But recently, as with movies, it seems big publishers have long concentrated their money on a handful of games, or, as the Marvel Cinematic Universe has shown, on the franchise model.

“Even when I was at Take-Two for GTA IV, we were the first, and others followed, to develop a strategy of fewer, bigger titles,” Feder said. And just like movies, the indie games have a tough time breaking through the noise. Feder said the pressure falls on games in the middle, those with big budgets and smaller audiences.

Feder said that when he ran Take-Two, he saw the industry’s next frontier as characters that look like movie actors rather than figures in a game. Of GTA VI, he said, “This gets as close to it as I’ve ever seen.”
