Coaching key to Alabama’s literacy success, report finds

The state’s approach to placing a dedicated literacy coach in every school influenced the success seen later in Mississippi, a Fordham report says.

“The story is so fascinating to me because it’s very rare in education that you can draw a straight line from policy to achievement scores improvement. And in this case, people across the state seem to agree on what works—because they spent the early 2000s doing it," said Loewus.

Beginning in 2012, Alabama, however, reduced funding while expanding the coaching cadre to K-12 and across other subjects like math. By 2019, Alabama ranked last in the nation in 4th grade reading.

In the meantime, Mississippi had noticed Alabama’s early success and replicated the model. “They flip-flopped,” Loewus explained. And by 2019, Alabama looked at Mississippi and passed comprehensive legislation that more or less reinstituted its reading initiative, with funding for 750 school-based coaches, 100 regional coaches, and 10 staff at the state Department of Education to monitor the entire process, she said.

The Alabama legislation also protects coaches’ time: they cannot be pulled into substitute teaching when someone calls in sick, proctor tests, or help with recess. “That is so unusual,” Loewus said. “In other schools, coaches are the first ones to be pulled. They don’t have their own class.”

The state superintendent Eric Mackey requires district superintendents to sign a memorandum of understanding before they receive their annual allocation, and “when principals start to pull coaches for other things, he gets a phone call,” she added.

Overall, the legislation in Mississippi is mostly similar, Loewus said. The program there was initially funded by a now-shuttered philanthropic group called the Barksdale Reading Institute, but the state has since taken over the budget. “It’s really top down in both places,” she said. “It’s key having really resolute state leadership, champions at the top who are not letting this go.”

Loewus emphasizes that the coaching alone won’t achieve desired results unless the coaches are centrally trained in research-based reading instruction.

“The fact that everyone is getting the same training, and it’s centralized, and based on research, is really important. Protecting coaches’ time is really important. Having steady funding is important," Loewus added. "Having the structure of coaches having coaches is incredibly important.”

About 44 states and Washington, D.C., have passed science of reading policies, according to a 2025 update from Whiteboard Advisors.
