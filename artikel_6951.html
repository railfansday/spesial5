Why Men Are Posting Thirst Traps in Red Light Face Masks

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Gonzalo Fernández Escribano knew all about the benefits of red-light therapy for his skin. Fewer wrinkles, a brighter complexion, minimizing acne—he’d experienced it all firsthand when he tried out LED masks during his facials. In March 2025, he purchased one for at-home use. Little did he know it would come with an extra benefit as a provocative prop for a sexy photo.

"I use my red-light mask right before bed, so I’m already shirtless,” says Escribano. “Let’s just say the sexy-looking picture is easily convenient to take.”

Escribano is one of many men online who’ve embraced the red-light thirst trap. More and more, image-conscious guys with followings built around some combination of beauty, fitness, wellness, travel, are pairing their red-light routines with the occasional shirtless selfie. The trend is especially prevalent in gay-male internet culture, where faceless torsos shots, sometimes featuring beefy chests and six-pack abs, have been part of the visual language of dating apps and social media for years. A shirtless red-light-mask pic is a variation on the theme: The body and the anonymity are still there—only now there’s a skincare routine attached.

Jayson Stacy, a celebrity groomer, noticed his friend wearing an LED in his Instagram profile picture three years ago. At the same time, Stacy had recently gotten a mask himself and decided to follow suit, posting a photo of his own after Mardi Gras under the guise of some much needed recovery.

“It’s almost like a sexy Halloween costume, but you don’t have to wait until October,” Stacy says. Escribano agrees, saying it makes him look like a superhero. Strong shadows, glowing skin, and a Sith-like red glow mix together in a thirsty trifecta meant to slow someone’s scrolling.

For Stacy, red-light masks strike the perfect balance between wellness and plausible deniability. “You’re supposed to use the mask after you wash your face, so most are fresh out of the shower before using,” he says. “It’s like, ‘Oh, look at my morning routine,’ which gives the perfect excuse to be mostly unclothed. Then you have the mask on, you feel like someone can’t see your face, so it can feel like a layer of protection.” In other words, the skin care routine becomes the reason for the thirst trap, even though it’s only half the motivation.

Red-light therapy is a noninvasive treatment that uses low-level LED light to stimulate cellular activity and boost collagen production. The technology has become especially prominent over the last few years thanks to its ability to reduce inflammation, soften fine lines, and promote skin and muscle recovery and hair growth. (That is, as long as you are willing to invest in high-quality equipment and use it every single day).

Celebrities have been documenting their LED routines for years, from January Jones to Kim Kardashian. But those posts were mostly framed around the masks’ intended purpose: improving your skin. Somewhere along the way, though, a certain breed of extremely online men—trainers, models, artists, content creators, and guys who simply know their angles—found a second purpose for the masks: as an accessory for a hot photo. The masks are turning up in bathroom mirror pics, bedroom shots, and buried several slides deep in otherwise unrelated photo dumps, often without any mention of skin care at all. And judging by the comments—fire emojis, heart eyes, and the occasional “yummy”—their followers seem to be noticing something other than the mask.

The thirst traps are also surfacing on social media at a time when curiosity around red-light therapy is booming. A 2025 study analyzing Google Trends data found that search interest across red-light-therapy-related terms increased 118% after February 2024. And by 2025, Google reported that searches for “red light therapy” had reached an all-time high. As these futuristic devices have rapidly flooded our feeds, maybe it was only a matter of time before they made their way out of the skin care selfie and into something a little thirstier.

In some ways these images are being ushered into well-trodden territory. In 2021, TikTok’s viral Silhouette Challenge had users transform themselves into barely distinguishable, body-forward silhouettes under a red filter, turning the color itself into a visual shorthand for sex appeal. Meanwhile, for gay men, shirtless photos are hardly a novelty, especially on dating apps like Grindr and Sniffies, where headless torsos are practically part of the experience. “The headless torsos definitely all blend together at this point,” says John Arthur Hill, who compares swiping through hookup apps to looking into “a lukewarm batch of Chili’s skillet queso” and trying to figure out what’s cheese, meat, or a bell pepper.

And even when the most impressive six-pack can get lost in an endless doom scroll, red light can add just enough intrigue to get someone to look twice. For Adam Pent, an OnlyFans creator who’s no stranger to posting thirst traps himself, that curiosity is part of the appeal. “It [a red-light-mask thirst trap] is definitely more interesting because I’d be curious about who’s behind the mask,” he says. To Pent, there’s something creative about the whole thing. “I guess it makes the photo a bit more artful too? ‘Art thot’ vibes I guess, unless it looks like sponsored content.” It also makes him want to shop. “I’m less focused on the body and more like, ‘Should I just get one already?’”

The red-light thirst trap also holds a certain mysterious appeal. “I’ve been posting ‘masked’ selfies for quite some time because I think it’s a really hot aesthetic and such a cool fantasy,” Michael Anthony, a celebrity makeup artist, says. For him, it’s not just about looking good in the photos. “The fantasy of anonymity and pleasure, while doing something for yourself that will make you look and feel better—it’s all so sexy.”

An LED face mask is a luxurious way to get an at-home glow, and Shark's is one of our favorites. It helps to fight acne and fine lines, and it can help depuff around the eyes for those long nights.

Tattoo artist Alvin Archibald sees a similar appeal. “There’s a subtle tension between vulnerability and control that makes it interesting,” he says. “The mask takes away your facial expressions, so the body becomes the focus. It creates this interesting dynamic where you’re both exposed and concealed at the same time. There’s something almost intimate about it, like you’re getting ready for an audience without necessarily admitting it.”
