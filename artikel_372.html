COP31: No agreement on climate science, disinformation spreading

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Attacks on climate science and disinformation risk delaying key measures at COP31 in Antalya. As the UN climate talks draw to a close, how will that affect efforts to come to an agreement?

The annual UN climate conference is the place where countries negotiate the world's plans to limit global warming and adapt to its consequences — and who will pay for climate action as well as damage from storms, floods and droughts.

But this year at COP31 in Antalya, an ongoing dispute over the timing of a key assessment of global warming is risking the scientific foundations of global climate action, say observers.

"We are extremely concerned about the situation," said a diplomat with the European delegation who could not speak on the record, adding that it came down to the future of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), the world's leading body of climate scientists.

The IPCC's scientific assessments, published every six to seven years, provide the basis for major international climate agreements, including the 2015 Paris Agreement. The IPCC began evaluating the latest climate science in 1988 and its most recent report was published in 2023.

Researchers should have actually already started work on the next edition but the last plenary session was put on hold in October at the urging of Russia, Iran, Saudi Arabia and Kenya.

"The IPCC finds itself in a difficult situation," a spokesperson from the German Environment Ministry told DW at COP. "We stand ready to help resolve this issue, so that the panel can continue its work. To that end, we are holding many discussions with various countries to explore possible solutions."

At the core of the dispute is the question of whether future UN climate talks base their negotiations on the latest scientific knowledge — or not.

That's especially relevant for the next "global stocktake," due in 2028, a report card on the world's climate efforts, which will shape action plans for countries around the world to lower planet-heating greenhouse gas emissions.

The European Union, Brazil, the United Kingdom, small island states and several other countries especially at risk of the effects of climate change have called for the IPCC's seventh assessment report to be published in 2028, so it can be used to inform next steps for climate action. The report covers climate protection and impact.

"It's much more difficult without these scientific reports. Otherwise, we're just talking to ourselves or relying on information that's no longer current," said a delegation source.

But Russia, Iran, India and Saudi Arabia want the IPCC report postponed until 2029, after the global stocktake.

The official reason: the tight timeline would be detrimental to the quality of the research, and could hinder the participation of researchers and governments representing developing countries.

Disagreements over budget issues have also held up the IPCC's work. Among other expenses, the funds are used to pay the travel arrangements for the hundreds of volunteer scientists, especially those from poorer countries, to attend the IPCC sessions.

"No budget means no more IPCC," said a member from another delegation, also speaking on condition of anonymity.

It hasn't come to that point yet, but without planning certainty the whole process risks being delayed — along with the climate negotiations that depend on this knowledge.

Researchers with the IPCC, however, have said the timeline is attainable.

"We have no time to lose," said an open letter released in October signed by more than 100 researchers from 36 countries — including India and China. "We stand committed to continue working to deliver the [seventh assessment report] in a manner that is rigorous, timely and inclusive."

But while the dispute over the timeline won't alter the scientific findings themselves, it could delay their political impact.

In its pre-COP communications, France accused the countries delaying the report's publication schedule of attempting to "obstruct science in order to lower the ambition of the global stocktake."

The IPCC isn't a stranger to attempts at political persuasion: At past climate conferences, delegates from many countries have regularly negotiated the exact phrasing of the summary reports intended for policymakers.

Speaking at COP31 on November 17, EU Climate Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra said it was "fundamental to protect the integrity of climate science and the role of the IPCC as the source of best available science."

Although there is no vote on the IPCC at COP31, it's possible that its significance and a timeline for the next report will be included in the final declaration of the climate talks, expected this weekend.

The IPCC has been warning for years that disinformation is hindering global climate action. And the struggle for information and control over the narrative in Antalya hasn't just been limited to the IPCC.

This was apparent in a document DW obtained that had been sent anonymously to several less wealthy countries' delegations in the run-up to COP31.

It claimed to explain the nature of Turkey's proposed goals for boosting electrification to 35% by 2035, but went on to misrepresent several core details of the plans to make them seem less appealing to developing countries than they are in reality.

At least two countries confirmed to DW that they had received the document. It did not state where it hailed from and was labeled as an "equity assessment."

The presentation portrays electrification as a business venture undertaken by wealthy countries at the expense of poorer nations. The International Energy Agency, however, has calculated that developing countries could save around $100 billion (€90 billion) in fossil fuel imports by 2035 through electrification.

The presentation also gives the impression that all countries must meet fixed electrification targets. In fact, according to the UN, the 35% target represents a global goal, with individual countries free to determine their own national pathways.
