8 Prime Video Fantasy Shows Where Every Episode Is a Masterpiece

Fantasy asks viewers to step into a completely different world and promises them that it will feel just as real as the one they live in. That's easier said than done, though, because building a world from scratch takes huge budgets and endless patience. This is where plenty of fantasy shows end up buried under confusing lore and clunky exposition. Others look stunning but forget to give audiences characters worth caring about.

Prime Video has quietly become one of the boldest homes for the genre because it has bet on everything from sprawling book adaptations to some of the most creative original animated series. The best of them understand that magic and monsters only matter if the people caught in the middle feel human. Some of these shows are epic, while others operate on a much smaller scale, but every one of them tells a story worth getting lost in. This is a list of Prime Video fantasy shows where every episode is a masterpiece.

My Lady Jane takes one of history's saddest stories and gives it the happy, chaotic rewrite nobody knew they needed. In real life, Lady Jane Grey was Queen of England for just nine days before she was executed. The show throws all of that out the window. Here, Jane (Emily Bader) is a bookish, sharp-tongued teenager who is forced into an arranged marriage with the reckless but charming Guildford Dudley (Edward Bluemel), who also turns into a horse every day. Jane soon finds herself caught in a royal conspiracy, all in a version of England torn apart by tension between ordinary humans and people who can transform into animals.

The show is pure fun from start to finish, and its cheeky sense of humor never gets boring. Jane and Guildford's enemies-to-lovers romance is a delight, and their bickering is just as entertaining as their sword fights. Beneath the silliness, though, the show has a lot to say about power and the people history forgot. Its modern soundtrack and playful attitude make My Lady Jane feel fresh in a genre that can often take itself too seriously. Prime Video canceled it after one season, but it remains one of the most charming fantasy titles on the platform.

Undone is unlike anything else on TV, and it's impossible to forget once it's over. The story follows Alma Winograd-Diaz (Rosa Salazar), who, after a car accident, starts seeing visions of her late father, Jacob (Bob Odenkirk), who died when she was a child. Jacob tells her that she can now bend time, and he needs her help uncovering the truth about his death. As Alma tests her new abilities, she starts slipping between past and present while her family is convinced she's having a breakdown.

Undone is created by the minds behind BoJack Horseman and uses rotoscope animation, where real performances are drawn over by animators. The technique gives the show a dreamy, slippery feel that matches Alma's state of mind perfectly. The real genius is that the show never tells the audience whether her powers are actually real or just a sign of mental illness. That uncertainty turns a time-travel story into a deeply moving look at grief and family history. Undone is an underrated masterpiece, and it deserves a much bigger audience thanks to the sheer brilliance of its storytelling.

Good Omens is what happens when the end of the world becomes a buddy comedy. The series, based on the beloved novel by Neil Gaiman and Terry Pratchett, follows Aziraphale (Michael Sheen), a fussy, book-loving angel, and the stylish demon Crowley (David Tennant), who drives a vintage Bentley. After living on Earth for thousands of years, the two have grown rather fond of humanity and each other. When they learn that the Apocalypse is just days away, they team up to stop it, only to discover that the Antichrist has been accidentally misplaced.

The magic of Good Omens is its central friendship. Sheen and Tennant have the kind of chemistry that can't be faked, and the show slowly reveals that the bond between an angel and a demon might be the most powerful force in the universe. The British humor is sharp and silly, but the series also has real heart, especially when it explores how both characters feel like outsiders on their own sides. Season 2 leans even further into their relationship and ends on a gut-wrenching cliffhanger. Good Omens is witty and warm, and it's one of the most rewatchable and accessible fantasy shows ever made.

The Legend of Vox Machina follows a group of friends playing Dungeons & Dragons and, in doing so, becomes one of the best fantasy shows on TV. The animated series is based on the first campaign of Critical Role, a web series where a group of voice actors plays D&D together. It follows a band of misfit mercenaries who are more interested in drinking and getting paid than saving the world. Soon enough, though, they find themselves facing vampires, dragons, and gods, and they're forced to become the heroes nobody expected them to be.

The Legend of Vox Machina is special in how quickly its foul-mouthed jokes give way to genuine heartbreak. Each member of the group is running from something painful, and the show takes its time exploring every one of them, especially the gunslinger Percy (Taliesin Jaffe) and his dark quest for revenge. The animation is gorgeous, and the battles are thrilling, but the real draw is watching this dysfunctional group become a true family. The series also has a fascinating backstory, since it was funded by a record-breaking Kickstarter campaign from fans. The Legend of Vox Machina is definitely raunchy but epic in every way, and it's a must-watch for anyone who has ever rolled a twenty-sided die.

Invincible starts like a typical superhero story and then spends every episode tearing that very idea apart. The series follows Mark Grayson (Steven Yeun), an ordinary teenager whose father, Omni-Man (J. K. Simmons), is the most powerful superhero on Earth. When Mark finally develops powers of his own, he can't wait to follow in his dad's footsteps. Then the shocking final moments of the very first episode turn his world upside down, and Mark soon learns that being a hero is far more complicated than he ever imagined.

The show, based on Robert Kirkman's comic, is famous for its brutal, bloody fights, but violence alone isn't what makes it great. Invincible is really about a son trying to figure out whether he can be a better man than his father, and the scenes between Mark and Omni-Man are some of the most emotional in animation. Simmons is terrifying and heartbreaking as a father torn between his mission and his family. The show also builds a massive universe of heroes and villains without ever losing sight of Mark's personal story, which makes it one of the best superhero series ever, where every new season deepens the lore and raises the stakes.

The Wheel of Time brought one of fantasy's most beloved book series to life on an epic scale. The show, based on Robert Jordan's novels, follows Moiraine (Rosamund Pike), a member of a powerful order of magic-wielding women known as the Aes Sedai. The story begins when Moiraine arrives in a quiet village called the Two Rivers, convinced that one of five young villagers is the reincarnation of the Dragon, a figure destined to either save the world or destroy it. She then leads them on a dangerous journey across a world full of dark forces, and none of them know which one carries that terrifying destiny.

The show truly found its footing as it went along, and The Wheel of Time Season 3 is widely considered its best. Its biggest strength is its world, which feels enormous and lived-in, with rival kingdoms and ancient prophecies hiding around every corner. It also handles magic in a way that feels dangerous and costly, which makes every spell carry real weight. Prime Video canceled the series after three seasons, which only makes its strongest episodes more bittersweet. The Wheel of Time is an ambitious fantasy epic, and fans of the books will find plenty to love.

Carnival Row takes the fairy-tale creatures of folklore and drops them into a grimy, Victorian-style city. In the Burgue, magical refugees known as fae have fled a brutal war in their homeland, only to be met with prejudice and poverty. Rycroft Philostrate (Orlando Bloom) is a detective investigating a string of gruesome murders that are terrifying the city. His case is complicated by the return of Vignette Stonemoss (Cara Delevingne), a fae refugee and his former lover, who is hiding secrets of her own.

Carnival Row is a fantasy show through and through, but it has so much more to say beneath all the magic and myth. The show uses its narrative to tell an emotionally heavy story about immigration and discrimination, and it does so without ever feeling pretty. Its moody, gaslit setting looks gorgeous, and the murder mystery in Season 1 keeps things moving at a relentless pace. The show only lasted two seasons, but Carnival Row remains one of the most underrated fantasy dramas on any streaming service.

The Rings of Power takes viewers back to Middle-earth thousands of years before The Lord of the Rings. The story is set during the Second Age, and follows a young Galadriel (Morfydd Clark), a fierce elven warrior who refuses to believe that the dark lord Sauron (Charlie Vickers) is truly gone. As she hunts for any sign of him, peace across Middle-earth slowly begins to crack. Elves, dwarves, and humans all face their own growing threats, and the story builds toward the forging of the legendary Rings of Power.

This one is easily the most expensive TV series ever made, and every dollar shows on screen. The landscapes are breathtaking, and the show delivers moments of pure spectacle that feel worthy of Peter Jackson's films. More importantly, it fills in a part of Tolkien's history that has never been put on screen, and fans get to see how the rings came to be and how trust between the races fell apart. The friendship between the elf Elrond (Robert Aramayo) and the dwarf prince Durin (Owain Arthur) is one of the show's highlights. The Rings of Power may divide some fans, but it's Prime Video's most ambitious swing, and it brings Middle-earth to life like never before.
