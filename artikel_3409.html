There's Actually a Simple Explanation for All These Widespread Outages

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

It feels like every time you look at your phone lately, another company is having an outage. Last week, there was Microsoft, whose outage took down services from Outlook to Teams. Then, the FAA grounded all flights across the U.S. following "communication issues." This week, a number of banks experienced outages, as did Apple. Even the NSA's internal communications network went dark. Some of the biggest corporations and organizations in the world can't seem to keep their services running, leading to widespread disruptions and confusion. When it's a one-off, it's an annoyance; when it's outage after outage, it starts to feel like a pattern.

That pattern, in turn, has begun to fuel anxiety among the general public. What could possibly be causing Apple, Microsoft, JP Morgan Chase, and the NSA alike to go dark at roughly the same time? Is it a coordinated cyberattack? An AI revolt? Is the end finally nigh? To an outside observer, it certainly appears like something coordinated and nefarious is happening here. Secretary of State Marco Rubio is insistent that there is "zero evidence" that hackers or bad actors caused any of these outages, and President Trump is particularly unconcerned by the whole ordeal, saying, "It's life," and "Things like that happen."

But to skeptics of the U.S. government, these trickles of information aren't necessarily reassuring. While I agree with calls for someone from the government (perhaps the president himself) to make an official announcement explaining the situation, the root cause does appear to be clear at this time: The short answer? AI is at fault here, but not in the way you think.

Love it or hate it, AI is a force to be reckoned with. While you might know that AI can generate hyper-realistic videos and manage your emails on your behalf, certain models are adept at discovering new security vulnerabilities in software. These models aren't just helpful tools; they far surpass the abilities of human researchers. You might remember Anthropic initially pulled the brakes on its breakthrough model, Claude Mythos, when it found that the model could root out security vulnerabilities in just about anything.

Mythos, and models like it, are a double-edged sword. On the one hand, they can be major assets for developers and researchers to find new security vulnerabilities that humans otherwise wouldn't have discovered, and patch them accordingly. Unfortunately, the same can be said for bad actors, who can use these models to learn about new security flaws and attempt to exploit them before researchers have a chance to find them. As such, software developers across the spectrum, from small-time devs to major corporations, have been racing to patch as many security vulnerabilities as they can find—and they're finding a lot.

You might notice your PC's security updates, for example, are shipping with more patches than usual—historic levels, even. Microsoft keeps breaking its own records with its Patch Tuesday updates, including over 1,400 fixes with its October update. The company's October 2025 Patch Tuesday update, by comparison, included fewer than 180 fixes.

It's important to understand that just about every company that develops software is furiously working to patch its systems before hackers or AI agents exploit vulnerabilities first. And while there are successes (Microsoft is now protecting its customers from thousands of flaws each month), this speed is also inadvertently breaking things along the way. That's not just speculation: According to The Washington Sun, both Wall Street executives and the NSA explained to members of Congress that patching security flaws discovered by AI models caused this week's widespread outages.

Barring some campaign to cover up a coordinated attack on systems and services, it does seem like these outages are simply the result of companies breaking their own products in an attempt to stop AI-assisted hackers from exploiting undiscovered flaws. But that's not all that reassuring, either. AI models are only going to keep getting better at finding vulnerabilities; likewise, AI agents are only improving at hacking into systems autonomously. Companies will need to ramp up their efforts to patch their software before both hackers and AI agents discover those flaws first, and so the cycle will continue. Unless something changes, we may see routine outages as companies try to fix things as quickly as possible.

The implications aren't specific to any one company, either. Our world increasingly relies on fewer companies to keep us all connected. We saw that last year, when cloud hosting providers like AWS and Cloudflare each experienced outages. When these services go down, so do all of the networks that rely on them. If AWS or Cloudflare break something while trying to patch their systems, whole parts of the internet go dark.

Of course, it'll only be more difficult for the general public—if not the developers themselves—to recognize when outages are the result of maintenance or a cyberattack. Just because recent outages don't appear to be nefarious in nature, that doesn't mean future outages will be so innocent. For the time being, however, it seems we can worry less about an active cybersecurity incident, and more about how AI is disrupting modern life in ways few of us saw coming.
