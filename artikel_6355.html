Evolution may work very differently than scientists thought

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Scientists propose a surprising new way species may evolve, revealing how changes in body size could drive evolutionary divergence without competition.

Many stories about how one species branches into several eventually land on the same driving force: competition. Two populations start fighting over the same food, the same space, the same mates.

The losers adapt or disappear. It’s a tidy narrative – but Bryan Jennings, an evolutionary biologist at the University of California, Riverside, thinks it skips a chapter.

Jennings has spent years puzzling over a gap in adaptive radiation theory – the framework that explains how one ancestor species can fan out into many.

Darwin’s finches are the textbook example: different beak shapes for different seeds, sculpted by competition.

But what happens before two species even share the same turf? What sets the divergence in motion?

In a new study, Jennings proposes that the answer is deceptively simple: a change in body size. And the consequences of that change, he argues, are far wilder than anyone predicted.

Here’s the thing about growing bigger or smaller: your parts don’t all follow the same script. A human baby’s head is enormous relative to its body; by adulthood, the proportions have shifted dramatically.

The same logic applies across species. As organisms evolve different body sizes, their traits – tail length, limb proportions, beak shape – scale at different rates. Biologists call this allometry.

Jennings initially hypothesized that when one population evolves a different body size, its traits would change proportionally.

This process is called geometric scaling. Think of it like enlarging a photograph. Everything gets bigger, but the ratios stay the same.

His data, however, told a very different story.

To test his hypothesis, Jennings turned to pygopodid lizards. These are bizarre, snake-like creatures found across Australia and New Guinea that have lost nearly all their limbs over evolutionary time.

In some species of the genus Delma, tails can reach 4.5 times the length of the body. That extreme variation made them perfect subjects.

The work had roots in Jennings’s PhD research at the University of Texas at Austin, but the theory crystallized during COVID-19 lockdowns in 2020, when he revisited the data alongside classic work by Stephen Jay Gould on geometric similarity.

To push the analysis further, he secured funding for a 40-day museum blitz across Australia in 2023, measuring more than 1,000 preserved specimens at six institutions.

A follow-up visit to Harvard’s Museum of Comparative Zoology brought the total to 1,755 specimens spanning 31 species.

He tracked three traits tied to locomotion and predator escape: snout-to-vent length, tail length, and body girth. Across 126 pairwise species comparisons, the results were unambiguous.

The geometric-scaling-first model predicted that recently diverged species would show proportional trait changes. Instead, Jennings found the opposite.

Among the six most recently diverged species pairs – those that split less than two million years ago – none consistently followed the geometric scaling pattern across all three traits.

When Jennings estimated interspecific scaling slopes for these pairs, only 3 of 18 measurements (17%) even roughly approximated geometric scaling.

A full 72% showed what he calls “reverse evolutionary allometry.” The trait changes were far more extreme than proportional scaling would predict.

“Rather than simple geometric scaling, the data showed evidence of a more extreme decoupling between trait development and body-size evolution,” Jennings said.

“I call this broader process disassociational scaling.”

Jennings’s revised model proposes two stages.

First comes disassociational scaling: a population evolves a different body size, and the relationships between its traits begin to shift dramatically.

Tails that should scale proportionally don’t and body girth changes at rates nobody predicted. The initial divergence is messy and extreme.

Then comes re-adaptation. Natural selection steps in over longer evolutionary timescales to fine-tune those scrambled proportions, restoring or improving trait function.

The data were consistent with this proposed sequence.

Among species pairs that diverged over two million years ago, 63% to 90% of trait relationships had shifted toward the more stable evolutionary patterns the model predicted for later stages.

“In other words, competition between species is not necessary for the initial divergence,” Jennings said.

The implications stretch well beyond Australian lizards.

If a simple change in body size can dramatically reshuffle an organism’s proportions, some of those new trait combinations might accidentally unlock access to entirely different ecological resources.

“If changes in body size produce substantial changes in the proportions of traits, those changes could sometimes result in trait combinations that allow a population to exploit a different ecological resource,” he said.

“I call this proposed process ‘niche scaling’ when it occurs in the context of adaptive radiation.”

“Competition could subsequently refine those differences through natural selection. Instead, I’m proposing that an important part of the story occurs earlier – when one population first begins diverging from another.”

Jennings is careful to position his theory as a complement to existing ideas, not a replacement.

Competition and natural selection still matter, but competition may not be necessary to initiate the divergence.

“My theory more fully accounts for beak evolution in the iconic Darwin’s finches and antler evolution in the enigmatic, extinct Irish elk,” he said.

“We still need to determine how widespread disassociational scaling and niche scaling are across different groups of organisms.”

The study is published in the Evolutionary Journal of the Linnean Society.
