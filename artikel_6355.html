Your favorite foods may soon contain less sodium

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Most of the sodium Americans eat comes from packaged and prepared foods. A new FDA rule could help cut sodium at the source.

Starting in December, a new FDA rule will let food makers replace some or all of the added salt in products such as bread, cheese, and canned vegetables.

That could make a difference because Americans get more than 70% of their sodium from packaged and prepared foods.

One option is a salt blend that replaces some sodium chloride with potassium chloride. Few Americans use salt substitutes at home.

Daniel W. Jones, a professor emeritus at the University of Mississippi School of Medicine, chaired the committee that wrote the 2025 U.S. blood pressure guideline, which recommends potassium-enriched salt substitutes.

In the journal JAMA, Jones said the FDA rule could help reduce sodium intake.

Table salt is sodium chloride. In a potassium-enriched salt substitute, some of that sodium chloride is replaced with potassium chloride.

Potassium chloride is a salt made with potassium in place of sodium.

According to Jones, a blend of 25% to 30% potassium chloride is recommended. A higher proportion is not accepted by American tastes, which is part of the reason.

Stores sell a wider range, from blends of equal parts to pure potassium chloride. The equal-part products are usually labeled “light” salt.

Wanpen Vongpatanasin of UT Southwestern Medical Center directs the center’s section on hypertension.

Vongpatanasin noted that blood vessels are affected by sodium and potassium in opposite ways. Sodium makes arteries stiffer, while potassium generally relaxes the blood vessels.

“You get a twofer with a potassium-enriched salt substitute,” Jones said.

A person who switches consumes less sodium and more potassium, he added. Both changes are considered healthy.

Blood pressure is measured in millimeters of mercury, the units used in a reading such as 120 over 80. Jones noted that a blend with 25% potassium chloride lowers blood pressure by 5 to 7 points on average.

The top number is lowered by 8 to 10 points with most blood pressure drugs, he added.

Jones also explained the significance of such a drop for people with high blood pressure. Their risk of heart attack, heart failure, stroke, and kidney disease is reduced with every 10 points lost.

The reduction was estimated by Jones at about 15% to 20%.

The substitute was tested in a trial that was published in 2021 and conducted in 600 villages in rural China. The 20,995 participants had either survived a stroke or were at least 60 years old with high blood pressure.

The villages that received the potassium blend were selected at random and the remaining villages continued to use regular salt. One quarter of the blend consisted of potassium chloride.

The participants were followed for almost five years. About 29 strokes a year were recorded among every 1,000 people who cooked with the blend.

Roughly 34 strokes a year were recorded among every 1,000 people who used regular salt.

Deaths from any cause were also less frequent in the villages that used the blend. Blood pressure differed less than Jones’s average. The top number was about 3 points lower in villages using the blend.

Vongpatanasin and her colleagues examined how many people use these products. A national health survey of about 37,000 U.S. adults was analyzed for that purpose.

In the survey years 2013 and 2014, 5.4% of adults said they used a salt substitute. No other survey round recorded a higher share. By 2017 to 2020, that figure was 2.5%.

Use was low throughout and later fell by more than half. A substitute was rarely used by patients being treated for high blood pressure, either. Fewer than 1 in 10 of them reported it.

Taste is one reason. Vongpatanasin noted that potassium chloride can taste bitter or briny. The effect is stronger in larger amounts and for people who prefer heavily salted food.

She advised cooking with the substitute, along with other seasonings, instead of sprinkling it on cooked food. People become accustomed to the taste in about two weeks, Jones said.

Cost was also identified as a barrier in the 2025 guideline in which the World Health Organization suggested the switch. The guideline noted that the blends can cost more than regular salt and that public awareness is low.

A small consulting arrangement with the drugmaker AstraZeneca has been disclosed by Vongpatanasin.

She is also named as a co-inventor on a University of Texas patent for potassium magnesium citrate. No revenue has been generated by that patent to date.

Only a small part of dietary sodium is changed when a household switches completely to a substitute.

Roughly 11% of the sodium eaten by Americans is added during cooking or at the table. That estimate was reported by the FDA.

Average intake in the United States has been put at about 3,400 milligrams a day by the same agency. About 2,300 milligrams of sodium are contained in one teaspoon of table salt.

Federal dietary advice sets that amount as the daily limit, and the 2025 heart guideline aims for less than 1,500.

The recommendation of the WHO applies only to salt that people add themselves.

This limit was explained by Rain Yamamoto, a scientist in the nutrition and food safety department of the agency.

The advice is most relevant in countries where most salt is consumed through home cooking, Yamamoto said. The United States does not belong to that group of countries.

“Standards of identity” are maintained by the FDA for foods such as bread, sour cream, and mayonnaise.

The ingredients that a product needs before it can carry such a name are listed in each standard. Salt was allowed or required under 139 of those standards while a substitute was not permitted.

In October, the agency issued a final rule that lifted the restriction on substitutes in all 139 standards. Some or all of the salt in those foods may be replaced when the rule takes effect in December.

The affected foods include mayonnaise, salad dressing, many cheeses, canned vegetables, canned fish, bread, and pasta.

Potassium-enriched salt is not suitable for every patient. People with chronic kidney disease, especially end-stage disease, should not use potassium-based substitutes.

Other types of substitute such as herb blends can still be used by those patients.

Blood potassium can also be raised by several kinds of medicine. Some diuretics are included in that group and are commonly known as water pills.

Two common families of blood pressure drugs are included as well. These are the ACE inhibitors such as enalapril and the angiotensin receptor blockers such as losartan.

A doctor should be consulted first by patients on those drugs so that blood potassium can be monitored.

The WHO’s recommendation excludes pregnant women and children because evidence in these groups is limited.

Serious events linked to high blood potassium occurred at similar rates in both groups.

However, the trial had excluded people with serious kidney disease and people who were taking potassium supplements. Blood potassium was also not tested routinely during the trial.

No new labeling requirements were added by the FDA rule, which makes those exclusions relevant to packaged food.

A required warning about high potassium on those foods was declined by the agency. Potassium chloride will still be listed by name among the ingredients.

Use of a substitute is allowed by the FDA rule but no manufacturer is obliged to change a recipe.

No minimum level of replacement was set and no penalties were listed for companies that keep regular salt.

The rule leaves the choice of substitute to manufacturers.

Potassium chloride is mentioned in the rule as one example beside yeast extracts, MSG, and amino acids. Sodium in bread or cheese could therefore be lowered by a manufacturer with no potassium added.

A related question was left open by the 2021 trial in China. The blend was used for home cooking in that trial and people who ate most meals elsewhere had been excluded.

Packaged food that already contains the blend was not examined in that trial.

Concern about the rest of the diet was expressed by Sotiria Everett, who is a dietitian at Stony Brook University. Little effect is expected from cooking with a substitute if deli meats, cheese, and bacon are still eaten, she said.

A fruit or vegetable at every meal was recommended by Everett because potassium intake is then increased naturally.

The rule was published on October 13 and takes effect 60 days later in December. A report on progress toward the voluntary sodium targets of the FDA is also expected later in 2026.

The decision to use potassium salt in pursuit of those targets will be made by each manufacturer.

The full report was published in the journal JAMA.
