Stuck: How a frozen housing market is hitting buyers, sellers and the economy

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Autumn is usually a busy time in the housing market, but not this year. A sharp spike in mortgage rates in September has pushed the already chilly real estate sector into a deep freeze.

Buyers and sellers are moving to the sidelines, leaving those in the real estate business without much business.

The average rate on the popular 30-year fixed mortgage hovered around 6.60% in July, according to Mortgage News Daily. By the start of October, it was at 7.61%, a full percentage point higher.

"That shock, that rise of 100 basis points, is one of the fastest three-month increases we've seen in decades," said Zillow CEO Jeremy Wacksman on CNBC.

While the jump in rates exacerbated the problem, the slowdown in the housing market has been building for years. Rising home prices have pushed the dream of homeownership out of reach for a growing number of Americans. The number of active buyers nationally has been declining for six straight months and in October hit its lowest level since the aftermath of the financial crisis, according to Redfin.

The supply of homes for sale is also low. Builders are now faced with higher costs for land, labor, materials and energy. They simply can't afford to build the cheaper homes that are currently in demand. Single-family building permits, an indicator of future construction, were down 13% in October year over year, according to the U.S. Census. And builder sentiment in November dropped to the lowest level since 2012.

This slowdown in the entire housing ecosystem is impacting every facet of Americans' lives and livelihoods.

"Where you live determines so much. It determines where your kids go to school. It determines the jobs that you have access to, right? It determines whether or not you're able to be close to your family," said Chen Zhao, chief economist at Redfin. "What we've seen with the housing market these past four years, with this historically low level of turnover, is that a lot of people really are stuck in their home."

Closed sales of existing homes fell to a 16-year low in October, according to the National Association of Realtors. Pending sales, an indicator of future closings, hit a record low on the 25-year-old index from the Realtors.

While the pace of home price increases has slowed recently, prices are up well over 50% from pre-pandemic levels. Part of that is because homebuilding has slowed to a crawl.

It all has Ryan Deighan, who wants to buy a home, wondering if he ever will.

"I genuinely don't know, and that's the scary part," he said.

Deighan and his wife have been renting in Boston for six years. He is a chiropractor; she works in private equity. Even with a combined salary of more than $200,000 a year, he says they're stuck.

"Making what I am and what my wife makes, it just feels like we're on paper doing well, but it feels like we are still kind of fighting to put down certain amounts toward a house," Deighan said.

Being priced out of homeownership, he said, is changing what he called his "checklist" of how he once pictured his future.

"You graduate college, you find someone, you get married, you have the home with the white picket fence and the dog, and then the kids," he said. "That just looks a little different for us."

The numbers don't just affect would-be buyers. They are hitting sellers, too.

Robin Welch has been living in her Cleveland-area home for more than 20 years. Her children are now grown and moved out. She would like to downsize to a condominium, but the math doesn't make sense — in part, because her current interest rate is much lower than any new rate she could get. This has been dubbed the mortgage "lock-in effect."

"It actually costs more money to buy less of a home," said Welch. "I would have to pay into an HOA, which would be another monthly expense on top of increased property taxes and increased interest rate."

Welch has also done upgrades to her home over the years and doesn't want to have to start doing that on another home.

Welch is part of why the housing turnover rate, which is how often homes get new owners, has now dropped to just 3.8% a year, well below the 5% rate in the decade following the financial crisis, according to Cotality.

In addition, the national mobility rate, which is how often people move from region to region, has been falling for two years. It now sits at 8.3%, down from 11.1% a decade ago, according to Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies tabulations of U.S. Census Bureau data. Mobility hit a high of nearly 18% in the late 1980s.

"It's largely because of the interest lock-in effect, but also because of home prices just being so high," said Riordan Frost, senior research analyst at Harvard's JCHS. "Those things combined have made it hard both for existing homeowners to move and for people to get into homeownership."

Americans have nearly $35 trillion of their wealth locked up in housing, a record high, according to Cotality. But the slowdown in the housing market, Frost said, impacts the accumulation, handover and use of that wealth. It also affects the labor market.

"Any kind of potential job would have to really outweigh … the price of taking that mortgage with a higher rate, or the price of moving, or the price of getting into a higher rent unit," Frost said.

The wealth gap between homeowners and renters now is nothing short of massive. The most recent survey of consumer finances last year by the Federal Reserve showed that the median homeowner net wealth is about 43 times the median renter net wealth.

"It has kind of knock-on effects for the rest of the economy. It has effects on the labor market and how good of a match we can get between employees and employers. It has an effect on education. It has an effect on just GDP growth," said Zhao.

When homes aren't selling and people aren't moving, those who work in that business are stuck as well. The entire housing ecosystem is churning much more slowly, and the sudden jump in mortgage rates is causing deals to fall apart.

"Right after Labor Day, we went live with a bunch of listings. They all went under contract over the asking price," said Dana Bull, a real estate agent in the Boston suburbs. But then rates spiked.

"It was a shock to the system," Bull said. "I think all of them came back on the market. We fell out of contract." Asked if she is seeing colleagues leaving the market, she answered: "People are thinking about it. It's tough."

The economy has lost close to 2,000 jobs in real estate and leasing over the past year and 4,400 jobs in residential construction, according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics.

Jobs in the mortgage industry have been falling since June and are down 38% since their last peak in the second quarter of 2021, according to the Mortgage Bankers Association. That was when mortgage rates were less than half what they are today.

Shant Banosian, president of Rate and the top mortgage originator in the U.S. by dollar volume, said his business is down about 30% this past year.

"Whenever change happens quickly, there's a slowdown," said Banosian, who has been in the business for 23 years. "It requires more of us to go out and advise, educate and guide. So, what I experienced was I just needed to spend more time on marketing, branding, relationship building. The conversations are longer because people want more information to try to navigate this market."

Bull agrees. She said her job has gotten harder as well.

"We've done double the work this year, having homes go under contract, fall out of contract, putting them back under agreement, but definitely more negotiating," she said. "That's sort of my plea to both buyers and sellers: We need to get back to a space where we're comfortable negotiating."
