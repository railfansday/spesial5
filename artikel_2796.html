The NFL is taking over Thanksgiving. But how much football is too much football?

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

You know how Thursday's going to go, right? You're going to load up your plate and feast, you're going to take a moment to catch your breath, you're going to dive back in for more, you're going to grab a quick nap, and then after the sun goes down you're going to go back for one more helping.

I'm talking about your NFL diet, of course, but you'll probably do the same thing with turkey, too. And as with turkey leftovers, there'll come a point when you realize that the fifth helping isn't nearly as much fun as the first.

The NFL has honed its Thanksgiving Day slate into a sleek, efficient set-your-oven-timer tradition. You'll watch the Lions, a team that's finally fun to watch instead of being the equivalent of Thanksgiving green bean casserole. You'll watch the inescapable Cowboys, once again almost ready to make a run. And you'll try to stay awake for the evening game. (You will fail.)

It's been this way since 1934, when the Lions began playing on Thanksgiving; since 1966, when the Cowboys joined them; and since 2006, when the league added the third game. Every year, before a captive holiday audience, these games do numbers; last year's Chiefs-Cowboys game was the most-watched NFL regular-season game in history.

And now, with Thanksgiving firmly in hand, the NFL is looking to run up the score. Three years ago, the league rolled out a Black Friday game kicking off midafternoon, so you'll have something to watch as you scroll through sale after sale. This year, the Shield is planting the flag on another spot: Thanksgiving Eve.

Traditionally the province of impromptu high school reunions and travel nightmares, the night before Thanksgiving will now host an NFL game. This year, the Packers will play the Rams on Netflix while you're running around trying to see if you bought yams and figuring out how to defrost a frozen-solid 20-pound turkey in just a few hours. And that's just the start of the NFL turkey festivities.

On Thanksgiving Day, the Lions will play the Bears, and then we'll all try to keep our dinner down as the Eagles play the Cowboys. The evening game will feature one of the best matchups of the year — Chiefs-Bills — and Friday, the Broncos will meet the Steelers. (Even though literally any game would do numbers on Thanksgiving, the NFL is showing some mercy. They've stashed Jets-Dolphins — a game more unhealthy than undercooked turkey — on Sunday rather than exposing America to those kinds of toxins on a holiday.)

Is all this football too much football? Depends on how you frame the question.

The holiday football format has held up like few other pop culture traditions in American life, from radio to television to cable to streaming. And it's based on a simple American truth: We would rather do anything than talk to our families.

OK, that's not quite accurate. It's theoretically possible to both watch football and talk to your family. Encouraged, even, considering how quickly family conversations can swing from friendly banter to open hostility. Long-simmering resentments, gossip, political disputes, in-law trouble, questioning of life choices … why throw open the doors to that furnace when everyone can come together and rail on the Cowboys defense?

The league, so famous for owning Sundays, will broadcast games on five of six days starting on Wednesday.

There are two ways to look at this explosion of holiday football, from both the fan and the economic perspective. For the vast majority of Americans, the NFL's land grab — which also includes Christmas Eve and three games on Christmas Day — means that there are ever fewer hours when the NFL isn't demanding your attention.

As holiday problems go, "too much football" isn't exactly a terrible one. And hey, if that's what gets you through the holidays, no shame whatsoever. But isn't there something to be said for a bit of peace and quiet, too? Maybe sitting by a fire, or catching up with friends and family, or watching an ungrateful Peppermint Patty crash Charlie Brown's Thanksgiving? There are millions of NFL fans whose lives would measurably improve if they weren't tying their emotional state to their team — you know the ones, I don't have to name them — so maybe give that a try for a couple hours?

Which leads to the larger point: the more football we see, the less important each individual game becomes. For now, Mark Cuban's infamous prediction for the NFL — pigs get fat, hogs get slaughtered — still isn't anywhere close to truth; the league continues to fatten up, year after year. Maybe that'll change, maybe the arrow will flatten out instead of continuing to go up and to the right, but it hasn't yet.

But popularity is context-dependent. The NFL is popular in part because of its scarcity; you wouldn't get eight-figure viewership levels for literally every single game if teams played, say, 162 of them in a season. The league, so famous for owning Sundays, will broadcast games on five of six days starting on Wednesday, and if you think the league doesn't have its eye on similar expansions throughout the season, we have a St. Louis Rams jersey to sell you.

The NFL specializes in creating capital-E Events out of thin air. The Thanksgiving Eve game on Netflix will boast fanfare literally worthy of a Super Bowl, including a halftime show featuring U2. The five Thanksgiving games will air on five different networks and services — Netflix, Fox, CBS, NBC and Prime — each with its own spectacle, its own flash-and-dazzle to catch and hold your attention through the holiday noise.

We're getting perilously close to old-man-yells-at-turkey-leg territory here, but when everything is a spectacle, nothing is. Thanksgiving dinner — turkey, stuffing, cranberry sauce, et cetera — is so damn delicious because it comes around only once a year. But every week? Maybe not so much. Sometimes, you don't know how unique something is until it's everywhere.

Enjoy Thanksgiving, friends, and enjoy the football. It'll all be worth watching. Yes, even Cowboys-Eagles.
