'I want to destroy capitalism, not France': On the streets with the French ultra left

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Watch: The BBC's Katya Adler speaks to protesters taking part in recent demonstrations

France is in turmoil. Crippling public debt, a "lame-duck" president with six months left in office, and massive social unrest - sparked by student protests which have erupted into looting and violence.

This Saturday marked the sixth weekend in a row of demos against soaring prices, worsening public services and alleged police brutality. Violence by protesters is down on last month but still present.

President Emmanuel Macron has publicly blamed riots on "delinquents" and "ultra-left groups" whose aim, he says, is to bring down the French Republic.

That's quite a claim â and one that needs examining.

The unrest in France has become a political football ahead of key presidential and parliamentary elections next year. The left in France blames the centre and right for the cost-of-living crisis that's making French people so upset and angry. They blame the hard left for fomenting violence (which they deny). While the nationalist right pledges, if elected, it will return order and security to France's streets.

So, who exactly are these "ultra-left" groups Macron speaks of? And how organised and dangerous are they?

It's infamously hard to speak to members of the radical left. They don't much like what they call mainstream media. We're viewed as part of an establishment many of them despise.

My best bet was to head out into the protests and try to approach people dressed in all black and ski masks or balaclavas (worn, they say, to hide their identity from police). It's a quasi-uniform associated with left-wing rioters or disruptors, known as the Black Block.

You see them at demos all over Europe. In France, they tend to appear from city side streets, as daytime protests are dying down, dusk is falling and riot police begin to emerge in full force.

The BBC's Katya Adler talking to a protester

French intelligence drastically underestimated the outpouring of anger, looting and destruction following protests last month. More than 1,000 police officers were reportedly injured, young protesters had bones broken and two reportedly lost a hand in altercations with police armed with stun grenades. Le Monde newspaper has an article, external it is constantly updating to investigate a rising number of reports of protester injuries. The French Insurers Federation estimates damages to shops and private property on 17 October alone - the day of the biggest riots - came to around â¬207m (Â£175.5m).

Since then, the French authorities are trying to tightly manage demos. Police presence is heavy. Protesters say heavy handed.

Towards the end of one of the protests this month, we saw would-be rioters vastly outnumbered, glass bottles and homemade paint bombs at the ready to throw at police. They were no match for the batons, stun grenades and tear gas the officers had to hand.

French police pepper spraying protesters during a demonstration by French high school and university students in Paris earlier this month

As we were being forcefully kettled in by police, I tried to talk to would-be rioters. Few wanted to engage but I then met 18-year-old Nas (not his real name) and friends. They would only speak to me if they could hide their identity. This was to avoid police detection, they said.

Milling, you could say provocatively, by baton-at-the-ready police â they seemed to fit a radical-left rioter stereotype.

"How can you justify the violence?" I ask them.

"Without violence, you don't achieve anything," one of them tells me. "It's the only way to get our voices heard."

"We come from an area with a bad reputation. Our schools are a mess. Our teachers quit. You need qualifications to get a decent job. There is supposed to be equality in France. We won't give up our fight."

These young men, we discover, come from the sprawling housing estates common on French city outskirts - the banlieues - associated with deprivation, unrest and second or third generation immigrant families.

They weren't politicised or organised by the radical left, they insisted. Just angry.

Left-wing rioters or disruptors, known as the Black Block, tend to dress in all black, wearing ski masks or balaclavas

Earlier, I had spotted two hard-left student activists, standing masked on top of a bus shelter, above the thronging crowd of protesters waving banners sporting the red anarchist sign and the symbol for the Antifa (anti-fascist) movement.

I put it to them that France has a proud tradition and respect for the right to protest but that the country has been horrified by the violence it's seen over the last weeks.

They were keen to downplay the rioting but - again â would only speak if they could remain anonymous.

"Violence ends up reflecting badly on our movements. We don't like it. But what's a smashed shop window compared to police using stun guns on protesting school kids? Also, people [in France] are regularly going cold and hungry. They're losing faith in this society."

How could they believe violence would bring about a juster society, I wondered?

They shrug off my question. In France, these days, the real violence is state violence, they say.

Some protesters don't support the Black Block's violence but say the state's violence is worse

French intelligence estimates there are 5,000 "ultra-left" activists in the country.

Anticapitalist Starflix (that's his codename) counts himself as one of them. The 17-year-old is also a member of the violent Black Block movement. We'd arranged to meet on the edge of the protest, by a narrow canal.

Fearful about being apprehended by police, he asked to remain anonymous.

He showed me a couple of homemade paint-bombs - eggshells filled with paint and sealed with medical tape that he throws at police officers. He carries them in his rucksack, wrapped in a royal blue Italia football shirt.

"We throw the paint bombs at the visors on riot police helmets, so they have to lift them up in order to see and that makes them suffer their own tear gas that they've sprayed at us," he tells me.

"We pick up stones and rocks too. Anything we find on the streets of Paris, to throw."

I put it to him that he and others in the Black Block are causing a lot of problems on French streets: damaging businesses, hurting police officers, destroying the demos for peaceful protesters. What is he trying to achieve?

As he's under 18, his parents give us permission to speak to him. They share his politics, Starflix tells me, but not all rioters - masked and dressed in black as he is - do.

"The Black Block isn't a single organisation. It's a technique. Some of us use violence to get government reform. I want to bring about a revolution.

"Other people are opportunists who use anonymity to steal things. When we all wear the same clothes, it makes it harder for police to identify us."

When I ask if he is scared he might get hurt or arrested, he admits he is but says what frightens him more is the thought that society might not change.

Social media plays a huge role in the riots. Starflix tells me he and his friends use Signal and Telegram to arrange to meet at protests.

"The Black Block isn't a single organisation," says protester Starflix

There are numerous websites and Instagram profiles, mainly hosted by left-wing organisations, offering tips for protesters and rioters, such as: keep your phone at home so police can't track you, cover your face to avoid being identified and emulate martial arts expert Bruce Lee and "be like water" - appearing and disappearing into crowds. That's advice passed on by the Hong Kong protesters eight years ago.

While not formally organising the protests or violence with these posts, could the fact groups host this information online be viewed as a kind of incitement or manipulation of youngsters whose feelings are running so high in France?

All the young rioters I spoke to insist they are not being herded or directed, but is there something bigger behind what's happening?

Ultra-left protesters spoke to the BBC on condition of anonymity

It takes our team a long time, but they manage to find a seasoned activist with the Black Block who would speak to us. I put to him Macron's assertion that the ultra left has organised groups that aim to bring down the French Republic. Is that true?

"It's totally false of the authorities to say we are all organised. And the intelligence services know it," the activist scoffs. "It's a manipulation of the French public to make them think there is an enemy within."

"Basically, people turn up to a protest, get dressed in black and do their own thing. The uniting factor is rage and not just in France. You see the same rage [in protests] in Italy, Germany, Spain. It's exploding by itself."

I ask if the anti-capitalist presidential candidate Jean-Luc MÃ©lenchon and his France Unbowed party (LFI) are somehow linked to the violence. They are present at most protests, supporters handing out campaign pamphlets to the chanting crowds.

But the activist denies this, telling me MÃ©lenchon is "a capitalist because he's part of the system". He says the LFI and the trade unions try to "calm" unrest, while he believes things need to be "stepped up a notch".

Why had he decided to talk to us, I ask - does he want to collapse the French government?

"I just want to say we don't love violence. I don't want to destroy France. We want to destroy a system that is a capitalist system, of course, but to change the society in a way that we think will be better for the most people, for more people.

"More and more find it hard to make ends meet. In the countryside, people can't even afford to fill their tank with petrol."

You have to leave the city, heading out into the gloriously autumnal French countryside, to meet some of the more organised, hardcore elements of the ultra left.

Limoges Town Hall, allegedly damaged by protesters, has become a symbol of the current state France appears to be in

This is a world of eco activism and known saboteurs. One man remains in police custody linked to the fire during protests at Limoges Town Hall last month. The devastated, yet somehow still elegant, 19th century building has become a symbol of the current state France appears to be in. An acrid smell still hangs in the air one month on.

The 34-year-old arson suspect, who insists he's innocent, comes from a hamlet near Limoges, known for its anarchist, environmentalist community. His father, a fellow activist, is also known to French intelligence. We approached the father and the suspect's lawyer for comment. They didn't want to speak.

Instead, we met a 39-year-old father of one, a local full-time ultra-left activist for more than two decades. Like the other individuals we spoke to for this investigation, he would only talk to us on condition he could remain anonymous.

"We were all shocked by the [town hall] fire â even as we watched it happen in front of us - but it's our duty to take action," he tells me.

"I can't leave this world to my son in the state that it's in. Look how military vehicles and brute force are used by the state against our young students.

"It's amazing the people haven't taken up arms against them. Do we need a policeman killed in order for the state to listen?"

"As a child, I was educated with French values â liberty, equality, fraternity - but when I look at the social injustice in the world now, I'm disgusted. As a father, I can't let this go on. If I have to, I would take a bullet to protect the youth of this country."

These are dangerous-sounding words - but the radical left doesn't appear to be big, armed or united enough to seriously threaten France.

Upcoming presidential elections offer a crossroad, with polls predicting a run-off between Marine Le Pen and Jean-Luc MÃ©lenchon

We repeatedly approached French intelligence. They didn't want to comment. A sign perhaps of the huge sensitivity of the current situation, especially with France hurtling towards an extremely charged presidential election.

But Michel Klein, a recently retired former police commissioner, told me he didn't think the radical left posed a serious threat to the French state.

"I believe there is a will from the radical left and the anarchists to try and destroy the Republic but they obviously don't have the means to do it," he told me.

He sees no risk of civil war in France (a possibility you hear some in the country discuss these days) but he identified a rising risk of clashes between far-left and far-right groups ahead of the presidential election.

Polls currently predict a run-off in the vote between nationalist candidate Marine Le Pen and anticapitalist MÃ©lenchon.

Far-right vigilante groups have begun appearing on the edge of protests in a number of cities across France to "protect French streets", they say.

The popular hard-left presidential candidate MÃ©lenchon attends most demos, his supporters handing political pamphlets to the chanting crowds, but he denies any links with violence.

France is so polarised, there's little sign next year's presidential vote will calm things. This turbulent mix of left and right, social and political unrest, feels like a perfect storm.
