Dreaming of Cats and Dogs

The deep relationships people form with domestic cats and dogs can be among the strongest emotional bonds in their lives. Since we know that dreams offer an accurate reflection of our most important relationships in waking life, it can be predicted that cats and dogs will appear with some frequency in people’s dreams, and this turns out to be true. Interpreting the symbolic themes and images in these dreams can lead to a variety of psychological insights. They can also lead to a new perspective on the possibility, even the likelihood, that our cats and dogs are dreaming about us.

Dream researchers have found that dreaming about animals in general is more frequent for children than for adults, more for tribal communities than for modern Westerners, and more for pregnant women than non-pregnant women. Cats and dogs are usually at the top of the list of most-dreamed about animals, so the distribution patterns found in these studies give us a rough clue about who is most likely to experience feline- and canine-related dreaming.

Who appears in dreams more often, cats or dogs? A review of the collections in the Sleep and Dream Database (SDDb) finds that people dream slightly more about dogs than cats. A search for dream reports of 10+ words in length with at least one reference to a dog-related word found 1,088 instances out of 34,475 dream reports, or 3.2% of the total. A parallel search for dream reports of 10+ words in length with at least one reference to a cat-related word found 759 instances out of 34,475 dream reports, or 2.2% of the total.

Looking at the Hall and Van de Castle norm dreams from the 1950’s, a slight variation appears in relation to gender. The 491 dream reports from the males included 11 (2.2%) with a dog-related word but only 2 (0.4%) with a cat-related word, whereas the 490 dream reports from the females had 10 (2%) with a dog-related word and 9 (1.8%) with a cat-related word.

As with any aspect of dream content, the dreamer’s personal context matters. Here, the relevant factors include whether the dreamer has pets now, grew up with pets, feels anxiety around animals, or considers themselves a “dog person” or a “cat person. It also matters how the dogs or cats appear in the dream: healthy or sick, friendly or aggressive, inside or outside, alive or passed away.

Since ancient times, animals in dreams have been regarded as special bearers of natural wisdom. Modern psychoanalysts regard animals in dreams as symbols of our primal desires and wild instincts. Animals can also appear in dreams as cultural metaphors (e.g., a cat = night hunter, a dog = loyal companion).

An early historical example comes from the medieval Japanese monk and Buddhist teacher Myoe Shonin, who recorded the following in his dream journal in 1206:

“I dreamed of two puppies. One was white and the other was of several, rich colors. The mixed-color dog was attractive beyond limit. Thus I fixed a container full of rice. It seemed to me that all the people there used small, open containers as feeding dishes. (Although the white dog went into a fire, it did not burn.)” (168-169)

Myoe did not interpret his dreams, but rather meditated on their visionary qualities. Here, the dream puppies elicit a vivid experience of amazing beauty, compassion for others, and magical resilience.

An aggressive cat figures as a vivid symbol of danger in a dream reported by Anna Bonus Kingsford in 1877. Kingsford was a medical doctor, animal rights activist, and theosophical mystic whose dream journal included this entry:

"I dreamt that I had a beautiful bird in a cage, and that the cage was placed on a table in a room where there was a cat. I took the bird out of the cage and put him on the table. Instantly the cat sprang upon him and seized him in her mouth. I threw myself upon her and strove to wrest away her prey, loading her with reproaches and bewailing the fate of my beautiful bird. Then suddenly some one said to me, 'You have only yourself to blame for this misfortune. While the bird remained in his cage he was safe. Why should you have taken him out before the eyes of the cat?'” (38)

Kingsford said the dream came at a time when she was on the verge of sharing publicly some of her unusual psycho-spiritual experiences, and she interpreted the dream as an unambiguous warning not to do so.

In his 2003 study of a long dream series from a middle-aged woman (“Barb Sanders”), G. William Domhoff found several reports in which kittens were neglected, deformed, or starving. Sanders’ own cats were healthy and fine, so the dreams seemed to point to an indirect symbolic meaning. A closer look suggested a connection between “stray cats” and “lost men,” who were indeed a major concern and problem in her waking life. (128)

Anyone who has lived with a cat or dog has noticed how at certain points during their sleep they whimper and growl, their muscles and whiskers twitch, and their eyes flutter under their eyelids. If we observed the same behavior in a sleeping human, we would surely assume the person is dreaming. And if we knew that person was fed, cuddled, groomed, and loved by a particular individual, we would be on solid research grounds in predicting that person dreams frequently about the caregiving individual.

How much of this, from the REM-dreaming connection to the interpersonal continuities, applies beyond humans to other animal species? The honest answer is, we do not know. But the tide of scientific evidence is moving towards ever greater recognition in non-human animals of emotion, cognition, and high-order consciousness. Can dreaming be far behind?
