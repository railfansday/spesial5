CAA’s Bryan Lourd Urges Congress To Pass AI Legislation To Protect Americans “Famous Or Not”

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

CAA chief Bryan Lourd is urging the full Congress to pass the No Fakes Act and deliver it to President Trump for signature, a move Lourd says would protect everyone, famous or not, from nonconsensual digital replicas of their voices and likenesses.

In a guest essay published in today’s New York Times, Lourd writes, “The Senate and House should move quickly and deliver the bill to President Trump for signature. That would ensure Americans retain control of their images and voices in the A.I. era.”

The No Fakes Act was approved unanimously by the Senate Judiciary Committee in June, and, Lourd contends, there “is momentum to pass it through the full Congress before the end of the year.” He calles it “one of the few A.I. bills with true bipartisan support.” Watch on Deadline

Hollywood, Lourd writes, “is not afraid of new technology.”

“The objection from actors, musicians and other creators is to a world in which anyone can use their face or voice without permission,” he writes. “In fact, many artists are already embracing A.I. on their own terms. Some of them are making deals with companies that are creating their A.I. likenesses, with their consent.”

Lourd poses the issue as not just for Hollywood. “A content creator learned on her honeymoon that a YouTube ad was using an A.I. version of her to sell erectile dysfunction supplements,” he writes. “Is all this legal? Right now, it’s an open question. But it shouldn’t be.”

“Hollywood doesn’t need America’s leaders to devise a new concept for new circumstances,” writes the chief executive and co-chairman of Creative Artists Agency. “We need them to extend and enforce the rules that already exist. The use of Americans’ faces, voices, performances and creative works is already subject to rules at the federal or state level that say, in short: You need to get permission, and you need to pay for the use.”

“This principle has guided the entertainment industry for more than a century,” he continues. “Whether it can continue to protect Americans may be decided in the coming weeks. The No Fakes Act would create a federal prohibition on creating nonconsensual digital replicas of any person’s voice or likeness — famous or not.”

The No Fakes Act, Lourd says, “would apply a tried-and-true formula to the A.I. era: control, consent and compensation. It would set a uniform federal standard to replace outdated, ill-fitting state laws, many of them written with an analog world in mind. Its supporters include all sides of the table: SAG-AFTRA, the union representing actors and other performers; the Motion Picture Association, representing the studios; and tech companies including IBM, Google and OpenAI.”

Earlier this month, Lourd told Deadline’s Dominic Patten, that the issues around AI are “about the fundamental relationship between technological progress and creative rights, and who determines the value of creative work, who decides how that work is used, and whether creators will share in the opportunities AI creates.”
