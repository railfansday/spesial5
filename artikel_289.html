AI has intensified investors' interest in public records. Over the last few years, venture-backed startups have raised upwards of $100 million to collect data from thousands of agencies and turn it into AI-powered "signals" that help businesses sell to governments. At the same time, chatbots are making it easier for individuals to file requests.

As agencies struggle to tell whether they’re dealing with a citizen, a chatbot, or a sales pipeline, some states are passing legislation that makes it easier to deny requests. That could potentially make public offices less transparent.

In July 2021, a wildfire burned through a forest on the University of Alaska, Fairbanks campus, stripping away the moss and soil insulating the permafrost below. Five years later, we followed researchers into the forest to investigate how fighting fires could help keep that carbon locked in the ground.

Their probe revealed an escalating problem: As climate change fuels more frequent and severe fires, they’re accelerating permafrost thaw and releasing greenhouse gases. Now, researchers are trying to break that cycle. But how much should we spend, and what risks are worth taking?

Teaching a computer to use human language requires an inhuman amount of data. An LLM can easily churn through a hundred thousand times more words than a person will experience in the process of mastering their mother tongue.

This yawning divide between children and machines is called the data efficiency gap. It raises a tantalizing question for researchers: How can children learn so much with so little?

By reverse-engineering how they learn, scientists hope to create more efficient AI models—and perhaps settle enduring questions about language and children's developing minds. Here’s how they’re trying to close the data gap.
