Christie’s Buzzy 20th-Century Sale Totals $626.6 M., with Starry Lots Above $80 M. by Kandinsky and Klimt

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

By Brian Boucher, Harrison Jacobs November 18, 2026 12:22am

At its packed Rockefeller Center saleroom on Tuesday night, Christie’s staged a sometimes lively, sometimes listless two-hour-and-40-minute sale of 20th-century art, led by an $85.8 million abstraction by Wassily Kandinsky, closely followed by an $82.3 million landscape by Gustav Klimt. The house estimated the 74-lot sale to bring $511–$743 million, once adjusted for five works withdrawn before the sale. (Along the withdrawn works was a Frida Kahlo estimated at $2 to $3.5 million, which was pulled just hours before the proceedings were set to begin.) The hammer total for the night came to $521.1 million, slightly above the low estimate. With the house’s fees added, the sale totaled $626.6 million. Records were set for Hans Bellmer, Fernando Botero, Wassily Kandinsky, Wifredo Lam, and Cornelis Cornelisz. van Haarlem. At the same time, there were some failures to launch: works by Fernand Léger, Henri Matisse, Joan Mitchell, Amedeo Modigliani, Henry Moore, and Jackson Pollock did not sell. Still, the house found buyers for 68 of the 74 lots offered, for a healthy sell-through rate of 92 percent.

In the room were market players who formed a walking power list. Among them were dealers Jeffrey Deitch, Larry Gagosian, Thaddaeus Ropac, Christophe van de Weghe, and Dominique Lévy and Brett Gorvy, as well as advisors including Amy Cappellazzo, Kim Heirston, Laura Paulson, and Jussi Pylkkänen, formerly the house’s global president. Royalty for the night was David Nahmad, whose family offered the Kandinsky. He sat in his customary spot in the front row and was the underbidder on a few lots, including the Picasso painting Le Baiser, consigned by artist Jeff Koons.

Christie’s equivalent sale last year, which combined selections from the collection of Robert and Patricia Ross Weis with the 20th-century evening sale, totaled a whopping $690 million, making Tuesday’s take 9 percent lower.

Tuesday night’s sale started slow, with the first four lots hammering below estimate, though the last of those, Hans Bellmer’s Jointure de boules (La Poupée), set a new auction record for the artist at $1.67 million with fees. The bidding picked up after that, with a strong showing of in-the-room bidders peppered among the specialists on the phone and a very active online bidder from Florida. Works by Yves Tanguy and Isamu Noguchi hammered well above estimate after spirited bidding, at $4.6 million and $1.2 million, respectively. The Noguchi hammered for double its high estimate; with fees, the price landed at $1.54 million.

That energy carried the crowd well into the star lot of the evening, Wassily Kandinsky’s Betonte Ecken (1922–23), which bore a $60–$80 million estimate. Before the sale, the house said the work was guaranteed to set a new record. It sold after a short bidding war between Christie’s global president Alex Rotter; Sara Friedlander, chairman of postwar and contemporary art, Americas; and art advisor Ralph DeLuca. Rotter came away the victor, with the winning $74 million bid. With fees, the work landed at $85.8 million, nearly doubling Kandinsky’s previous high of $44.8 million, paid for the artist’s 1910 painting Murnau mit Kirche II (Murnau with Church II) at Sotheby’s London in 2023.

Partway through the sale, DeLuca said of the results, in a text to ARTnews, “The flight to quality is astounding.” That description was as apt for Tuesday’s bidders as it was for DeLuca. He was the underbidder on a number of top lots Tuesday night and lost the Kandinsky by only one bid. (Per the Baer Faxt, De Luca was the winner of the Tanguy.)

To wit, the Claude Monet picture Champ d’avoine et coquelicots, offered three lots later, drew bids from three phone bidders as well as two bidders in the room. That activity sent the painting, which comes from the collection of William I. Koch, soaring past its $12–$18 million estimate. It hammered for $22 million, or $26 million with fees. The Auguste Rodin sculpture Le Penseur, taille de la porte dit “moyen modèle,” offered next and also from the Koch collection, hammered at $6.4 million. With fees, it came to $7.9 million, more than double the high estimate. Another Monet offered later in the sale, La berge du Petit-Gennevilliers, drew the longest bidding of the evening (nine minutes), though one could blame auctioneer Adrien Meyer for some leniency there. Ada Tsui, senior vice president and head of 20th/21st century art for Asia Pacific, and Arco Yu, an associate vice president and specialist who heads the 20th and 21st century art evening sale for Asia Pacific, traded bids in interminable $100,000 increments while they stood shoulder to shoulder, to take the work from a $5 million starting bid to its eventual hammer price of $10.5 million, just a smidge above the $10 million high estimate. With fees, the work’s total came to $12.8 million.

The biggest fireworks came a handful of lots later, when Gustav Klimt’s landscape Litzlbergkeller (1915–16) hammered for $71 million after a five-minute contest between Max Carter, the house’s global chairman, and Rotter, who again came away with the prize. The hammer price came smack in the middle of the $60–$80 million estimate, but with fees, it landed at $82.3 million.

By contrast, seven works consigned by Jeff Koons failed to ignite much excitement. The combined low estimate for the group was $104 million, but the hammer total fell shy, at just $87.8 million, with four pieces coming in below their low estimates. With fees, the group totaled, coincidentally, $104 million. The set was led by the Picasso Le Baiser, which hammered at $26.5 million, far below its $40 million low estimate; with fees, the piece brought $31.2 million. A bright spot came with a large canvas by Cornelis Cornelisz. van Haarlem, Hercules and Achelous. Though it hammered for $11.5 million, shy of its $12 million low estimate, it sold for $13.9 million with the house’s fees, setting a new record for the Dutch Old Master. Other works by Picasso, Tilman Riemenschneider, and Édouard Manet filled out the group; see a full report here.

The energy seemed to peter out considerably after the sale of La berge du Petit-Gennevilliers, which followed the Koons trove. Five of the six unsold lots came in the back half of the sale, and 14 of the following 46 lots hammered below estimate. Of the 11 lots in that grouping that overperformed, Diego Rivera’s Vendedora de flores, de Tehuantepec, from the Koch collection, proved the biggest surprise, as four specialists duked it out over seven minutes of bidding before the work hammered for $8.1 million, more than double the $3.5 million high estimate. Sophie Jensen, a client advisory associate, won the contest. The total with fees came to $10 million, within spitting distance of Rivera’s auction record of $14.1 million, set at Christie’s New York in 2022.

Rounding out the night’s top 10 were works by Alberto Giacometti, Henri Matisse, and Mark Rothko. (Noticing a theme here?) The Giacometti, a four-foot-high 1949 bronze, Figure, demie grandeur (no. 2), a trademark elongated rendition of a woman, was offered without reserve and with an estimate of $20–$30 million. The collector Herbert Lust had bought it from Chicago collector Joseph R. Shapiro in 1970, and it had been off the market ever since. It hammered for $22 million to a phone bidder via Katharine Arnold, the house’s vice chairman and head of postwar and contemporary art, after a three-minute contest. With fees, the result was $26 million, good for the fifth-best result of the evening.

While it hammered below its $10–$15 million estimate, Wifredo Lam’s Ogue Orisa (Eggue Orissa, l’herbe des dieux) did break a record for the artist. After a handful of bids, it hammered for $9.5 million, or $11.6 million with fees. His previous auction record was $9.6 million, set at Sotheby’s New York in 2020. (The winner, again per Baer Faxt, was collector Gary Nader, also the reported winner of Jean-Michel Basquiat’s Untitled (Fist), which sold for $6.2 million with fees.)

Similarly, Fernando Botero’s Horse, the 11-foot-tall rotund bronze sculpture currently residing outside Rockefeller Center, landed within estimate but broke a record for the artist. The work hammered for $4.2 million to a bidder on the phone with Maria Los, deputy chairman and head of client advisory for the Americas. The total with fees of $5.24 million squeaked it past his previous top result of $5.1 million, set at Christie’s in 2023 for the 1979 painting The Musicians.

Tuesday night’s sale was the second of the week, following Sotheby’s two-part Monday night sale, which generated $378.7 million across 74 lots. A sale of works from the collection of cable television magnate Ted Baum and interior designer Ruth Baum netted $110 million, followed by the house’s Now and Contemporary Art sale, which fetched $268.8 million. Other major sales will follow at Sotheby’s and Christie’s on Wednesday and Thursday nights, respectively.

New York gallerist and art advisor Cristin Tierney put the sale in the context of tech failures at banks, the national air travel system, and even the National Security Agency that plagued the U.S. in the run-up to the sale. “Days and days of software outages, air travel chaos and stock volatility,” she told ARTnews in a text partway through the sale, “but you know what you can count on? Art. Turns out a market based on supporting culture, belief and creativity is the most consistent one of all.”

Advisor Gabriela Palmieri reached for an aviation metaphor to describe the evening. “This week is a congested airspace,” she said in a conversation outside the saleroom. “The houses are doing the most amazing job, because they have to land a lot of planes. There’s incredible elasticity in the market, but the sheer volume of lots is staggering. Anything that fell short, you know, the market is recalibrating. Buyers are drawing the line in certain places. But the houses are achieving a lot of beautiful results.”
