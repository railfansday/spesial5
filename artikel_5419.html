I Looked Beyond Silicon Valley and Found a New Playbook For Building Startups

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

You're viewing the US website. Looking for the Site edition?

Founders in small countries can’t rely on abundant capital or elite networks. Here’s what startups in big markets can learn from them.

Opinions expressed by Entrepreneur contributors are their own.

Silicon Valley teaches founders to think big. Smaller startup markets teach them how to build when almost nothing is guaranteed.

Over the past year, I have met founders and investors across the Baltics, Georgia and Guatemala through Baltic Fintech Days, a 500 Global Eurasia Demo Day in Tbilisi and Volcano Summit. These ecosystems differ in language, history and access to capital, but the strongest founders shared a common advantage: They could not rely on a large domestic market, abundant venture funding or proximity to the world’s most powerful networks.

That constraint shaped how they built. They thought internationally earlier, stretched resources further and cultivated relationships across borders. As capital becomes more selective and growth at all costs continues to fall out of favor, founders everywhere can learn from companies built in markets that have never had the luxury of excess.

Here are five lessons from some of the world’s smallest startup ecosystems.

A founder in the United States can build a large company without ever leaving the domestic market. Founders in Latvia, Lithuania, Estonia, Georgia or Guatemala rarely have that option.

The entire population of Lithuania is smaller than that of Los Angeles. Latvia has fewer than 2 million residents, while Georgia has fewer than 4 million. Even Guatemala, the largest of the markets I visited, represents only a fraction of the purchasing power available in the United States.

As a result, international expansion is not a distant ambition for many of these founders. It is part of the original business model.

At Baltic Fintech Days, I met entrepreneurs who were thinking about cross-border payments, compliance and market entry well before reaching the scale at which an American startup might begin considering international growth. Their home markets gave them useful places to test, but not enough room to stop there.

This forces founders to ask important questions early: Can the product travel? Can it serve customers across languages, currencies and regulatory systems? Does the problem exist beyond the founder’s immediate network?

Building globally does not mean launching in 10 countries at once. It means avoiding assumptions that make international expansion unnecessarily difficult later. Founders should consider localization, payment infrastructure, regulation and cross-border distribution while the company is still young enough to adapt.

Founders love to talk about resourcefulness, but the concept takes on a different meaning in ecosystems where capital, talent and customers are all more difficult to access.

At 500 Global’s Eurasia Demo Day in Tbilisi, I saw companies working to gain attention far beyond their immediate geography. They could not assume that investors would already understand their markets, recognize their universities or trust their local references. They had to communicate the problem, the opportunity and their credibility with unusual precision.

That constraint can produce better companies.

When capital is scarce, founders have less room for performative hiring, unnecessary tools or growth that cannot be tied to customer demand. They learn to test assumptions quickly, sell before everything is perfect and use partnerships to extend their reach.

Founders in major technology hubs should apply the same discipline even when more resources are available. Ask what the company would prioritize if it had half the budget, half the team and half the time. The answer often reveals which initiatives are truly essential.

Scarcity is painful, but it can also create focus. A company that learns to operate efficiently before raising significant capital may be better prepared to survive market corrections, fundraising delays and unexpected competition.

Smaller markets are often discussed only in terms of what they lack. That framing overlooks what they can offer.

The Baltics have developed deep expertise in fintech, digital infrastructure and cross-border business. Georgia sits at the intersection of Europe and Asia and is developing a generation of founders accustomed to navigating multiple markets. Guatemala offers access to Central America, proximity to the United States and entrepreneurs with firsthand knowledge of industries and consumers that remain poorly understood by many global investors.

At Volcano Summit in Guatemala, the value of the ecosystem was not simply that it brought international investors into the country. It created a place where local and regional founders could build relationships without first relocating to San Francisco, Miami or New York.

Founders should resist the urge to erase where they come from in an attempt to appear more investable. Geography can be a source of differentiated insight.

A founder who understands regional payment behavior, informal commerce, remittances or local regulation may see an opportunity long before an outsider does. The goal is not to build a business that only works locally. It is to turn local knowledge into an advantage that can travel.

The most compelling question is not, “Why would a major company come from this market?” It is, “What can a founder see from this market that others cannot?”

In mature startup hubs, founders can mistake proximity for access. They may attend dozens of events, collect hundreds of contacts and still fail to build relationships that materially help the company.

In smaller ecosystems, relationships often function as infrastructure.

One trusted introduction can connect a founder to a customer, investor or market that would otherwise be difficult to reach. Government agencies, accelerators, diaspora networks and local operators frequently play an outsized role in helping companies cross borders.

I saw versions of this at Baltic Fintech Days, through 500 Global’s network in Georgia and at Volcano Summit. These gatherings mattered because they connected local credibility with international reach. They reduced the distance between founders and people who could help them enter new markets.

Founders should approach relationship-building with the same discipline they apply to product development. Instead of asking how many people they met, they should ask whether they are building trust with the right customers, partners and advocates.

That also means becoming useful before asking for something. Founders can share market intelligence, make introductions and offer thoughtful feedback. In smaller ecosystems, reputations travel quickly. Generosity and reliability compound.

A founder in a well-known ecosystem benefits from familiar signals. Investors recognize the schools, employers, accelerators and other funds around the table. Founders from less visible markets must often establish credibility without those shortcuts.

That makes storytelling a core business capability.

The strongest founders I encountered did not simply explain what their products did. They connected a local problem to a much larger shift. A payments challenge became a story about global commerce. A compliance product became infrastructure for the international workforce. A regional marketplace became evidence of changing consumer behavior across emerging economies.

Founders should not expect investors or customers to do this translation themselves.

A strong pitch should answer four questions: Why does this problem matter? Why is the opportunity larger than it first appears? Why is this team uniquely equipped to solve it? And why is now the right moment?

This is especially important for founders building outside established technology centers. Investors may incorrectly interpret an unfamiliar market as a small opportunity. The founder’s job is to distinguish the company’s starting point from its potential destination.

Talent is distributed far more evenly than capital, attention or access. Some of the world’s most ambitious founders are building in places that receive only a fraction of global venture investment.

The Baltics, Georgia and Guatemala are not interchangeable, nor should they be reduced to a single category of “emerging markets.” Each has its own strengths, challenges and history. What connects them is the resourcefulness required to build beyond the limits of a small domestic ecosystem.

Founders everywhere can learn from that mindset.

Start globally. Use constraints to sharpen your decisions. Turn local knowledge into an advantage. Treat relationships as infrastructure. And make the scale of your ambition impossible to misunderstand.

The next category-defining company may not come from the place investors visit most often. It may come from the market whose founders learned to build without waiting for the world to notice.
