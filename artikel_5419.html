Top Chamber of Commerce official says the midterms weren’t just about prices: They were about 20 years of 2% growth

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Less than two weeks after voters handed Democrats both chambers of Congress in an election defined by frustration over the cost of living and perceived corruption, the U.S. Chamber of Commerce’s top policy official warned a room of executives that Washington might already be misreading the message.

Neil Bradley, the Chamber’s executive vice president, chief policy officer and head of strategic advocacy, said Monday at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum in Detroit that the affordability anger is real. He also argued it sits on top of something voters have been signaling for two decades that “no one has been paying attention to”: 20 years of roughly 2% real GDP growth, after 60 years of more than 3%.

That gap is the difference between a kid who grows up richer than their parents and one who doesn’t. Harvard economist Raj Chetty and his co-authors found in a 2017 paper that the share of Americans earning more than their parents fell from about 90% for children born in 1940 to 50% for children born in the 1980s.

“The concept of the American dream isn’t 3% growth, but it’s highly correlated to it,” Bradley said.

Bradley’s real target was what Congress does next. “There’s a real temptation to hit the easy button,” he said, like capping credit card interest rates, price controls on pharmaceuticals, or limits on stock buybacks and dividends. These are “proposals that both ends of the political horseshoes are coming together on” and they might ease voters’ angst “for a brief period of time,” he said, “but they won’t solve our long-term challenges.”

The horseshoe part is accurate. President Trump called for a one-year, 10% cap on credit card rates in January, and Sens. Bernie Sanders and Josh Hawley have a bill to do it. The Senate’s version of this year’s defense bill carries new restrictions on buybacks and dividends by defense contractors.

The list also lines up with the Chamber’s membership. Card issuers, drugmakers and any public company with a buyback program would pay for each of those buttons. Bradley’s alternative is growth: expand supply, expand trade, and expand the labor force “through legal immigration reform.”

Bradley’s numbers check out against the government’s. Real GDP grew at a compound rate of about 3.5% a year from 1950 to 2005 and about 2% a year from 2005 to 2025, according to Bureau of Economic Analysis data compiled by the St. Louis Fed. At 3%, an economy doubles in about 23 years, roughly the time from birth to a first job. At 2%, it takes about 35.

His election count holds, too. In the 11 national elections since 2006, voters took control of the House, the Senate or the White House away from the party holding it every time except 2012.

Chetty’s research complicates Bradley’s argument when it comes to the remedy. The paper found that “increasing GDP growth rates alone cannot restore absolute mobility” to the levels of the 1940s cohort. Shifting the distribution of growth back to the more equal pattern that cohort saw “would reverse more than 70% of the decline.” Bradley acknowledged this point, too: “Of course, a doubling in pie doesn’t guarantee that anyone’s individual slice will be bigger.”

Bradley’s case for optimism was what he called “a little-known record”: 5.68 million new business starts last year. “No one’s an entrepreneur if they’re pessimistic about the future,” he said.

Census tracks those as business applications, meaning requests to the IRS for an employer ID number, not businesses that opened. Summed from the bureau’s monthly data, applications came to about 5.67 million in 2025 before seasonal adjustment, the most for any full year since the series began in 2004.

The applications that Census flags as likely to become businesses with payroll tell a different story. Those totaled about 1.69 million in 2025 on a seasonally adjusted basis, down from 1.86 million in 2023 and lower for a second straight year. More Americans are filing to start something, and fewer of the filings look like future employers.

About nine in 10 voters said the cost of living was a crucial or major factor in their vote for Congress, according to The AP Voter Poll.

The new Congress is sworn in in January. The Congressional Budget Office’s January outlook has real GDP growing 1.8% a year in 2027 and 2028.

Nick Lichtenberg is business editor and was formerly Fortune's executive editor of global news.
