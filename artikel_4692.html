The Ability to Imagine Peace

Posted November 8, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

The act of dreaming places us squarely in the realm of the unexpected. Falling asleep, we leave a familiar world of work and family to enter a landscape of the unknown. Not only are the settings of dreams unexpected, but in our dreams we often act in ways that come as a complete surprise upon waking. Asleep, we could be playing pickleball with our second-grade teacher, or hopping onstage to jam with a jazz band at a huge stadium.

In waking life, we may have famously frozen back in elementary school when our teacher asked us a question, and we may never have picked up an instrument to play in a band. The feelings in dreams of hitting the ball back this time, however, and the courage to be seen onstage and improvise with a group, are real (Kahn, 2013, Tuan, 2001). This embodied component to dreaming is one way that dreams help us to evolve, expanding our imagination for what is possible. As the “I” of the dream, we have a physical experience that grows our sense of self, enabling us to bring these new awarenesses into waking time (Kahn, 2013).

The expanded imagination that dreams offer extends beyond feelings to that of exploring different outcomes. Being able to respond instead of freezing creates a back-and-forth dialogue; spontaneously leaping onstage, instead of sitting on the sidelines, shows us we have a special sound to share with others.

In dreams we get to experience new ways of approaching current challenges without having to pay a real-life price. We “try out” different scenarios and “run the numbers” on what might work best in our conscious experiences. However, because these dream experiments elicit real feelings, we change in the process—our internal database gets updated, which shifts our waking perspective (Hobson 2014, Thompson, 2025).

Imagining new feelings and scenarios are important components to solving challenges, even if that challenge is a conflict with another person. Once we imagine a new possibility, we can make it tangible to transform our waking time.

I once dreamed I was having a deeply connected, loving conversation with a friend I was fighting with in waking life. Sitting shoulder to shoulder, smiling contentedly at each other, in the dream we not only were talking, but I reached over and took his hand.

Waking up from this dream was a shock. Prior to the dream, I couldn't imagine not being mad at what seemed to be now an ex-friend! But the intense feelings of connection in the dream reminded me that we once were very good friends. Despite the current disagreement, deep down was a real bond that was still present. I realized it was possible to experience these feelings again, and the dream even showed me what needed to happen next.

In the dream, I had reached across the gap to take my friend’s hand. I understood upon waking that each of us had retreated to a silent corner and one of us had to make the first move toward reconciliation. I decided that could be me. I acted on the dream, set up a meeting, and I remained open in our discussion instead of retreating again. We resolved our differences, mostly because I now felt and saw that we could.

Conflicts with others often seem impossible to resolve. They follow a certain pattern that creates a structure, like the gap between me and my friend that the dream brought into focus. This structure lives as an inner image. Once we see it, we can solve it.

A client recently recalled a dream of a seemingly simple lunch with her mother. Seated at a comically small table, the mother makes a comment about the dreamer’s behavior. Suddenly the lunch felt dangerous, and suspicious men began to circle around the table. With increasing fear, she feels like there is no way to break the circle and get out.

With this dream, the construct of the relationship was clear; what wasn’t obvious was how to get out of it. In response, I suggested a waking dream exercise, having her close her eyes, return to the dream, and find a way out. She immediately sees a person pick her up, at which point the entire circle breaks up, and suddenly she is free. She reported: “I had no idea such an ingrained structure could so easily dissolve.”

Experiencing how easily dug-in structures can shift, even in dreamtime, gives us the courage to try out other solutions in waking life. After all, nothing changes until our minds change first.

Dreams can play a significant role in problem solving for our waking lives by opening our imagination to new possibilities. But what about taking that approach to a more global level? Can dreams help us with the challenges we face as a larger community?Consider a recent New York Times podcast interview of Mirjana Spoljaric Egger, president of the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC). Ms. Egger’s job is to monitor world conflicts, usually in person, and to help negotiate an end to these catastrophic problems. In the interview, she repeatedly states that she believes humans fundamentally do not want conflict. While the interviewer pressed her to comment about the state of the world, implying that the situation overall was perhaps quite dire and maybe even hopeless, Ms. Egger simply and clearly responded by suggesting we start by imagining peace. How often do we approach a disagreement, or think about our communities or world, in terms of peaceful outcomes? Whether personally or professionally, most of us default to a worst-case scenario. And yet, what would happen if we all began to see the possibility of creating peace in the world? How would that change our actions and the way we approach situations? How would that transform our attitudes about other people and ourselves? And most importantly, how would these changes shift outcomes?

Being able to imagine peace is often the first step to making peace. One of the tools for developing that imagination is dreaming. Dreaming reminds us of possibilities, helping us to experience feelings of connection and wholeness so that we remember what is possible and have the blueprint for making it tangible. Dreaming and the imagination are the cornerstones of change.
