Anxiety, ADHD, or Both?

If your mind never seems to shut off, it’s easy to assume you have anxiety—but that may not be the full story. Those racing thoughts might be better explained by the mental hyperactivity of ADHD, the worry that comes with anxiety, or both. While the distinction may not feel useful when you’re overwhelmed and just need relief, it can make a difference in how you find that relief. That’s because if you’re trying to manage your anxiety and still struggling to keep up, understanding what’s behind those experiences matters—especially for women whose ADHD has gone unrecognized while they’ve been labeled anxious, scattered, or “too emotional.”

For some women, the path to an anxiety diagnosis begins with the strain of managing unrecognized ADHD. Difficulties with organization, planning, remembering, and following through often create repeated experiences of falling behind. Over time, anticipating those struggles can bring worry about what might be forgotten or how everyone’s expectations will be met.

By the time a woman seeks support, worry, restlessness, irritability, and sleep disruption may be her most pressing concerns. It makes sense that anxiety becomes the focus. But without exploring what came before it, the difficulties contributing to that anxiety may remain unexplored:

Unrecognized ADHD → chronic overwhelm → persistent activation and worry → anxiety identified while ADHD remains overlooked

The anxiety is real, but does it tell the whole story?

With ADHD, mental restlessness can involve moving quickly between ideas, distractions, and things you’re trying to remember. When keeping up takes considerable effort, that mental activity may become urgent: sorting through competing thoughts, remembering what needs to happen, or finishing something before attention shifts. It can feel overwhelming without necessarily beginning with fear.

Anxiety is more focused on fear, worry, or anticipating a negative outcome. With generalized anxiety, concerns can extend across many areas of life. Anxiety may show up mentally through persistent worry and physically through tension, a racing heart, or difficulty settling.

ADHD thoughts may shift rapidly between topics, while anxious thoughts often return to the same concern or feared outcome. These patterns offer clues rather than firm dividing lines. ADHD-related difficulties can give someone plenty to worry about, while anxiety can make attention and organization harder. The two also commonly co-occur. In one U.S. population study, nearly half of adults age 18-44 with ADHD also met criteria for an anxiety or related disorder in the previous year.

One question worth asking is, “Does worry help me function?” This may seem odd when the goal is to feel less anxious. But for some women with ADHD, worry has become a way of staying on top of demands. Mentally rehearsing what needs to happen and anticipating what might be forgotten can feel protective. Relaxing, meanwhile, can feel risky, as though something might fall through the cracks.

Anxiety can become a form of scaffolding, helping to hold daily life together when other supports are missing. Internalized pressure may help initiate a task, while repeatedly checking work can become a way to catch missed details. Over time, it can be difficult to imagine functioning without that constant vigilance.

This doesn’t mean anxiety is necessary for managing ADHD. It invites a closer look at what worry has been helping accomplish—and what could replace it.

Overcompensation is one reason women with ADHD may be overlooked, as appearing “normal” can hide the effort and stress involved. Some may arrive everywhere early to compensate for time blindness—difficulty sensing how much time has passed or estimating how long things will take—while sacrificing time for their own needs.

Societal expectations to be productive at work, attentive at home, and responsible for everyone else’s needs add pressure. Those expectations also come with real work. The Pew Research Center found that in U.S. opposite-sex marriages where spouses earn roughly the same, wives spend approximately 4.6 hours weekly on housework compared with husbands’ 1.9 hours, while husbands have 25.2 hours of leisure time compared with wives’ 21.6. This reflects the “second shift”: unpaid household and caregiving work alongside paid employment.

For women with ADHD, those additional responsibilities mean more planning, organizing, and remembering, with less time to recover. Understanding that anxiety means considering both executive functioning difficulties and how much a woman is expected to manage.

If certain weeks feel harder than others, hormonal changes may contribute, particularly during the luteal phase, after ovulation and before the next menstrual period. Estrogen fluctuates during this phase and declines before menstruation. Emerging research links these fluctuations with changes in ADHD symptoms. This may partly reflect estrogen’s role in modulating dopamine signaling: As estrogen drops, changes in dopamine activity may affect attention and emotion regulation.

Greater difficulty focusing can increase anxiety about keeping up. Tracking attention, anxiety, sleep, and mood alongside the menstrual cycle can help identify patterns and guide treatment conversations. Hormonal changes can also make symptoms harder to navigate during puberty, postpartum, perimenopause, and menopause. A clinician familiar with hormonal influences on ADHD can help tailor treatment, including considering cycle-based dosing—adjusting ADHD medication around the menstrual cycle—or other individualized medication adjustments during hormonal transitions, when appropriate.

If worry has been helping with remembering, preparing, or following through, it makes sense that letting go feels difficult—even if relying on it is not sustainable long-term.

Women-Centered ADHD Treatment (W-CAT) considers both underlying ADHD needs and how women have adapted to keep up. Treatment planning includes considering medication with a prescribing provider alongside recommended W-CAT skills: cognitive offloading—putting reminders and plans outside the mind—sharing responsibilities, and breaking tasks into manageable steps. These are paired with support for regulation, building self-compassion, and addressing distressing beliefs.

Some anxiety may ease as ADHD is better supported, while some may need its own treatment. Before expecting yourself to let go of worry, consider how much you’ve been asking it to hold—and what support you deserve in its place.
