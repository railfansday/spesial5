The 2027 BMW 740 xDrive Builds on Excellence

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Revised design and an updated interior sharpen the case for BMW’s biggest executive sedan.

BMW’s 7-series has been in a race with the Mercedes-Benz S-class for nearly 50 years over the German executive sedan crown. But an avant-garde, new-generation reimagining in 2022 knocked the long-wheelbase sedan off its pedestal. Four years later, BMW has tweaked the 7-series’s front and rear design and upgraded the interior with better technology. Also, it’s lost the “i” from the end of its badge, as part of BMW’s new policy on model naming. Can a shorter name, a prettier face, and new screens push it back into contention?

The 2027 BMW 740 xDrive’s most significant change is apparent at first glance. Horizontal grille slats inside the sizable kidney grille replace the previous vertical ones. Split headlights stick around, with a new vertical, indented integration below the daytime running lights. Extended LED taillights are also new for 2027. Inside, the BMW 740 receives much of the same treatment that’s been given to the Neue Klasse EV models, including a new four-spoke steering wheel, a snappier operating system, and a sleek panoramic display.

Engine: turbocharged and mild-hybridized 2998-cc inline-six

Transmission: 8-speed automatic, all-wheel drive

In the hills above Lake Elsinore, the 740 xDrive showed off just how magically BMW’s adaptive chassis control system is, especially in its more able Professional guise. This uses full air suspension and active roll stabilization through an electrical system that applies torque against the sway bars to counter lean under cornering loads. So equipped, the 7-series rewarded a spirited pace on California canyon roads, despite a 4800-pound curb weight sitting atop a 126.6-inch wheelbase.

Front-end turn-in was sharp, and the minuscule amount of body roll effectively hid the magnitude of weight swinging around. Like many recent BMWs, the steering was nicely weighted but largely mute, forcing dynamic awareness to come from the base of the seat instead of through my fingers. While plentiful electronically ordered handling adjustments were no doubt happening in real time, these intrusions felt imperceptible behind the wheel.

But the 740 isn’t a one-trick pony, either. Out on the highway, the 7-series serves a different customer. The same roll stabilization technology that keeps it planted in the canyons can decouple the roll bars for straight-line driving, reducing unwanted movements in the cabin and allowing the air suspension to soften even more. The result was a ride that felt effortlessly smooth over freeway joints but never turned wallowing. Luxury vehicles shouldn’t translate bumps and dips inside the cabin, and the 740 expertly balances stability at the wheel with a supple experience in the rear seat. Cruising at California highway speeds was where the 7-series felt truly at home.

Similarly, the turbocharged and electrified 3.0-liter inline-six engine is powerful and smooth, but certainly not shouty. With 394 hp, the 740 has the same power as a base-model X5, but it didn’t feel lacking at the helm. The 398-lb-ft torque peak comes in at 1850 rpm, which pushed the big sedan along gracefully without apparent effort. But winding the engine to the 5200 rpm at which peak power arrives elicited a pleasant, if speaker-amplified, song—although it felt largely unnecessary given the muscle of the torque curve.

Paired with the ever-amicable ZF eight-speed automatic transmission, the powertrain of the 740 felt like a means to an end rather than a especial highlight, but that’s how most buyers of large luxury sedans will expect it to be. Sure, you can paddle-shift through the gears and enjoy the gearbox’s satisfyingly snappy responses, but how many owners are likely to regularly do so?

BMW is proud of its hands-free Highway Assistant semiautonomous driving system, and so I put this to the test on I-5 South. The SAE Level 2 system can drive hands-free up to 85 mph as long as you keep a forward gaze, and it did so well. From slowing for construction zones to autonomous lane changes, the semiautonomous system handled busy California traffic smartly and smoothly.

My only real complaint with the updated 7-series was its soft brake pedal. Even with the sizable standard rotors, the soft pedal tuning made the heavy sedan feel underbraked, requiring more force than I was expecting to need to apply for a quick stop. Plus, like most of the R&T team who have encountered it, I don’t like the new four-spoke steering wheel with its strange disconnected spokes and awkward haptic touch controls.

With a mild facelift and the same powertrains as before, the 2027 7-series’s most noticeable upgrades are all inside. BMW uses many buzzwords and acronyms to describe the new interior experience, but the most important one is the Panoramic iDrive system. As in the X5, iX3, i3, and new 3-series, the Neue Klasse direction has removed the old physical iDrive controller and replaced it with a fully touchscreen system. The 7-series now has a 17.9-inch central screen, a recessed 14.6-inch passenger display, and the long panoramic gauge cluster that sits atop the dashboard.

In practice, the new user experience was a mixed bag. I’m quick to criticize any vehicle that lacks tactile controls, and the glamour of BMW’s new system doesn’t belie this fault. Save for a scrolling volume control near the nub shifter, everything from climate control to vehicle settings must be manipulated through the central touchscreen. The screen responds quickly and has a permanent menu with obvious icons for climate control, vehicle settings, navigation, and media inputs. However, tapping on any of these icons once is only the start of a multistep process, much of which will be done with eyes off the road.

To adjust the suspension or ride height on the 740, I had to click through four menus before the change was complete. Even the seat’s lumbar support has to be adjusted through the touchscreen. That’s a bridge too far in my book.

Despite these annoyances, BMW’s new system mostly excels at providing driving information with the projected gauge cluster. Illuminated on a span of masked glass across the dashboard, this instrument display was perfectly placed and kept my field of vision clear. BMW’s projection direction is smartly tuned, with speed placed directly in front of the driver and navigation to the right. Interesting but superfluous information like elevation is projected nearer to the passenger side. My only real complaint was a hard-to-decipher tachometer.

My Frozen Purple 740 xDrive was well appointed with white Merino leather seats that were heated, cooled, and massaging. The seating experience was unimpeachably comfortable except for the loud fan noise while using the ventilation function. Seating adjustment is now done through door-mounted buttons, allowing BMW to make the seats even wider. The equipped Dolby Atmos system was predictably excellent, and interior sound levels were nearly silent even without active tunes.

As brands like Audi and Lexus move away from the ultraluxury sedan market, BMW’s 7-series is left with a single natural competitor—the Mercedes-Benz S-class. But the fight isn’t exactly equal, either. The 740 starts at $101,350 in a rear-wheel-drive configuration, while the most affordable S-class starts at $114,100. Mercedes offers more horsepower and torque for the money, but the luxury-sedan buyer is unlikely to be motivated exclusively by performance.

Of course, if they are, then the 740 sits at the base of the range, with both the hybrid 750e and twin-turbo V-8 M760 positioned above it. Plus, of course, the continued novelty of the fully electric i7 EV, which shares the same bodywork.

Far be it from me to pretend reviewing a BMW 7-series is hard work, but the massaging seat feature certainly helped me calm down after hours of driving. BMW offers a variety of intensity settings and location-focused massages, meaning you can tailor the experience to where you need it most. It isn’t too hard or unnatural-feeling, and it still provides genuine relief.
