The Nasdaq-100 Is Down More Than 10% From Its High, but the Broad Market Is Down Far Less. Here's How Gaps Like This Have Ended Since 1986.

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Twelve past gaps like this one go back to 1986. Did the broad market usually follow tech down?

The broad market, as measured by the S&P 500 (^GSPC -1.03%), has held up much better than tech stocks this fall. The index closed at 7,561.67 on Friday, Nov. 13, just 3% or so under its Oct. 6 record of 7,818.93.

The Nasdaq-100, which leans heavily on big tech and growth stocks, reached its own high close of 31,224.47 the same day. But by Nov. 13, it had fallen to 27,943.74, around 10.5% under the peak.

A gap like this doesn't come along very often. Digging through both indexes' daily closes, I counted 12 past cases since the mid-1980s, plus one in July this year.

But when tech stocks drop this far on their own, does the broad market usually follow them down?

I used Yahoo Finance's daily closes, which don't include dividends, and took each index's best close in the prior 252 trading sessions (around a year) as its high.

A day counted if the Nasdaq-100 closed 10% or more below that level and the S&P 500 closed at most 4% under its own. And so a long slide wouldn't count again and again, a new case needed to start at least six months after the last qualifying day of the one before.

The Nasdaq-100's daily history on Yahoo begins in October 1985, and the first case turned up around a year later, on Oct. 30, 1986.

Most of the others came before 2001, with two in 1997. Three more followed in 2004, 2005, and 2006. After that, the pattern didn't return until March 2021.

This year's case started on July 29. Because the current gap opened on Nov. 2, only about three months later, it doesn't count as a new case by my six-month rule.

Three months after each of the 12 past cases began, the S&P 500 was higher in 11 of them, and the median gain was around 7%. A year later, the index was up again in 11 of the 12, and the median gain came to about 11%. Across every trading day since late 1986, for comparison, it finished the next three months higher around 70% of the time, typically rising about 3%. And it ended the next year higher about 79% of the time, with gains of about 12% at the median.

In other words, the three months after those gaps went better than normal. But the following year looked a lot like an ordinary one.

Most of those gaps closed because the Nasdaq-100 recovered, not because the broader market caught up. In 11 of the 12 cases, it hit a new one-year high within 12 months, after a median wait of 81 days. The July case went the same way, with the Nasdaq-100 setting a new high 55 days later.

Of course, the broad market didn't always stay calm. In six of the 12 cases, the S&P 500 experienced a 10% or greater decline at one point in the following year, including the 1987 crash. Still, it finished the year up in five of the six.

The only case that went badly began on April 3, 2000, a week after the Nasdaq-100's March 27 record close.

Within two weeks, the S&P 500 was more than 10% below its peak. It was down about 2% three months later and roughly 27% a year later. The Nasdaq-100 fell about 66% in that year, and it didn't close above its March 2000 peak again until November 2015.

What set 2000 apart was the run before it. In the previous 252 sessions, the Nasdaq-100 had climbed about 84%, versus about 14% for the S&P 500. As of Nov. 13, both indexes were up about 10% over the last 252 sessions.

Sure, a strong run alone didn't decide things. The Nasdaq-100 had gained about 67% in the year before the February 1999 case, and the S&P 500 still rose about 9% over the next 12 months. But I think today's gap looks much more like the ordinary cases than 2000.

For buy-and-hold investors in an S&P 500 index fund like the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO -1.04%), which closed near $693 on Nov. 13, the history arguably weighs against seeing the gap as a warning. Tech stocks might keep dropping for a while, and this gap may take months to close. But in most of these cases, the broad market didn't follow them down.

I'd consider adding to a fund like VOO now, maybe little by little. Still, 12 cases isn't many, and one was 2000.
