Kick LSU out or admit the SEC is powerless. There’s no middle ground | Opinion

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Let's begin with the only thing that matters. This isn't saber rattling from the SEC.

Either LSU is expelled once the SEC gains legal cover from a federal court, or the SEC doesn't have a conference. It has 16 satellite programs eventually — and more than likely, quickly — doing whatever they please.

That's an existential threat to the health and welfare of the biggest, strongest and most successful conference in college sports.

It's no longer a petty game of egos. No longer a circus created by LSU football coach Lane Kiffin and basketball coach Will Wade, and everyone in LSU's orbit getting sucked into the villainous vortex and Tiger Walking all the way to wildly underachieving seasons.

It's not SEC commissioner Greg Sankey trying to save his job or save face. It's not a legal bluff.

The SEC can't twice threaten to expel LSU from the conference, and not follow through with it. Can't keep moving the red line until LSU finally says no more, and capitulates.

This is happening. If a federal court gives the SEC the legal cover to expel a charter member of the strongest conference in college sports, there's no turning back when the SEC presidents meet Dec. 3 to determine LSU's fate.

I don't need to remind the SEC presidents what happened when the Big 12 could no longer control Texas. The university worked a $300 million deal with ESPN in 2011 for the Longhorn Network, a divisive move that eventually led to the near implosion of the Big 12.

It took all of nine months for Texas A&M to leave for the SEC, and two more months for Missouri to follow. A decade later, Texas took Oklahoma with it to the SEC — and left the Big 12 to add from the American Conference to stay afloat.

The SEC can't keep lobbing idle threats at LSU, can't think a hefty fine and multi-game suspensions for coaches Kiffin and Wade will make it all go away. This is more than just the SEC vs. LSU.

It's about a defining self-governance model the SEC desperately needs to prevent complete legal anarchy. If schools can find local, provincial judges to do their dirty work for player eligibility, it eventually moves to questioning governance.

LSU wants the ability to do what it wants, when it wants — because some local yokel parish judge thinks the Tigers should be allowed to do X, Y or Z. LSU doesn't think it's a problem that Kiffin and Wade actively pursued former professional players and convinced them to use favorable legal avenues to gain eligibility — despite SEC rules.

Who cares when or how or why the SEC demanded no professional players on rosters. They did, and as soon as they did, that's the end of the story. Period.

The SEC, like the NCAA, isn't a singular force lording over, and imposing its will on, 16 athletic departments. The SEC is an association of 16 universities, whose presidents and chancellors run the conference.

They use Sankey, the commissioner, as a consigliere of sorts. He advises on any number of decisions, he gains consensus of the 16 members, and he goes back to the presidents to explain the details and give his opinion.

After that, it's all up to the presidents and chancellors. They make a decision, and Sankey publicly delivers it as a "conference decision."

And every nob from every corner of the SEC, and from Miami to Seattle and San Diego to Syracuse, thinks it's Sankey running the show.

The presidents and chancellors don't want to feel the public heat, so they pay Sankey $4 million annually to take the arrows. But when a member school tries to embarrass the league, tries to embarrass their consigliere, it's war.

So yeah, it's self-governance and survival. It's doing everything it can to keep the league together, with equal partners, and at the top of the college sports landscape.

If the SEC receives legal cover from a federal court, it has no choice but to expel LSU. Or their hold over the remainder of the league, their desire for an efficient and effective self-governance model, ends with a failed bluff.
