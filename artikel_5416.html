YIMBY groups bare claws in lawsuit over LA bid to stop housing project

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

YIMBY Law and allied housing groups filed a petition in Los Angeles County Superior Court seeking approval of 398 homes under AB 2011. The case challenges the city’s mountain lion habitat finding and cites the Housing Accountability Act.  AI Summary

A mountain lion that may never have set a paw on a golf course is now at the center of a lawsuit against Los Angeles over a proposed housing development.

As expected, housing groups sued the city this week to force approval of 398 homes at Woodland Hills Country Club. The developers invoked Assembly Bill 2011, which allows ministerial approval of housing in commercially zoned areas if the project includes affordable units. City planners blocked it by declaring the course a puma habitat.

Yes In My Back Yard, Sonja Trauss, the California Housing Defense Fund and Californians for Homeownership filed the petition in Los Angeles County Superior Court. Trauss is founder and executive director of YIMBY Law.

They asked a judge to set aside the city’s Aug. 20 determination and order the project approved under AB 2011 and the Housing Accountability Act.

The petition argues the city applied the wrong evidentiary standard to deny the project. It highlights the city’s own habitat report, which shows no documented GPS locations of mountain lions on the property. The petition notes that the city’s ecologist dated the report one day before the denial and the developer never saw it.

“This insanity of mountain lion habitat in an urban area on an active golf course would be the single biggest abuse of state housing law in the history of California,” developer’s attorney Dave Rand wrote on social media. “It won’t stand.”

Rand’s client isn’t part of the lawsuit other than being identified as a real party in interest.

While petitioners seek a court order, Rand has threatened the city with a lawsuit to recover damages. Rand sent the city a letter with a deadline to reverse course or face a lawsuit seeking at least $20 million in fines, attorney’s fees, costs and compensatory damages.

The base fine is $10,000 per unit. AB 712, signed into law last year, raises it to an automatic $50,000 per unit if the city has lost in court at least once in the same housing element cycle.

Rand’s letter also cites an Oct. 23 ruling from the Second Appellate District that a homeowner could seek damages when a city misses a mandatory permit deadline. That case involved an accessory dwelling unit, not AB 2011.

Plaintiffs note that Councilmember and district representative Bob Blumenfield wrote to constituents Dec. 9, 2025, saying that he would pursue public input and environmental review. That was less than three weeks after the application was filed.

The petition alleges he then introduced motions, wrote to state legislators and the governor, and published op-eds, all seeking a way to stop the project. Planning staff, meanwhile, sent letter after letter demanding more studies, producing a prolonged 9-month back-and-forth.

On Aug. 20, the city declared that an operating golf course, in regular use by golfers and staff, is mountain lion habitat. Blumenfield called the determination “Great news!” on the Council District 3 website.

But the state Department of Housing and Community Development countered with a notification that the city is violating state law. The petition says the city has not reversed it.

The department set a Nov. 25 deadline for the city to respond to its letter. It said it could refer the matter to the state attorney general.
