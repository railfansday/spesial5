United Airlines Executive Says Iconic Dulles Mobile Lounges Will Retire, But “Won’t Be Forgotten.” What Comes Next?

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

United Airlines wants Washington Dulles to move beyond its mobile lounges. But when I asked Andrew Nocella whether he actually likes them, his response suggested that retirement may not mean disappearance. Could one become a bar or café? Could a limited transportation role survive? His answer left room for some speculation.

During a press gaggle at Washington Dulles yesterday, I asked United Airlines Executive Vice President and Chief Commercial Officer Andrew Nocella a simple question: does he like the people movers? (it was in the context of their future place at an evolving IAD).

“I love it,” he responded, before explaining:

“The people movers, of course, is part of my history. I can remember, back in the day, coming out to the aircraft through the people mover, so it’s part of my history. But I do understand the need to evolve and change and innovate. And the people movers need to be innovated out. There are better ways to do it. But it will be an important part of the history of Dulles Airport and I can assure you that People Moves, while they will be retired, they won’t be forgotten.”

These vehicles are technically known as mobile lounges, though people movers is a fitting description too and I’ve called them “moon buggies” for years. Whatever you call them, I found that last assurance intriguing, even if we should be careful not to read too much into it.

Nocella did not announce a preservation project or describe a future use. But promising they will not be forgotten invites the question: how might Dulles preserve something so closely associated with the airport while finally moving beyond the need to use it?

My favorite possibility is turning one into a bar or café.

Imagine a retired mobile lounge parked in a suitable location, with its distinctive shape preserved and the interior converted into somewhere to have a drink while watching aircraft. Keep some of the original seating,  add photographs explaining its history, and suddenly an airport frustration becomes an attraction.

I would happily stop for a cappuccino. I suspect plenty of aviation enthusiasts would make a point of visiting too.

There would obviously be practical questions about access, utilities, and where to put it…maybe the wheels would have to be removed. A vehicle designed to transport passengers is not automatically suitable for operating a restaurant. But as a concept, it strikes me as a fun way to preserve a piece of Dulles without requiring anyone to ride it to immigration.

Nostalgia becomes much easier to appreciate when you can choose whether to participate!

Think how cool it would be to take one of these and put it inside the new Polaris Lounge in Concourse E that will be coming in the years ahead.

Another possibility is that the mobile lounges disappear from most journeys but continue in a narrower role.

I wonder, for example, whether there might be a useful way to run passengers between the D and E gates during the airport’s lengthy transition. Could a mobile lounge provide a convenient connection while the rest of the concourse development catches up? It woudl certainly save a lot of walking.

That is a question, not something Nocella proposed and I do realize that keeping an aging fleet operating for a limited purpose could undermine the savings and simplicity that retirement is supposed to deliver.

Still, “retired” can mean different things. Retired from routine passenger service would not necessarily preclude an occasional special ride or some other carefully limited use.

Of course, “won’t be forgotten” may mean something much less elaborate.

A preserved vehicle, a display in the terminal, or a design reference inside a future lounge could honor the mobile lounges without retaining their transportation function.

I would prefer preserving at least one actual vehicle rather than relying exclusively on photographs. These machines are unusual enough that future travelers should have an opportunity to see what generations of Dulles passengers experienced. I still love them and will always love them.

Nocella’s answer was affectionate, but clear about the need for change. He did not offer a retirement date or reveal what remembering the mobile lounges will look like.

But I do hope that assurance becomes something tangible. A mobile lounge café or bar would get my vote, preferably inside the new Polaris Lounge. A useful transitional shuttle or occasional heritage ride could be interesting too and much easier to maintain than a vintage aircraft.

For years, passengers have boarded these vehicles because they had to. It would be wonderful if Dulles eventually gave us a reason to step inside one because we wanted to.

What would you do with a retired Dulles mobile lounge?
