From Premier Inn to Rick Stein, Britain’s Travel Businesses Are Paying for Higher Labor Costs

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

The UK's 2025 increases in minimum wage, payroll tax and property tax were meant to fall on big employers. Company filings now landing show they hit hospitality and travel businesses of every size, from Premier Inn's owner to a celebrity chef's seaside restaurants and a regional travel agency.

"The 12 months to 28 December 2025 were exceptionally challenging for the UK hospitality sector," the directors of The Seafood Restaurant (Padstow) Ltd wrote in accounts filed this week with Companies House, the UK's company register. Rising labor and property costs, which they called "government forced increases," cost the group "in excess of £600,000 [$800,000] in 2025."

The group, which runs restaurants, hotel rooms, a cookery school and shops, reported a pre-tax loss of £814,000 ($1.1m). That was almost double the year before, on flat revenue of £18.9 million ($25.1 million). Revenue is down 17% since 2022. UK company filings show that three policy changes that took effect in April 2025 — a higher minimum wage, a higher employer payroll tax applied to more of each worker's pay, and a cut in the pandemic-era property-tax discount for hospitality — are pushing hospitality and travel businesses into losses. Rick Stein's The Seafood Restaurant (Padstow) Ltd reported a pre-tax loss of £814,000 ($1.1 million) on flat revenue of £18.9 million ($25.1 million), with the directors attributing more than £600,000 ($800,000) of cost to what they called "government forced increases"; sister company Stein's Trading saw its loss nearly triple. The same pattern appears at scale and at the small end: Whitbread said higher UK employment costs and property taxes added £160 million ($213 million), equal to a third of its previous year's pre-tax profit, and it is closing all its branded restaurants and cutting 3,800 jobs; travel agency chain Dawson & Sanderson swung to a £567,000 ($754,000) loss with a payroll tax bill up 30% despite employing 15 fewer people; and the Grand Hotel in Eastbourne swung to a loss as competition pushed room rates down. Hotels face a further squeeze after being left out of two rounds of property-tax relief given to pubs and music venues, with taxable values up an average of 76% in this year's England and Wales revaluation, according to Knight Frank. Demand has softened in places but has not collapsed — the common thread is that costs are rising faster than these businesses can raise prices without losing customers.
