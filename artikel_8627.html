Beware, A Disability Can Force You Into Early Retirement

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Earlier this year, I was sick for two weeks. Unfortunately, I’ve had asthma since I was a kid. So when a respiratory illness hits, I tend to cough and suffer longer than the average person.

During COVID, my asthma flared up more often, likely due to the stress of having a baby during a pandemic.

It’s the type of breathing where you’re annoyingly conscious of every breath. You can never quite fill your lungs to 100%. In my case, it felt more like 75%.

On Wednesday, August 12, still sick, I decided to keep a previously scheduled consulting call with an FS reader. I had booked it assuming I’d be better by then. I wasn’t.

By the end of our 1 hour, 15 minute session, where I spent about 80% of the time talking, I was wiped out. My energy had dropped to maybe 30%. When you’re talking nonstop, you’re not breathing deeply or regularly. I felt like I was slowly suffocating while discussing their asset allocation.

The call reminded me of the many days I felt physically drained talking to clients during my 13 years in finance. As my responsibilities and stress grew, I eventually hit a breaking point. I’d rather earn nothing and be free and healthy than keep grinding myself down.

Just the other day, I couldn’t bend down to pick something up without a sharp pain shooting through my spine. I almost collapsed.

The last time I felt pain this bad was in 2023. I had spent an hour sitting on the ground signing copies of Buy This Not That for readers who donated to victims of the Maui fires. My dad is from Hawaii, and seeing thousands of lives upended overnight weighed on me. The Financial Samurai community ended up donating about $48,000. But when the signing was over, I couldn’t stand up and I called my wife for help.

My theory is that these episodes are triggered by a combination of prolonged stress and me forgetting I’m not 25 anymore.

This time, I clearly forgot. On Sunday, I played two hours of 4.5-level pickleball, then taught my kids for another hour. On Monday, I played 1.5 hours of intense tennis with plenty of serving. Then on Tuesday, several dads in our dad bod challenge shared their gym Personal Records, so naturally I went for personal records too. Instead of my usual three sets of 10-12 reps, I did five sets with progressive loading.

Wednesday morning, my back was already grumbling. So of course I went on a hilly 30-minute walk at 7:05am before breakfast and the school drop off. Walking downhill to school, the pain returned. By the time I got home, I could no longer walk normally.

If I still had a day job, there’s no way I could have gone into the office. And if my asthma flared up at the same time, I couldn’t speak to clients for more than an hour or two a day.

Not being able to provide for my family would be a terrible thing.

These two episodes forced me to confront a few uncomfortable truths:

Some people picture disability as a dramatic car accident or a fall off a ladder. Reality is far more boring. Accidental injuries make up only about 9% to 12% of long-term disability claims, while musculoskeletal problems like back and joint pain are the leading cause, at 26% to 33% of claims.

According to the Social Security Administration (SSA), roughly one in four 20-year-olds will be out of work for at least a year because of a disabling condition before reaching normal retirement age. That’s one quarter of all 20-year-olds, not just those in high-risk occupations.

In other words, the thing that takes you out of the workforce probably won’t be a skydiving mishap. It’ll be your back after one too many weightlifting attempts.

Before getting into what to do, it’s worth understanding the two main types of disability coverage. They work very differently.

Short-term disability (STD) covers you for a temporary inability to work, like recovering from surgery, a bad back, or a serious illness. Benefits typically replace around 50% to 70% of your income and last anywhere from a few weeks to about six months. The waiting period, called the elimination period, is usually short, often one to two weeks.

Many employers provide short-term disability as a benefit. A handful of states also run their own programs, including California (SDI), New York, New Jersey, Rhode Island, and Hawaii (TDI). If you’re self-employed, you probably have nothing unless you bought it yourself.

Long-term disability (LTD) kicks in when a disability lasts longer, usually after a 90-day elimination period. It typically replaces about 60% of your income and can pay for a set number of years or until age 65. This is the coverage that actually protects your family’s financial future. Short-term disability is a speed bump. Long-term disability is a car crash.

And no, don’t count on Social Security Disability Insurance to save you. SSDI is hard to qualify for, has a five-month waiting period, and the monthly benefit is modest. It’s a floor, not a plan.

If you have a job with benefits, log into your HR portal today and look at your LTD coverage. Many group policies cap monthly benefits, which means high earners may only be replacing a fraction of their income.

If there’s a gap, buy a supplemental individual policy, ideally with an own-occupation rider. It’s portable, so it stays with you if you leave your job. If you’re self-employed like me, individual LTD is the only coverage you’ll have. Get quotes while you’re young and healthy, because pre-existing conditions get excluded or priced up later.

Even with great coverage, there’s a gap. Short-term disability might make you wait a week or two, and long-term disability usually makes you wait 90 days. Meanwhile, your mortgage, tuition, and Costco runs don’t pause.

Keep at least six months of living expenses in cash or Treasury bills. If you have kids, a single income household, or are self-employed, aim for 12 months. If you’re wondering where the line is between prudent and paranoid, here’s my take on how much savings is too much. The emergency fund is your bridge from healthy to covered.

A single income stream is a single point of failure. If that engine stalls, the whole plane goes down. Your goal is to build a jet with at least four engines, so you can lose one and still land safely.

The best time to start a side hustle is while you still have a steady paycheck and good health. You get to experiment without pressure, and if it fails, you still eat.

I started Financial Samurai in July 2009 while working full-time in finance. I’d wake up early and write before work, then publish again on weekends. For three years, it made very little money. But by the time I left Corporate America in 2012, the site had become a real second engine alongside about $80,000 a year in passive investment income.

Without that second engine, I never would have had the courage to negotiate my way out.

Here’s the key for disability planning: prioritize side hustles that don’t require your body to show up. Driving for Uber or coaching tennis stops the moment your back gives out. Writing, a blog, a YouTube channel, an online course, or a self-published ebook can keep paying while you’re flat on your back.

If you need ideas, my wife put together a list of the 20 best side hustle ideas to make extra money. If you want to go the online route, here’s how to start a blog and build your own platform. I’m biased, but owning your own corner of the internet is the gift that keeps on giving.

One engine is a job. Two engines is a cushion. Four or more engines is freedom.

Your peak earning years are also, conveniently, your peak health years. Don’t assume your income will keep climbing forever. Assume it could drop to zero tomorrow, then save as if that’s true.

I saved over 50% of my after-tax income starting with my first job out of college because I knew I couldn’t last a lifetime working 70+ hours a week. I didn’t know my lungs and back would eventually join the protest, but the logic held up.

Front-load your savings. Max out your 401(k) and IRA, and invest the excess in a taxable brokerage account. Pay down high fixed costs where you can.

The lower your required monthly expenses, the less income replacement you need if disaster strikes. A paid-off house is one of the best disability insurance policy nobody talks about.

Your ability to earn over the next 20 to 30 years is likely worth millions. Yet we manage our portfolios more carefully than our bodies.

Get annual checkups. But lock down a life insurance policy before you see a doctor for something more. Manage your stress before it manages you. Warm up, cool down, and stop trying to beat the other dads’ PRs after a weekend of nonstop racquet sports. Ask me how I know.

While you’re at it, get your paperwork in order. A will, a durable power of attorney, and a healthcare directive are the basics. But if anyone depends on your income, term life insurance is the most important piece. A disability takes away your ability to earn for a while. Death takes it away for good.

The irony is that the health problems that make you worry about disability also make life insurance more expensive, or harder to get. Lock in coverage while you’re still healthy enough to qualify. I’d compare term life quotes on Policygenius, which lets you see prices from multiple top insurers in one place.

The scary thing about disability is that it rarely announces itself. One day you’re serving aces and going for gym PRs. The next, you can’t walk your kids to school.

Being unable to breathe normally or walk for a few days made me realize how fragile everything is. If you’re the provider for your family, that fragility is your responsibility to plan for.

So check your disability coverage this week. Build your cash buffer. Start a side hustle that doesn’t depend on your body showing up. And for goodness sake, stretch.

Sam, this article really hit home for me. At age 55, I began experiencing symptoms of an extremely rare disease. Fortunately, the stroke spared my speech, cognition, and memory, but permanently impaired my left-side motor control and sensation. Even with insurance, some rehabilitation required appeals or out-of-pocket payments. My stroke also forced me to sell my interest in our development business to my longtime partner. Although we had planned for a buyout in the event of death, disability was a different matter, and we ultimately negotiated an amicable agreement. I was fortunate to have accumulated substantial savings and investments before becoming disabled. Equally important, I was blessed with supportive family and friends, including a sister who’s a professional financial advisor, a physician brother-in-law, a daughter who’s an ER nurse, and a son who’s a mental health therapist. Unable to return to work, I focused more on managing our investments. With my sister’s guidance, we maintained a buy-and-hold approach through major market downturns. I’m a testament to time in the market, not timing the market, and the power of compounding. Our net worth has more than tripled since before the stroke. Meanwhile, rehabilitation remains a lifelong project. Now 71, I recently returned to my longtime neurological physical therapist after a three-year break. I’m still looking for ways to improve my independence, mobility, and quality of life. Forced retirement at 57 certainly didn’t mean the end of a productive and purposeful life. Financial independence can’t restore your health, but it provides options when life changes unexpectedly. My advice: Save and invest early, maintain good insurance, appreciate your support network, and don’t postpone enjoying life while you’re healthy.
