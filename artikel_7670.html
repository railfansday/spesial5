Defamation Case by Ex-Husband Against Ex-Wife, Based on Her Allegations to Police, Can Go Forward

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

Knowingly false accusations about a person made to the police can, in most states, lead to defamation liability. Merely negligently false accusations are usually protected by so-called "qualified privilege": The theory is that people who are unsure about something, or may have misheard or misunderstood matters, should still be encouraged to report matters to the police so that the police—who have better skills and resources to investigate matters—can figure things out. But deliberate lies, which can deceive the police and thus damage their target, are generally unprotected (again, in most states).

Here's an illustration of how this can play out; the opinion, Howard v. Knepp, decided by Colorado Court of Appeals Judge Jerry Jones, joined by Judges Matthew Grove and Timothy Schutz, was just designated as precedential on Wednesday, though it had been issued as a nonprecedential decision in August:

[Ex-husband] Howard and [ex-wife] Knepp were married in January 2014. Seven years later, in January 2021, they jointly filed for dissolution of their marriage. By Knepp's account, "[t]he dissolution proceeding was drawn out and highly contentious."

On March 29, 2022, while the dissolution case remained ongoing, Knepp telephoned the Arvada Police Department to report that Howard had engaged in various acts of fraud. As now most relevant, she told an officer that Howard had opened credit card accounts in her name with Citibank, Vectra Bank, American Express, Capital One, and U.S. Bank without her knowledge or permission. She claimed that she had only learned of those credit cards in December 2020, when she obtained a credit report showing the accounts. Following an investigation and consultation with the First Judicial District Attorney's Office, the Arvada police "determined that there was not enough evidence to continue a criminal investigation into this specific allegation."

In June 2023, Howard filed a complaint against Knepp asserting a claim of defamation and defamation per se. Therein, he alleged that Knepp had made the following false and defamatory statements in her report to the police:

With respect to the credit cards, Howard also alleged in the complaint that (1) Knepp knew about them all along because she had opened the accounts herself; (2) various communications showed that Knepp knew about the cards "well before December 2020"; and (3) Knepp knew that Howard hadn't forged her signatures because "she was the one who signed the relevant contracts."

The court allowed Howard's case to go forward:

Knepp argued … that Howard couldn't establish a likelihood of prevailing as to [the relevant elements of defamation] because (1) the credit card allegations were materially true (and therefore not defamatory) and (2) there is no evidence of malice….

With respect to those issues, Howard's affidavit made the following allegations:

In addition, Howard submitted a number of documents, including (1) various emails from Knepp to Howard acknowledging the existence of and approving the accounts as far back as January 2014, and, in at least one case, referring to the card as "my card"; (2) text messages from Knepp to Howard acknowledging and requesting action on the accounts; (3) emails and pictures in text messages sent by Knepp to Howard showing that the account statements were addressed to Knepp; and (4) voicemail transcriptions of messages left by Knepp about messages she had received from American Express and U.S. Bank representatives, which themselves were transcribed. {As well, the police reports and follow-up internal police documentation show that the police had a very difficult time obtaining additional information from Knepp.}  …

To be sure, Knepp (via her own affidavit) denied much of what Howard alleged. But her denials and limited supporting documentation don't "defeat[ ] [Howard's] claim as a matter of law." At most, Knepp casts doubt on the veracity of Howard's evidence. At this stage of the case, that isn't enough to warrant dismissal of Howard's claim….

Dan Ernst (Ernst Legal Group, LLC) represents Howard.
