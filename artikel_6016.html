The Plot to Overthrow the Electoral College Must Fail

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

North Carolina Electoral College representatives sign the Certificates of Vote after they all cast their ballots for President-elect Donald Trump at the State Capitol building in Raleigh, N.D., December 19, 2016.

Article II, Section 1 of the Constitution sets out the process by which Americans select their president. Alexander Hamilton marveled at the time, in Federalist No. 68, that it was “almost the only part of the system, of any consequence, which has escaped without severe censure, or which has received the slightest mark of approbation from its opponents.” The core of that process is that the members of the Electoral College are selected in each state. With only the Twelfth Amendment’s modification in 1804 to adjust how the vice president is selected, this same system has been in continuous use for 238 years. It is how George Washington, Abraham Lincoln, Franklin D. Roosevelt, Ronald Reagan, and Barack Obama were elected. Now, a nefarious plot aims to overthrow the American system.

Democrats have been railing against the Electoral College since losing the 2000 and 2016 elections while winning a plurality of the popular vote. These partisan sour grapes have often been dismissed as empty talk because the Electoral College is established by the Constitution, so only an amendment could abolish it. An amendment requires a two-thirds vote of Congress and the consent of 38 states, both of which are out of reach on a party-line vote.

A proposed workaround has sailed through the legislatures of 18 blue states and the District of Columbia: the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact. The theory is that these states would award their electors not to the winner of the popular vote in their own state, but to the winner of the national popular vote. It may be that a state can decide on its own to do this. States have not always picked electors by statewide popular vote. Many states at the Founding had their state legislatures choose electors. South Carolina did so all the way to the Civil War. Colorado in 1876 was the last state to do so, but only because it had just been admitted to statehood and claimed not to have time to organize a statewide vote. Other states have awarded electors to the winners of each House district, as Maine and Nebraska do today; this system was upheld by the Supreme Court in 1892.

But the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact is not just a unilateral decision by individual states about how to award their electors. If it had been, it would already have been effective in 2024, and Donald Trump would have won the Electoral College 530–8 with 49.7 percent of the popular vote. Instead, it is styled as an agreement that goes into effect only if states composing a majority of the Electoral College approve it. At present, the signatories control 222 electoral votes. With Democrats gaining trifectas in Pennsylvania, Michigan, Wisconsin, Nevada, and Arizona, they conceivably could pass the compact in states controlling 283 electoral votes. They also now control the New Hampshire state legislature, although with a veto-proof majority only in one of its two chambers, they would have to get through Republican Governor Kelly Ayotte.

Passage is not certain. Michigan Democrats have previously rejected the compact in 2023 and 2024, and in Nevada (a smaller state that would be swamped under the compact), the Democratic governor vetoed it in 2019. Some of the small-state signatories, such as Delaware, Vermont, Rhode Island, or New Mexico, might yet have second thoughts now that they are playing with live ammunition.

Moreover, under Article I, Section 10, interstate compacts require the consent of Congress. Even if Democrats passed it in the next two years with the aim of changing the rules for the 2028 election, Trump would surely veto it. After the 2030 Census, a number of the signatory states are expected to lose House seats, which will further narrow the margin for keeping it in effect. Then, there’s the question of whether the Supreme Court would allow individual states to gang up and effectively nullify the entire constitutional design of our presidential election system.

Legal obstacles aside, this is a bad idea. To start with, the very durability of our system over such a long period is an argument in its favor. No other nation has used the same system for choosing its head of state and government for so long. Leaving aside the dubious precedents of 1824 (when popular vote records were not kept in the most populous state) and 1876 (when fraud was rampant in both parties and Southern Democrats engaged in mass violence against Republican voters), the Electoral College has only delivered a different outcome from the national popular vote three times in our history. In each case, the national vote was fairly close and neither candidate got a popular majority, so the Electoral College provided the legitimacy of a majority result that the popular vote could not. This may have frustrated Californians, but it was not just the tyranny of a few small places with no people in them. In 2016, Trump won the popular vote in seven of the ten largest states. It is nearly impossible to win a national popular majority without carrying the Electoral College.

We have an Electoral College because we are a nation of diverse and distinct states. The American people are long accustomed to this system. The people ratified the Constitution and its amendments state-by-state. The Senate is elected by the people in states, the House by people in districts. Both political parties choose their presidential nominees by primary elections in each state. Americans have never before held a nationwide popular vote for any purpose. Our election systems, run by state and local officials with a variety of rules for the timing of casting and counting ballots and for confirming the qualifications of voters, are not designed for such a vote.

Our campaigns today still prioritize appeals to the most closely divided states. This encourages presidential candidates to pitch at least some of their message to voters in the center, and also to travel the country in order to garner coverage in statewide media and the support of state and local power brokers. A national popular vote campaign would drive us even further down the road of nationalizing the campaign media environment and encouraging candidates to rally voters in their party’s strongholds. It would also exacerbate the divide between urban and rural or small-town America, given how the largest cities could effectively ally themselves against their own in-state neighbors.

Few of the world’s democracies conduct a nationwide vote for the chief executive. In France, which is one of those, the first man to win such a vote seized power in a coup before his first term ended. National popular plebiscites are far more common in dictatorial systems, where the president has more control over who counts the votes. One of the reasons why the January 6 mob was never a serious threat to overturn the 2020 election was that the state-by-state winners had already been publicly certified in 50 separate state capitals. This was a deliberate feature of the system: As Hamilton wrote, “as the electors, chosen in each State, are to assemble and vote in the State in which they are chosen, this detached and divided situation will expose them much less to heats and ferments . . . than if they were all to be convened at one time, in one place.”

We would inevitably need to overhaul our entire election system to accommodate a nationwide vote. It is not a coincidence that such a nationalization of elections is favored by many of the same people who want to do away with the Electoral College. As things stand today, the whole country would have to wait on California, which not only takes weeks to count votes, but does so in order to count late-arriving ballots and to “cure” defective ballots — a process bound to erode confidence in a close election. That matters little when nobody doubts the outcome in California. But by making elections turn on the margins of victory in the deepest-blue or deepest-red jurisdictions, whose election officials are uniformly of the same party, the incentives for all manner of electoral malfeasance would be supercharged.

The 1888 election provides a vivid, if extreme, example of how the Electoral College limits the scope of mischief done by the corruption of voting systems in particular states. The eleven states of the former Confederacy were then well into the post-Reconstruction suppression of the black vote in their states. As a result, the Democratic nominee, Grover Cleveland, won those states by almost 25 points, with over 60 percent of the vote. This propelled Cleveland to a narrow plurality in the national popular vote even though his Republican opponent, Benjamin Harrison, won a popular majority in the rest of the country. Yet the Electoral College gave Harrison the victory because his support was not as concentrated in an unrepresentative handful of states in which the vote was compromised.

If a nationwide vote would be a headache, a nationwide recount would be a nightmare. Nobody who lived through the 2000 and 2020 post-election controversies should have any illusions that such a thing could be conducted in a civil and orderly fashion in today’s America. Rather than wait to learn that lesson the hard way, we should step back from the brink of overthrowing the system that has chosen our nation’s leader from the beginning.
