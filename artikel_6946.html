The 27 Best Video Games of 2026 (So Far)

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

With Grand Theft Auto VI finally in gamers’ hands, 2026 might go down as one of the greatest years in the medium’s history.

Every product was carefully curated by an Esquire editor. We may earn a commission from these links. Here’s how we test products and why you should trust us.

Will 2026 go down as one of the best years in video games? There just simply aren’t enough hours in the calendar year to play through all of the amazing games that came out before Thursday, November 19, and now we have to add on hundreds of hours of Grand Theft Auto VI.

Of course, the industry’s economic woes cannot be overstated. It’s a dark time when even prolific studios downsize, slash budgets, and lay off talented developers with invaluable institutional knowledge. Even the GTA VI designers—whose game is already heralded as a masterpiece—are still fighting for better working conditions. So it’s hard to actually call 2026 a great year in gaming when it’s also been a year of heartbreak and uncertainty for the future.

But the games are still great. Even just a few hours into GTA VI, it’s obvious that it’s a massive achievement for Rockstar Games that’s well worth the wait. And just this past month, Nintendo dropped a remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time that features beautifully upgraded graphics from the original 1998 classic. Elsewhere, Resident Evil Requiem nailed the franchise’s decades-long identity split with a game that’s half third-person action and half first-person survival horror. Plus, action games like 007 First Light, Control Resonant, and Onimusha: Way of the Sword let loose the power fantasies we’ve only dreamed of.

But it’s not all blood and guts and guns and swords. Earlier this year, the coming-of-age Mixtape caused a ruckus among gaming circles, though anyone who’s played it and loved it will tell you just how much it’s a love letter to bygone summer days. Oh, and a spin-off Pokémon title about building adorable towns and protecting the environment is currently the highest-rated game in the franchise’s thirty-year history.

So, unless you’re already dedicated to spending all of Thanksgiving and Christmas in GTA VI’s Vice City, some of the best games Esquire’s editors have played through in 2026 so far.

GTA VI hadn’t even been out for twenty-four hours yet and critics were already calling it a masterpiece. To be fair, video game critics call a lot of titles masterpieces, but it was clear from everyone’s immediate reaction to Vice City that Rockstar Games spent the last decade making something truly special. Sure, there are hundreds of hours of side quests and town-foolery to get lost in, but GTA VI also features a Bonnie & Clyde-esque love story between its two playable protagonists—Jason and Lucia—which breaks up the beloved smash-and-grab chaos of the Grand Theft Auto franchise and makes the gamer question just where the point between a video game and real life begins.

At its most basic, Nintendo’s The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time remake isn’t all that different from the classic, 1998 original. The game certainly looks a lot better on the Nintendo Switch 2 than it did on the Nintendo 64, now that the polygonal characters and sharp outdoors have been converted to more living and breathing graphics. But at the end of the day, you’re still playing the same game that you played in 1998. Don’t take that as a knock, though! Ocarina of Time still tops “Best Video Games of All Time” lists for a reason.

There are times playing Star Wars: Galactic Racer when you will feel like you are going too fast. And yes, the rate at which it will feel like that will shock you. But that’s pod racing! And better than any racing sim, let alone a Star Wars video game with voice acting as good as Galactic Racer, it truly feels like you’re speeding around in a hunk-a-junk that you just cobbled together with space garbage and jet engines.

Following up Fire Emblem: Three Houses was already a tall task, let alone having to follow Fire Emblem: Engage. Both titles combined the best of Japanese RPGs with the new slice-of-life school relationship activities introduced in Three Houses. And now Fortune’s Weave, something of a trilogy-capper for the series, is the most maximalist entry yet. With dozens of characters, choices, a vast map, and four unique story quests that all overlap, there’s so much to do in the latest Fire Emblem game that they could wait another five years before the next entry.

The Blood of Dawnwalker is the debut game from Rebel Wolves, a new studio started by former creatives from The Witcher 3: Blood Hunt. So if Dawnwalker feels eerily like a Witcher game, you’re not crazy. That being said, the vampire game’s controversial timer—which forces players to make quick decisions with big impacts on gameplay—fully separated the RPG and proved that the new studio was ready to try out exciting ideas all its own.

Remedy’s Alan Wake 2 topped Esquire’s Best Video Games of 2023 list three years ago, so you can bet we were excited for the studio’s next game, Control Resonant. The latest is a sequel to 2019’s Control, which now finds its former protagonist Jesse Faden missing. So, support character Dylan Faden, now in the spotlight, takes control to deal with the foreign entities and save his estranged sister.

Can video games have too much of a good thing? Apparently not. Phantom Blade Zero, the umpteenth samurai RPG since Sekiro: Shadows Die Twice and Ghost of Tsushima, dazzled gamers with its combat and fluid martial arts combos. And while it firmly isn’t a soulslike—the developer explicitly rejects the genre, actually—that still doesn’t mean Phantom Blade Zero is a walk in the park either.

How about a game that’s a little less stressful? If you need a break from the fast-paced action, check out Big Walk, a co-operative puzzle game about, well, going on a long walk. The charming game allows players to communicate only if they’re close to one another on Big Walk’s big map, providing a bit of a challenge as you progress on your journey while also adding some intimacy to the digital experience. Plus, the characters are cute as hell.

Star Wars fans ate good this year. If racing isn’t your thing, check out Zero Company, a turn-based tactics game that falls somewhere in between Fire Emblem and XCOM. Though the game isn’t all that challenging on easier difficulties, players have found a lot of fun and repeat plays with the game’s permadeath mode that doesn’t allow characters to return after they die.

Rock out with your claws out. Insomniac applies its winning formula for triple-A superhero games to flesh out the most popular X-Men member of all time. The five-foot-four berserker stars in his own solo adventure in a universe where mutants live in secret. While piecing together his own memories, Wolverine meets a telepath named Jean Grey. Together they must stop a powerful industrialist from unleashing a weapon that could kill all mutants. A hyper-violent tour through some of its title antihero’s classic comics, Wolverine doesn’t quite reach the same heights as Spider-Man, but the way it lets players truly feel like “The Best There Is” is gold.

If you could rewind to your last day of school, what would it look like? Who are you with? And most importantly, what’s on the stereo? At the start of summer, developers Beethoven & Dinosaur released the gorgeous narrative game Mixtape, a loving homage to coming-of-age cinema with a tastefully curated soundtrack. (We’re talking Joy Division, Silverchair, B.J. Thomas, and more.) Set in the summer of 1995, the game sees three California teens relish their final hours together before going their separate ways. While Mixtape kicked up a storm among gamers over what defines a “game”—you can’t die or fail, nor are any of the things you do in Mixtape remotely challenging—it’s still a special experience that I argue illuminates how special games can be as a storytelling medium. Your adolescence probably didn’t look exactly like Mixtape, but the game can nonetheless trigger old memories.

As the world waits with bated breath to see who will play the next James Bond on the big screen, video games have a Bond to call their own in actor Patrick Gibson. From developer IO Interactive, of the smash Hitman series, comes 007 First Light, a high-octane espionage action game that follows a new James Bond in the early days of his career at MI6. Unlike the Hitman games, which are mired in the darkness of Agent 47’s deadly profession, 007 First Light revels in the fantasy of Bond’s perilous and pleasurable lifestyle. It’s basically the closest any of us will get to feeling like James Bond short of renting an Aston Martin for a test drive.

It’s been 12 years since Yacht Club Games released Shovel Knight, which was deemed by many to be one of the leading retro-inspired indie hits of the 2010s. Now Yacht Club has done it again with Mina the Hollower, which mines plenty of inspiration from gothic dungeon crawlers like Castlevania and Game Boy–era Legend of Zelda games, with modern flourishes à la Dark Souls. Players control Mina, a genius 19th-century mouse who must slay monsters and restore power to her home. Don’t let its cute looks fool you: Mina the Hollower is a challenging game that will have you sweating and smiling all the way through.

Remember dropping quarters into Marvel vs. Capcom 2 back in the day? The legacy of Marvel’s 2D-fighting heritage lives on in Marvel Tōkon: Fighting Souls, a shiny new team-based fighter with an anime-inspired take on the Marvel universe. Heroes like Captain America, Iron Man, Spider-Man, Storm, Deadpool, and Blade—and even villains like Doctor Doom—team up to save the world from an alien menace. The game is developed by Arc System Works, arguably the modern masters of the genre, boasting titles like Guilty Gear, BlazBlue, and Dragon Ball FighterZ, and the studio applies its decades of expertise to one of the finest and most nuanced fighting games we’ve ever had the pleasure of throwing our controllers at.

There’s no shortage of killer samurai games nowadays. Which is perhaps why Capcom saw fit to resurrect its dormant Onimusha franchise with the exceptional Onimusha: Way of the Sword. Essentially a reboot of the series (whose last title was released all the way back in 2006 for the PlayStation 2), Way of the Sword follows legendary swordsman Miyamoto Musashi as he comes into possession of the Oni Gauntlet and must cleanse the world of a supernatural evil. While not quite a Souls-like, the game offers an adequately complex combat system that ensures sword slashing never gets stale. And slicing demons in half feels oh so satisfying.

If you, like me, have fond memories of Initial D, Forza Horizon 6 is an invitation to awaken the sleeping gearhead within. The open-world racing series finally makes its way to Japan, the birthplace of drift racing, to let players roam dense city streets and wide-open mountain roads in tricked-out Hondas, Nissans, and exotic supercars. The Forza Horizon series has reached its peak with this sixth installment, with photorealistic graphics that can fool you into believing you’re actually speeding through traffic halfway around the world.

After almost 30 years of Resident Evil games, it’s no surprise that the ninth installment is another polished thrill ride. Capcom plays the hits to great success in Resident Evil Requiem, a survival-horror game that hits the right notes even when it’s serving up Leon S. Kennedy fan service. But in the roughly 10-to-18-hour campaign (a great length!), the gameplay of FBI newbie Grace Ashcroft is more horror focused and full of fresh twists on the Resident Evil formula. Though we won’t spoil it here, Esquire’s review in February also teased that the latest Resident Evil game contains “one major set piece in particular that rivals some of the best action-movie moments in recent years.”

Don’t let Pokémon Pokopia’s cozy gameplay fool you—the latest spin-off of the cutesy monster-collecting phenomenon is actually one of the deepest games in the franchise’s nearly thirty-year history. Sure, you will spend most of your time in Pokopia building houses for your Pokémon to live in or meticulously gardening. But underneath the casual novelties of building a perfect little Pokémon town lives a surprisingly emotional story about climate-change disasters, grief, and the importance of taking care of Earth’s natural environment.

Saros is a gameplay-first, story-second action game that feels incredible. Much as with the developer’s previous game, Returnal, the third-person shooting is inspired by arcade shooters where bullets fill your screen nonstop. Unlike Returnal, it’s not brutally difficult. Gameplay is fast and exhilarating—and sometimes overwhelming—but it doesn’t take long before you know exactly which projectiles to absorb, which to dodge, and which to parry. The PlayStation-exclusive title also has one of the best gameplay loops of the year, and it does more with the DualSense controller’s advanced haptic feedback than any other game on the Sony system. Merging the run-based progression of Hades with a more traditional level-based structure to enormous success, Saros is difficult yet approachable, rewarding players with a ton of resources to invest in upgrades as it slowly unfurls its mystery-box story bit by bit.

With the release of No More Heroes III in 2021, Grasshopper Manufacture finally put the saga of Travis Touchdown to rest and kicked off its next era with Romeo Is a Dead Man. The studio’s latest game retells Shakespeare’s famous love story mashed up with 1980s sci-fi, Japanese tokusatsu, and grind-house cinema. The game puts players in control of Romeo Stargazer, a small-town deputy who falls in love with an amnesiac named Juliet. But when Romeo dies, he is reborn as a half-man, half-machine superhero who embarks on a quest to find his missing love. Romeo Is a Dead Man feels like immersing oneself in an obscure Troma film, and it’s as madcap and magnificent as it sounds.

Remastered video games are always a tough sell—though with every Nintendo fan having no issue coughing up $30 to play Pokémon FireRed & LeafGreen on the Switch 2 this year, it seems times have changed. Luckily Dragon Quest VII Reimagined is much more than a port to a newer system. The remake, which includes many quality-of-life improvements, was rebuilt from the ground up with glorious 3D animation. If you’ve never played the classic Japanese RPG before, the charm of this latest remake is enough to make you a Dragon Quest fan for life.

If you’re too scared to play horror games alone, let Reanimal’s multiplayer gameplay bring people together. Set in an island town overrun with monsters and mysterious creatures, Reanimal follows a nameless brother and sister who must survive the island’s horrors if they want to save their friends. With a story as cryptic as some of its puzzles, Reanimal carries the baton of other memorable platformers like Limbo and Little Nightmares to deliver an otherworldly experience. Although Reanimal can be played alone, it is a journey best taken with someone by your side.

Video games are all about rewarding the player for progression, and there may be no better equivalent to this feeling in the real world than rock climbing. Cairn, a new indie title that focuses on this extreme sport, captures the similarities beautifully. As the player works their way up the mountain, juggling the character’s stamina and gauging the next best foothold, it is amazing how intense and immersive Cairn feels compared with its relatively beautiful visuals. But as with any good climb, the surprisingly difficult journey is more than worth it when you finally reach the top.

If you thought Doom had too many pixelated demons and not enough rubber-hose whimsy, Mouse: P.I. for Hire is your game. Heavily inspired by classic Disney, Fleischer, and MGM cartoons—and clearly taking some cues from the 2017 hit Cuphead—Mouse: P.I. for Hire puts players in control of an anthropomorphic mouse detective on his latest case in Mouseburg. The game’s fast-paced first-person-shooter action is delightfully complemented by its (literally) cartoonish antics. Hand-drawn pistol and shotgun blasts and reloading animations are as much a treat to watch as the game is fun to play. If gaming today feels more complicated than ever, Mouse: P.I. for Hire brings it back to basics with a novelty coat of paint.

Nintendo should have called the new Mario Tennis game Mario Tennis Chaos, because the player needs to understand right up front that skills on the court can carry them only so far in Mario Tennis Fever. Much like the 24 drivers per race in Mario Kart World, a calamity of new power-ups renders tennis into a zany, uncontrollable farce of a sport that becomes one of the best Mario sports titles in Nintendo’s history—once you learn to let go and embrace the nonsense.

The decades-old Marvel vs. Capcom series still reigns supreme as the best superhero tag fighter in video games, but Invincible VS is set on taking the throne. Based on the hit Amazon series (with some of its stars reprising their voice roles, like Oscar-winner J.K. Simmons as Omni-Man), Invincible VS is a 3v3 tag-team fighting game featuring playable characters from Robert Kirkman’s comic-book universe. The game tells a stand-alone story adjacent to season 3 in which Mark must stand with his allies to save Earth from the Viltrumites. (Hence the fighting.) Although its mechanical controls are relatively simple compared with those of other fighting games, it’s no less elegant and fun, and the game features some gnarly cel-shaded 3D animation that’s simply gorgeous. Besides, it’s another way to pummel your buddies to a bloody pulp. Talk about threatening us with a good time.

Dead Space? More like Dad Space. Pragmata is Capcom’s latest original game and a potential franchise in the making, its story centered around a systems engineer named Hugh and his foster-father relationship with an adorable android nicknamed Diana. With a rogue AI overtaking a research station on the moon, Hugh and Diana must work together to take the AI offline and make it back to Earth. Between its breakneck gameplay involving grid-like puzzles (which must be solved on the fly) and its endearing story about parenthood, Pragmata is what you might get if you asked ChatGPT, “What if Alien and Marvel’s Logan could be a single video game?”
