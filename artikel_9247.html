Most Teens Care, So Why Don’t They Always Help?

A student notices a classmate eating lunch alone. Another sees a peer being excluded from a group chat. Someone knows a friend has been struggling but hesitates before reaching out. Why?

Most adults assume the challenge is getting young people to care. But after spending years studying adolescent compassion, I have come to believe that caring may not be the central problem.

We often imagine compassion as a straightforward process: Notice suffering, feel concern, take action. Yet anyone who has worked with adolescents knows that the path is rarely so simple. Young people are navigating friendships, social hierarchies, identity development, and concerns about belonging, all while learning how to manage their own emotions. In these moments, compassion is not simply a feeling. It is a decision unfolding within a complex social world.

In our recent research developing the Youth Compassion Measure, an assessment of compassionate responding, we find that most adolescents recognize when a peer is struggling. What varies is whether that recognition translates into helping. Across hundreds of students, four factors consistently shape whether compassion becomes action: peer dynamics, social risk, emotional regulation, and intrinsic motivation.

Understanding these factors may help parents, educators, and youth-serving professionals better support compassionate action in young people.

One of the clearest findings from our work is that adolescents were more likely to help when the person experiencing distress was someone they knew well.

At first glance, this may seem obvious. Most of us are more likely to check on a close friend than a stranger. Yet this finding points to a deeper truth about adolescence: Compassion unfolds within relationships.

During adolescence, friendships become central to daily life. Young people are figuring out who they are, where they belong, and how they fit into their social worlds. Relationships provide security, identity, and connection. Unsurprisingly, those same relationships shape compassionate responding.

This means that compassion is not simply an individual trait. It is also social.

When educators talk about creating compassionate schools, they often focus on teaching empathy or kindness. Those efforts matter. But our findings suggest that fostering strong, supportive relationships may be just as important. Young people appear more willing to take compassionate action when they feel connected to the people around them.

In other words, belonging may do more than improve well-being. It may help create the conditions that allow compassion to flourish.

If relationships explain who adolescents help, social risk helps explain why they sometimes do not.

Consider a common scenario. A student witnesses a peer being teased. They know the behavior is hurtful. They may even want to intervene. Yet stepping forward could carry consequences. They may worry about being judged, excluded, or embarrassed, or becoming a target themselves.

Our findings suggest that adolescents are often weighing these possibilities, consciously or unconsciously, before deciding how to respond.

This is one reason public conversations about bullying can be so frustrating. When videos circulate online, attention quickly turns to the bystanders. Why didn’t anyone help?

The question assumes that recognizing suffering naturally leads to action. But social reality is more complicated.

Young people are often assessing multiple things at once: the seriousness of the situation, who is involved, who is watching, what might happen if they intervene, and what might happen if they do not. What appears from the outside as indifference may actually be a moment of conflict between compassion and self-protection.

This perspective does not excuse harmful behavior or passivity. But it does encourage us to understand helping as a social act rather than a purely moral one.

If we want young people to act compassionately, we need environments where compassionate action is socially supported instead of socially costly.

Perhaps the most surprising finding from our study is that recognizing suffering alone did not consistently predict helping.

Many students understood that someone was struggling. The difference was what happened after that recognition occurred.

One of the strongest predictors of compassionate action is distress tolerance, the ability to remain engaged in the presence of emotional discomfort. This matters because witnessing another person’s suffering is often uncomfortable. We may feel sadness, anxiety, uncertainty, helplessness, or fear of saying the wrong thing. Adolescents experience these emotions, too.

Imagine a teenager whose friend is crying. The challenge is not simply recognizing that the friend is upset; it’s navigating the delicate space between overidentifying with another person’s suffering and distancing oneself from it. Too much emotional absorption can lead to overwhelm and withdrawal. Too much distance can lead to disengagement. Compassion requires something different: remaining connected to another person’s experience while maintaining enough emotional balance to respond effectively.

People sometimes assume that compassion and empathy are the same thing. They are not. Empathy allows us to resonate with another person’s experience. Compassion involves channeling that awareness into a desire to alleviate suffering. Doing so requires emotional regulation. Without it, concern can become overwhelm, uncertainty, or avoidance. Instead of moving toward suffering, we move away from it.

Our findings suggest that compassionate action depends not only on recognizing another person’s distress, but also on the ability to stay present with that distress without becoming consumed by it. In this sense, emotional regulation is not separate from compassion; it helps make compassion possible.

For parents and educators, this finding has important practical implications. Teaching young people how to navigate difficult emotions may strengthen their capacity for compassionate action. Skills such as mindfulness, emotional awareness, reflection, and distress tolerance are not simply tools for managing stress. They may also be the very capacities that help adolescents transform caring into action by staying engaged when helping feels uncertain, vulnerable, or emotionally difficult.

Even when adolescents care and have the emotional skills to stay engaged, one additional factor matters: motivation.

In our study, intrinsic prosocial motivation was one of the strongest predictors of helping. Simply put, adolescents who genuinely wanted to help others were more likely to translate concern into action.

This may sound self-evident, but it carries important implications.

Adults often try to encourage helping through external incentives, praise, rewards, or behavioral expectations. While these approaches can influence behavior, our findings suggest that compassionate action is especially powerful when it emerges from internal values and genuine concern for others.

Young people are constantly asking themselves what kind of person they want to be. When helping becomes connected to identity, meaning, and purpose, compassionate action appears more likely to follow.

This suggests that fostering compassion is about more than encouraging kind behavior. It is also about helping young people develop a sense of responsibility toward others and a meaningful connection to shared humanity.

Taken together, these findings challenge a common assumption.

There is a tendency to treat moments of inaction as moments of moral failure. We see a young person remain silent, fail to intervene, or walk past another person’s suffering, and we assume we understand what happened.

Yet our research suggests that these moments are often more complicated than they appear.

They unfold within social worlds shaped by belonging, friendship, identity, uncertainty, vulnerability, and the deeply human desire to remain connected to others. They are influenced by relationships, social risks, emotional capacities, and personal motivations.

What looks like indifference from a distance may sometimes reflect a struggle between compassion and the realities that make compassionate action difficult.

This perspective shifts the conversation from a question of character to a question of conditions.

Rather than asking whether adolescents are compassionate, we might ask: What conditions help compassion become action?

The encouraging message from this research is that the factors influencing compassionate action are not fixed traits.

Emotional regulation skills can be taught.

When we think about compassion this way, the goal becomes larger than helping young people recognize suffering. The goal is helping them develop the capacities that allow caring to become action.

Perhaps this is what adolescence reveals so clearly. The distance between caring and acting is rarely empty space. It is filled with questions about belonging, uncertainty, responsibility, and risk.

Young people are learning to navigate that space every day.

Our responsibility as the adults who support them is not simply to teach them to care. It is to create the relationships, communities, and opportunities that help them act on the care they already possess.
