How Does Mindfulness Change the Brain in Drug Addiction?

Why does a reminder of drug use command attention while food, relationships, and other everyday rewards lose their pull in drug addiction? Our impaired response inhibition and salience attribution (iRISA) model of addiction describes this imbalance as a seesaw: drug-related cues gain value and salience, capturing attention and feeling important, at the expense of everyday activities and relationships. This imbalance then makes regulation of craving and drug use behavior harder.

Mindfulness-oriented recovery enhancement (MORE) has already been shown to reduce opioid use, craving, and pain in people who misuse opioids or have opioid use disorder, often with chronic pain. We wanted to understand what changes in the brain occur with this therapy, and whether those changes relate to craving and abstinence.

In this randomized clinical trial published in JAMA Psychiatry, with Yuefeng Huang as first author, we assigned 86 people receiving inpatient treatment for opioid use disorder, primarily involving heroin, to MORE or psychoeducational supportive therapy for eight weeks. All were stabilized on methadone or buprenorphine alongside their inpatient care.

MORE was developed by co-author Eric Garland, PhD, now at the University of California, San Diego. It includes mindfulness, cognitive reappraisal (reinterpreting or distancing from drug-related cues to reduce their emotional pull), and savoring (attending to the pleasant sensory features and positive feelings elicited by natural rewards such as food, nature, and social experiences).

The comparison therapy provided support and education about addiction-related themes, including coping with stress, managing cravings, and addressing relapse triggers, but did not include MORE’s three core components. Both therapies were delivered in the same therapist-led group format, lasting two hours a week for eight weeks, with homework assignments in both.

We added functional magnetic resonance imaging before and after therapy to compare brain responses from before to after MORE versus supportive therapy. The main picture-task analysis included 59 participants with usable scans at both time points. Participants reappraised drug pictures, savored food pictures, and viewed these images. A subset of 37 participants also watched a drug-themed movie (Trainspotting) without instructions to regulate their reactions.

People assigned to the mindfulness-oriented group showed changes in brain function that were associated with lower craving. When reappraising drug images, the brain’s dorsolateral prefrontal cortex became less active—a brain region involved in managing reactions to drug cues.

From before to after treatment, drug-biased prefrontal activity decreased with MORE relative to supportive therapy. This reduction was seen during reappraisal as compared to passively viewing drug pictures or savoring food pictures.

In an earlier analysis of the pretreatment scans that included some of these participants, the people with opioid use disorder showed greater activity during drug-cue reappraisal relative to food savoring, compared with people without addiction, and this activity was associated with greater addiction severity. In the current trial, the pattern shifted with mindfulness. One possible interpretation is that this region no longer needed to work as hard to regulate responses to drug cues. Within the MORE group, larger reductions in drug-biased prefrontal reappraisal activity were associated with larger reductions in craving.

Another region, the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, helps us regulate emotion and assign value to experiences. Within the mindfulness-oriented group, greater increases in its response while savoring food pictures were associated with larger reductions in craving. Together, these findings suggest a shift in the balance between responses to drug cues and everyday rewards.

The movie offered a different way to examine these responses. In our earlier movie study, frontal brain responses to the same drug scenes were more similar across participants with opioid use disorder than across participants without addiction. That report also found reductions during treatment when participants in MORE and supportive therapy were analyzed together.

In the new analysis, the reduction from before to after treatment in these shared medial prefrontal responses was greater with MORE than with supportive therapy. Across both treatment groups, greater reductions in shared responses to the drug scenes in the movie were associated with greater reductions in scene-induced craving (craving felt during the movie scenes). The movie finding was less robust after accounting for pretreatment differences between the groups.

The mindfulness-oriented group also showed greater increases in abstinence duration (days since last drug use) than the supportive-therapy group at follow-up, approximately four months after treatment. However, no significant association between brain changes and abstinence duration was detected.

Future studies should include more subjects, especially at follow-up, and more women. Independent replication of results and testing their generalizability in other addiction groups (in outpatients with addiction or those no longer receiving medications, or those with addiction to other drugs) are needed.

We show that about two months of behavioral intervention integrating mindfulness, reappraisal, and savoring, added to medication and other inpatient care, may change how the brain processes drug-related cues and food cues. These changes were related to reduced craving, a core clinical symptom of addiction.

Using both pictures and a movie was important; it allowed us to examine deliberate regulation alongside responses to unfolding scenes without instructions. This approach brings us closer to understanding both the effort involved in managing drug cues and the pull those cues can exert in daily life.

Separate research on remote delivery suggests mindfulness-oriented recovery enhancement could reach more people who need it. Our imaging trial examined inpatient care.

For people with addiction, the implication is that the seesaw may be adjustable: structured practice may help reduce the dominance of drug cues and make room for other everyday rewarding experiences. Our results suggest that brain function can change during recovery, and that behavioral treatment can complement medication by helping people learn different responses to cues.
