Why Do We Keep Gifts From Our Exes?

With the holidays fast approaching, many of us might be thinking of what to give our partner. Or maybe we are thinking about what special present our partner will give us. What we probably do not want to consider is that the gift, which initially brings joy, may outlast our actual relationship. This made me start to wonder—once the relationship ends, do you keep the gift, or do you toss it away? And do you tell a new partner that the item was from an ex, and if so, how do you explain why you are keeping it?

In a preliminary study, my research team asked 83 heterosexual adults in the United States what items they kept from a past romantic relationship, and why they had hung onto them. What we did not expect was how many people kept items. In our sample, 71 percent of women and 72 percent of men said they kept things, with clothing and jewellery the most common. What was also interesting were the reasons for keeping items; women reported being more sentimental, saying they wanted to keep the gift because of the memories, while men were more practical, saying they kept items because they were useful.

Why would someone hang onto something? One evolutionary psychological study by Saad and Gill (2003) proposes that gifts are a way for a potential mate to show their romantic interest and commitment and to signal that they have resources. If correct, then keeping a gift from an ex might remind us that somebody once thought we were worth the effort, and I suspect that reminder is hard to throw in the trash.

But how did new partners feel about it? We performed a short follow-up survey of 76 heterosexual adults in the United States who were in a relationship to imagine that their new partner had kept some items from an ex. Not surprising was that some items bothered new partners more than others. A sweater was not a big deal, but a sex toy bothered about half. Photos were an issue for about a third of respondents. The figure includes our preliminary findings.

What matters the most, though, was the motive behind keeping the item, as it provides a window into one’s current relationship status. When the explanation revolved around it being part of a memory, most people (59 percent) said they would be comfortable with the item, while 17 percent said they would be uncomfortable. An explanation that an item was useful was likewise seen by about half (54 percent) of respondents as a comfortable reason. Not surprisingly, when the explanation reflected unfinished business with an ex, new partners grew increasingly uncomfortable. Saying that one wanted to keep an ex’s gift because they were not ready to move on or still felt attached to the ex led a third of respondents (29 percent) to say they were uncomfortable.

Evolutionary psychology also provides a possible explanation for why a new partner might be uncomfortable. Jealousy is often described as an alarm that sounds when a rival threatens a valued relationship, and an ex is the most obvious rival. Research shows a large number of people get back together with an ex; in one study of young adults in the United States, 44 percent had broken up and reconciled with their current or most recent partner (Halpern-Meekin et al., 2013), and some research suggests it is more than 60 percent (Dailey et al., 2009). That item might matter if it hints to a new partner that the ex is still in the running.

We also found a sex difference, with men being generally more uncomfortable with a new partner hanging onto any keepsake for any reason. Men also tended to keep items only if the ending was a good one, but they tossed items from a bad breakup. Women, in contrast, were much more likely to keep an item, even after a breakup ended badly. Why would men and women differ? At this point, I can offer some speculated answers. Maybe men tend to be more upset than women by the sexual side of a partner’s infidelity, and a keepsake from an ex is a daily reminder that there was someone before. Men are also the ones who more often give gifts as a courtship tactic, which may make them quicker to see another man's gift as a rival’s investment. As for women keeping items even after a bad ending, a costly gift (like jewellery) holds its value no matter how the relationship ended. In addition, if the item is kept for the memories, as women told us in our first study, those memories are part of her own life, which a bad breakup does not take away. A necklace kept for the memories is a souvenir of a period in the keeper’s life, reflecting where she lived, who she was, and what that year felt like. That period happened and is part of her story, whether the relationship ended kindly or horribly; perhaps a bad breakup changes how she feels about the ex, but it does not erase the memories that came before.

A new partner might not care much about that worn red sweater in your closet, but they will care about whether the ex that gave it to you still has a spot in your heart that is unresolved. If the honest answer is that it was a memory, a moment in time, and that there is nothing that remains unfinished, keep the sweater and enjoy it. If the honest answer is yes, the sweater is not going to be your biggest problem.
