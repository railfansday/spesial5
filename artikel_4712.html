5 Steps to Prevent and Recover From Burnout

Updated November 18, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

It’s the number one question I get: “How can I cope with the feeling of overwhelm from all the stress and complexity in my life?” Sometimes the symptoms are physical or emotional. For others, the problem manifests in difficulty focusing, cognitive fog, and impaired performance. My experience validates the data indicating we are in a national crisis around burnout and the impact of how life is speeding up.

Stress and burnout are widespread. A 2023 APA survey, “Work in America,” revealed that 77% of workers reported experiencing work-related stress in the previous month (American Psychological Association, 2023). A similar Gallup poll reported that 27% of U.S. full-time employees felt burned out either very often or always (Gallup, 2026). There isn’t much consolation in knowing that you are not alone, other than that this indicates there are good reasons for your predicament.

This trend that continues to accelerate is due to the gap between our ability to adapt and the exponential changes taking place in our society, along with how childhood survival learning freezes our ability to adapt. I’ve written about this previously: The 4 Horsemen of Stress: Why Managing Stress Is So Difficult | Psychology Today

It’s important to make sure that the symptoms you are experiencing are not held as a badge of honor or proof of how hard you work. They are signals of deep physiological dysregulation and that something needs to change. In this essay, I want to encourage you by presenting the first five steps you can take on The Path to recovery or prevention, and turning this into a challenge to reconnect with your biological ability to adapt. This is a route toward healing, recovery, greater resilience, and an adaptive biology better able to meet the world’s accelerating pace of change and complexity.

Begin by honestly recognizing where you are, along with being willing to put energy into this process; such as with my resilience assessment to help you identify the patterns, pressures, and symptoms that need attention. It is important to acknowledge your starting point, so you accept that the road to greater resilience will take time and thus require your patience.

There is also a bigger role for acceptance in a world where so much is outside your control. This is a key to resilience. Everything you do requires energy. Yet, you have a limited amount of what I refer to as personal energy. Every time you hear about a tragedy in the media, or deal with a person who is unconsciously bothering you, it can easily trigger an emotional and physical stress response. Your mind-body goes into the stress fight-or-flight response, which drains your energy. But there is no benefit to this use of your energy, and at the same time, it takes away from your body’s ability to heal and recover. Solution: begin by recognizing that every stress you engage with drains your energy. Start making better choices about where you choose to send that energy. Decide to use your energy only where it can be of benefit.

At the foundation of recovery is self-love: the belief that you deserve to be healthy and happy. Self-love includes accepting yourself – blemishes and all. My use of the word “accepting” doesn’t mean you like everything about yourself, but simply an acknowledgment of reality: This is where I’m at right now. The paradoxical theory of change states that growth begins with accepting where you are in this moment – because you can’t be anywhere else.

Judgments such as “I should be further along” or “I shouldn’t have made that mistake” ignore your reality and the truth in this moment. This carries the unconscious message that you are not good enough and is undermining. That interpretation often begins in childhood and can push you in the direction of burnout. Give yourself 30 days to follow this suggestion of radical self-acceptance. Then notice if it results in greater calm and peace, and perhaps a lessening of your symptoms. Note: the qualities of a healthy internal voice include loving yourself, being accepting and compassionate with yourself, as well as being supportive and caring. Treating yourself to this grounding will result in your fastest recovery and growth.

You arrived here, in part, because you have been trying so hard. You want to do well; you put pressure on yourself and may have judged yourself too harshly. Recognizing that you are here not because you are defective, but because you have been striving so intensely, can make recovery easier. You are removing the stigma of inadequacy while owning your capacity. Take this challenge to be your best positive coach.

How can you translate your hard work and motivation to support your own self-care? Identify a practice of recovery, such as the use of meditation, a relaxation exercise, or biofeedback, that engages the parasympathetic branch of your nervous system. Think of this as a training that reengages your innate ability to adapt and recover.

We get stuck in old patterns. These patterns are a barrier to adaptation and growth. In fact, whenever you get close to breaking through to new ground, it can trigger uncomfortable or awkward feelings. (Tied to some form of “this isn’t me”.) These feelings then interrupt progress and cause you to pull back.

To break this pattern, I invite you to focus on your many accomplishments. You may already appreciate your successes, but it’s the identification of your skills and temperament that led to those achievements that translate results into belief in yourself. For success not to feel like it was magic, identify your unique ingredients that led to your successes. The hard work, the ability to set goals and focus, the persistence and the courage, and the skills that they demonstrate about you. These can be applied for success with new challenges. Use these to trust yourself to grow and establish a new pattern of balance and recovery, rather than burnout.

A key ingredient here is a willingness to sit with – to tolerate - the discomfort that comes from entering new territory. This is called, “growing pains”. A belief in your capacity to learn, recover, and respond differently based on a more accurate assessment of your achievements, creates the foundation for new habits and greater resilience. What you are simply doing is shifting your focus away from your shortcomings to your successes – and the skills and abilities you have already demonstrated.

This shift in focus will result in less fear or anxiety. And this is the foundation for regaining your ability to adapt. We have been conditioned to over-extend our stress response, while short-changing the opposite, the recovery response. Acknowledging your abilities creates the psychological space to take time to engage in the necessary recovery practices mentioned above.

One entryway to the healthy path forward is adopting a growth mindset. My previous steps led to this umbrella concept of how to engage with the world. It means that when you encounter something difficult and want to give up or distract yourself, instead, you remind yourself that this is a situation that is challenging you to grow and learn. I know that it’s easy to feel overwhelmed in this process. So many things to do and change and address. The key here is to have a map of the territory. This begins with knowing what The Path forward is. It is based on my 9 pillars of resilience. It’s a blueprint. Thus, whenever you take steps based on any of the 9 pillars, this puts you onto The Path. When you are on The Path you don’t have to worry about what you are not doing, as you are already achieving success for that day. (Focusing on what is, rather than on what isn’t.) This creates a powerful and practical framework that will consistently move you towards resilience, adaptation and away from burnout. In this article, I’ve already identified the first pillar – having a loving and accepting relationship with yourself; the fourth pillar – physical balance and mastery (engaging the recovery branch of your nervous system; the fifth pillar – mental balance and mastery – having a growth mindset; and the sixth pillar – emotional balance and mastery – tolerating the emotional discomfort of growth.

Resilience is about the ability to “bounce forward”. Not simply to return to your old baseline or pattern, but to use each stress to challenge yourself to learn, grow and move forward with greater awareness, flexibility and resilience.

Challenge yourself to follow these five manageable steps that put you on The Path and then appreciate the effort. And remember, successful recovery and adaptability will happen one step at a time.
