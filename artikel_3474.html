Peggy’s Wedding Broom & Its Meaning In The Gilded Age Explained

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

The following article contains minor spoilers for The Gilded Age season 4, episode 3, "Ain't That Good News".Wedding bells rang for Peggy Scott (Denee Benton) and Dr. William Kirkland (Jordan Donica) in The Gilded Age season 4, and their nuptials included an incredibly poignant tradition. Though the Gilded Age characters enjoyed a fairytale romance all the way to the altar, it wasn't without its complications and tumult, mostly in the form of William's mother, Elizabeth (Phylicia Rashad), who disapproves of everything about Peggy, from her family background to her past as a married woman to her writing career.

However, in The Gilded Age season 4, Elizabeth begrudgingly begins to bring Peggy into the fold since it's clear that her son is determined to marry her. This is more of a forced truce than a genuine change of heart, though. When Elizabeth invites Peggy and her mother, Dorothy (Audra McDonald), into her Newport ladies' club, the Daughters of Friendship, it isn't an olive branch but a shameless attempt to sanitize Peggy's past and make her more palatable to high society as Elizabeth's future daughter-in-law.

Despite Elizabeth maintaining her version of playing nice, The Gilded Age season 4, episode 3 sees her completely upend her efforts. When Peggy's parents present the soon-to-be newlyweds with the broomstick they jumped over when they were married, Elizabeth is horrified, and William tells her that if she can't accept the Scotts, she shouldn't come to the wedding. Ultimately, Peggy persuades her to change her mind, and Elizabeth witnesses the couple jump the broom in the most meaningful scene in The Gilded Age ​​​​​season 4.

When Peggy's father, Arthur (John Douglas Thompson), and Dorothy were first married, their union wasn't legally recognized because he was enslaved at the time. Though they did eventually have a legal celebration, their initial ceremony involved jumping over the broom they would later gift to Peggy as a symbol of their commitment.

Though there are multiple, conflicting accounts of where the jumping the broom tradition originated, for the African American community, the practice began in the 19th century, at a time when enslaved people did not have the legal right to marry. Accounts of the American tradition's origins differ, with some saying that those who were enslaved chose to jump the broom themselves, while others say that they were forced to do so by their enslavers (via Brides). In Arthur and Dorothy's case, The Gilded Age is adapting the former.

One episode into The Gilded Age season 4, and Bertha Russell's already got a huge secret — one that could have the most devastating consequences yet.

The tradition is widely practiced by North American Black couples today, though it had fallen into disuse at the time Peggy and William were married in the HBO original series. Sonja Warfield, the series' co-showrunner, said, "We got to take some poetic license, artistic license and keep it because we were trying some other things, but really it needed to be that because that represents the division between the families." (via Deadline) It certainly did, with Elizabeth even willing to miss her only son's wedding over it.

Interestingly, the idea for Peggy and William jumping the broom came from Audra McDonald. Warfield revealed, "We were on set filming Season 3, and Audra McDonald turned to me and she said, ‘When they get married next season, I want them to jump over the broom because Elizabeth will hate that."

And hate it, she did. Elizabeth's disdain for the practice is entirely rooted in her contempt for the Scott family, specifically Arthur's status as a former enslaved man. As a high-society woman, Elizabeth wants to assimilate, and for her, that means erasing ties and memories of the pre-Emancipation era, and jumping the broom flies in the face of that. However, for Peggy and the Scotts, the tradition is one of reverence, honoring their heritage, which William appreciates, and this is why he gives his mother the ultimatum.

Up until this point, Peggy has shown remarkable patience with Elizabeth, constantly making concessions to keep the peace, though she refuses to compromise on either the broom or her cousin's Fisk Jubilee singers, which Elizabeth also objects to. When Peggy goes to Elizabeth in an attempt to get her to attend the wedding, rather than making a show of putting her foot down, Peggy appeals to Elizabeth's sensibilities as a Christian woman and a mother of good character. She reminds Elizabeth that because she raised William right, he will follow the Bible's guidance, which means that he will always side with his wife.

Peggy keeps her words simple and assertive, and concludes by leaving the decision up to Elizabeth. Happily, Elizabeth not only attends the wedding but holds her tongue as the newlywed couple jumps over the broom. It was a beautiful scene in The Gilded Age, not only symbolic of Peggy's family history but also of her and William leaping into the future together, where they will always choose each other first.
