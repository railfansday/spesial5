How attackers turned IT management, red-team and VPN software against its users

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Security tools used as weapons, from the SolarWinds Orion backdoor to cracked Cobalt Strike copies, show how software built to manage or protect networks can become the way in

The software trusted with the deepest access to a network is also the software an attacker most wants to control. Network monitors, remote management consoles, VPN gateways and penetration-testing kits hold privileges that ordinary applications lack. When an intruder takes over that access, the defenses themselves carry the intrusion.

This selection of 10 cases looks at security tools used as weapons, along with the IT management and remote access software that sits beside them. It is not a ranking or a complete list. Nine of the cases trace a different route by which a protective or administrative tool ended up serving an attacker.

Some routes run through the supply chain. In supply chain attacks, intruders tamper with a trusted product or abuse the channel a vendor uses to reach its customers. A routine update or management command then delivers the payload. The SolarWinds Orion and Kaseya VSA incidents both followed versions of that path.

Other cases involve dual-use security software. Red teams use commercial frameworks such as Cobalt Strike and Brute Ratel C4 to imitate real adversaries. Cracked and stolen copies of those frameworks have reached ransomware crews and other malicious actors. Mimikatz was released to demonstrate weaknesses in how Windows handles credentials, and attackers later used it to steal them.

Attack tools reportedly built by governments can escape too. EternalBlue, an exploit widely reported to have been developed by the U.S. National Security Agency, was leaked by a group called the Shadow Brokers in 2017. It was then used in the WannaCry and NotPetya outbreaks.

Another group covers equipment at the edge of a network and software with standing access inside it. A flaw in a VPN gateway such as Ivanti Connect Secure can turn perimeter security gear into the entry point. Remote support platforms such as ConnectWise ScreenConnect draw attackers because they already hold broad legitimate access. Other attackers have loaded legitimately signed but vulnerable drivers to gain kernel-level access and switch off endpoint detection tools.

The final case is different in kind. The U.S. government restricted Kaspersky products, citing the risk of exploitation by the Russian government. Kaspersky has denied the allegations.

CrowdStrike $CRWD's faulty update in July 2024 is excluded. It was an error, not a weaponization. Every case here involves deliberate misuse by attackers or, for Kaspersky, a government's stated concern about potential misuse.

Attackers hid a backdoor, later named Sunburst, inside software updates for SolarWinds' Orion network management platform. SolarWinds said about 18,000 customers received the tampered update.

The backdoor was discovered in December 2020. By then, the tampered Orion update had already reached customer networks through the vendor's normal distribution channel.

Orion is network management software. Organizations run it to monitor and administer their networks. That role places it inside the environment it oversees, with access to the systems it watches.

The attackers did not need to break through each victim's defenses one at a time. The malicious code arrived inside an update from a vendor the customers already trusted. Installing a vendor update is ordinary maintenance work. In the Orion case, that ordinary step delivered the backdoor.

That is what makes the incident a supply chain attack. The target was the supplier, not each end organization. Once the Orion update had been tampered with, every customer that installed the affected release received the backdoor along with it.

The 18,000 figure counts customers who received the tampered update. It measures how widely the backdoor was distributed. It is not a count of organizations where the attackers took further action.

The U.S. government attributed the operation to the SVR, Russia's foreign intelligence service. That attribution is the U.S. government's conclusion about who ran the campaign.

Software updates exist to fix flaws and keep products current. In the Orion compromise, the update channel itself carried the intrusion into customer networks.

In July 2021, the REvil ransomware group exploited Kaseya's VSA remote management software and used it to push ransomware to the customers of managed service providers. Kaseya said fewer than 60 of its direct customers were affected. It put the number of downstream businesses hit at roughly 1,500.

VSA is IT management software. Managed service providers are the outside firms that run IT for other companies. They use tools of this kind to look after many clients' computers from a single console.

Through that console, a provider can install software, run scripts and push changes to machines it never physically touches. The same access that saves time on routine maintenance can deliver anything the operator chooses to send.

That made the Kaseya incident a case of a legitimate administration tool turned against the networks it was meant to look after. REvil did not have to break into each victim one at a time. It turned VSA itself into the means of distribution.

The ransomware reached victims by the route that ordinary IT work takes. To the affected machines, the instructions came from a trusted system they were set up to obey.

The two figures Kaseya gave show how that layering works. Its direct customers sat at one level. The businesses those service providers supported sat at the next.

An attack on the first level spread to the second. That is how an incident Kaseya measured in dozens of direct customers reached well over a thousand businesses further down the chain.

Most of the businesses hit were not Kaseya's own customers. Their exposure came through the service provider they had hired to run their IT. A single provider's console could carry the attack to the client machines it managed.

Software that holds administrative reach over many networks gathers that reach in one place. Whoever controls the console can use everything it was built to do.

Cobalt Strike is a commercial adversary-simulation tool that Raphael Mudge created for red teams. Cracked and stolen copies have since been widely used by ransomware crews and state-linked groups. A product built to test defenses became one used to defeat them.

Red teams are security staff or outside consultants hired to act like attackers. They try to break into an organization's network with its permission, then report what the defenders missed. Adversary-simulation software gives them a ready-made kit for that work. It lets testers mimic how real intruders behave instead of building every component themselves.

That design goal is the root of the problem. A tool built to reproduce real attacker behavior convincingly does the same job in an actual attack. The software has no way to tell an authorized tester from a criminal.

Mudge built Cobalt Strike as a commercial product meant for legitimate testers. Cracked copies are versions altered to get around the product's licensing controls. Stolen copies are genuine builds taken from the people entitled to use them. Either route puts the full toolkit in the hands of people the seller never meant to serve.

The tool's core component is Beacon, its payload. A payload is the software that runs on a machine after an intruder gets in. Beacon gives its operator a foothold on that machine. It checks back in with whoever controls it and waits for instructions. For a red team, that foothold shows how deep an attacker could reach. For a ransomware crew or a state-linked group, the same foothold is a working base inside the victim's network.

Defenders face a specific puzzle as a result. Spotting Cobalt Strike activity on a network does not, by itself, reveal whether an approved exercise or a real intrusion is underway. Security teams have to confirm whether anyone authorized a test before deciding how to respond.

The case also differs from attacks that depend on a software flaw or a tampered update. Nothing in Cobalt Strike had to break for it to be misused. The product did what it was designed to do. The only thing that changed was who was running it.

Mimikatz is a tool that pulls passwords and other authentication material out of a Windows computer's memory. Benjamin Delpy released it in 2011 to demonstrate weaknesses in how Windows handles credentials. Attackers later adopted it for credential theft and lateral movement.

The program targets something most Windows users never see. Passwords and other authentication material can sit in a computer's memory. Mimikatz extracts that material and hands it to whoever runs the tool.

Delpy's purpose was demonstration. The tool turned an abstract weakness in Windows credential handling into a concrete result. A defender running it on a machine could see what an intruder with the same access would be able to recover.

That visibility also made it useful to attackers. Intruders folded Mimikatz into their operations, where it served two purposes.

The first purpose is credential theft. A recovered password or piece of authentication material lets an intruder act as a legitimate user. Actions taken with valid credentials can look like ordinary activity on a network.

The second purpose is lateral movement. This is the process of spreading from one compromised computer to others on the same network. Credentials taken from the first machine can unlock the next one. Each new machine can then yield more credentials, and the cycle can repeat.

NotPetya used credential-harvesting techniques of this kind.

Mimikatz is an example of dual-use security software. The same output that shows a defender an exposure gives an attacker usable credentials. The tool did not create the weakness it exposed. It made that weakness straightforward to act on for anyone holding a copy.

EternalBlue is an exploit for a flaw in Microsoft $MSFT's SMB protocol, and it was widely reported to have been developed by the U.S. National Security Agency. A group called the Shadow Brokers leaked it in April 2017, and attackers used it in the WannaCry outbreak in May 2017 and later in NotPetya.

SMB, short for Server Message Block $SQ, is the protocol Windows computers use to share files and printers across a network. The flaw EternalBlue targets sits inside a service built for routine communication between machines.

EternalBlue moved from intelligence tool to publicly available attack code. Software written to break into systems did not stay with the people who built it. Once the Shadow Brokers released it, anyone with a copy of the leak could reuse it.

Microsoft had already issued a patch, MS17-010, in March 2017. The name is the bulletin number Microsoft assigned to its security update for the SMB flaw. That fix arrived about a month before the leak. Windows systems could therefore be patched before the exploit became public.

WannaCry spread roughly two months after the patch shipped. Computers that had applied MS17-010 were protected against this exploit. Computers that had not installed it remained exposed to the same flaw.

NotPetya later used EternalBlue as well. That made the exploit a shared component of two separate outbreaks. Each one depended on the same SMB flaw that MS17-010 had already addressed.

EternalBlue's origin sets it apart from tools sold commercially to security teams. It was not a product, a red-team framework or an IT management platform. It was an offensive capability, widely reported to originate with a government agency, that escaped into public circulation.

A patch protects only the systems that install it. The month between MS17-010 and the leak, and the weeks between the leak and WannaCry, formed the window that separated protected networks from exposed ones.

Brute Ratel C4 is a commercial command-and-control framework that Chetan Nair built for red teams. A cracked version circulated in 2022, and security researchers reported that malicious actors were using it in real attacks.

A command-and-control framework, often shortened to C2, is the software an operator uses to direct implants on compromised machines. It sends instructions and collects results. It also keeps the connection between the operator and those machines alive.

Red teams use this kind of tooling to play the part of an intruder. They do it during authorized tests of an organization's defenses, with the client's permission.

Brute Ratel was built to evade endpoint detection and response, or EDR, tools. EDR tools watch individual computers for suspicious behavior and alert defenders when they find it. For a red team, that evasion is the purpose of the product. A test only shows whether defenders can catch a skilled intruder if the simulated intruder behaves like one. A framework that slips past security products gives the client a realistic picture of its blind spots.

The same feature takes on a different meaning once the tool leaves licensed hands. A cracked version is a copy with its licensing protections stripped out. Anyone who obtains it can run it without paying for or registering it. The vendor then has no record of who holds that copy or what they use it for.

When a cracked copy of Brute Ratel circulated in 2022, its evasion features became available to people with no authorization to attack anyone. Those features did the same job in a real intrusion that they did in a sanctioned test.

Brute Ratel follows a pattern already seen with Cobalt Strike, another commercial red-team product whose cracked copies reached attackers. The two cases share one underlying tension. Software that convincingly imitates an attacker is, by design, useful to an actual attacker.

Bring your own vulnerable driver, or BYOVD, is a technique in which attackers load a legitimately signed but flawed driver onto a machine. The driver gives them kernel-level access, which they use to switch off endpoint detection tools.

A driver is software that lets an operating system talk to hardware or perform low-level tasks. On Windows, many drivers run in the kernel, the most privileged layer of the system. Code running there can see and change almost anything on the machine, including other security software.

That level of access is why Windows expects kernel drivers to carry a digital signature. The signature tells the operating system who published the code and that it has not been altered since. It does not tell the system whether the code contains a bug.

BYOVD exploits that gap. Rather than writing a malicious driver and trying to get it signed, an attacker brings along a real one that already passed the signing process. The file is genuine. The flaw inside it is what the attacker wants.

Once the vulnerable driver is loaded, the attacker abuses its weakness to carry out actions with kernel privileges. That can include shutting down the processes of endpoint detection and response products, often called EDR. Those tools are designed to resist being stopped by ordinary programs. Kernel access removes that protection.

Some of the drivers abused this way come from security and anti-malware products. A component built to help defend a computer becomes the means of blinding its defenses. That makes the technique a direct example of dual-use security software turned against the systems it was meant to protect.

BYOVD is usually not the first step of an intrusion. Loading a driver on Windows requires administrator rights. Attackers generally reach for it after they already have that foothold. Its job is to clear the way for whatever comes next, such as stealing data or deploying ransomware, while the monitoring tools are offline.

In January 2024, attackers exploited two vulnerabilities in Ivanti Connect Secure and Ivanti Policy Secure. Organizations use these products to control who gets into their networks. The flaws are tracked as CVE-2023-46805 and CVE-2024-21887.

Connect Secure is a VPN product. It sits at the edge of a network and decides which remote users may reach internal systems. That position is what made the flaws serious. The device meant to keep intruders out became the way in.

Policy Secure handles a related job at the same boundary. Both products belong to the category of perimeter security equipment. This is the gear that faces the internet on an organization's behalf.

A zero-day is a flaw that attackers exploit before the vendor has shipped a fix. Defenders have no patch to apply when it is first used against them. For owners of Connect Secure and Policy Secure devices, that meant the exposure could not be closed at first by the routine step of installing an update.

The U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, or CISA, responded with Emergency Directive 24-01 on Jan. 19, 2024. The directive ordered federal civilian agencies to disconnect the affected Ivanti products.

That order reversed the usual direction of network defense. Agencies typically add security appliances to reduce risk. Under this directive, the required step was to take the security appliance offline.

Taking a VPN gateway offline also cuts off the remote connections it exists to provide. The directive required agencies to accept that loss rather than keep the exposed devices running.

The Ivanti case differs from attacks that tamper with a vendor's software updates or abuse a stolen testing framework. Here the weakness sat inside the product itself. Attackers did not need to slip code into a vendor's release. They targeted flaws in equipment that was already installed and trusted at the network boundary.

In February 2024, ConnectWise disclosed critical flaws in ScreenConnect, its remote access software, including an authentication bypass tracked as CVE-2024-1709. Attackers exploited the flaw after the disclosure. A tool built for IT support became a route into the networks it was meant to help manage.

An authentication bypass is a specific kind of failure. It lets someone reach parts of an application that should sit behind a login without supplying valid credentials. In most software, that is a serious problem. In remote access software, the stakes are higher. The login is the main barrier between an outsider and every system the tool can reach.

A bypass also differs from a stolen password. The attacker does not need to guess, phish or buy anyone's credentials. Strong passwords and careful account hygiene offer little protection when the check that should demand them is skipped.

ScreenConnect belongs to the category of remote support tools. Software of this kind lets a technician connect to a computer from another location to view the screen, take control of it and fix problems. That job requires broad, trusted access to the machines the tool serves. An attacker who gets past the login inherits that access instead of having to build it.

This is why remote support tools are a favored entry point for intruders. The access already exists, and an organization granted it on purpose. Sessions run through a remote support tool can look like routine IT work. That makes an intruder harder to tell apart from a legitimate technician.

In this case, attackers moved after ConnectWise made the flaws public, not before. A disclosure tells defenders what needs fixing. It also tells attackers where to look. The period that follows is a race. Any ScreenConnect server left unfixed stays open to anyone who has read the same advisory.

Nothing about ScreenConnect had to be altered or smuggled out. A flaw in how the software checked who was allowed in was enough. That flaw turned the product's own legitimate access against the organizations that relied on it.

The U.S. government has restricted Kaspersky security software in two stages, citing the risk that the Russian government could exploit it. Kaspersky denies the allegations, and no confirmed attack is part of this case.

The first restriction came in 2017. That year, the Department of Homeland Security ordered federal agencies to remove Kaspersky products. The order applied to the government's own systems. It did not cover private companies or households.

The second restriction reached further. In June 2024, the U.S. Commerce Department announced a ban on selling Kaspersky software in the U.S. That step carried the issue past federal computers and into the wider U.S. market.

Taken together, the two steps shifted the focus from the federal government as a buyer to Kaspersky as a seller.

Both actions rested on the same stated concern about exploitation by the Russian government.

That concern follows from the job security software is built to do. An antivirus or anti-malware product needs broad access to the machine it protects. It inspects files and running programs. It also receives regular updates from its maker. That reach is what makes such a product useful to defenders. The same reach is why the U.S. government treated possible outside influence over the vendor as a national security question.

The concern centered on the vendor, not on a specific flaw in the code. No disclosed vulnerability or patch is part of this case. A patch can close a flaw, but it cannot answer a question about who stands behind the software.

This sets the Kaspersky case apart from most examples of security tools used as weapons. In those cases, attackers tampered with, stole, cracked or exploited a legitimate tool. Here, the issue is a policy judgment about trust in a vendor. The U.S. actions describe a risk of misuse. They do not establish that Kaspersky software was turned against its users.
