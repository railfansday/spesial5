10 Best Winter Hiking Boots

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Today’s winter hiking boots are waterproof, insulated, and compatible with traction aids such as microspikes and snowshoes. While 200-gram insulated mids are fine for commuting and half-day hikes down to about 10-20 degrees (F), we recommend buying boots with higher calf coverage and 400-gram insulation for all-day hikes and mountain climbs in colder temperatures. We are experienced winter hikers with hundreds of successful mountain summits, and we understand what it takes to stay safe and comfortable on long, challenging winter hikes.

Here are our top 10 winter hiking boot recommendations:

You can’t use your regular 3-season boots or shoes for winter hiking because they lack waterproofing and insulation. Most insulated winter hiking boots are guaranteed waterproof out of the box. Many have rubber or waterproof synthetic soles, so you don’t have to worry about water soaking into the fabric. If moisture does get into your boots, insulated winter boots will still keep your feet warm. The same isn’t true of leather hiking boots, even when treated with waterproofing creams and sprays. If your regular hiking boots absorb water in winter, they can freeze and cause frostbite.

Winter boots should be waterproof and insulated to keep your feet dry and warm, have laces for a secure fit, be comfortable enough to wear all day, and be compatible with traction aids like microspikes and snowshoes. While the insulation you need varies, winter boots with non-removable synthetic insulation are the lightest and therefore the easiest to hike in. Synthetic boots are also lighter and more waterproof than leather boots.

Some boot manufacturers provide temperature ratings, but there’s no standard way to measure how warm winter boots are. Understand that these ratings are estimates at best and that users will have varying experiences based on their activity level, health, weight, sex, metabolism, and other factors.

Most 400 g insulated winter hiking boots suit all-day winter hikes and snowshoeing in subzero temperatures. They have calf-high uppers, which makes them warmer than mids because more of your lower leg is covered by insulation. Many manufacturers claim they’ll keep your feet warm down to -40F, but that assumes you’re actively hiking, not sitting around doing nothing. Realistically, they work best in temperatures from 30F down to -15F.  If you plan to climb 4000-footers in winter in New Hampshire’s White Mountains, the Adirondacks, Maine, or Vermont, we recommend using 400 g boots.

Most 200 g winter hiking boots are ankle-high mids and retain less heat on the lower leg than calf-height 400g boots. On the other hand, they’re a little easier to hike in because they’re lighter and feel more like regular hiking boots. 200 g boots suit temperatures from 30°F to 10°F and are ideal for milder winter conditions and shorter hikes, where you’re less likely to get caught out after dark when temperatures drop.

Mountaineering boots have rigid soles that work with crampons that lack flexible center bars, for rugged mountains with steeper slopes or thick ice. You can hike in them, but it feels very different from a soft-soled 400g or 200g insulated winter hiking boot. Most insulated mountaineering boot manufacturers don’t rate their boots’ temperature ranges, so you have to rely on anecdotal word-of-mouth about how warm they are. If you hike across glaciers or go on trips led by mountaineering guides, it’s usually best to ask them which boots to bring. They may even rent you boots.

While you should take the manufacturer’s temperature ratings with a grain of salt, they are a good indicator of a boot’s relative warmth. It’s difficult to compare warmth between boots with different insulation, but knowing how much insulation a boot has can help you compare models from the same manufacturer.

In addition to providing more protection, a beefy toe cap won’t collapse when you wear microspikes or crampons.

Your boots’ soles should provide good traction on loose or packed snow. Look for boots with a deep tread, like a Vibram sole, though you’ll likely augment your boot with microspikes.

Look for boots with a soft cuff that closes off the gap between your leg and boot.

A gaiter ring is a small ring attached to the top of the boot that you can hook your gaiters onto. It’s not the end of the world if your boots don’t have one since you can still hook the gaiter to your boot laces.
