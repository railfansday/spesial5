The Stock Market Is Sounding an Alarm, But History Has Good News for Investors

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Even high valuations can be used as opportunities.

By David Dierking – Nov 19, 2026 at 5:45AM EST

10 stocks we like better than SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust ›

Two of Wall Street's best-known valuation metrics are both signaling warnings.

The Shiller cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio, which measures the value of the S&P 500 (^GSPC +0.76%) relative to inflation-adjusted earnings over the past 10 years, is at 40 right now. The only other time it was at this level in the past 150 years was during the peak of the 2000 tech bubble.

The Buffett indicator, named for a metric popularized by Warren Buffett that measures the value of U.S. stocks relative to U.S. gross domestic product (GDP), is also at (or near) all-time highs. That number is around 250%, far above where it's been at any point in its history.

A lot of headlines suggest that these are potential disaster signals for the market. But they're not predictors. They're merely valuation measures, and as we've seen over the past several years, high valuations don't mean they can't go higher.

In fact, there's good news to be found in some other financial metrics.

You probably know what happened following the tech bubble peak. After a record high in March 2000, it took the S&P 500 roughly 13 years to reach another new all-time high that it was able to sustain (It hit a new high in October 2007, but the Great Recession busted it down again almost immediately).

The Shiller CAPE ratio went from 44 at the tech bubble peak all the way down to 14 at the financial crisis bottom, the lowest it had been to that point since the 1980s.

The Buffett indicator nearly touched 150% during the tech bubble peak, which was a record at the time, but it briefly fell below 60% during the financial crisis. The Buffett indicator did, however, hit a new all-time high back in the late 2010s and has headed steadily higher since. The only real interruption was the 2022 bear market.

Here's the good news. If you look at the time frame from the peak of the tech bubble through today, the S&P 500 is up just over 700%. That translates to around 8% growth annually. Yes, even with 13 years of essentially no return on investment, an investment in large-cap U.S. stocks still produced an average annual return that wasn't that far off from its long-term average of 10%.

I'd reiterate that the Shiller CAPE ratio and the Buffett indicator are valuation measures. They're not recession predictors or bear market signals. They simply tell you how expensive stocks are relative to a baseline at any moment in time.

Plus, these two numbers aren't the only way to value the U.S. stock market. If you consider the forward price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio, which looks at forecasted earnings for the next year, the State Street SPDR S&P 500 ETF (SPY +0.84%) trades at just 19 times the next 12 months' earnings. That's the lowest it's been since the end of 2023. The Invesco S&P 500 Equal Weight ETF (RSP -0.26%) trades at a forward P/E ratio of just 16.7.

In other words, not every valuation measure is flashing warnings. If companies can deliver on earnings over the next 12 months, there's no reason to think that a big valuation contraction is imminent.

I wouldn't make any radical changes to your portfolio's asset allocation. If you'd sold stocks when valuations looked high over the past several years, you would have missed out on a gain of more than 100% since the beginning of 2023 for the S&P 500.

The biggest takeaway is that nobody knows what stocks will do next. So there's little sense in trying to trade your brokerage account as if you do. As long as your long-term asset allocation is consistent with your long-term goals, there's little reason to make any big changes to it.

The S&P 500 still works well as a portfolio core. Even if the index does fall sharply from here, continuing with regular contributions into your brokerage or retirement accounts can help buy shares at discount prices, lower your average cost basis, and actually improve your portfolio's long-term returns.
