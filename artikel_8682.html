Garmin’s Best Cycling Radars Are Discounted for Black Friday—Here’s How the RearVue 820 and RTL515 Compare, and Which Is Best for Your Rides

The benchmark Varia RTL515 is cheaper, but the RearVue 820 adds range, battery, and smarter tracking.

Cycling radar entirely changed my bike rides. After a speeding pickup passed dangerously close to me on a ride in June 2025, I installed a radar system on my road bike. Since getting a radar, I have ridden over 7,500 miles on paved and gravel roads, and I’ve had a radar mounted on my bike for almost all of them—I cannot imagine riding without it. It’s the best safety upgrade I’ve made in more than three decades of riding and something every road and gravel rider should try.

For Black Friday, Garmin—the market leader in cycling radar—has its two top-performing Varia radar systems on sale. This includes the benchmark RTL515 and the new RearVue 820 released earlier this year. Both are exceptional radar units, and our top-tested models—but which one is best for you, or as a holiday gift for a cyclist in your life?

All cycling radar systems work essentially the same. The radar unit emits pulses of electromagnetic radio waves that bounce off vehicles approaching from behind before returning to the device. Using the reflected beams, the device calculates and tracks vehicles’ distance, direction, and relative speed, and sends that tracking data to your cycling computer. The head unit then provides an audible warning and graphically displays vehicles’ relative location and speed, giving you time to react accordingly. Most devices also integrate the radar with an LED taillight to alert drivers to your presence.

What separates good radar units from bad ones is the proprietary algorithms each brand uses to tune sensitivity and detection for their respective devices. A good radar consistently provides clear warnings of approaching vehicles, but bad ones can provide frequent false alarms, or worse, fail to warn of an approaching vehicle.

In the year and a half since I started using a radar light on my rides, I’ve ridden a dozen radar systems from nine brands. Several work well and I use them on my bikes, and a few of them were duds. However, of all of the cycling radars we’ve tried, none are as consistently reliable as Garmin’s Varia models.

Picking the Varia model that’s best for you (or a rider whose safety you care about) depends on a few factors. Both are excellent units, but why overpay for tech you can’t use or don’t need on your rides?

The RTL515 is the benchmark cycling radar system, and we judge all others against it. The 515 works more reliably than any other radar we tested—Other systems might have longer battery life, weigh less, or pack a brighter light, but none are as well-rounded or as easy to use as this Garmin. It detects vehicles up to 153 yards away, and its 65-lumen flash is visible to drivers from 1.2 miles.

The 515 packs up to 8 hours of battery life per charge, and weighs 72 grams (without a mount). It connects to most cycling computers featuring Bluetooth or ANT+ compatibility, but Garmin’s Varia radar phone app is also stellar. If you ride with your phone on your bar, it can serve as a display, and it can deliver audio notifications through headphones even if you’re not listening to music or a podcast.

Garmin’s newest cycling radar improves on the Varia RTL515’s greatness. Compared to the 515, the RearVue 820 has more battery life (up to 15 hours), a brighter flashing light (100 lumens), and greater vehicle-detection range (up to 191 yards). Plus, the 820 adds a brake-light feature that increases rear light intensity as you slow.

Crucially, the RearVue 820 incorporates features found on no other radars (from Garmin or any other brand). While other radars often drop vehicles when they slow down behind you, the 820 tracks vehicles traveling at your same speed; it’s a handy feature you don’t know you need until you try it. Additionally, if you use a Garmin Edge X40- or X50-series computer, the radar tracks a vehicle that changes lanes behind you. It also shows icons to indicate each vehicle’s size (small, medium, or large).

The RCT715 is basically the RTL515 with a rear-facing camera. However, it is larger, heavier, and has a shorter battery life than the 515. It’s so heavy (148 grams) that it requires a proprietary mount. The camera also greatly reduces battery life—in continuous video mode, it might not last through long rides. However, the 715 is worth considering for bike commuters, riders who advocate for better cycling infrastructure, or those who want peace of mind.
