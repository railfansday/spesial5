How Becoming a Parent Can Change Your Memories of Your Own Parents

It was around Christmas time when Emily* noticed that something was wrong. She had just welcomed her second son in September and still felt emotionally raw from the experience. Part of Emily’s postpartum planning had revolved around having her mum look after her three-year-old while she took care of the new baby. But every time she visited her parents, something in her tensed up, her mind went foggy, and instead of feeling the safety and relaxation she had been craving, she felt on edge.

Emily sought help from me in therapy just before Christmas when she realized that the prospect of spending the holidays with her parents was making it difficult for her to sleep and the few precious hours she had between feeds were taken up with anxious rumination. She couldn’t make any sense of it. It was like a switch had flipped and the previously supportive, albeit slightly emotionally disconnected, relationship she had had with her parents was in pieces.

I work a lot with mothers and birthing people in the perinatal phase, and the change they experience in their relationship with their parents is something that comes up very frequently. Sometimes this change is positive, where they become more understanding of parents who were trying their best at the time. Sometimes it’s disorienting, like Emily’s, and the relationship seems to worsen. Either way, it’s important to meet yourself with compassion and curiosity when unexpected feelings or memories resurface during this period of your life and try not to go through this transition on your own.

Emily came to see me for help with anxiety and insomnia, and a few sessions into our therapy she started to talk about memories that had been appearing for her, scenes from her childhood that suddenly had taken on new meaning or had popped up out of nowhere. It was as if her memories had changed, which made Emily feel crazy and even more anxious because she felt like she couldn’t trust herself anymore.

In the crudest terms, we tend to think of memory as a filing cabinet—a place where scenes, facts, annoying songs, and embarrassing moments are stored to be recalled, like when we try to remember what we had for dinner last week or where we went on holiday three years ago.

When I talk about memory with my clients, they usually understand that it is not a perfect system, that it can alter over time and be very subjective. What is harder to convey is that decades of research suggest that memory is not in fact a filing cabinet or a library catalogue—it’s a construction. Every time I try to remember where we went on holiday three summers ago, my brain doesn’t retrieve but rather constructs a memory of our trip to Italy. That means that each time we recall something, that memory can change slightly or take on new meaning. Researchers Daniel L. Schacter and Donna Rose Addis found that, in fact, remembering and imagining look very similar in terms of activation in the brain.

Why does that matter? It can help make sense of what happened to Emily and what happens to many of my clients when they become parents and their relationships with their own parents change. Some of this is explained by a shift in roles: When a child becomes a parent, the dynamic of the child-parent relationship naturally has to change, which can be difficult for both parties. Grandparents and new parents might find it challenging to adapt to who is in charge and “who knows best.” But sometimes the difficulty is more subtle, and it’s related to memory.

Emily struggled because she had started to have visceral memories of herself at about three to five years old, even though she had never actively thought about her childhood much before. She associated that phase in her life with a feeling of paralysis that she couldn’t explain. She told me how exhausted she felt every time she left her parents’ house now, like she had been holding her breath for hours.

Emily knew from whispered family stories that her mother had dealt with bouts of depression through much of her childhood. She also knew that her parents’ marriage hadn’t always been as placid and quiet as it was now. She had always had memories of them fighting late at night but had never thought much of it. Now she remembered her mother in tears, shouting and doors slamming. She remembered hiding under her bed.

Memory is heavily influenced by our current state of mind. Martin Conway, in his papers on autobiographical memory, explains that when we try to retrieve a memory or remember something spontaneously, we construct that memory in the moment, partly based on our current goals. If we are hungry, we might construct a memory of a cafe we saw on our walk two weeks ago. If we have a fight with our partner, we might suddenly remember all the times they disappointed us, when we usually are able to accept their imperfections.

When Emily became a parent, her goals changed dramatically, and it changed what memories her brain predicted might be useful for her.

Emily realized that her experience of being her son’s age was one of bracing and zoning out because she had felt disconnected from her mother and scared of her father at the time. As a parent, Emily was suddenly able to attune to what it felt like for her to be three years old. Her three-year-old self did not feel safe, and she did not believe her child was safe, either.

Conway notes that the memories we are able to construct have to be coherent with how we think of ourselves, our beliefs about ourselves, and the world and people in it. According to Conway’s theory, our brains filter, rationalize, or dismiss memories that are inconsistent with who we believe we are.

Before Emily had children, she believed, largely unconsciously, that her childhood experiences were irrelevant to who she was and that she hadn’t really been affected by them. When she became a mum, her visceral memories of childhood fear did not match up with who she believed she was anymore. Her goals had changed from surviving and rationalizing to tuning in to and protecting her child. Her brain was telling her she had to shield her child from something that she had experienced as frightening as a child.

Other clients of mine have found that becoming a parent had the opposite effect. They were able to look at the younger versions of their parents with more compassion and kindness, reconstructing memories through the eyes of an exhausted parent who was trying and barely coping.

Karen and her parents had had a difficult relationship. Karen’s parents divorced when she was young, and Karen had always felt her mother had been unavailable when she was a child. She experienced a lot of anger when thinking about her childhood. She had memories of having to take on responsibilities too early, looking after her siblings, and cooking and cleaning while her mother was at work. Karen had purposefully moved away from her parents and had spent years asserting her independence. She had never wanted children because she had such strong memories of what it had been like to be a young caretaker and not being able to feel like a child. When she met her husband, she realized that creating her own family was more important to her than she had thought.

Karen came to see me for anxiety. She was worried about losing her freedom when she became a mother, and we thought about how her childhood experiences shaped her experience now. I saw Karen for quite some time after she gave birth, and something shifted for her when she became a parent. She remembered instances of seeing her mother frazzled, rushed, and overwhelmed and how hard she had tried to find time to be there for Karen and her siblings. Karen started to realize how impossible it must have been for her mother to raise three children on her own while working.

Karen tried to talk to her mother and learned about how ashamed she was for burdening her daughter and unsure about whether Karen wanted her in her life. To her surprise, Karen experienced her mother’s relationship with her own daughter as reparative. She could make sense of her anger and her need for freedom and see that what happened in her childhood was not something her mother had intended.

For Emily, too, her experiences meant she had to rebuild the relationship with her mother and find a more honest foundation. She was able to have conversations with her about what it would have been like for Emily growing up, and, to Emily’s surprise, her mother also listened and apologized for not having checked in with her sooner. It started a healing process for Emily that involved listening to what her three-year-old self might have felt and acknowledging that she had been trying to rationalize her way out of feeling for most of her life. She had to slowly let herself experience that her parents were safe now; she decided to not rely on them for child care but let the relationship grow more slowly.

What helped Emily was hearing her mother validate what happened to her as a child. It was something Emily would have needed when she was younger. She was also able to make new meaning of her relationship with her parents through therapy—an act of shared social construction with another human, the therapist, that fulfills some of our core needs of being heard, seen, and understood.

Karen similarly needed to feel heard by her mother. Her anger dissipated when she was able to put herself into her mother’s shoes and see that her and her mother’s experience could exist side by side.

What can you do if parenthood is shifting your relationship with your own parents? Here are steps that I have found are helpful for my clients.

Find support. The first step in coping with a difficult experience is to not try to go through it alone. We are social beings who need to feel understood by others. Seek out a friend or a sibling if you are struggling with feelings you do not understand.

Meet yourself with self-compassion. When strong emotions come up, we might tell ourselves, “I’m overreacting,” “I’m too sensitive,” “I just need to get on with things and stop complaining.” Self-criticism can perpetuate a cycle in which we invalidate our experiences until they become impossible to ignore. It also won’t help to be overly critical of yourself if you believe you’ve been judging your parents too harshly, now that you have the perspective of being a parent yourself.

Seek out conversations. If thinking back on your childhood memories brings up a perspective change on your childhood, speak to your family, your parents or siblings, and learn about how they experienced that time and you as a child. It might help you feel more compassion with your parents who may have been struggling, and can also help you understand yourself better as a person.

Set boundaries, if needed. If memories from your childhood suddenly take on new meaning, it might be important to think about boundaries and clear rules to set with your child’s grandparents. If you felt unsafe or uncomfortable as a child, it is OK to make it clear how you expect the adults in your child’s life to behave. This can, of course, be easier said than done, and it can be useful to seek help to deal with feelings of guilt or anxiety around setting boundaries with your parents.

Seek out professional help. Remember that every experience is subjective—your version of what happened might be different from that of your siblings or your parents. That does not make it any less valid. While Emily was lucky that her mother acknowledged her experience, your family members might not always be so receptive and might be more defensive about any wrongdoing. Seeking professional help can be especially useful when you are dealing with a family system that is less likely to take responsibility or want to change.

Becoming a parent changes your world in a thousand ways and can dramatically alter the way you look at your own childhood. Self-compassion is hugely important in navigating all the transformations you might go through as a new parent. Finding others that meet you with understanding and care is the first step in making sure you can meet yourself and your child in the same way.

*The stories in this article are a combination of experiences of several clients; any identifying information has been changed.
