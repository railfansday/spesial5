Hyundai Facing Class-Action Lawsuit Over Driver Monitoring System's Alleged False Alarms

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

According to the lawsuit, Hyundai knew its driver monitoring cameras had issues, but suggested drivers try fixes like different eyeglasses.

Automakers are constantly striving to make cars safer. New technologies such as automatic emergency braking, lane-keep assist, and driver monitoring systems were all developed to prevent crashes. However, according to a recent class-action lawsuit, one of those technologies designed to keep us out of trouble may be causing more trouble than it’s worth.

According to ClassAction.org, a 70-page complaint filed September 16 in the U.S. District Court for the Central District of California alleges that Hyundai's Direct Driver Monitoring System (DDMS) in the 2024-2026 Hyundai Santa Fe, 2025-2026 Ioniq 5, 2025-2026 Tucson, 2025-2026 Santa Cruz, and 2026 Palisade incorrectly identifies attentive drivers as distracted. The plaintiffs argue that this goes far beyond simple annoying warning messages, as the vehicles could also disable the cruise control system and even trigger unexpected automatic braking.

The logic behind Hyundai's DDMS is sound. According to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, 315,167 people were injured by distracted driving in 2024 alone. Hyundai is attempting to reduce this number by using their DDMS to check for signs of distracted driving, with the setup working with the Forward Attention Warning (FAW) system to monitor the driver’s eyes, face and head position using a camera. When it detects what it believes to be distracted driving, it will alert the driver; if the system repeatedly detects an inattentive driver while cruise control is engaged, it can deactivate cruise control and initiate an emergency stop, bringing the vehicle to a halt.

But several Hyundai owners claim the DDMS is flagging drivers who are fully alert and paying attention to the road. According to the plaintiffs in this case, Hyundai knew about this potential flaw nearly two years ago when the company issued technical service bulletin 25-GI-001H in January 2025. According to the lawsuit, Hyundai’s proposed solution to the problem was that drivers “need to wear different sunglasses or glasses, adjust their seats, and look at the road.”

The lawsuit states that Hyundai's own owner's manuals acknowledge that its driver monitoring system may malfunction under a surprisingly wide range of normal driving conditions: wearing sunglasses, prescription glasses, heavy makeup, or a hat can interfere with the camera; so can bright sunlight, laughing, squinting, turning your head, or simply being too tall or too short. Even a bumpy road or the position of your hands on the steering wheel can reportedly confuse the system. (In other words, quite a few things people routinely do while driving could potentially trigger false warnings.)

The lawsuit seeks damages and other relief for affected Hyundai owners, but the allegations have yet to be proven in court.
