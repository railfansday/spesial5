Modernizing Nursery Rhymes for the 21st Century

Back in the 1960s when I was growing up, nursery rhymes were an initial gateway to music for children. Their simple melodies, repeated lyrics, and predictable rhyming schemes gently introduced infants and toddlers to the fundamentals of music. What the words actually meant was of little, if any, concern to us young listeners. But times change and these traditional rhymes fell out of favor.

Revitalizing these classic verses—by preserving their developmental benefits while modernizing their themes—has become the life's passion of early childhood music educator Pam Gittleman. Raised in a musical family, Gittleman was a talented flutist through her college years. However, she did not plan on a career in music. After earning an MBA in marketing, she spent a decade in the corporate sector at a prominent advertising agency before leaving to raise her family.

Drawing on a desire to work with children, she leveraged her musical background to reinvent herself as a preschool music teacher. Over seven years at a summer day camp, she experimented with various teaching methods for children aged three to five, ultimately designing a curriculum centered on singing, movement, and call-and-response activities. As her own children grew up, she pivoted to teaching at nursery schools during the academic year.

Over the years, Gittleman noticed a striking trend: the young children in her classrooms were unfamiliar with the traditional nursery rhymes that prior generations knew by heart. Curious, she questioned parents and fellow educators and discovered that the internet had changed how families vetted content. Today’s parents were consulting childhood development experts and sharing their findings online. Many of them found traditional nursery rhymes to be outdated, dark, and inappropriate for young minds, so they avoided the genre entirely.

While recognizing the validity of these concerns, Gittleman’s professional training and instincts told her that the time-honored nursery rhymes still held value for teaching language skills as well as social and emotional intelligence. To bridge this gap, she began rewriting the lyrics.

Her venture received a major boost in 2020 when she enrolled in the year-long Advanced Leadership Initiative (ALI) at Harvard University. Despite pandemic-related disruptions, she had access to advanced graduate courses in education, thus establishing a firm theoretical foundation for how music and storytelling contribute to social and emotional learning (SEL) in early childhood.

Gittleman emphasizes that the linguistic structure of nursery rhymes is vital for early language acquisition. For instance, in the verses of “This old man, he played one, he played knick-knack on my thumb," children begin to learn the concept of rhyming. Educators can then challenge them to find other words that rhyme with "one," effectively expanding their vocabulary. Furthermore, because "one" and "thumb" don’t rhyme strictly speaking, children also learn that the concept of rhyming has flexibility.

Since many nursery rhymes are songs—set in a rhythmic structure and vocalized to musical notes—they stimulate important neurological pathways. Rhythm, for example, is processed simultaneously in the auditory and motor regions of the brain. Nursery rhymes capitalize on this connection, helping children regulate their motor functions through alternating periods of movement and rest. Gittleman notes that mastering this physical self-regulation translates to better behavioral and social functioning with others.1

Nursery rhymes can introduce basic math skills, too. Returning to the example of “This Old Man,” the song helps children learn to count, reaching ever higher numbers as they grow older. The rhymes are also repetitive—as children catch on to patterns, they learn to predict and reproduce them on their own. The excitement and anticipation of what comes next feeds the motivation and reward centers of the brain, allowing young children to experience joy while making music and learning language.

Yet another contribution of nursery rhymes is found in call-and-response verses. The call-and-response structure mirrors conversational patterns, teaching young children the social importance of taking turns.2

Equipped with decades of classroom experience along with academic knowledge obtained through her program at Harvard, Gittleman has systematically updated classic lyrics to be of service to social and emotional learning.3 Prioritizing kindness, empathy, and gratitude, she transformed "Three Blind Mice" to "Three Kind Mice" and, under her pen, the story of “Humpty Dumpty” has been reimagined so that when he falls off the wall his friends come to his aid, rather than leaving him in pieces on the ground.

Gittleman has since published several volumes of her work under the title Nursery Rhymes for Kinder Times®.4,5 These books feature engaging illustrations for young children and structured text to help growing children develop literacy skills. Pairing each rhyme with emojis and guiding questions, she provides adults with tools to enhance their children’s engagement with the stories while nurturing rich conversations that support emotional intelligence. When she introduced the curriculum to a local preschool, an informal survey of parents and teachers confirmed a positive impact on the children's social and emotional learning.6

Seeking to expand her reach, Gittleman utilized her advertising background to pitch her updated rhymes to the music industry. Her efforts won major support in 2022 when legendary children's performer Raffi—along with his protege, Lindsay Munroe—recorded 15 of her modernized rhymes. Since then, prominent children's recording artist Laurie Berkner has also recorded some of her material.
