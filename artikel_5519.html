What 1,000 U.S. real estate ads reveal about lead generation

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Reviewing 1,000 active U.S. residential Meta ads found pricing mentioned in 38% of ads, while explicit qualification cues appeared in 7.7%. The findings suggest lead handling and follow up should differ by offer type and destination.  AI Summary

In Texas, a homebuilder advertised new homes starting around $240,000 and sent visitors to its community website. A property search service offered free listing alerts through a Meta Instant Form. Both wanted buyer inquiries, yet the buyers were signing up for different experiences. I would hesitate to hand those contacts to an agent as if they meant the same thing.

I reviewed 1,000 active residential real estate ads across 20 U.S. regions, collected from Meta’s public Ad Library on October 15, 2026. Buyer-focused ads accounted for 73% of the sample. The sharpest divide was between property-search and lead-generation pages, which used Instant Forms in 66% of their ads, and homebuilders, where that figure was 4%. It is a striking difference for businesses competing for many of the same people.

The sample excluded rentals, commercial properties, mortgage services and unrelated promotions. I capped the number of ads from each Facebook page so large advertisers would not overwhelm the results, then classified the intended audience, advertiser type, offer, written price information, qualification cues and click destination. The data went through additional checks before the figures were calculated.

Individual listings made up 29% of the sample, new construction 27.5%, and broader home-search offers roughly 17%. An inquiry on a specific listing gives an agent an immediate subject for a conversation. Someone requesting alerts for an entire neighborhood may have no property in mind and could be months from making a decision.

When I look at a lead-generation campaign, that difference shapes my advice more than the choice between a form and a website. I want to know whether the sales team is set up for property inquiries or for a longer search relationship. Those are different workloads, even if both begin with a name and a phone number.

Seller advertising offered another contrast. It accounted for 19% of the ads, with agents and teams responsible for 41% of that group and cash homebuyers for 27%. One Utah cash buyer cited a $420,000 sale completed in 33 days, a claim I could not independently verify. Its offer emphasized selling without repairs or a traditional listing. An agent replying with a generic home-valuation ad would be competing against a much more specific promise.

Price or monthly-payment information appeared in the written copy of 38% of ads. For individual listings it was 60%; for new-construction offers, 53%. Among broad buyer campaigns, it fell to 22%. Only 9% of seller-focused ads mentioned a price-related figure.

I was more interested in what the copy left unsaid. Just 7.7% of ads included an explicit qualifying cue, such as budget, financing readiness or purchase timing. For ads using Meta Instant Forms, the figure was about 10%. We did not inspect the questions inside those forms, so I would not claim advertisers skipped qualification altogether.

Still, an agent cannot respond intelligently to every inquiry in the same way. If a campaign advertises homes within a defined price range, I would rather see that range stated clearly than leave the salesperson to discover a basic mismatch on the first call. With monthly-payment offers, financing assumptions deserve equal attention.

Overall, 51% of ads led to websites or landing pages, 30% used Meta Instant Forms, and about 5% invited conversations through Messenger, Instagram or WhatsApp. Among builders, 88% sent traffic to their websites. A development page can explain a property before anyone speaks to sales; an instant form can capture interest sooner.

Neither choice tells us how well a campaign performs. But it does affect the work that follows. A search service can use saved listings and alerts to keep a buyer engaged. If a small brokerage has no process for staying in touch, borrowing that same offer may fill its CRM with people it will never properly serve.

My first check would happen after the click. I would submit the form myself, open the resulting CRM record and see what the agent actually receives. Does it identify the property that attracted the buyer? Could someone on the team call back without asking the prospect to repeat the entire inquiry? If the answer is no, I would fix that before buying more traffic.

For listing ads, I would test putting a realistic price range in the written copy, then listen to the conversations it produces. The point is to find out whether prospects arrive with clearer expectations. A higher cost per inquiry could be acceptable if more of those conversations lead to relevant showings.

For a broad search offer, I would make sure the team has a reason to contact people again. Asking about purchase timing may be helpful if an agent uses the answer to plan follow-up. Adding five questions to a form that nobody reads solves nothing.

I also would not copy a large search platform’s campaign simply because its ads are everywhere. It may have an entire operation built around people who are still browsing. A brokerage with a handful of agents needs a funnel suited to the people it can actually help. That is the decision I would make before choosing the ad format.

Oleh Odukalets is a performance marketer with seven years of experience in paid acquisition and more than $1 million in real estate ad spend managed across international markets.
