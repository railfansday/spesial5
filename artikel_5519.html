T. Rowe Price warned Treasury yields won't peak until a recession hits

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Arif Husain, the firm's head of global fixed income, said the Fed could raise rates until something breaks

T. Rowe Price $TROW said Treasury yields won't peak until the U.S. economy enters a recession, according to Bloomberg.

"Yields won't truly peak until there is a recession," said Arif Husain, who leads global fixed income at the firm, in a report, according to Bloomberg. "There is a chance that the Fed could hike until something breaks — essentially an overtightening policy mistake."

Husain regards inflation as the bigger near-term worry, ahead of the country's fiscal problems. He said the Federal Reserve may need to lift borrowing costs higher than markets currently anticipate.

He also flagged the possibility of a bear flattening in the U.S. yield curve, in which short-dated debt sells off harder than long-dated debt. He added that it would hardly shock him if the curve inverted "in the relatively near future."

Husain pointed to another potential trigger for yields topping out: developed-market governments taking tangible action to get their finances in order. "With yields on French government bonds climbing rapidly in October — to the point that some French corporate bonds yielded less than the sovereign — I'm watching to see if any other countries are learning the lesson," he wrote, according to Bloomberg.

Husain said the Treasury gains in October came as traders closed out bearish bets, not because investors were rushing back to havens. He said he would need to see "a lot more evidence — outside of a seasonally illiquid period" to believe the safe-haven explanation, according to Bloomberg.

The benchmark 10-year yield reached a peak of 5.36% this year, driven by inflation worries stoked by the Iran war. It then slid to roughly 4.86% after a selloff in technology shares drew investors back to sovereign debt. Husain initially floated a 6% 10-year yield less than two years ago, at a point when the market was tilting toward Fed rate cuts.

Yields had been hitting 24-year highs of 5.365% on the 10-year and 5.733% on the 30-year before pulling back after well-received government bond auctions. Money markets were pricing in three rate increases over the following 12 months.

Consumer prices then rose 3.7% over the 12 months ending in September, above the 3.6% economists had forecast. The 10-year yield rose to 5.304% that day.
