8 winter workouts and how many calories they burn

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These winter workouts that burn calories range from skiing and ice skating to shoveling snow, with AccuWeather's calorie estimates for each of the eight activities

Cold weather changes where people exercise more than whether they can. Snow, ice and short days close off some routines, but they open up others. Many of the activities that define winter in northern climates ask a lot of the body, from gliding across a frozen pond to clearing a driveway after a storm.

This guide covers eight winter workouts that burn calories, drawn from a longer list of cold-weather activities published by AccuWeather. The eight include sports such as skiing, snowboarding and hockey. They also include snowshoeing and ice skating, along with running, hiking and walking. The final entry covers outdoor chores that AccuWeather also counts as exercise: shoveling snow, chopping wood and raking leaves.

For each activity, this article gives AccuWeather's calorie estimate. Most of those figures are expressed per hour. Raking is the exception. The source gives that range without a time unit. The estimates cover a wide spread. Walking starts at 200 calories an hour, the low end of AccuWeather's range for it. Running reaches as high as 1,200 calories an hour at the top of its range. For cross-country skiing, the source notes that dedicated skiers sometimes pass 1,000 calories an hour.

These numbers are estimates, not fixed values. AccuWeather does not state the body weight, intensity or method behind any of them. The calories a person burns will vary with factors the source does not spell out. The figures offer a rough basis for comparing activities, not a precise count for any single workout.

The slides follow the order in which AccuWeather presents the activities. That order is not a ranking. Each slide stands on its own. Readers can go straight to the activity that fits their location, budget or schedule. Some entries, such as hockey, revolve around a rink. Others fit into daily life, such as walking the dog or running errands on foot.

AccuWeather also offers one general caution. It warns that anyone exercising outdoors in winter should dress for the weather to avoid hypothermia and frostbite.

Skiing of any kind, whether downhill or cross-country, can burn 500 or more calories in an hour, according to AccuWeather. That one estimate covers both forms of the sport.

The figure climbs for the most committed athletes. AccuWeather reports that dedicated cross-country skiers sometimes go past 1,000 calories per hour. The outlet presents that as a high-end figure for serious devotees of cross-country skiing.

Neither number is fixed. AccuWeather does not give the body weight, pace or effort level behind its skiing estimates, so an individual skier's burn will differ.

Cross-country skiing has two main methods. CBC describes the classic method as the traditional style of the sport. A classic skier glides along parallel tracks with a rhythmic motion that resembles running or walking.

The second method is skate skiing. Skate skiers push off their skis at an angle instead of gliding straight ahead. CBC compares that motion to the stride a hockey player uses on the ice.

Snowboarding burns an estimated 300 to 450 calories per hour, according to AccuWeather. That range falls below the outlet's estimate for skiing, which it puts at 500 or more calories an hour for both downhill and cross-country skiing.

Even the top of the snowboarding range, 450 calories an hour, sits under the 500-calorie floor AccuWeather gives for skiing.

AccuWeather describes snowboarding as less intense than skiing. It also presents the sport as more oriented toward fun than skiing is.

Ice hockey burns an estimated 400 to 600 calories an hour, according to AccuWeather. The source sets hockey apart from the other winter workouts it lists as the one built around a team.

The estimate spans 200 calories from its low end to its high end. AccuWeather gives the same 400 to 600 range for snowshoeing.

AccuWeather points to two ways to get into a game. The first is to join a team that plays at a rink. That route connects a player with an existing group and a place to play.

The second route is less formal. A player rounds up friends who have their own gear and organizes a game with them.

Both routes depend on other people to make a game happen. AccuWeather does not say how many players a game needs, how often rink teams play or what joining one costs.

Snowshoeing burns an estimated 400 to 600 calories an hour, according to AccuWeather. The forecaster presents it as a beginner-friendly alternative to skiing that costs less.

The equipment is simple to put on. Snowshoes go on over a regular pair of winter boots.

AccuWeather's estimate for snowshoeing is the same 400-to-600 range it gives for ice hockey.

Snow depth changes the workload. The National Park Service notes that snowshoeing a trail can take far more effort than walking the same trail on foot without snow. How much more depends on how deep the snow lies.

Particularly deep snow calls for a different technique. The park service recommends the kick-step for those conditions. The snowshoer kicks the foot while lifting it. That motion shakes extra snow off the front of the snowshoe.

The basic walking motion also changes on snowshoes. They force a wider stride than a normal walk. The park service warns that this wider gait can lead to achy hips.

Snow builds up on the gear itself, too. The agency advises stopping now and then to clear clumps stuck in the snowshoe's spikes. A trekking pole can help with that task, according to the park service.

Running burns an estimated 600 to 1,200 calories per hour, according to AccuWeather. The weather company treats running as a year-round workout. A routine built in spring or summer does not have to stop when temperatures fall.

The top of that range is double the bottom. The low end, 600 calories, matches the upper limit of the 400 to 600 calories an hour AccuWeather estimates for both hockey and snowshoeing.

Running's range overlaps with AccuWeather's hiking estimate of 430 to 650 calories an hour only between 600 and 650. Its low end also sits above the 470-calorie ceiling AccuWeather gives for walking. Ice skating, which AccuWeather puts at up to 700 calories an hour with real effort, tops out near the bottom of running's range.

Winter changes the ground underfoot. AccuWeather advises runners to plan their routes with icy patches in mind before heading out.

Timing is its second point. Temperature and visibility both shift over the course of a day. AccuWeather suggests weighing both when choosing a time to run.

Clothing is the third consideration. AccuWeather recommends breathable layers for cold-weather runs. AccuWeather's three tips for winter runners cover the route, the hour and the clothing.

Footwear matters on slick surfaces too. The National Institute on Aging recommends nonskid, rubber-soled shoes with low heels for people exercising outdoors in snow and ice. That kind of shoe makes a slip less likely.

The institute also lists precautions for anyone exercising outside in the cold. It recommends learning the signs of hypothermia. It also advises telling others where you will be and carrying a fully charged mobile phone.

The National Institute on Aging suggests considering an indoor workout when it is very windy, cold, icy or wet outside. An online workout video or a person's own routine can fill in on those days.

Ice skating can burn up to 700 calories an hour, according to AccuWeather. That estimate applies when the skater puts real effort into the session.

That condition shapes how to read the number. AccuWeather presents 700 calories as a ceiling for hard skating, not as the typical result of a trip to the rink. The source gives no figure for a slow, casual loop around the ice. That kind of outing falls outside what the estimate describes.

The way AccuWeather states the figure also sets skating apart from most of the activities in this article. Snowboarding, snowshoeing, running, hiking and walking each come with both a low and a high estimate. Skating has only an upper limit. The one other activity here described the same way is vigorous snow shoveling, covered with the other winter chores.

Without a stated floor, the skating figure is most useful for comparing upper limits. On that basis, the ceiling for hard skating sits above the top of AccuWeather's ranges for snowboarding and snowshoeing. A skater who wants to approach that ceiling has to keep the effort high for the full hour.

Winter hiking burns an estimated 430 to 650 calories an hour, and walking burns an estimated 200 to 470 calories an hour, according to AccuWeather. Of the two, hiking has the higher range.

The two ranges do not sit apart. They share a band from 430 to 470 calories an hour. Near the top of its range, walking matches the low end of the hiking range. The activity's name alone does not settle which outing burns more on a given day.

Walking also needs no special trip. AccuWeather counts walking the dog and running errands on foot toward this activity. A walk to the store or a loop around the block with a pet fits that description. Those examples fold the exercise into routine outings rather than setting it apart as a separate workout.

AccuWeather gives less detail on hiking. It does not describe the terrain, pace or distance behind its hiking range. It also gives no separate figure for hiking through snow. That leaves the hiking estimate as a general guide, not a figure for any particular trail or condition.

Shoveling snow, chopping wood and raking leaves can each burn hundreds of calories, according to estimates from AccuWeather. It puts vigorous snow shoveling at up to 500 calories per hour and chopping wood at 400 to 500 calories per hour.

The shoveling number is an upper limit. AccuWeather attaches it to vigorous shoveling, not to shoveling in general. The source gives no figure for shoveling at an easier pace.

Chopping wood comes with a range rather than a single ceiling. The bottom of that range is 400 calories per hour. The top, 500 calories per hour, matches the most AccuWeather gives for hard shoveling.

Those chore figures sit near several of the sports on AccuWeather's list. The wood-chopping range falls inside the 400 to 600 calories an hour the source gives for ice hockey and for snowshoeing. The shoveling ceiling equals the floor of its estimate for skiing, which starts at 500 calories an hour.

Raking leaves carries an estimate of 250 to 400 calories. AccuWeather does not attach a time frame to that range. Without one, the raking figure cannot be lined up against the hourly numbers for shoveling and wood chopping.

AccuWeather groups these three tasks as winter chores alongside sports such as skiing, hockey and ice skating. Unlike hockey or skating, none of the chores requires a rink or a team. The source does not say how much snow, wood or leaf cover its estimates assume.
