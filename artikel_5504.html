Nearly $12 billion in 2026 midterm spending, and 6 races that show it

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Free daily briefing on global business news.

2026 midterm spending neared $12 billion, and these 7 big-money fights, from the Texas Senate race to three House contests past $50 million in ads, show where the cash went

Money is the easiest part of a campaign to count and one of the hardest to tie to a result. The 2026 midterms put that gap on full display. Total spending in the cycle reached nearly $12 billion. NPR described it as the most expensive U.S. election cycle yet, ahead of even the 2024 presidential race. The headline on that same NPR report offered a counterpoint: political ads aren't that effective.

That tension runs through this list. The record 2026 midterm spending did not flow evenly. NPR reported that most of it favored outside groups aligned with Republicans. Much of that outside money moves through super PACs. These groups can raise unlimited sums from individuals, corporations and unions as long as they do not coordinate directly with a candidate. That structure lets a single large donor shape a race in ways that capped contributions to a campaign cannot.

The money reached down the ballot as well. NPR reported that three House contests each passed $50 million in ad spending, the first time that had happened. All 435 House seats are on the ballot every two years. That puts control of the chamber up for a vote in every midterm.

The seven entries that follow are a selection, not a complete ranking. They cover the Senate races in Texas and Michigan, California's race for governor, House contests in North Carolina, New York and Florida, and the cycle as a whole. Each slide sets out who spent, how much where a figure is available, and where the money came from.

Whether the spending paid off is the harder question. Several reported outcomes could not be independently confirmed when this was written. Where a result is unconfirmed, the slides say so instead of guessing. Where reports give conflicting spending totals, the disputed figure is left out. What remains is a race-by-race record of where the money went. That record can be read against the argument in NPR's headline about the limits of political advertising.

Elon Musk put more than $150 million into super PACs backing Republican Ken Paxton in the 2026 Texas Senate race. Democrat James Talarico raised $145 million for his own campaign, mostly from smaller donors, while Paxton's campaign raised $22 million.

The contest matched two Texas officeholders. Paxton has served as the state's attorney general since 2015, after earlier terms in both chambers of the Texas Legislature. The Texas House impeached him in 2023, and the Texas Senate acquitted him later that year. Talarico, a former schoolteacher, has served in the Texas House of Representatives since 2019.

Total spending in the race ran well over $500 million. Published totals conflict, so no exact figure appears here.

The two sides paid for their campaigns in different ways. Most of the money working on Paxton's behalf came from outside groups, not from his own campaign. Musk's contributions alone came to more than six times what Paxton's campaign raised.

Talarico's money followed a different path. Contributions from smaller donors went directly to his campaign, which controlled how the money was spent. Counting candidate fundraising alone, Talarico raised $123 million more than Paxton.

Combined, Musk's super PAC money and Paxton's campaign fundraising came to more than $172 million. That sum exceeds Talarico's $145 million. It still covers only part of what was spent in the race.

The race also tested a long Republican hold on Texas Senate contests. Before 2026, Texas had last elected a Democrat to the U.S. Senate in 1988, when Lloyd Bentsen won reelection. Bentsen was also the Democratic vice presidential nominee that year.

Reporting from NPR argues that political ads aren't that effective. Texas puts that argument to a direct test. One candidate relied mostly on smaller donors. His opponent had a single donor's nine-figure super PAC commitment behind him. The final result could not be confirmed from a dated report when this was written. This slide therefore does not name a winner or judge whether the money paid off.

Republicans outspent Democrats on general election advertising in Michigan's open Senate race, $143.5 million to $117 million, according to NPR. That gave the Republican side, backing Mike Rogers, a $26.5 million advantage over Democrats backing Abdul El-Sayed.

The Republican total was about 23% larger than the Democratic one. Combined, the two sides spent $260.5 million on general election ads in the state. Those figures cover the general election only. Money spent in either party's primary is not included.

The seat opened when Gary Peters, a Democrat first elected to it in 2014, announced in January 2025 that he would not seek a third term. Peters had served three terms in the U.S. House before moving to the Senate. His exit meant neither party had an incumbent on the ballot.

Rogers came into the race with a statewide campaign already behind him. He is a former Republican congressman from Michigan who chaired the House Intelligence Committee. In 2024, he ran for the state's other Senate seat and lost narrowly to Democrat Elissa Slotkin.

El-Sayed is a physician and epidemiologist who previously ran Detroit's health department. His first statewide bid came in 2018, when he ran for governor. He lost that Democratic primary to Gretchen Whitmer, who went on to win the office.

Both of Michigan's Senate seats were held by Democrats going into the 2026 cycle, by Peters and Slotkin. A Rogers win would give Republicans a pickup in a state that voted for Donald Trump in 2016, Joe Biden in 2020 and Trump again in 2024.

No dated report confirming the outcome of the race was available when this was written. That leaves open the question of whether the spending paid off.

The result will still test the ad gap clearly. A Rogers victory would match the Republican advantage in general election ads. An El-Sayed victory would mean the side that spent $26.5 million less took the seat. That outcome would fit the argument in NPR's headline that political ads aren't that effective. One race cannot settle that question on its own.

California's governor's race drew $349.4 million in total spending, according to NPR. Most of that money went out during a crowded primary, before the field narrowed to two finalists for November.

NPR's reporting credits the primary spending with clearing out wildcard candidates. It says the result left the Democratic establishment in control of the race. That claim is the only point in the available reporting that speaks to whether the money paid off.

California's election rules give the primary unusual weight. The state uses a top-two primary, in which every candidate for governor appears on a single ballot regardless of party. The two candidates with the most votes advance to the general election, even if they belong to the same party.

Voters adopted the system by approving Proposition 14 in June 2010. It took effect with the 2012 elections. It covers state offices and congressional seats but not the presidential primary.

A same-party November matchup is possible under these rules. In 2016, two Democrats, Kamala Harris and Loretta Sanchez, faced each other in the general election for a U.S. Senate seat from California. No Republican appeared on that November ballot.

The previous open-seat race for governor followed a more familiar pattern. In 2018, Democrat Gavin Newsom and Republican John Cox finished first and second in the primary. Former Los Angeles Mayor Antonio Villaraigosa, also a Democrat, placed third and was eliminated. Newsom then won the general election.

The 2026 seat was open as well. Newsom was reelected in 2022, and California limits governors to two terms. Voters set that limit through Proposition 140 in 1990.

In a top-two system, a crowded field splits the vote among many candidates. A candidate needs only to finish second, not first, to reach November. Spending in that phase can decide which two names remain on the ballot.

The candidates' names and the eventual winner could not be confirmed when this was written. Neither could the split of the $349.4 million between campaigns and outside groups, or the vote results.

The race for North Carolina's 1st Congressional District, between Democrat Don Davis and Republican Laurie Buckhout, passed $50 million in ad spending, according to NPR. Davis has held the seat since January 2023, so the contest was a defense of a Democratic incumbent.

The $50 million mark counts advertising alone. Staff salaries, field operations, polling and other campaign costs fall outside it. The figure is a floor for ad spending in the district, not a full accounting of what campaigns and outside groups spent on the race.

No precise ad total for this district was available. Nor was a split of the money by party, candidate or outside group. The reporting also does not show which side bought more airtime.

Those gaps limit any verdict on whether the money paid off. A spending advantage can be weighed against a result only when it is clear which side held it. A dollar total also says nothing about how the ads ran, whether on broadcast television, cable or digital platforms.

The outcome could not be confirmed when this was written. No winner or vote margin for the district was available.

That leaves the race as an open test of NPR's argument that political ads have limited effect. A loss by the bigger spender would fit that argument. A win would not settle it. A district's partisan lean, the candidates and the national mood all shape how people vote. Ad money is only one input.

The House race between Republican Rep. Mike Lawler and Democrat Cait Conley in New York's 17th District drew more than $50 million in ad spending, according to NPR. That total covers advertising alone, not the full cost of either campaign.

The district sits north of New York City in the lower Hudson Valley. It takes in all of Rockland and Putnam counties, along with parts of Westchester and Dutchess counties.

Lawler first won the seat in 2022. He defeated Democratic Rep. Sean Patrick Maloney, who chaired the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee at the time. Maloney had moved into the district after New York's 2022 redistricting. Two years later, Lawler held the seat against former Rep. Mondaire Jones. He entered the 2026 cycle as a two-term incumbent, with Conley as his Democratic challenger.

Joe Biden carried the district in the 2020 presidential election. That placed Lawler among the House Republicans representing seats Biden won.

The NPR figure is a combined total. It does not show how much each party, campaign or outside group spent. It also does not say whether one side outspent the other on the airwaves. Without that split, there is no basis for judging which campaign got more for its money.

The result could not be confirmed when this was written. No dated report naming a winner or giving a margin in this race was available. Whether the spending paid off for Lawler, for Conley or for the groups backing them cannot yet be judged.

The national pattern offers only partial context. NPR's coverage indicates that heavy ad spending yielded less and less for Republican challengers this cycle. Lawler was not a challenger in 2026. He was defending a seat he had already won twice. That national finding does not settle how ad money performed in this district.

What the record does show is scale. More than $50 million went to advertising in a single suburban House seat.

Florida's 25th District, where Jared Moskowitz faced Scott Singer, was one of three U.S. House contests in the 2026 midterms to pass $50 million in ad spending, according to NPR. The other two were North Carolina's 1st District and New York's 17th District.

The $50 million mark is a floor, not a final tally. NPR gives no exact ad spending figure for this district. The true total may be higher, and the available reporting does not say by how much.

The figure also counts advertising only. It leaves out other campaign costs, such as staff, field operations and voter outreach. The full cost of the contest is therefore larger than the ad figure by itself.

The reporting does not break the ad money down by spender, either. It does not say how much came from the Moskowitz campaign, the Singer campaign or outside groups such as super PACs. Without that split, there is no way to tell which candidate held the advertising edge. It also leaves open whether the candidates controlled most of the money or whether groups acting independently of them did.

The outcome is unconfirmed as well. The available material reports neither a winner nor a margin. No dated report confirming the result was available when this was written.

NPR's headline argues that political ads aren't that effective. Moskowitz vs. Singer cannot yet test that claim. For now, the race shows how high House ad budgets climbed in 2026, not whether the money moved votes. Settling that would take two records the reporting does not supply: a district-level breakdown of ad spending by side and a certified vote count.

Spending on the 2026 midterm cycle came close to $12 billion. In the fight for the House, NPR's report argues that the heavy ad spending bought less and less for Republican challengers.

That record came in a year with no presidential race on the ballot. Every federal contest in 2026 was for a seat in Congress.

The House was the main arena for testing what the money could buy. A party needs 218 seats to hold a majority. That puts every competitive district into the national count.

The phrase "less and less" describes diminishing returns. Read that way, each added round of advertising produced a smaller gain than the one before it. NPR's report does not say the spending had no effect on Republican challengers. It says the effect shrank as the money grew.

Challengers usually start with less name recognition than incumbents. Paid advertising is one of the main ways a campaign introduces a newcomer to voters. That makes Republican challengers a direct test of what ad dollars could and could not do.

NPR's report gives two reasons the money did not deliver more for them. It points to a national focus on affordability. It also says voters rejected the status quo.

Both explanations rest on issues and voter mood, not on the volume of advertising. Affordability is about costs voters meet in their own budgets. An ad can frame those costs, but it cannot change them. A rejection of the status quo is a judgment on current conditions. A campaign can argue about those conditions without controlling them. That reasoning lines up with NPR's case about the limits of political ads.

The same report says Democrats passed the 218-seat threshold and won the House majority. It also says Democrats won the Senate. A dated, independent report confirming either result was not available when this was written.

Whether the cycle's record spending paid off for either party depends on those final results. Until they are confirmed, the question stays open.
