Pinch Hitting for the Geriatricians

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

— As the population ages without enough elder specialists, we all need to help out

America is growing older, like it or not. And as this happens, we need to find ways to take better care of our older and oldest patients.

Many elderly patients will live out those years with health and vitality, the path we all wish for.

But many of them will become infirm, challenged by the devastating effects of chronic diseases and the development and discovery of life-altering terminal illnesses. We need to be able to take better care of people at the end of life, as well as in the years leading up to that point.

What this country certainly needs, in addition to tens of thousands more primary care doctors, is a huge number of new geriatricians.

The "sexier" (higher paying) specialties like cardiology and gastroenterology have people flocking to them, but there is certainly no dramatic growth in the number of people choosing to enter geriatrics. Until that happens, we all need to do a much better job of taking care of people in their twilight years.

In outpatient medicine, we've always tried to ensure patient's wishes for end of life were known and clear to them, their family members, and loved ones, and all others caring for them.

Not having these discussions before it's too late leads to nothing but trouble down the road. Family members disagree about what their loved one wanted, and it often doesn't end well.

When we see young patients, healthy folks with no major medical issues, and we suggest they assign a healthcare proxy, sign an organ donor card, or complete a form outlining what they would want done in the event of catastrophic illness, they often look at us like we're crazy.

Usually at the point when it finally starts (needs) to come up, it's too late, or close to too late.

Whenever patients come see us, they have a barrage of forms thrown upon them by the administrative team at our practice. They need to complete so many screening questions, surveys about social determinants of health, food insecurity, housing insecurity, tobacco use, depression, domestic violence screening, substance use disorders screening, and more.

It's not until we finally get to the Medicare Annual Wellness Visit Annual Health Review that a question about advanced directives even comes up. And at that point, the templated questions just ask if they have healthcare proxy or if they've filled out any form of advanced directives.

Wouldn't it be great if at every opportunity when a patient was seen and taken care of in our healthcare system, this gap was closed, or at least an effort to fill in the blanks was made?

Whenever I get my driver's license renewed, they ask me if I want to be an organ donor.

In the same vein, when someone comes into a new provider's practice, we should really get to know the patient up front by getting to know what they would want should things take a turn, a terrible turn, for the worse.

If they say yes, they have an advanced directive, we should give them an immediate opportunity to upload it to the system, make that an expectation of getting care in the practice.

We won't see them without scanning a copy of their insurance card at every visit; why should we allow someone to be under our care without this crucial information?

If they don't have any of this, then push the blank forms to them, shower them with useful educational materials, help them understand how important this is, give them a Medical Order for Life-Sustaining Treatment (MOLST) form and a Living Will and a Medical Power of Attorney form -- whatever our patients need to let us know what they should really want us to know.

But how to get the conversation going between the patient and their providers?

Smart systems should be able to do this pretty easily, and the time to do it is not when a catastrophic illness has been diagnosed or at the point where critical timely decisions need to be made.

Sure, there will be situations where people want to update these things, change their minds, special circumstances, something in their situation has changed that makes them want to do a little more or a little less.

But the comfort that comes from knowing and being ready to respect a patient's choices should be something we could build into the system and make foolproof, or nearly so.

Right now, filling out a MOLST form, enrolling a patient in a hospice program, even getting someone started in the new Medical Aid in Dying program in New York State, seems much more complicated than need be.

Getting a patient the care they need to spend their last days in comfort, surrounded by their loved ones, free from pain and anxiety and minimizing suffering, should be paramount to the interests of society and any civilized healthcare system. We should be able to overcome whatever bureaucratic nonsense exists that make these processes so onerous.

Fewer rules, fewer checklists, less co-signing, less witnessing, less notarizing, less lawyering.

When the time comes for each of us, we all want things to go smoothly, no one wants to see another person, especially someone they love, suffer.

Geriatricians are masters of this stuff, but until a time where we have thousands more who can help take care of patients during these challenging moments as they approach the end of their lives, we should build up stronger, better, and more respectful systems that don't make this all so complicated.

Having the discussions we need with every patient is the hard part; the paperwork need not be that burdensome.
