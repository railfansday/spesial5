FALLEN STAR. Sotheby’s is pushing back against an 11th-hour restitution claim for its star lot, an estimated $180 million Vincent van Gogh painting, reports ARTnews. The heirs of Samuel Fischer, a Jewish publisher whose family fled Nazi Germany, asked Sotheby’s to withdraw the painting from its Wednesday auction and prove that it was not sold under duress. However, what constitutes duress is up for debate. One indicator is whether a painting sold for a fair price, which, according to new details, appears to have been the case. The auction house told ARTnews that the Fischer family sold the Van Gogh in question, Châtaigniers en fleurs (1890), for 68,000 Swiss francs, or about $22,000, through a dealer in June 1936. The sale took place in the Netherlands, when it was not yet occupied by the Nazis. That price was a relatively strong one at the time. However, the family’s lawyers point out that the heirs were already on the run from by then from the Nazis, who had begun banning the family’s publishing house from printing many of its authors. The sale, they claim, would not have happened under normal circumstances. SCHOOL’S OUT. The new Lucas Museum of Narrative Art has its critics, ARTnews’s own Maximilíano Durón being one of them. But lately, new issues have been raising eyebrows. For one, the institution is keeping its outdoor plaza around the museum permanently closed off, despite it being intended as “a generous civic space,” as ARTnews reported. More recently, we’ve learned from L.A. Material, that a promised education program, touted as central to the museum’s mission and a key reason it was granted a 99-year lease on public land at the rate of $30 annually, is nowhere in sight. One explanation is that about a year ago, the museum fired most of its education department. Now, its newly built classrooms are kept closed to the public, there is no sign the museum is looking to hire replacements, and local educators have been left ghosted. A follow-up story in the Los Angeles Times quotes a museum spokesperson stating: “We’re actively plotting our overall programming slate, and kids- and family-focused offerings will be included in that rollout in 2027.” At this stage, the museum is already far short of what it told local authorities, including that the museum would launch its education program prior to opening, for children of all ages. In a statement to LA Material, the museum spokesperson added that the future educational program would now “be focused on older students.”

HAD ME AT HOT CHOCOLATE BAR. The new, £437 million ($576.8 million) London Museum is opening November 28, and its director spills on all it will have to offer in an interview with the Times of London. Admittedly, we are particularly intrigued by plans for a 24-hour hot chocolate bar. The so-called “temperance Cocoa Room,” open all night, and museum hours until 11 p.m. and midnight, are some plans that Sharon Ament, the museum’s director, hopes to implement at the institution’s new home, a renovated 800-year-old meat market. Temperance rooms were created in the 19th century to provide non-alcoholic beverage options, and one such room focused on hot chocolate was discovered at the museum site during construction and subsequently preserved. “We are reviving that idea of a meeting place for people who would prefer a hot chocolate to a beer,” said Ament. “And I want it to be open all night, even if there’s only me in there.”
