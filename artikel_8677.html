Real Estate Syndications – Vet the Sponsor Before You Trust the Projected Returns

Before you invest in a real estate syndication, vet the sponsor, not just its projected returns. Ask how its past deals did when rates jumped, how much of its own money is in this one, and whether the loan’s rate can rise with the market. Commit only money you can leave in for five to seven years.

A syndication’s projected returns are the sponsor’s own forecast, and the sponsor collects fees whether or not they come true.

A syndication pools money from passive investors, the limited partners, to buy one large property, such as an apartment complex, and borrows the rest of the price. The sponsor runs it and typically sells after five to seven years. It earns an acquisition fee when it buys, an asset management fee each year, and a promote, often 20% to 30% of the profit above a set yearly return investors get first, called the preferred return. Only the promote depends on how investors do, so the sponsor’s skill and honesty matter more than the projections.

While rates were low, the syndicator Tides Equities bought apartments with floating-rate loans, whose interest rises with market rates. Its rate caps, which limit how high the rate can go, lasted only two or three years. When its first loans came due, rates had jumped, and Tides couldn’t afford new caps or a refinance, The Real Deal reported. Lenders foreclosed on some of its complexes, and anyone with $50,000 in one of those deals lost all of it.

Vet the sponsor yourself, even when a real estate investing platform lists the deal. Ask it to show you:

Then check the deal. For a floating-rate loan, ask when the rate cap runs out and when the loan comes due. Compare the rents and sale price the sponsor assumes with what similar buildings rent and sell for now. Also find out whether the sponsor can make a capital call, a demand for more money, and what happens to investors who can’t pay.

Size your stake so you could leave it alone for seven years and still afford a capital call.
