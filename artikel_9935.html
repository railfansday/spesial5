For the first time, a C++ standard failed to pass the national bodies

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Local 'minimal viable product that everyone agrees on' tanks the C++ standard.

It looks like it happened! A historic first, for better or worse.

This is a sign that C++ is (still) struggling with its governance (see a previous article on cultural issues within C++ here). This is a big deal even if you do not care about programming languages. C++ is arguably the most important programming language in existence.

Your computer runs on C or C++. The web browser you are using to read this is written in C++. ~All programming languages depend on C++ (often through LLVM). Likewise, ~all websites with performance requirements (e.g. YouTube) depend on C++, ~all data storage, game engines, finance, etc.

In a world that is drowning in exciting new vulnerabilities in ~all software, the ability of C++ to change and adjust to changing times affects you.

Let’s take a step back and get an overview. The initial source was a post by Robert Seacord on LinkedIn:

In a comment, J. Daniel GarcÃ­a adds: “There were 12 countries that abstained. In several cases because they could not reach to a common position. That is another signal of a contested draft.”

Now, before we get into the facts: This is not a good look for C++.

There have been multiple fights over the future direction of the C++ language, but no C++ standard ever failed to be signed off by the national bodies.

The national body vote is considered to be a formality. By the time the vote reaches the national bodies all disagreements should’ve been sorted out by WG21 (Working Group 21, i.e. the C++ Standards Committee).

Truly controversial features should never make it this far, since they should never be added to the C++26 working draft in the first place. This is a sign of division in the C++ community, in some form or another, and completely independently of whether you agree with this result or not.

Here, “Added to the C++26 working draft” is essentially accepting the pull request. Everyone is working towards the C++26 release, and people contribute to it with papers. Papers lead to votes, which then lead to amendments of the draft. Finally (this is where we are) wording passes happen, and it gets signed off, rubber-stamped and released. Phrased like this, the ISO process sounds sensible enough, but trust me, it’s actually kind of a nightmare.

Needless to say, the debates are raging, with comments on all sides: So many discussions.

In total, there were 20 P-members, and 22 total members (ignoring abstaining). I heard that the US abstained, but take that with a grain of salt.

tl;dr: WG21 voted to include Contracts in C++26. Several high-ranking members of WG21 are strongly opposed to Contracts, and so are several national bodies. The national body vote was the ’last resort’ place to prevent Contracts from being canonicalized as part of the C++ standard.

New keywords that replace assert macros with a standardized way to enforce runtime checks. Most importantly, you can add pre and post checks to ensure that all arguments and return values fulfill certain conditions.

Looks simple, right? You’d think so, but no!

First, due to issues with virtual functions, Contracts for virtual functions were already cut from the proposal.

Second, it’s not entirely obvious how this will interact with constexpr or function inlining.

Third, there are compile flags to selectively enable or disable the enforcement. E.g. “If a contract is violated, do you want to 1) kill the program, 2) call a ‘contract-violation handler’ and then kill the program, 3) log an error, 4) ignore completely (and not even perform the check, for performance reasons)?”

Big question: You compile your code with enforce and are linking a prebuilt library that pulls in the same dependency as you, but with Contracts set to ignore. This is fine according to Contracts. The linker gets to keep one of the copies. What happens? Do Contracts run or not? What if a call is inlined across translation units?

Is there even a way for the user to guarantee that checks always run? (The answer is no. What? You were hoping that this feature could be used to ensure certain checks guarding against Undefined Behavior would always run, no matter what? Don’t be silly—this is C++.)

Oh, did I mention that code inside of predicates is just standard C++, so the compiler is allowed to elide checks due to Undefined Behavior?

But do feel free to take it from the source: The “Contracts paper” can be found here, and if you read closely, you’ll find some funny statements that really ruffled some feathers:

It is ill-formed, no diagnostic required (IFNDR) if multiple first declarations for the same function are in different translation units that do not have the same sequence of function contract specifiers.

All of this leads to so many funny issues, some of which can be poorly summarized as “Well, who even knows whether your Contracts are even going to be enforced, this is C++, the compiler can do whatever it wants lol”.

If you think “Gee, this proposal looks rather complex and messy”, keep in mind that the Contracts paper was explicitly designed to be a minimal viable product that everyone would be able to agree on.

If you only faintly paid attention to C++, you might be going into this assuming that Profiles (aka Bjarne Stroustrup’s initiative to make C++ safer via magical fairy dust, also controversial and considered to be ‘impossible to implement’ by some) are the sticking point, but no, Profiles did not make it into C++26.

Oh, y’know, it’s just because that’s all everyone has been talking about.

Most obviously, the pro-Contracts camp released “Do not remove Contracts from C++26” on Oct 23rd, i.e. around the time when the national body ballot closed. Vinnie Falco (the most outspoken voice against Contracts) countered with “Do Remove Contracts From C++26” on the very same day1.

If you’ve been paying attention, there’s a whole trail of papers and events here.

We’ve got talks, and Bjarne Stroustrup, the creator of C++ himself, saying “it’s claimed to be a minimum viable product. It is not minimal. I don’t think it’s viable”.

We’ve got multiple papers, say “Contract Concerns”, “Contracts make C++ less safe — full stop!”, or “Contracts’ fundamental flaws” (all reasonably short) opposing the behemoth that’s the Contracts paper and its rationale document (put together, almost 300 pages).

We’ve had multiple votes. Most importantly (Hagenberg, Feb 15th, 2025), the vote to formally add Contracts to the C++26 working draft, turned out 100 in favor, 14 opposed, 12 abstain. The multiple votes to remove Contracts from the draft all failed, most recently in Croydon, which is also when an amendment to support a mechanism to ensure mandatory contract enforcement failed.

If your eyes are glazing over at this point, I don’t blame you!

The whole situation is a mess, and trying to keep track of it from the outside is a fool’s errand: You’re basically forced to track dry meeting notes, and read between the lines looking at the various drafts people are publishing. Don’t do it.

This is not Contracts’ first rodeo: A different version of Contracts was up for debate around 2018, and was kicked out not too long after. At this point SG21 (‘Study Group 21’) was formed, dedicated to revamping the Contracts concept to get us the new “Minimum Viable Product” that’s currently part of the draft.

The whole situation went back and forth, resulted in fourteen revisions on the Contracts paper, many comments from the national bodies during the process, and finally in some people explicitly calling for the national bodies to veto C++26. (We call that ’the nuclear option’. FYI, some of the people signing this are themselves sitting on national bodies.)

What you need to understand is that the Hagenberg vote (on adding Contracts to the working draft) was strongly in favor of Contracts. 100 in favor vs. 14 against is not a weak result. This makes it incredibly puzzling that this final vote failed so badly (12 for, 8 against is bad).

One narrative on what happened is the following:

“There’s a loud minority strongly opposing Contracts in C++26. This group includes people with a lot of influence, e.g. Bjarne Stroustrup. Some members of this group actively lobbied the national bodies to vote against Contracts, even though WG21 achieved alignment, and agrees that Contracts should ship in their current form.”

This agrees with the evidence we’ve seen (votes in favor, some people strongly opposed). Herb Sutter is suggesting this in his latest paper, see here. (Herb Sutter, for the record, was the chair of the C++ Committee and is (next to Bjarne) perhaps the second-most-important person anyone in the C++ world listens to.)

This is plausible, but I don’t believe it. Not entirely, anyway. Yes, lobbying happened, and Bjarne’s opinion is something people listen to. It’s possible it was the deciding factor, but if it was the deciding factor, then this is a sign that the process itself is broken, and puts undue value onto status.

Even Herb Sutter acknowledges that Contracts were contentious. In footnote 4 on his paper, anyway.

Some of the NBs that voted Disapprove had participated and had expressed concerns about contracts without having to be lobbied (importantly, those were the first-ever Disapprove votes on any C++ DIS).

In other words, before any lobbying happened we already had a historical first, the strongest ever NB disagreement with a draft.

Importantly, it looks to me as though the committee just repeatedly ignored the comments/feedback of the national bodies throughout the process, and then ran face-first into a wall at the vote.

If you think “Oh, you are saying that the committee is just straight-up incompetent and/or evil, then?” No, not really. The committee process is just such a mess that it semi-naturally leads to situations like this. The people working within this system have become so used to the process that they’re barely capable of seeing it.

(Over the years many people who did not find this process workable just left entirely. There’s a huge selection bias effect.)

Part of what makes the process bad is that it’s very “all or nothing”, and the process is so slow that working on the standard is painful for everyone. More on that later.

Anyway. I don’t believe that it’s all just the lobbying. The discussions were already way underway. Evidence:

Taking a look at “Do not remove Contracts from C++26”, it refers to the “23 NB comments on contract assertions”.

Taking a closer look at N5028 and N5031, we see the committee rejected at least one strong anti-Contracts comment from each of the following countries: Sweden, Spain, the United States, France, Romania, the Czech Republic and Finland.

These comments included multiple asks to not ship Contracts in their current form at all, resolve safety issues, make Contracts reliably non-ignorable, fix the const issues, and change it such that standard library hardening phrasing does not depend on Contracts.

What did the committee think? That all of these asks were completely irrelevant? Yeah, no shit people are going to vote against something or abstain if you ignore their concerns.

This was preventable! People told you ahead of time that they were strongly opposed! The national bodies played their part correctly here. They gave you feedback ahead of time, and then voted accordingly.

There were at least four comments asking specifically “Please do not make the work on ‘standard library hardening’ depend on the inclusion of Contracts into C++26.” As it stands, this coupling is still there, i.e. the comments were ignored.

In fact, now that the future of Contracts is shaky, the “Do not remove Contracts” paper includes the following line:

At the time of writing, there is no wording for removing contract assertions from C++26, and producing it is not a purely mechanical exercise. It would require:

removing or respecifying C++26 standard-library hardening [P3471R4], which is written in terms of contract assertions and has never had complete, reviewed wording that is independent of contract assertions.

The audacity. The national bodies were very explicit in saying that they do not want standard library hardening to depend on Contracts.

You went and ignored those comments, and are now complaining about the effort that would be necessary to walk things back? You were given all the heads-up you needed, you just ignored it!

Honestly, probably not. Herb Sutter is looking at the situaation from the political angle, and analyzing whose beliefs have more weight based on their status.

Bjarne Stroustrup’s beliefs (as the creator of C++) hold a lot of weight. This is correct.

Quoting Herb Sutter’s discussion on the matter:

Over half of the NBs that voted Disapprove on the C++26 DIS had never participated in WG21 at all and/or never before expressed any concern about contracts in C++26 (most sent no comments on the C++26 CD comment ballot; one sent contracts-unrelated CD comments), yet voted against the DIS after being lobbied with messages citing Bjarneâs opposition.

“Look! It’s just Bjarne having a lot of status!” he says, and he is completely right in saying so, but he is also acting as though putting huge pressure on members to “disagree and commit :-)” hadn’t always been part of it. The C++ Standards Committee always voted in terms of factions.

It was always a political mess where Bjarne’s explicit support was absolutely critical, and where “I know you may disagree with this, but you should vote in favor to get it in” was a part of the process.

Quoting Herb Sutter and Bjarne, yet again from Herb Sutter’s paper:

(Ed.: Bjarne added, when reviewing this paper, that one reason previous DIS ballots were unanimous was his private and public encouragement.)

Why are Contracts authors so obsessed with getting Contracts into C++26? Why not wait for the next release?

Well, C++ has this funny “release train model” (also known as “the frame rule bus stop analogy” in Super Mario Bros. speedrunning). A new version of the C++ standard releases every three years. If your work gets voted into the standard in time, it becomes part of it. If it doesn’t, you have to wait another three years for the next release.

Three years is a long time, and it’s an especially long time to work on a single C++ feature. Getting a C++ paper accepted into the standard is a process so notoriously draining that ~all of the “C++ people” I know just stopped bothering, and stopped working with WG21. It’s bad.

If Contracts don’t get in this cycle, there’s a good chance they never will. They will be nitpicked over the next three years, endlessly litigated, and frankly, I think there’s a good chance several of the people working on Contracts just won’t want to deal with that, and might instead drop out of the process entirely, leaving Contracts dead and buried.

Until the process itself changes, which, god knows how that could happen considering the requirements presumably imposed by ISO, it’s hard to imagine this changing.

This is why the pro-Contracts side released the following statement in their paper:

If the DIS ballot has failed, we should seek another ballot without altering contract assertions or removing them from C++26;

In other words, they are playing a game of Chicken: “We believe you are going to give up first.”

They know full well that a failed C++26 will hurt everyone. But, because the WG21 process is so insane that a loss here risks killing Contracts entirely, and would require another three years of endless litigation and fights, the stakes are high.

If C++26 fails, I’ll pour one out. Not for you, but for the many, many people who tried to make you better, and failed3.
