Skyrim Developer Explains Why Todd Howard’s Ideas ‘Just Work’

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Todd Howard once said “it just works” while showing off Fallout 4’s settlement building, and the internet has never let him forget it. Usually, someone brings it up when a Bethesda NPC does something physically impossible. But according to Skyrim’s former lead designer Bruce Nesmith, there’s a less sarcastic reason Howard’s ideas so often work: he knows when to get out of the player’s way.

From Arena to Skyrim, we rank every Elder Scrolls game by story.

Speaking to Steven Lake on The Examined Game, Nesmith explained what it was like working with Howard and why the two designers didn’t always see eye to eye. He also recalled a Skyrim feature he was convinced would confuse players, only to discover that Howard had made the right call.

Nesmith said Howard has always been unusually good at putting himself in the shoes of an ordinary player. That sounds straightforward, but it can be surprisingly difficult when you’ve spent years building a game and know every system inside out. The temptation is to add another layer of depth, even when it does little to improve the moment-to-moment experience.

Nesmith admitted that he was often the one pushing for more complexity. Howard, meanwhile, would question whether players actually wanted it. As Nesmith recalled, Howard’s argument was simple: “They’re going to want something simple and more digestible here.”

Making something more accessible doesn't necessarily mean sacrificing depth.

That isn’t the same as saying RPGs should have no depth. The point is that not every interesting idea makes for an enjoyable mechanic. Sometimes players want to explore a ruin, cast a spell, or pick a fight without first consulting three menus and a spreadsheet.

Nesmith also talked about the value of competing perspectives on a development team. One artist, for instance, disliked how Oblivion’s Detect Life spell covered the creatures and characters artists had worked so hard to detail. Skyrim’s version made the effect more costly to keep active, encouraging players to use it briefly instead of walking around with everything glowing all the time. It was a small change, but one that came from listening to a different concern.

There was one disagreement Nesmith remembered particularly well. Howard wanted Skyrim’s skills and perks presented as constellations in the night sky, letting the artwork shape the way players moved through character progression. Nesmith preferred something more conventional – a list.

He was certain the constellations would be confusing. Even as development continued, he remained unconvinced. Then Skyrim came out, and players responded warmly to the presentation. Nesmith later admitted, “I was dead dog wrong on that.”

Personally, I still think Skyrim falls short of Morrowind as an RPG, but the constellation-based skill menu is one of the things it absolutely got right. I’m actually surprised to learn that it was Howard’s idea. Turning character progression into something so visually distinctive, rather than presenting players with another list of skills, was a clever decision. It’s the sort of approach I’d love to see more RPGs adopt, because making something more accessible doesn’t necessarily mean sacrificing depth.

Of course, “it just works” means something very different to anybody who has spent a few hundred hours with Bethesda’s games. We have Eurojank for ambitious, rough-edged European RPGs like the Gothic series. For all their quirks, those games can still be a lot of fun. Bethesda’s own particular flavor has sometimes been affectionately called Amerijank, and I can’t say I’ve ever found that description entirely unfair.

I can’t imagine playing one of Bethesda’s Elder Scrolls or Fallout games without mods. At the very least, I’d want an unofficial community patch, like the Unofficial Skyrim Special Edition Patch. That’s not because I dislike these games. Quite the opposite. I’ve spent enough time enjoying them to know how often the community ends up fixing things Bethesda left behind.

Some of that roughness is understandable. Skyrim and Fallout 3 give players enormous spaces to wander through, objects to mess with, systems that interact in unexpected ways, and the freedom to ignore the main story for hours. The more complex a machine is, the more ways it can fail. The same applies to video games, which is why RPGs and open-world sandbox titles are often particularly prone to bugs. It doesn’t excuse every broken quest, but it helps explain why these worlds can be both a mess and incredibly hard to leave.

Starfield made that bargain harder to accept, at least for me. Its scale was certainly ambitious, but much of the exploration lacked the density, surprises, and sense of place that made wandering through Skyrim or the Capital Wasteland so rewarding. When that urge to see what’s over the next hill is missing, familiar Bethesda quirks become a lot less charming.

That’s what makes Nesmith’s comments interesting as Bethesda continues work on The Elder Scrolls 6. Howard’s best decisions were about knowing where things belonged, and making sure the player had a reason to keep going despite everything that didn’t quite work.

The Elder Scrolls has some of the most interesting characters in RPGs, and its protagonists are up there with the best.
