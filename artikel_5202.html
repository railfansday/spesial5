Treasury bought back $6B in bonds, but dealers say bigger isn’t enough

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Treasury's latest $6 billion buyback shows why Wall Street dealers want the government to rethink how it buys bonds, not just how much it buys.

The US Treasury bought back $6 billion of older government bonds on Nov. 4, hitting the full limit of its latest operation. On paper, that looks like a success for a program designed to make the Treasury market easier to trade.

But there's a catch. One bond accounted for $4.9 billion, or 82% of everything Treasury agreed to buy. Add another $950 million from a second bond, and just two securities made up almost 98% of the entire purchase. Of the 40 bonds eligible for the operation, only seven were bought.

This is the kind of concentration Wall Street dealers have been worried about for months. In feedback published by Treasury on Nov. 4, dealers said the larger buybacks were helping market liquidity.

However, they also explained why simply increasing the program size might not be enough to achieve that.

Their feedback came before Wednesday's operation, so they weren't responding to this particular result. We now know the Treasury can buy billions of dollars of bonds and still leave dealers holding the securities they hoped to sell. The amount purchased is important, but so is how those purchases are spread around.

The Treasury market is enormous, but not every government bond trades equally easily. The newest issues attract the most attention and typically have the deepest markets. Older bonds, known as off-the-run Treasuries, can be harder to sell quickly without accepting a lower price.

That's where dealers come in. They buy bonds from customers, keep them on their books, and look for someone else willing to take them.

But holding those bonds costs money, sometimes a lot of money. That's why dealers often have to borrow to finance their positions, exposing them to price changes while they wait for another buyer. The longer they hold a bond, the more those costs and risks compound.

Treasury's liquidity-support buybacks give them another potential buyer: the government itself.

In September, Treasury began larger buybacks of longer-dated bonds, at least doubling the previous $2 billion maximum for those operations. The idea was to provide a more reliable outlet for older securities.

According to the Nov. 4 dealer feedback, most dealers reported at least modest improvements. They said the purchases had made it easier to find an exit for older bonds, reduced the risk of sudden price distortions, and helped keep pricing relationships between similar securities more stable.

While that's meaningful progress, the Treasury got mixed answers when it asked whether the changes had improved dealers' ability to handle customer trades.

A lack of unity among dealers is what makes these buybacks so controversial. We now have dealers that welcome a large government buyer but aren't necessarily becoming more willing to buy bonds from customers.

If the Treasury keeps concentrating its purchases in a handful of issues, dealers holding other, older issues still have to find other buyers or keep financing the position. In other words, the buyback program can work without working equally well across the entire market.

Dealers wanted better, earlier communication about operations, smaller purchases more frequently, and limits that would help distribute Treasury buying across a wider range of individual securities.

More frequent operations would give dealers more opportunities to sell, as they'd get another chance sooner.

Limits on how much of one security the Treasury can buy could also prevent a single bond from absorbing most of the available capacity. Each bond has its own identifying number, called a CUSIP, so dealers suggested using per-CUSIP limits to spread purchases more broadly.

Some dealers specifically warned about the risk of missing a concentrated buyback and being left to manage the interest-rate exposure from bonds that remained on their books.

This is exactly what we saw on Wednesday. According to the official data, Treasury received $39.6 billion in offers for an operation capped at $6 billion. That means dealers and other eligible participants offered more than six times what Treasury ultimately accepted.

And despite that demand, one bond maturing in November 2041 absorbed more than 80% of the purchases.

It's worth being careful about what those numbers tell us. Treasury doesn't publish the full breakdown of rejected offers, so we don't know whether every one of the 33 eligible bonds that received no purchases was actually offered. We also can't assume Treasury made a bad decision by concentrating its buying.

This is because buybacks are extremely competitive operations. Treasury evaluates the prices offered and the relative value of the securities, so if one bond presents particularly attractive offers, buying more of it may make sense for taxpayers.

The problem is that getting the best available prices and helping the widest range of sellers aren't necessarily the same thing.

Treasury also has existing limits designed to preserve the amount of individual securities circulating in the market. Dealers are asking for changes that could distribute purchases more evenly within those constraints.

They discussed other possibilities, including alternative pricing methods and transactions that would exchange one security for another. But those would be more complicated to introduce, and Treasury said they would require further work.

For now, the most immediate requests are simpler: communicate earlier, buy more often, and avoid having so much of the operation go to just one security.

For investors watching Treasury buybacks as a potential source of liquidity for Bitcoin, a $6 billion buyback doesn't automatically mean $6 billion in fresh liquidity is headed toward risk assets.

First, the $6 billion is the face value of the debt Treasury agreed to retire. Because those bonds were trading below face value, the cash purchase amount was approximately $4 billion before accrued interest.

Second, Treasury continues issuing new debt to finance the government even as it buys back older securities.

So the program primarily changes which bonds are outstanding and how easily dealers can manage them. It doesn't automatically increase the amount of money available to buy Bitcoin.

However, a connection may still exist through financial conditions. An Oct. 30 Federal Reserve study, using data through June 2025, found that Treasury buybacks reduced dealers' bond inventories and eased some risk constraints for trading desks.

But the researchers found no statistically significant reduction in dealers' repo activity.

Repo is essentially short-term borrowing backed by securities. It's one way dealers and their customers finance bond positions. That's important because taking bonds off dealers' books doesn't necessarily eliminate the financing demands tied to the broader market.

For Bitcoin to benefit, easier Treasury trading would need to translate into better financing conditions or greater investor capacity to take risk. Wednesday's purchase result alone can't tell us whether that happened.

The next question is whether Treasury changes the program in ways that make those improvements more consistent.

In its latest quarterly plan, Treasury said it anticipates up to $70 billion in liquidity-support buybacks from November through January, alongside a separate $25 billion for cash management purposes. It also confirmed that it's evaluating changes to purchase limits, scheduling, frequency, eligibility, and execution.

That gives dealers a chance to see whether their feedback leads to something concrete.

The real market test won't be whether Treasury manages to spend its full buyback allowance. It'll be whether more bonds become easier to trade, dealers become more willing to handle customer orders, and financing pressures actually ease.

A $6 billion buyback might look impressive on paper, but for a dealer still holding a bond nobody wants to buy, the announcement's size only goes so far.
