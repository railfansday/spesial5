Cemeteries Are the Unsung Hero of Travel

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

I was flipping through old journals recently and came across an entry dated December 31, 2017. I was in Todos Santos and had risen early to watch the sunrise over the Sierra de la Laguna mountains. There were scribbled goals for the new year, and reflections on what I wanted to leave behind. But the part that stood out most was a slice of the surrounding atmosphere—rooster crows echoing in the streets, and sunlight spilling onto a colorful cemetery in the distance. It reminded me that for as long as I’ve been traveling, I’ve always sought out the local cemetery. And it’s always left an indelible impression.

If you’re not a fellow taphophile, allow me to make the case. Spending time in cemeteries invites you to sink in. It sticks your mortality front and center and reminds you that you won’t always be here (stress!). It’s also a reminder to appreciate today, and remember those who aren’t around anymore. And as it pertains to visiting the dearly departed while traveling, cemetery strolls offer a distinct way to familiarize yourself with the local people. How a culture approaches death conveys a great deal. (In the case of Mexico, graveyards are not somber, but rather, awash in rainbow colors and elaborate decorations. Very lively!)

On a less morbid note, cemeteries are also really lovely for architecture and natural landscapes. History, too. And with the spooky season in full swing (which I’d argue lasts well beyond Halloween), there’s never been a better time to become a cemetery convert. Below, nine destinations worth planning a trip around.

I’m very lucky to live less than 10 minutes from this world-famous cemetery in Brooklyn. Green-Wood was founded in 1838 and is now a National Historic Landmark. Its paths stretch across 478 hilly acres, winding beside tombs belonging to iconic figures such as Jean-Michel Basquiat and Leonard Bernstein. It’s also a Revolutionary War historic site, and a haven for birdwatchers (over 260 species have been spotted).

When I lived in Los Angeles, outdoor movie screenings at Hollywood Forever Cemetery were a common occurrence. But despite being one of the trendiest ways to spend a Saturday night, this cemetery dates back to 1899. It serves as the final resting place for numerous Hollywood legends, from Judy Garland and Cecil B. DeMille to Eve Babitz. There’s even a cenotaph marker for Toto from The Wizard of Oz for fans to memorialize the adored cairn terrier.

Established in 1821, this breathtaking cemetery features an array of architectural styles, including Neo-Gothic, Neo-Maya, and even Art Deco. Its tree- and mausoleum-lined avenues are home to over 25,000 burial sites, including Felipe Carrillo Puerto (a progressive champion of human rights who was assassinated in 1924). Leading up to Día de Los Muertos, there is a festive procession called Paseo de las Ánimas that begins at the cemetery.

I’ll preface this with the unfortunate fact that Lafayette Cemetery No. 1 is currently closed to the public (reopening is still TBD). But that doesn’t stop me from declaring it my favorite in New Orleans. This 19th-century cemetery has appeared in films such as Interview With the Vampire and can still be enjoyed with a stroll around its scenic perimeter—which is punctuated by towering Spanish live oaks whose massive roots pry the concrete curb upward into a wonderfully spooky, uneven pathway. (If you’d like to go inside a New Orleans cemetery, don’t miss voodoo practitioner Marie Laveau’s tomb in St. Louis Cemetery No. 1).

This is the cemetery I’ve probably returned to the most over the years (and arguably the most famous in the world). It’s huge, so you’ll need to set aside an entire afternoon to explore its 110 acres that play host to thousands of burial plots. Also in the thousands: trees (about 5,000), making this landscape exceptionally beautiful as the leaves change colors. Don’t miss the highlights—Jim Morrison, Frédéric Chopin, Oscar Wilde, Édith Piaf—but also give yourself time to wander without a destination.

This is one of my favorite day trips from New York City this time of year. Hop on the Metro-North and get off at Philipse Manor Station. From there, it’s about a 10-minute walk to this 19th-century cemetery where writer Washington Irving is buried. After meandering around 90 acres of landscaped grounds, join a special event like an adaptation of Irving’s Bracebridge Hall complete with traditional carols and holiday cheer.

This cemetery was designed to resemble a city (rather than a burial ground) and is world-renowned for its neoclassical gates and beautiful maze of tree-lined paths. There are just under 5,000 burial vaults (all above ground), which include imposing mausoleums made of marble. La Recoleta Cemetery is the final resting place for dozens of Argentine presidents, as well as Nobel laureates and cultural figures.

Located a quick stroll from Hampstead Heath—my most beloved green space in all of London—this legendary cemetery is home to a fascinating mix of cultural figures ranging from Karl Marx to George Eliot (Mary Ann Evans, author of Middlemarch). Travelers can explore both the east and west sides of the cemetery without a guided tour, but why not book one for your first visit to get the full lay of the land? Highgate was modeled after Paris's Père Lachaise Cemetery and now operates as a historic park, nature reserve, and active burial site.

On a peaceful bluff of the Wilmington River, Bonaventure is one of the country’s most gorgeous cemeteries. Live oaks draped in Spanish moss hover above Victorian sculptures and timeworn tombstones belonging to famous figures such as John Muir. (Fun fact: during his thousand-mile walk to the gulf, Muir ran out of money and ended up sleeping in the cemetery, which he later wrote about in a famous chapter titled “Camping Among the Tombs.”)
