We are living through an era of doubts about what constitutes reality, and trepidation about the effects AI will have on jobs and livelihoods. That leads to an important question: how can you build a career around being yourself?

Peter Drucker, the father of modern management (1909-2005), was a master at tapping into his own uniqueness while encouraging people to examine what made them unique and valuable to others. Nearly sixty years ago, in his classic book The Effective Executive, he wrote that “the effective executive tries to be himself; he does not pretend to be someone else. He looks at his own performance and at his own results and tries to discern a pattern.”

Consider what made Drucker unique. Many people, then and now, engaged in the same activities as his: writing, teaching, and consulting. But nobody did it quite like him, and it is why people worldwide continue to pay attention to what he wrote, taught, and advised.

Over a period of many years, people wanted to be in Drucker’s presence, either when he gave presentations and keynotes, lectures in classes and at conferences, or in one-on-one meetings, especially for his consulting clients. They wanted access to his mind, not only for in-person activities, but in the numerous books and articles he wrote in a career spanning more than 70 years.

We can strive for similar attention and effects, even though we are unlikely to have the same magnitude, as we express our individuality in daily activities and planning for the future. Making the most of being yourself is a deceptively simple concept, yet is not necessarily easy to carry out.

We can work on how we express what makes us unique and valuable in our own lives and work, and how we interact with other people. It means being genuine, sincere, and authentic, with a positive and resourceful attitude.

Being the most complete expression of yourself helps you to avoid the trap of comparing yourself to others, or engaging in unhelpful thoughts like impostor syndrome, with the nagging feeling that you don’t deserve or belong in your role. Taking advantage of, and looking for the fullest expression of our individuality and its potential requires self-awareness, confidence, high quality, and self-efficacy.

These areas of excellence can still be strengthened in the future. Being yourself does not mean remaining static; like Drucker, you should be open to change and to engage in self-development as you move into an uncertain future. One reason he remained at the top of his various professions for so many years is that he engaged in regular and fairly rigorous self-study projects (from world history to the works of Shakespeare). Everyone is a work in progress.

Being aware of our human strengths can also help other people to take advantage of being themselves. For instance, many of the examples of personal excellence listed above (e.g. listening, empathy and compassion, teaching, and leadership) lend themselves to collaborating with others to aid in their own self-discovery process.

“To be a leader today,” the leadership coach and author Hortense le Gentil recently wrote, “is not to compete with the machine. It is to become more human. To do the things that cannot be coded or digitized. Sure, we can tell AI to write with more empathy and compassion. But the output remains a simulation of human emotion.”

Generative AI is awe-inspiring. In the past four years, we’ve witnessed astonishing achievements. Yet AI can’t copy exactly what we do, who we are, or what makes us unique. How can we maintain and continue to develop that advantage, with AI’s inevitable improvements in the future?
