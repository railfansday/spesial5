The 8 Best Outdoor Security Cameras Keep an Eye on Your Home When You're Away

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Security cameras ensure that you can check what's happening around your home 24/7, capturing footage and alerting you within minutes if there's a problem.

Keeping your home safe these days takes more than a deadbolt and a dog with a menacing bark. Installing a few security cameras around your property is an easy and surprisingly cost-effective way to level up your home security, adding watchful eyes that track comings and goings 24/7. Until relatively recently, the only options for personal home security were comprehensive (and expensive) systems that were installed and monitored by security professionals. Now, you can set up your own cameras and home security system that saves you cash without sacrificing performance.

Outdoor security cameras in particular are your best bet to prevent break-ins. According to Security.org, simply having a clearly visible camera or security device on your property serves as a strong deterrent for trespassers. Outdoor security cameras are equipped with features found in indoor security cameras like high-definition video, night vision, two-way audio, and more. In addition to this, they come equipped with outdoor-specific features like weather-resistant exteriors, sound detection, and integrated motion-activated spotlights.

For this guide, we got our hands on the most promising options from well-established brands including Arlo, Wyze, Ring, and Eufy and tested them at our homes and offices. Read on below to learn about the best outdoor security cameras we recommend, along with expert advice to help you choose the right option for your home and budget.

One quick note before we dive in: It’s increasingly important to pick security gear that’s compatible with whatever smart home system you already have. For example, Amazon makes it easy to pull up a live feed from your Ring camera(s) on an Echo Show or Fire TV and has designed its products to more seamlessly connect with its other smart home devices. Same goes for Google products. If you already have a few items from a major smart home brand, consider buying gear that will be as compatible as possible with it.

There are two types of consumer-grade home security cameras: wireless battery-powered cameras that you can set up easily nearly anywhere, but require you to occasionally replace batteries, and wired models that connect directly to your home's electrical system.

Wired cameras are generally more expensive and require an electrician's help unless you're comfortable with home wiring, but require far less maintenance once they're installed. Their placement is also restricted to locations where you can wired them in.

Battery-powered cameras, by contrast, are only restricted by the range of your WiFi router. Of course, you'll need to access them every few months to replace their batteries. Some models feature solar panels, giving you renewable energy, but panels also impose some placement restrictions. (Namely, access to sunlight).

Wired cameras do fall short of battery-powered models in one niche scenario—when the power goes out. Unlike, say, wired smoke or carbon monoxide detectors, which have reliable battery backup, you can't count on a wired security camera to provide surveillance footage during an extended period of time if there's a power failure. It varies from model to model, but most wired cameras can only last for 30 minutes to, at most, a few hours before they go dark.

To avoid recording 24/7, which wastes power and storage space, outdoor security cameras usually feature motion sensors that trigger recording whenever there’s activity. Many cameras will also an alert to your companion app whenever they start recording.

Most cameras give you the ability to calibrate the motion sensor by setting motion zones in spots where you expect frequent false alarms, like a sidewalk. Others offer smart features that can differentiate between people, animals, and vehicles. You should also have the ability to customize your notifications, so you’ll only receive an alert in certain situations, like if it detects a person.

Furthermore, some cameras feature microphones, enabling audio detection, which can trigger recording and alerts based on nearby sounds that may be out of view (like a window breaking). Audio detection is a useful extra layer in a few specific situations, like when a single camera can’t cover the entire side of your home, but may also lead to more nuisance alerts. If you decide to use one, we recommend customizing your audio alert sensitivity if possible so you aren’t constantly getting notified when the neighbor’s dog barks.

The best outdoor security cameras record video in "Full HD" (1920 x 1080p) resolution, which captures enough detail to identify facial features or a car’s license plate. More expensive cameras record at higher resolutions, such as 2K or "Quad HD" (2560 x 1440p) or 4K Ultra HD (3840 x 2160) video, which deliver better detail. While higher resolution may help, 1080p video is usually clear enough to give you more than enough information in most situations.

Your camera’s field of view (FOV) is just as important as its resolution. Field of view refers to how wide of an angle the lens is and therefore, how much of your yard or porch is in view. With a wider FOV, your camera can see a wider area around your property, providing more coverage. For the best results, we recommend a model with a field of view between 130 and 180 degrees.

Many outdoor cameras also feature an integrated spotlight that can illuminate the surroundings if motion is detected, improving visibility in low-light situations. A spotlight also acts as a deterrent by making potential intruders more visible. Similarly, the majority of outdoor cameras offer some kind of night vision feature, which uses infrared (IR) lights to record a high-contrast black and white video in the dark. Top-of-the-line cameras are capable of recording standard video in low light, minimizing the need for night vision.

There’s no point in recording camera footage if you can’t store it for when you need it. Many manufacturers offer free cloud storage for a limited time—typically seven days–after which video is overwritten with new recordings. You can always download and save that footage, so long as you do during that initial window.

If you want to save your footage for a longer period, there are a couple of options. Many companies provide additional cloud storage for a subscription fee. There are also cameras that feature a microSD card slot for storing footage locally. This does require a bit of extra maintenance in the form of occasionally removing the card and either deleting or downloading the recorded data to free up more space on the card.

Security cameras are more than just their features. Nearly all security cameras are built to connect with other smart home devices. So, before buying any specific option, make sure it is compatible with the devices you already own. For example, Amazon makes it easy to pull up a live feed from your Ring camera(s) on an Echo Show or Fire TV. There are many brands that work with the majority of the smart home software available such as Apple, Google Nest, and Ring. Some have limited functionality on other smart home software systems outside of their brand, but still work with them. Furthermore, most security camera brands require a monthly subscription to gain full access to many of their features. These monthly subscriptions range in price and overall necessity with some basically essential to operate the cameras and store footage in the cloud while others are designed as a bonus add-on to the base product. Prior to purchase, make sure to take a close look at both the smart home compatibility as well as what comes with and without the monthly subscription service.

Most of the outdoor security cameras we recommend are sold individually or as part of a larger set. The number of cameras you need for your property will vary, based on your home security plan and how much camera coverage you want. A well-installed camera with a wide field of view can cover an important and vulnerable area on your property, such as a back door or garage.

That said, even a small home will need at least a few cameras to achieve full coverage on every ground-level door and window. You may need multiple cameras to reveal tricky blind spots created by corners, lawn features, and anything else that might impede a single camera position.

Don't freak out about needing a fleet of surveillance gear, though. Not everyone wants or needs to have every single square inch of their property under constant watch. It’s up to you to decide where you want an extra eye. The important thing is to make sure you know what you need before making a purchase.

Of course. There are no features of an outdoor security camera that would prevent it from working inside. That said, many companies make indoor cameras that are cheaper, and may feature a stand or mounting options that make more sense for blending in with your home or office.

You should always be careful when considering home security. Over the years, there have been multiple reports of strangers accessing live video feeds and recorded videos from home security cameras.

Luckily, there are simple ways to protect yourself. As with other devices, adhering to digital security best practices, such as keeping your smart home devices’ firmware up to date, creating a strong password and activating two-factor authentication wherever possible, will help prevent a breach.

You may also have concerns about who has access to video that’s stored in the cloud. Most companies ask for permission to review recordings for research and development purposes. Depending on the company, this request often comes in the form of an “opt-in” setting in the camera app, though some may turn it on by default unless you “opt out.”

Some may also share recordings with law enforcement. In some cases, they will only do so when compelled by a search warrant. A couple manufacturers, specifically Amazon (Ring) and Google (Nest), have said they will provide footage without a warrant if law enforcement claims it is an emergency.
