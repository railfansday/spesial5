Why researchers are paying attention to the jolt you feel just as you drift off

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

A new survey links frequent sleep starts, the sudden jolts that can happen as you drift off, to poorer sleep, more severe insomnia, and greater anxiety.

Most people have felt it: the sudden jerk as sleep arrives, a leg kick or a whole-body twitch, sometimes with a falling feeling that snaps the eyes open.

In a new survey, people who reported weekly sleep starts along with at least one other symptom reported poorer sleep than those who rarely or never had them.

They also report worse insomnia, more unhelpful beliefs about sleep, and more anxiety.

The survey was online and the answers were self-reported, so it can show a pattern and not a cause. The authors wrote that the jolts and the worry may feed each other, but their data can’t say which comes first.

Tanja Grünberger and Manuel Schabus of the University of Salzburg in Austria led the work with colleagues there.

Sleep researchers call the jolt a hypnic jerk or a sleep start.

Studies that have wired people up while they doze put it in the drowsy stretch between waking and light sleep. The recordings show a brief burst of muscle activity and often a faster heartbeat.

The 96 people in the survey who had sleep starts weekly described far more than a twitch. Muscle jerks were the most common feature, reported by 87.5%.

About two-thirds described a sensation of falling or losing balance, and about the same share reported tingling or numbness.

Nearly half had felt something like an electric shock, 47.9% had seen flashes with their eyes closed, and 21.9% had heard a loud bang that wasn’t there.

Dream-like images, often of falling, went with the jolt for 74%.

The feelings that came with the jolt were mostly unpleasant. Alarm and confusion followed for about four in five participants, and panic for 71.9%. Even so, 95.8% said they usually fell back asleep quickly.

The researchers recruited 194 adults in Austria and Germany, mostly women, with an average age of about 33. Anyone reporting restless legs, sleep apnea, epilepsy or similar conditions was left out.

Ninety-six of them reported jolting awake at least weekly plus at least one other symptom. The other 98 served as the comparison group.

On standard questionnaires, the weekly group scored worse on sleep quality and on insomnia severity.

They held stronger unhelpful beliefs about sleep, rated sleep as more important to them, and scored higher on anxiety and depression.

The weekly group also took longer to fall asleep and slept slightly less.

Daytime sleepiness was the one measure that did not differ between the groups.

Within the weekly group, the people who jolted most often were the ones with the poorest sleep quality, the strongest unhelpful beliefs about sleep, and the most anxiety.

Depression scores were not linked to how often the jolts came.

Together, sleep quality, sleep beliefs, anxiety, age and gender accounted for about 27% of the differences in how often people reported sleep starts. Younger people reported them more often.

Asked what made the jolts more frequent, participants pointed to stress, tension and emotional strain. Sleep loss came up less often.

“The more strongly participants held unhelpful beliefs about sleep and the more anxious they were, the more often they reported sleep starts – and in the moment itself, many described a strong pressure to finally fall asleep,” Grünberger said.

“Our data cannot show what causes what, but this pattern resembles what we know from insomnia.”

The pressure to sleep showed up in the numbers. Among the weekly group, 83.3% said the thought “I finally have to sleep!” ran through their heads around the jolt, and 76% said they had tried to force themselves back to sleep.

Just over half of those with weekly jolts said the episodes made it harder to fall asleep. Among those who reported a delay, it averaged about 43 minutes. About 41% said the jolts made no difference.

The authors read the pattern against a well-known idea in insomnia research: the harder a person monitors and strains for sleep, the more alert they become.

In that view, the jolt and the worry about the jolt can keep each other going.

To calm the jolts, participants most often tried steering their thoughts elsewhere at bedtime, relaxation exercises, exercise, and cutting noise and light in the bedroom. They rated those as only modestly helpful.

There is no standard questionnaire for sleep starts, so the team wrote its own items from the clinical literature.

The questions have not been tested against any validated measure, and the weekly cutoff that defined the groups was chosen after the data were in.

The survey also could not separate sleep starts from look-alike experiences, such as the loud-noise phenomenon called exploding head syndrome or vivid images as sleep begins.

People answered from memory, and no one’s sleep was recorded in a lab.

“Because there is no established questionnaire for sleep starts, we developed our own items based on the clinical literature,” Grünberger said.

“This allowed us to capture a broad range of experiences – but it also means that our findings now need to be confirmed with validated instruments and objective sleep recordings.”

The next step, Schabus said, is to test whether treatments that lower arousal and sleep worry, the kind used for insomnia, also ease distressing sleep starts. No one has run that test yet.

Until someone does, the survey offers a description of the jolt and the people it troubles, and an untested idea about why.

The full study was published in the journal PLOS Mental Health.
