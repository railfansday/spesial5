The Porsche 718 Boxster EV Does a Convincing Impression of a Sports Car

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

An early taste of Porsche’s electric convertible in prototype form excites but raises some tough questions.

The most important detail about the electric Porsche 718 Boxster isn’t that it’s an EV, it’s that it’s a Porsche sports car. The new electric sports car will be officially unveiled next month, but before then, Porsche is offering up a few details on a black-and-red-camoed platter. More important, beyond the limited intel, I got to spin laps in a prototype at the Porsche Experience Center Los Angeles. I also rode shotgun while an engineer hotfooted it through the Angeles National Forest.

I won’t keep you in suspense. As antithetical as an EV might be to the point of a Boxster, this 718 looks, dances, and even sounds (on the inside) like the flat-six-powered machine we’ve known and loved for years. What Porsche put together is an electric car that tries harder than any before at copying the experience of a mid-engine sports car. And there’s a lot more to it than just fake noises.

Where Porsche put the 800-volt battery pack in the Boxster flips the “every EV drives the same” trope on its side. Instead of the industry-standard floor-positioned battery pack, the 718 stuffs it exactly where the internal-combustion engine was traditionally housed in the Boxster: right behind the driver’s head. This changes the weight balance from the EV-typical 50/50 to a much more mid-engine-sports-car-like 40/60. The car’s polar moment of inertia is undeniably behind you, a new sensation in the world of electric cars. Maserati planned something similar for the MC20 Folgore but canceled that project before it made production.

Other big benefits come with this battery arrangement. Engineers have been able to position the seats just as low in the floor as in the gas-powered Boxster, maintaining a proper sports-car driving position. I can promise nobody will be begging for it to be lower. It helps the design too. While this prototype was covered in camouflage, it was evident that there wasn’t a thick battery pack ruining the Boxster’s proportions. Also unaffected by the transition to electric power are the Boxster trunk and frunk. Just by eyeballing them I could tell that they look to be nearly identical to the previous car in terms of capaciousness.

Deciding how large the battery pack should be was one of the biggest problems Porsche had to solve. We’ll need to wait for the reveal to know the car’s range and battery capacity, but everything is a trade-off when it comes to an electric sports car. All we know about its curb weight is that an “intelligent lightweight construction” will yield a vehicle weight “similar to current 911 models.”

For reference, a 911 GTS Cabriolet currently comes in around 3700 pounds. After many, many laps around the Porsche Experience Center, my gut tells me the Boxster weighs somewhere in the 3700–4000-pound range. Or, in other words, exceptional for an EV. And not so great compared with the outgoing 718 GTS 4.0, which managed to weigh in under 3200 pounds.

Without range figures to go by, my only reference point for expectations was that the car flashed a “be mindful of range” warning after about 40 minutes of push laps on Porsche’s track. There are plenty of gas-powered sports cars that would be needing a fresh tank at that point too, although obviously not 911s equipped with the extended-range fuel tank.

Audible entertainment is handled by Porsche’s E-Shift system, which will come standard. This virtual simulation of a PDK transmission and engine noise debuted in the Taycan, and the new Boxster is equipped with its own version of the software. Press the button on the steering wheel, and the naturally aspirated flat-six from the 981-generation Boxster GTS springs to life over speakers both inside and outside the car.

Engineers painstakingly measured, recorded, and reproduced the sound, then emulated a seven-speed PDK for you to shift through. Unlike the Hyundai Ioniq 5 N or the Genesis GV60 Magma, Porsche does not simulate a gas engine’s torque curve in E-Shift, preferring to maximize performance over experience. That said, there are still some theatrics to be found. Downshifting supports “engine” braking via electric-motor regen, and you even hear the “exhaust” popping and crackling on the overrun.

For many Porsche enthusiasts, it won’t be enough, especially when you remove the roof. The speakers are smart in that Porsche is able to simulate the noise as though it’s coming from behind you in traditional mid-engine fashion. But the spell is easily broken when the wind overpowers the speakers, or you gun it past a wall and there’s no delicious mechanical flat-six howl echoing back at you. It’s fake. I could tell straightaway. And everyone else will too.

But alas! I had exponentially more fun driving the Boxster with E-Shift than without, especially on the track. You can choose between manual and automatic gearchanges, and I largely left it in automatic. Hearing and remembering where the “engine” revved as I approached the braking zone helped me learn the track quickly and pick out my braking points by sound alone, very similar to Road & Track’s first experience in the BMW M3 EV prototype running a comparable system.

Then on corner exit, it supplied a faithful throttle reference that helped me trace little slides out of tighter turns in the permissive PSM Sport traction mode. As soon as I turned E-Shift off, I felt lost on the track. And call me old-fashioned, but silently skittering between corners with just the sound of tire squeal is sad. Even more so, because this chassis is a damn good one.

Porsche is being coy with the finer details but let on that numerous chassis components are based on those found in the 911. Boxster owners will be familiar with this hand-me-down strategy, and it continues into the electric era. The steering, braking system, and wheel-and-tire combinations are based on the 911. Pirelli P Zero R summer tires were fitted to staggered wheels (20s in front and 21s in rear) for my time in the car, with a 255-section width in front and a 305-section width in the rear. The new car has PASM damping tech, and engineers note both the damper and spring calibration are softer than those in an equivalent 911. The suspension’s controlled but comfortable composure over rough sections of the road confirmed as much. Thankfully, the brakes just felt like brakes with no weird regen sensation and plenty of feel at the limit.

My test car didn’t have it, but a sport suspension will also be available. And for the first time, the 718 gets optional rear-axle steering, not to help tighten its turning radius but to supply greater stability at high speeds where the rear wheels can turn in coordination with the fronts. There’s even a front-axle lift, picking up where the Spyder RS left off as the first Boxster with said feature.

The powertrain itself is all good news. Porsche wouldn’t supply an output number, but the car I drove employs a single electric motor in the rear. It’s reportedly powerful enough for a 0–60-mph run in under three seconds. Porsche says it will be the most powerful 718 so far, meaning more than the 493-hp output of the GT4 RS, and it will almost certainly be the quickest to 60 too. As with Porsche’s other EVs, there’s a push-to-pass mode you can activate for 10 seconds at a time, and using launch control unleashes maximum output too. But at no point was I wanting for more punch around the relatively tight track. Once the tires warmed, launching was effortless, and I didn’t doubt the sub-three-second claim for a moment. The same goes for the road drive, where the Boxster is powerful enough to need serious restraint to not end up doing jail speeds.

Porsche Torque Vectoring Plus, another feature derived from the 911, helps dole out power between the two rear wheels to maximum effectiveness. Between this power-distribution system and the Boxster’s happy tendency to kindly bend itself into corners under braking, this 718 has the balance and more jovial nature of the previous one. But another trait carries over: The rear shows a tendency to dance further out than you would expect from an EV with a more typical 50/50 weight distribution. And despite that Porsche puts big emphasis on keeping weight down (it even reduced the charge ports from its typical two to only one), there was no denying that this Boxster felt porkier than before. The difference was most obvious in the tighter corners where the mass couldn’t be disguised and the grippy front tires ultimately surrendered to the sheer weight that is being asked to turn.

Porsche says it will be launching the electric Boxster into the world with two versions to start. While it wouldn’t reveal the trims, I do know that the version I drove will be the spicier of the two, therefore corresponding with the previous Boxster S. I can’t be certain whether it’s a hit or a miss until I experience the final production car, but I can say this is much more than simply an electric powertrain transplant.

It challenges the hard-core among us in ways EVs simply haven’t before, and if it’s not your cup of tea, don’t worry. Porsche will keep making gas-powered performance cars. It’s just a shame that they won’t start under $100,000, where this one is projected to begin.
