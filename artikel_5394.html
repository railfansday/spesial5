10 cyberattacks that changed history, from the Morris worm to Colonial Pipeline

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 10 cyberattacks that changed history, from the 1988 Morris worm to the 2021 Colonial Pipeline ransomware attack, show how digital threats reshaped security, policy and business

Each attack on this list exploited a weakness that defenders had not yet treated as real. In 1988, that weakness was the open, trusting design of the early internet. By 2021, it was the business computer systems behind a major U.S. fuel pipeline. In between, attackers turned trusting inboxes, industrial controllers, factory-default passwords and routine software updates into ways in. The fallout forced governments, companies and ordinary computer users to rethink what needed protecting.

This list of cyberattacks that changed history follows that progression in chronological order. It begins with a graduate student's self-replicating program and an email worm that spread through a love-letter subject line. It moves on to the denial-of-service attacks that flooded a whole country's government, bank and media websites in 2007. It then covers malware that damaged uranium enrichment centrifuges in Iran. Later entries include destructive attacks on a Hollywood studio and on global businesses such as the shipping firm Maersk. The list ends with a ransomware attack that led a pipeline operator to halt operations as a precaution.

These famous cyberattacks matter well beyond the security industry. They tested the law, producing the first conviction under the 1986 Computer Fraud and Abuse Act. The 2007 attacks on Estonia were followed by the NATO Cooperative Cyber Defence Centre of Excellence, set up in Tallinn in 2008. Governments also began naming names. The U.S. and the U.K. have publicly attributed several of the attacks below to North Korea or Russia.

For businesses, the lessons are practical. An unpatched Windows flaw let ransomware move across networks. A trusted vendor's software update carried intruders into customer systems. An attack on business IT systems, not on pipeline controls, still stopped fuel shipments.

The 10 attacks below are a selection, not a ranking or a complete record. Each slide explains what happened, how the attack worked and what it changed. Where a government has formally attributed an attack, the slide names that government.

On Nov. 2, 1988, Cornell graduate student Robert Tappan Morris released a self-replicating program that spread across the early internet and slowed many connected computers. Morris became the first person convicted under the 1986 Computer Fraud and Abuse Act.

The program was a worm. A virus needs a person to open a file or run a program. A worm copies itself to new hosts on its own. It looks for reachable machines, gets in through a weakness and starts the cycle again from its new location.

The network Morris released it onto was small by later standards. Its users were mostly universities, research labs and government and military sites. Many machines were set up to trust one another. Security practices assumed that the people on the network were colleagues.

The worm took advantage of that trust. It targeted computers running versions of the Unix operating system. It used flaws in common programs, including the sendmail email service and the finger utility. It also abused trust settings that let one machine log in to another, and it tried to guess weak user passwords.

A design error turned the program into an outage. The worm was meant to check whether a machine was already infected. It was also written to copy itself in some cases regardless of the answer. Computers ended up running many copies at once. Those copies consumed processing power until the machines slowed or stopped responding.

System administrators spent days isolating machines, analyzing the code and cleaning up. Some institutions disconnected from the network entirely while they worked.

The legal response set a precedent. Federal prosecutors brought the case under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, which targets unauthorized access to computers. The verdict showed that releasing self-spreading code onto a network could be prosecuted as a federal crime in the U.S.

The incident also changed how defenders organized. Within weeks, the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency funded the creation of the Computer Emergency Response Team Coordination Center at Carnegie Mellon University. Similar incident-response teams now operate inside governments and companies around the world.

Morris later joined the faculty of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and co-founded the startup accelerator Y Combinator. The worm that carries his name made self-spreading code a recognized category of security threat. It also showed that on a connected network, one flaw can travel far beyond the machine where it started.

The ILOVEYOU worm spread worldwide in May 2000 inside an email that looked like a love letter. It showed that a message from a familiar sender could carry malware across continents without exploiting any flaw in the operating system.

The worm originated in the Philippines. Its emails carried the subject line "ILOVEYOU" and a brief note asking the recipient to open the attached letter. The attachment was named LOVE-LETTER-FOR-YOU.TXT.vbs.

That file name was the core of the trick. Windows hid known file extensions by default at the time. Many users saw only "LOVE-LETTER-FOR-YOU.TXT" and assumed it was a harmless text file.

The real extension, .vbs, marked the file as a VBScript program. Windows ran it as soon as someone double-clicked it.

Once opened, the script did two things. It damaged files on the infected computer, overwriting many of them, including image files, with copies of itself. It also used Microsoft $MSFT Outlook to email itself to the contacts in the victim's address book.

That second step explains the worm's reach. Each new recipient got the message from someone they knew — a colleague, a relative, a client. A note that might have been deleted if it came from a stranger looked safe when it came from a friend.

Security professionals call this technique social engineering. It targets human judgment rather than a software bug. The ILOVEYOU worm did not need to break into a machine on its own. It needed a person to click.

The flood of copies overwhelmed mail servers. Many companies and government offices took their email systems offline to stop the spread and clean infected machines.

Microsoft released an Outlook security update later in 2000. The update blocked users from opening script attachments such as .vbs files directly from email.

The same formula drives phishing today. A trusted-looking sender, an emotional or urgent hook and an attachment or link that does the damage remain standard tools for attackers. Corporate security training that warns staff about unexpected attachments addresses the exact behavior the worm exploited in 2000.

In April 2007, websites run by Estonian government bodies, banks and media outlets were flooded with traffic after authorities in Tallinn relocated the Bronze Soldier, a Soviet war memorial. It was among the first cyberattacks to strike a nation's online services across so many sectors at once.

The Bronze Soldier is a bronze statue of a soldier in Red Army uniform. It was unveiled in Tallinn in 1947 to mark the Soviet victory in World War II. Many Estonians linked it to decades of Soviet occupation. Many of the country's Russian speakers saw it as a tribute to the war dead.

The government moved the statue from central Tallinn to the Defence Forces Cemetery of Tallinn. The decision set off riots in the capital in late April. The online assault ran alongside the street unrest.

A denial-of-service attack does not break into a system or steal data. It overwhelms a server with more requests than it can handle, so legitimate users cannot get through. When the traffic comes from many machines at once, often hijacked computers in a botnet, it is called a distributed denial-of-service, or DDoS, attack.

Estonia's heavy use of online services raised the cost of the disruption. After regaining independence in 1991, the country moved much of its public life online, from banking to government services. In March 2007, weeks before the attacks, it became the first country to allow internet voting in a national parliamentary election.

That reliance cut both ways. A population used to banking and dealing with the state online had fewer offline routines to fall back on when those sites slowed or went dark.

The attacks also raised a question NATO had not yet answered. Estonia had joined the alliance in 2004. The open question was whether a cyberattack on a member could count as an armed attack.

The NATO Cooperative Cyber Defence Centre of Excellence opened in Tallinn the following year. The center later convened the international experts who wrote the Tallinn Manual, first published in 2013. The manual examines how international law applies to cyber conflict.

At its 2014 summit in Wales, NATO declared that cyber defense is part of its core task of collective defense. The alliance has since treated a serious cyberattack as a possible trigger for its mutual-defense commitment, to be judged case by case.

Stuxnet, a piece of malware discovered in 2010, targeted Siemens industrial controllers at Iran's Natanz uranium enrichment plant and damaged centrifuges. It showed that computer code could cause physical destruction, not just steal or erase data.

The target was an industrial control system. This is the kind of equipment that runs machinery rather than office networks. Facilities like Natanz rely on programmable logic controllers. These are small dedicated computers that tell motors, valves and pumps what to do.

Stuxnet was built to look for Siemens controllers and the Siemens Step 7 software that engineers use to program them. Earlier malware typically stole information, sent spam or crashed computers. Stuxnet was written to change how machines behaved in the physical world.

At Natanz, that meant interfering with the centrifuges that spin uranium gas to enrich it. The malware altered the speed at which the centrifuges ran. The machines depend on precise, stable rotation, and the changes damaged them.

The code was also designed to conceal its work. It fed operators readings that suggested normal operation while the equipment was being pushed outside its settings. That deception meant the damage could look like mechanical failure rather than an attack.

Stuxnet could reach systems that were not directly connected to the open internet. It was able to spread through USB drives. That route let it cross from ordinary computers into isolated industrial networks.

Its sabotage was narrowly aimed. Stuxnet checked for a specific configuration of equipment before running its damaging routine. On computers that did not match that setup, it did not attempt the sabotage.

The malware first came to light through VirusBlokAda, a small antivirus firm in Belarus. Security researchers around the world then spent months taking the code apart to understand its purpose.

Reporting has widely linked Stuxnet to the U.S. and Israel. Neither government has officially acknowledged a role.

The same families of controllers run power grids, water treatment plants, factories and other heavy infrastructure. Stuxnet demonstrated that an attacker with enough resources could manipulate that equipment through software alone. For the operators of those systems, it turned a theoretical risk into a documented case. An isolated network is not automatically a safe one.

In November 2014, attackers broke into Sony $SONY Pictures Entertainment. They stole and leaked internal data and wiped company systems. The FBI publicly attributed the attack to North Korea in December 2014. That statement turned a corporate breach into a geopolitical event.

The timing tied the intrusion to a single film. Sony Pictures was preparing to release The Interview, a comedy built around a fictional plot to assassinate North Korean leader Kim Jong Un. The attackers called themselves Guardians of Peace.

The hack combined two kinds of damage. The first was theft. Attackers copied internal material and released it publicly. The leaks included private emails, employees' personal information and unreleased films.

Leaked data does not come back once it is public. Journalists, competitors and anyone else online could read it. The studio had no way to recall it.

The second kind of damage was destruction. Wiper malware erases or overwrites data on infected machines. It can leave computers unable to start or function. At Sony Pictures, ordinary business tools went dark while the studio's private records circulated online.

Each problem makes the other harder to handle. A data leak is easier to manage when internal systems still work. A system outage is easier to manage when sensitive records stay private. Sony Pictures faced both at once.

The target also mattered. Sony Pictures is a film studio, not a defense contractor or a government agency. The attack showed that a private company could be drawn into a dispute between states because of what it produced. For entertainment, media and other consumer-facing firms, that exposure became part of the security picture.

The FBI's December 2014 statement marked a shift in how the U.S. government handled state-linked hacking. Naming a foreign government in public, rather than responding quietly, became a policy tool in its own right. North Korea denied involvement.

In January 2015, the U.S. imposed new sanctions on North Korea in response to the attack and other actions by its government. Public attribution backed by economic penalties gave the U.S. government a way to respond to a cyberattack without military action.

On Oct. 21, 2016, the Mirai botnet hit the DNS provider Dyn with a denial-of-service attack and disrupted access to many major websites. Mirai was built from hijacked cameras, routers and other connected devices that still used their factory default passwords.

A botnet is a network of compromised machines that an attacker controls remotely. Each infected device takes commands from a central server. A single home camera has little computing power or bandwidth. In large numbers, such devices can send enough traffic to overwhelm a target.

Mirai found its recruits by scanning the internet for devices with open remote-login services. It then tried a built-in list of common factory usernames and passwords. Many devices shipped with the same credentials, and many owners never changed them. Once inside, the malware added the device to the botnet and kept searching for more.

The choice of target explains why the outage reached so far. DNS, the Domain Name System, translates the website names people type into the numerical addresses computers use to find each other. Without a working DNS lookup, a browser cannot locate a site. That holds even if the site's own servers are running normally.

Dyn handled these lookups for many companies at once. Flooding its infrastructure with junk traffic meant legitimate requests for its customers' domains went unanswered. Users trying to reach those websites saw failures, although the websites themselves had not been hacked. Trouble at one provider became an outage for many companies.

Before Mirai, a weak password on a webcam could look like a risk mainly to that device's owner. The Dyn attack showed that the same weakness, repeated across a large population of cheap Internet of Things devices, could be turned against unrelated companies and their customers.

The owners of infected devices often had no sign that anything was wrong. A hijacked camera or router could keep working normally while also taking part in attacks. That made the problem hard to fix from the user side.

The episode also carried a lesson about concentration. Companies that relied on a single DNS provider had a single point of failure outside their own systems. Spreading DNS service across more than one provider is one way organizations reduce that exposure.

Mirai's source code was posted publicly online shortly before the Dyn attack. That release let other attackers build their own versions. Malware derived from Mirai continued to circulate in the years that followed.

WannaCry ransomware spread around the world in May 2017 by exploiting a Windows flaw through EternalBlue, an exploit tied to leaked U.S. National Security Agency tools. The organizations it disrupted included parts of the U.K. National Health Service.

Ransomware encrypts the files on an infected computer and demands payment for the key to unlock them. WannaCry displayed a ransom note asking victims to pay in bitcoin. Most ransomware of that period depended on someone opening a malicious file or link. WannaCry did not need that step.

The malware behaved like a worm. Once inside a network, it searched for other Windows machines carrying the same weakness in the Server Message Block $SQ file-sharing protocol. It then copied itself to them. A single infected computer could spread the infection across an office network without anyone clicking anything.

That flaw already had a fix. Microsoft $MSFT released a security update for it in March 2017. A group calling itself the Shadow Brokers published EternalBlue online in April 2017. Organizations that had not installed the update were exposed. So were those still running unsupported versions of Windows, such as Windows XP. After the attack began, Microsoft issued an emergency patch for Windows XP, a system it no longer supported.

In the U.K., the NHS felt the effects directly. Infected computers at some hospitals and GP practices forced staff to cancel appointments and operations. Some ambulances were diverted to other hospitals. The episode showed that an unpatched office computer could interfere with patient care.

The spread slowed because of Marcus Hutchins, a British security researcher. While examining the code, he found that it checked whether a particular web domain existed. The domain was unregistered, and he registered it. Once the address went live, new infections stopped carrying out their attack. The domain had worked as a kill switch.

In December 2017, the U.S. and the U.K. publicly attributed WannaCry to North Korea.

The lasting lesson concerns patching. WannaCry exploited a vulnerability that already had a published fix. It hit hardest at organizations that had delayed updates or kept outdated systems in service. The attack also raised questions about government agencies holding on to software flaws. A tool linked to intelligence work had ended up powering a global outbreak.

NotPetya first spread in June 2017 through a compromised update to M.E.Doc, an accounting program used by businesses in Ukraine. It looked like ransomware, but it worked as wiper malware built to destroy data rather than hold it for payment.

The outbreak began on June 27, 2017, the day before Ukraine's Constitution Day. Organizations that had installed the tainted M.E.Doc update became the first victims. From there, the code moved across internal networks on its own, with no clicks required from anyone inside.

Its spread relied on more than one method. It used EternalBlue, the same leaked Windows exploit behind WannaCry weeks earlier. It also harvested administrator credentials from infected machines. Those stolen credentials let it reach computers that had already been patched against EternalBlue.

The damage did not stay inside Ukraine. Companies with offices or network connections in the country were exposed. The Danish shipping firm Maersk was among those hit, and its disruption showed how one infected foothold could reach a global operation. The drugmaker Merck $MRK, the snack maker Mondelez $MDLZ International and FedEx $FDX's TNT Express unit also reported damage.

Infected machines displayed a demand for bitcoin and an email address to contact after paying. The German email provider hosting that address shut the account down. That cut off any contact with the attackers. The larger problem sat in the code itself. The identifier shown to victims had no working link to a decryption key. Paying could not restore files.

That difference separates the two categories. Ransomware is a criminal business model that depends on returning data to victims who pay. A wiper has no such incentive. NotPetya was built to destroy, and its payment screen gave victims a false impression that recovery was possible.

In February 2018, the U.S., the U.K. and other governments attributed NotPetya to the GRU, Russia's military intelligence service. The attribution had consequences beyond diplomacy. Mondelez sued its insurer, Zurich American, after the insurer cited a war exclusion to deny coverage for NotPetya losses. The dispute put a direct question to insurers and corporate buyers. It asked how cyber policies treat attacks that governments link to a foreign state.

Attackers hid malicious code inside software updates for SolarWinds' Orion network-management platform. The security firm FireEye disclosed the intrusion in December 2020.

Orion is software that IT teams use to monitor servers, network equipment and applications from a single dashboard. To do that job, it typically runs with broad access to the systems it watches. That level of access made it a valuable place to plant a backdoor.

The method is known as a software supply-chain attack. Instead of breaking into each target directly, attackers compromise a vendor that many organizations already trust. The vendor's normal distribution channel then delivers the malicious code on the attackers' behalf.

In the SolarWinds case, the tampered code was built into Orion updates that the company shipped to its customers. Organizations that installed those updates were following standard advice to keep software current. The update process itself carried the compromise.

FireEye uncovered the operation while investigating a breach of its own network. It named the backdoor Sunburst. The firm then published technical details so other organizations could search their own systems for signs of it.

In April 2021, the U.S. government attributed the operation to Russia's Foreign Intelligence Service, the SVR. That attribution placed the campaign in the category of espionage by a foreign intelligence agency. It was not presented as the work of criminals seeking payment.

The incident changed how organizations think about the software they buy. A digitally signed update from a known vendor had long been treated as safe to install. SolarWinds showed that a signature confirms where code came from, not that the vendor's build process was clean.

That lesson moved supply-chain security into government policy. In May 2021, President Joe Biden signed Executive Order 14028, which set new security expectations for software sold to federal agencies. One measure it promoted was the software bill of materials, a list of the components inside a product.

For companies, the practical result was closer scrutiny of vendors. Reviews of how suppliers build and sign their code joined the usual security questionnaires. Organizations also began asking how much network access a monitoring tool really needs. The focus widened from whether a supplier was reputable to how its code was produced and delivered.

In May 2021, the DarkSide ransomware group attacked Colonial Pipeline's business systems. The company halted pipeline operations as a precaution. Fuel supply disruption and panic buying followed in parts of the U.S. Southeast.

Colonial Pipeline operates a fuel line that runs from the Houston area of Texas to the New York Harbor region. It carries refined products such as gasoline, diesel and jet fuel to markets along the East Coast. Many communities in the Southeast rely on that flow. When it stopped, shortages appeared at gas stations within days.

The attackers did not seize the pipes, pumps or valves. Their target was the corporate information technology network. That is the side of a company that handles email, billing, accounting and records. The systems that physically control fuel flow are a separate layer, often called operational technology.

Colonial shut down the physical operation anyway. The company said it took systems offline proactively to contain the threat. An operator in that position cannot be certain that an intrusion has not crept toward the control systems. Restarting before the network is understood carries that risk.

The incident showed that an attack on ordinary office computers could interrupt a piece of national energy infrastructure. Security plans that treat corporate IT and industrial operations as separate worlds do not account for that dependence.

The FBI identified DarkSide as the group responsible in May 2021. DarkSide ran a ransomware-as-a-service model. In that arrangement, developers build and maintain the malware. Affiliates use it to break into victims, and the two sides split any ransom payments.

The structure resembles a franchise business. A small core of developers can extend its reach through many independent operators. That model also makes the groups harder to disrupt. Removing one affiliate leaves the rest of the operation intact.

Federal regulators responded within weeks. In May 2021, the Transportation Security Administration issued a security directive for pipeline operators. It required companies to report cybersecurity incidents to the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. It also required them to name a cybersecurity coordinator. A second directive followed in July 2021. Before the attack, pipeline operators had worked under largely voluntary federal cybersecurity guidance.
