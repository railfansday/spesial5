HRF Announces Funding of Seven Bitcoin Projects for People Living Under Authoritarian Regimes

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Together, these efforts help human rights defenders, journalists, nonprofits, and individuals hold, send, and verify their money freely under repression.

HRF’s grantees for Round III of 2026 are:

Many people want to self-custody bitcoin, but in much of the world, buying a hardware wallet is too expensive. CryoBrick transforms feature phones into offline Bitcoin signing devices. Because it looks and works like an ordinary phone, activists living under authoritarian regimes can self-custody bitcoin while maintaining plausible deniability. Technical Director Apalok will use HRF’s support to conduct comprehensive security hardening and peer review, integrate Bitchat for offline messaging, and publish how-to guides, expanding access to bitcoin and the property rights it protects to a far larger global user base.

Bitcoin’s benefits remain out of reach for many African communities because high-quality education resources do not exist in their local languages. Exonumia, an open-source project that translates Bitcoin educational content, is building a Bitcoin Glossary in 21 African languages. The project will coordinate a continent-wide community of translators through three in-person and 12 virtual translation sprints, where native speakers review and validate more than 250 terms. Because every term is validated by native speakers rather than machines, the approved terms give wallet developers a trustworthy foundation for localizing bitcoin software into African languages, extending access to bitcoin education and the financial freedom it offers. HRF’s support will see the glossary published as a free public resource.

In much of Africa, democratic elections remain a struggle and most countries are not fully free. Exacerbating this, evidence of fraud documented by citizens is often lost because paper forms go missing and photos and messages cannot be verified. Pawavox, created by Nigerian civic-tech founder Jason Omu, is a platform that gives election observers and human rights defenders a new way to record irregularities. Using Pawavox, observers capture short video or voice reports, each GPS-tagged and timestamped. This evidence is then published to Nostr, a decentralized communications protocol, as a record that is much harder for anyone to tamper with. HRF’s grant will help fund the completion of the Nostr integration and expansion across Africa, helping protect the integrity of the evidence on which democratic accountability depends.

Branded hardware wallets are a common way to self-custody bitcoin, but under repressive regimes, they can be expensive and attract unwanted attention. SeedSigner is an open-source signing device that users assemble themselves from inexpensive, widely available parts, so there is no finished-device supply chain for an attacker to compromise. It currently runs on a small computer, called a Raspberry Pi Zero, which can be unavailable in regions that need self-custody most. Lead maintainer Keith Mukai will use HRF’s support to migrate the SeedSigner code to more widely available hardware, ending dependence on any single manufacturer. Then, more individuals under repressive regimes will be able to hold their own bitcoin without trusting anyone else.

Nigeria is one of the world’s most active digital asset markets, but the legal path to acquiring bitcoin runs through exchanges that collect troves of identity and transactional data. At the same time, many rural Nigerian homes are receiving solar electricity for the first time, but much of that power goes unused in the daytime. Strandedwatt, a bitcoin mining initiative, installs small open-source Bitcoin miners in homes, converting surplus solar power into bitcoin, which is paid privately and directly to host households. The next step, backed by HRF, will see Bitnob CEO and project lead Bernard Parah deploy 100 Jua Kali miners in these homes and publish an open-source playbook other communities can replicate. Meanwhile, households excluded by the Nigerian financial system gain a permissionless path to freedom money.

A commercial Bitcoin hardware wallet has software and hardware the user cannot inspect, and carrying one can signal to outsiders that someone owns bitcoin. Specter DIY is a fully open-source, air-gapped Bitcoin hardware wallet that anyone can build from off-the-shelf components and verify line by line. There is no purchase record, and the finished device does not look like a Bitcoin wallet at all. For activists in Belarus, dissidents in Venezuela, and journalists in Burma, it is a path to financial self-sovereignty. HRF’s grant to the Specter Association, the nonprofit that maintains the project, helps fund hardware costs, AI tooling, and vulnerability bounties, ensuring the developers behind a self-custody tool dissidents can inspect and verify remain supported.
