Early Stress Can Change Your Brain

Most of us have an understanding of what it means to be stressed. We’ve all experienced the physical, cognitive, and emotional changes that stress can produce. In general, stress represents our physical and mental response to challenge, change, or threat. That response is different among individuals, changes over time, depending on what we’ve learned about how to handle challenges, our physical health, and even how tired we might be when we encounter a stressful event.

There are some general patterns to the way stress affects us. One pattern is that stress early in life can, in some individuals, change the response to later stress. The stress sensitization hypothesis proposes that early exposure to stressful events can amplify our later response to stress, making us more vulnerable to psychological distress like depression and anxiety (Stroud, 2018).

The stress sensitization hypothesis grew out of research by Robert Post in the early 1990s. Post noted that stressful events tended to be associated more with “the first episode of major affective disorder than with subsequent episodes” (p. 999). These first stressors sensitized our nervous system, changing the way that the genes for neurotransmitters and receptors are expressed, which in turn changes the ways in which neurons respond, ultimately changing our responses to the next stressful event. He proposed that these long-term changes in neural systems can lead to “stress sensitization,” or the risk that even very low-intensity stress events can trigger episodes of depression after the first stress exposure, and “stress autonomy,” where episodes of depression can occur even without the stressful event being present (Post 1992, Stroud, 2018).

The physical changes that characterize the stress response are collectively referred to as the “fight or flight response” (FFR). The FFR begins with activation of the sympathetic adrenal medullary (SAM) system, called the “fast” system because it is activated immediately, which ultimately tells the adrenal gland to release norepinephrine and epinephrine (adrenaline). SAM system activation increases muscle tension, respiration, and heart rate, as well as cognitive/behavioral changes like increased arousal, alertness, vigilance, and analgesia. The slower hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) system is also activated by a signal from the amygdala, ultimately telling the adrenal gland to release the hormone cortisol, which signals the liver to release glucose and fatty acids to fuel the body’s response to the threat (Chus, Marwaha, Sanvictores, Awosika and Ayers, 2024).

Short-term stress that can be resolved relatively quickly can enhance learning and memory, but long-term, chronic stress produces negative effects on both body and cognition. Essentially, all of the physical and psychological aspects of the stress response that help us handle stress in the short term can, if the stress continues without letting up, become maladaptive (Schwabe and Daskalakis, 2025). Part of that maladaptive response to long-term stress is the potential for an increased risk of depression and anxiety.

Stroud (2018) points out that about half of the people who experience one episode of depression do not go on to experience another episode, despite the fact that they have undoubtedly experienced subsequent major and minor stresses in life. Individuals also vary in resilience to stress and its effects. A complex array of genetic, social, environmental, and learned factors makes some individuals more resilient to stress than others.

Investigations into what makes people resistant to the effects of stress have centered on the SAM and HPA systems. In a review of the research into stress resilience, Ozbay, Johnson, Dimoulas, Morgan, Charney and Southwick (2007) note that “stress resilience seems to be associated with an ability to keep the HPA-axis and SAM system activity within an optimal range during stress exposure and terminate the stress response once the stressor is no longer present” (p. 36). Research into neural control of these two systems has focused on the role of the hypothalamus, which sits at the beginning of both the fast and slow systems.

Using a mouse model of stress, Pennington, LaBanca, Mahmud, et al. (2026) investigated the role of the anterior hypothalamus (AH) in creating stress sensitivity and the effects of the neural changes stress induces. Mice in group 1 were exposed to a high-intensity stressor (foot shock). Group 2 mice were not stressed at all. Ten days later, both groups of mice were exposed to a new, novel potential stressor (a sound they had not heard before), and their “freeze” response (the typical mouse response to something new and threatening) to this mild stress was assessed. Previously shocked mice froze for a significantly longer period of time than did non-shocked mice, indicating increased stress sensitivity in the shocked group. A scan of a marker for neural activity (c-fos) in the brains of these two groups of mice showed much higher activity in the hypothalamus of group 1 mice after exposure to the sound than in group 2 mice. This hyper-reactivity was particularly marked in the AH.

Recordings of activity in the AH neurons revealed that they responded specifically to negative, aversive stimuli and were inhibited by rewards. In addition, exposure to prior stress amplified the proportion of AH neurons that responded to negative stimuli, evidence of probable stress sensitization.

It appears that previous exposure to stress can reorganize the way the brain responds to subsequent stressful stimuli, via the AH. A better understanding of how the brain reacts to stress may help find ways to increase stress resilience and to decrease sensitivity to early stressors.
