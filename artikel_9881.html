There's two distinct types of "post-purchase services", and this law is only applying to one of them.

On one hand you have services that act more like an "interface" for an external service. This could be red light camera warnings, or in-car wifi over cellular, or maybe keeping your navigation maps updated. Those are allowed because they're a genuine ongoing service that by design must be maintained and provided externally. It's like a newspaper subscription, and it makes sense for that to be an ongoing charge

The other type is a hardware-activation. This is to enable and allow continued use of hardware that's already included in the vehicle, where no substantial outside service is being provided, such as BMW's heated seats. There are several reasons manufacturers do this, including it being easier to make cars with a standard feature package rather than custom ones a-la-carte, and also to be able to sell you a subscription later that you didn't originally want to purchase without expensive installation after purchase. This is the category New York is going after. It's been getting abused in several ways, including forcing users to 'rent' the use of hardware they have purchased and own, and in cases of one-time payment to be able to de-activate it if the car is resold and charge the new owner again to re-activate the hardware.

It boils down to greedy manufacturers trying to use technology and laws that they've rented senators to pass for them, to increase the amount of money they can charge without providing any additional value. Or in other cases they're cutting manufacturing costs which incidentally is providing more value to customers (like by installing heated seats in every car by default) but then instead of sharing the benefits of the advancement in manufacturing with the customer, they're trying to keep it all for themselves by forcing customer to pay for their half of what should be a shared benefit, doubling the manufacturer's return by zero'ing the customer's..

These shenanigans weren't physically possible to pull off a decade ago because the technology they're leveraging wasn't available back then. It's similar to the "nobody is allowed to own software anymore" we're getting deeper into every day. They didn't DO mandatory monthly software subscriptions in 1990 because there was no way to pull it off without data centers and widespread internet. They'd have done it then if they could. Now they can, so they do. The only reason your car isn't a mandatory monthly subscription is they haven't found a way to do "nobody gets physical media anymore" with a car. (yet)

I'd expect at some point in the not-so-distant future we're going to see a new automotive brand pop up that's monthly-rental-only on their cars. And that's going to trigger the same cascade we're seeing with computer software. Then it won't be too long before nobody sells cars anymore, they'll all be pay-as-you-go.
