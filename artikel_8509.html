If you can afford a loved one’s bail, pay it in full to the court. The money comes back when the case ends, as long as they make every court date, minus any fees or fines the court deducts. A bail bondsman’s fee, usually 10% of the bail, is gone even if the charges are dropped.

Say a judge sets $5,000 bail, the charges are later dismissed, and the court deducts nothing. A mother who paid her son’s bail in cash gets all $5,000 back, while a neighbor who paid a bondsman the usual 10% fee on the same amount is out $500 for good.

The court holds the cash until the case is over, which can take months, and some courts keep part of it. New York keeps 3% of it after a conviction, and Florida first withholds any unpaid court costs and fines the defendant owes. Even that 3% is less than a third of a bondsman’s usual 10% fee.

A bondsman gives the court a written promise to pay the full bail if the defendant misses court, and the fee pays for that promise. Whoever pays the fee usually signs as the cosigner, on the hook for that full amount, so the fee isn’t the most you can lose. The bondsman may also hold collateral against that debt, such as your car’s title or a lien on your house.
