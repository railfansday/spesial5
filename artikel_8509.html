In 1980, a benefits consultant named Ted Benna figured out how to use Section 401(k), a little-known provision Congress added to the tax code in 1978, to build the first 401(k) plan. It quickly became a favorite among companies looking to offer employees more retirement benefits.

Today, it is still the cornerstone of retirement planning in America. Even physicians who often have a 403(b), 457(b), or defined benefit plan available too, usually max out their 401(k) first.

Recently, Benna has made headlines for calling out his invention, the 401(k), for disproportionately rewarding the wealthy. He may have a point. But I still think the 401(k) is the best retirement account most workers can get, and that it’s still worth it for doctors.

In a recent Bloomberg interview, Benna argued that the modern 401(k) has grown too complicated and too costly, and that it benefits wealthier savers more than middle- and lower-income workers, the people who arguably need it most.

Affluent employees can afford to max out an account and reap the tax break on top. Benna believes low-income workers either lack access to a plan or can’t afford to contribute if they do.

Research seems to agree. Researchers at Vanguard, Yale and MIT found that about 44% of employer matching dollars go to the top 20% of earners at their companies.

And the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics has reported that while 70% of private-sector workers have access to a defined contribution (DC) plan such as a 401(k), only 49% participate. That means roughly 3 in 10 workers who could use a plan but don’t.

Most physicians sit on the other side of this. Every specialty earns well above the national average. Doximity’s latest compensation report names pediatric infectious disease as the lowest-paying specialty, at $217,819. By comparison, the average American salary is $69,770.

The reason I bring this up here is so that physicians who run practices can make sure their employee 401(k) structures do not reflect these statistics. More on that below.

The 401(k) has been one of the most effective tools for building middle-class wealth the U.S. has ever had.

Household stock ownership went up from 32% in 1989, right before one of the best multi-decade stretches for stock returns on record, to 58% by 2022. Almost one-fifth of American households now hold a net worth above a million dollars— much of it held in home equity and retirement accounts like the 401(k)..

So even if the account rewards people who already earn enough to contribute, it still enables actual wealth-building for millions of ordinary workers who’d otherwise have little beyond Social Security waiting for them at retirement.

Access and participation are both on the rise largely thanks to policy changes. Many states now require employers without a retirement plan to enroll workers in a state-run IRA program. Employers can usually opt out by setting up their own plan, which is pushing more of them to offer a 401(k).

Small businesses, which employ close to half the American workforce, have historically skipped retirement benefits altogether. Tax credits and cheaper providers are changing that. Cerulli Associates projects that the number of 401(k) plans will grow 36% over the next five years, passing one million by the end of the decade.

Auto-enrollment deserves a lot of the credit for the jump in participation.

Under SECURE 2.0, 401(k) and 403(b) plans set up after December 29, 2022 must automatically enroll employees, starting with plan years that began in 2025. Older plans, businesses less than three years old and employers with 10 or fewer workers are exempt.

The effect is large. Vanguard found that plans with auto-enrollment had a 94% participation rate, compared with 64% for plans where employees sign up on their own.

For 2026, the IRS raised the 401(k) employee contribution limit to $24,500, up from $23,500 in 2025. Workers 50 and older can add a catch-up contribution of $8,000 on top of that, up from $7,500, bringing the total to $32,500 for savers in that age bracket.

A newer “super” catch-up of $11,250 lets savers ages 60 to 63 put away even more. It replaces the regular $8,000 catch-up for those years rather than stacking on top of it, bringing their total to $35,750.

Many employed physicians don’t have a 401(k) at all. Hospitals and academic centers usually offer a 403(b), which works almost the same way, with the same $24,500 limit and the same catch-ups.

Many also offer a 457(b), which has its own separate $24,500 limit. If you have both, you can defer up to $49,000 in 2026 before counting any catch-ups.

One caution: in a non-governmental 457(b), the money technically belongs to your employer until it’s paid out, so it’s exposed if the hospital runs into financial trouble. Here’s our full guide to 457(b) contributions.

If you own a practice with staff, plan design decides who actually benefits. A safe harbor 401(k) is the common choice. You make a required contribution for employees, either 3% of pay or a match of up to 4%, which vests right away.

In return, the plan skips the annual nondiscrimination tests that can otherwise cap what owners contribute. Under SECURE 2.0, small practices can also claim tax credits of up to $5,000 a year for three years toward startup costs, plus $500 a year for adding auto-enrollment.

Owners who want to save more than the 401(k) allows often pair it with a cash balance plan. And if you’re a solo practitioner with no staff, a solo 401(k) or SEP-IRA is usually simpler.

More than 80% of employers who sponsor a 401(k) offer some form of match, usually tied to how much the employee contributes. Some employers match dollar for dollar up to a set percentage of salary.

Others match 50 cents on the dollar up to a higher contribution threshold. The formula changes how much match each dollar you contribute actually earns, and that’s where dual-income households can come out ahead.

A brief published by the Center for Retirement Research (CRR) examined whether married couples coordinate contributions to capture both employer matches. Just over 40% of couples actively coordinate to maximize their combined match and roughly one in five couples leaves match money uncaptured.

In one example from the brief, a couple decides how to split $480 a month in savings between two accounts with different match formulas. Moving contributions toward the partner with the more generous 1-to-1 match, as opposed to splitting evenly, nets the household an extra $30 a month in employer money.

Stretched over 30 years at a 5% inflation-adjusted return, that $30 monthly difference compounds into roughly $25,000 in additional retirement savings, without either partner setting aside a single extra dollar of their own income.

Source: CRR. Alt text: CRR chart showing how couples split 401(k) contributions to capture employer matches.

Plenty of couples capture both matches without planning it, because each partner contributes enough on their own. That works fine.

Coordination matters when money is tight, like when one spouse is still in residency, working part-time or paying down loans hard. In those years, send your savings to the richer match first, then fill the other.

Say a resident earning $66,000 and her spouse earning $120,000 can save $600 a month between them. Her plan matches 50 cents per dollar on the first 6% of pay. His matches dollar for dollar on the first 4%, up to $400 a month.

Split evenly at $300 each, they collect $450 a month in match. If he puts in $400 and she puts in $200, they collect $500. That’s an extra $600 a year in employer money from the same $600 a month.

Compare match formulas with your spouse, figure out which account pays the better rate, and adjust the split.

Finally, if you’re wondering about how aggressively you should contribute, I’ll say that starting early beats chasing a better return.

In an example from Forbes, we see two 25-year-old savers earning identical salaries. One enrolls immediately at a 10% deferral rate with a 3% match. The other waits five years, then contributes 6% to capture the same 3% match.

The second saver’s fund even outperforms the first, returning roughly 8.9% annually versus 8.4%, partly from a private equity allocation meant to make up lost ground.

By 65, the early starter still ends up with roughly $3 million, compared with $2 million for the saver who waited. That $1 million gap comes from two things: five fewer years of compounding and a lower savings rate (9% of pay versus 13%). The higher return didn’t come close to closing it. As you can see, when it comes to retirement savings, your timing often matters more than any other variable.

A dollar contributed at 25 has decades to compound and no later contribution, however large, can fully make up for a late start. The 401(k) is also one of the few accounts you can start on day one without worrying much about what happens when you change jobs.

That’s because, as opposed to pensions and employer-funded bonus plans, the 401(k) is portable. Your own contributions are yours the moment they hit your account, and they go with you when you leave. Employer matches can be different. Many plans vest the match over time, either all at once after up to three years or gradually over up to six.

Leave before you’re vested and you lose the unvested part, so check your plan’s vesting schedule before you time a job change. You can roll it into a new employer’s plan or an IRA, and it will continue to compound without interruption.

Benna may not be happy with how his invention turned out, but for me, looking at how it has created a new class of millionaires, it is the one non-negotiable account in retirement planning for physicians and beyond.
