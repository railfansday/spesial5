Swing when you’re winning? Biggest leaps across Fifa’s football rankings | The Knowledge

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Plus: clubs with roads on their team badges, most and fewest managers per league title, and more

“The Scotland men’s team’s highest Fifa ranking was 13th (in 2007) and the worst was 88th place (in 2005) – a difference of 75 positions,” writes Bogdan Kotarlic. “Is this the biggest gap between the best and worst ranking for one national team?”

The gap between Scotland’s best and worst Fifa rankings isn’t the biggest in men’s international football; in fact, Pete Tomlin calculates that a 75-place swing puts them joint 141st with Ghana and Seychelles. And, as Chris Lewis and others point out, there are a couple of other home nations who have had more dramatic swings. Wales and Northern Ireland both moved up 109 places: Wales from 117th to eighth under Gary Speed and then Chris Coleman, Northern Ireland from 129th in 2004 to 20th in 2017.

It is the speed of Scotland’s improvement that was most notable. “At two years, seven months and one day that feels like quite the rise,” writes Robbie Dale. “A look at the data shows it is bettered by only nine other countries. Colombia’s rise from a low of 51 to a high of 12 in exactly two years [between 2016 and 2018] is one notable example. Dominica and Kenya rose up the ranks by 70 and 69 places respectively over the course of just 16 months.”

Some of the biggest movers are countries who joined Fifa after the men’s rankings were introduced in 1992, which means they started at the bottom. “I immediately thought of Bosnia and Herzegovina,” writes JJ Galbraith. “They were ranked 173rd in 1996, shortly after playing their first game as an independent nation. Their pinnacle was 13th place in 2013 – a rise of 160 places.”

That 160-place gap is beaten by only two teams. Kuwait reached 24th in the Fifa rankings in 1998, when they won the Arabian Gulf Cup in style and finished second at the Asian Games. By 2017 they were 189th in the world, a difference of 165 places.

That is 18 places short of the record. “Montenegro were ranked 199th when they played their first international match in 2007,” writes Pete Tomlin. “In 2011 they managed to climb up to 16th, thanks to some strong results in Euro 2012 qualifying (victories against Wales, Bulgaria and Switzerland and a 0-0 draw with England at Wembley).”

Thanks to Pete, Mark Holt, Cameron McGlone, Sean DeLoughry and others for helping us compile this list of the biggest movers.

Pete Tomlin has also crunched the numbers for the women’s game. “The gaps are much smaller,” writes Pete, “due to the fact that the rankings have only been recorded since 2003, there are fewer teams and many of the national sides play very few matches.

“The biggest gap belongs to the Bahamas with a high of 96th and a low of 194th, a difference of 98 places. There are actually two women’s teams with a gap of zero, but this is because they have played so few games. Chad (156th) and Sudan (194th) have never moved.”

“Non-league minnows Plymouth Parkway reached the second round of the FA Cup the other weekend,” writes Jon Archer. “I noticed their club crest appears to feature a road going under a bridge. Are there any other clubs who have roads on their badge?” Indeed the Southern League Premier Division South side have the A38 on their crest, a nod to, erm, the nearby stretch of dual carriageway that helps give the club its name via their former ground at the Parkway Sports Club.

It is not a common occurrence and somewhat depends on how far you want to stretch things. Sunderland, for example, have a section of the Wearmouth Bridge on their crest, which certainly carries a road, though it is not visible. Similarly National League South’s Maidenhead United redesigned their badge in 2020 to celebrate the club’s 150th anniversary – it features a stretch of the town’s Thames river bridge, a nod to where the club played their first game back in 1870, which now carries the A4. Farther afield the German minnows TSG Backnang’s crest features the Murr Valley viaduct, which both overlooks the team’s ground and carries the B14, which links Stuttgart and Nuremberg.

Those might be a bit of a reach, but, far more definitively, across the West Country from Plymouth Parkway are tiny eighth-tier Willand Rovers of the Southern League Division One South. Their badge is a proper old shield effort featuring the River Culm, a castle tower … and the M5 motorway, which slices through the village to the north.

“Manchester United have 20 league titles, won by three managers. Liverpool have 20 league titles won by 10. Which teams have the most and fewest titles won by the most and fewest managers?” asks Will Marquand.

The answers above put Liverpool on two titles per manager and their great rivals down the M62 on 6.66 titles per manager (which is doing Alex Ferguson a great disservice but we have to find a way to make this question work). Daz Pearce offers up one-title-one-manager wonders Leicester (Claudio Ranieri), Nottingham Forest (Brian Clough) and Ipswich (Alf Ramsey) for the most managers per title. We would need a team winning only one title in their history in a season with two different managers to get lower than 1:1 but Dirk Maas has a more impressive example of a team with one manager per title.

“Since the inception of the Eredivisie in 1956, Feyenoord have secured 11 league titles, won by 11 managers: Jiri Sobotka in 1961, Franz Fuchs in 1962, Wilhelm Kment in 1965, Ben Peeters in 1969, Ernst Happel in 1971, Wiel Coerver in 1974, Thijs Libregts in 1984, Willem van Hanegem in 1993, Leo Beenhakker in 1999, Giovanni van Bronckhorst in 2017 and Arne Slot in 2023.” Alex Ferguson’s haul of 13 league titles over his 26 years in the Manchester United dugout makes United’s titles-per-manager ratio hard to beat and, probably, the definitive answer for fewest managers per title. Rangers have won 55 Scottish league titles with only nine managers for 6.11 titles per manager. That is as close as we could get. If you know of a club with a higher number of titles per manager, get in touch at knowledge@theguardian.com.

“It dawned on me recently that if Chelsea were to go on and win the Europa League this year on 15 May that, for a period of 10 days, they would be the holders of both major European trophies (as well as being technically the holders of the Cup Winners’ Cup),” wrote Brian Quinn in March 2013. “Has there ever been another brief period like this when a team held two European honours simultaneously? Perhaps going back to when there were three distinct European competitions?”

Chelsea will indeed claim a unique honour if they triumph at the Amsterdam Arena in May (spoiler alert: they did). Only four sides have ever won different European competitions in consecutive years – Juventus, Milan, Porto and Liverpool – and in each instance the teams were deposed as holders of one trophy before getting their hands on the other.

Milan won the much-missed Cup Winners’ Cup on 23 May 1968, but Slovan Bratislava were crowned the new champions on 21 May 1969, seven days before Milan hammered Ajax in the Bernabéu to win the 1969 European Cup. Similarly Juventus won the Cup Winners’ Cup on 16 May 1984, but Everton won that tournament almost exactly a year later on 15 May 1985, a fortnight before Juventus won the European Cup in a final overshadowed by the Heysel tragedy.

Likewise, Porto fell a week short in 2002-03 and 2003-04 after winning the Uefa Cup then the Champions League under José Mourinho, as did Liverpool between their Uefa Cup and European Cup wins in 1975-76 and 1976-77.

“Seeing Cuba thrash Grenada 9-0 in the Concacaf Nations League, I noted there were eight different scorers (Jorge Aguirre scoring twice). Has any team gone better?” asks Oliver Tuhey.

“My team, Tranmere Rovers, have appeared at Wembley nine times since 1990,” writes David Carruthers. “Can any other team never to have played in the top division beat this?”
