sinuous stone-roofed visitor center meanders through the mountains of china

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

a river's movement informs the geometry of two visitor centers, where sweeping roofs choreograph pedestrian journeys through the mountains.

The Huangguoshu Scenic Area in China‘s mountainous Guizhou province welcomes visitors beneath a pair of serpentine roofs designed by the China Academy of Urban Planning and Design (CAUPD) and AEPA&TSC. The two Beijing-based practices take their formal cues from the movement of the Baishui River, which runs through the region and feeds a waterfall.

The project comprises two visitor centers at the entrances to the Great Waterfall and Tianxingqiao scenic areas. From above, the architecture appears as a series of dark, sinuous ribbons among densely forested mountains. Much of the enclosed building volume disappears beneath grassy slopes, while the extended rooflines trace the paths of visitors through the landscape.

The project grew out of a need to accommodate increasing numbers of tourists in an area whose entrance facilities had become overwhelmed. During peak season, as many as 50,000 people could arrive at the waterfall site in a single day. For the architects, the organization of these crowds became a starting point for the geometry of the buildings.

At the Great Waterfall visitor center, the design team takes advantage of an existing five-meter difference in elevation between the entrance and exit of the scenic area. The terrain had previously complicated the circulation of tourists and shuttle buses, whose routes converged within a relatively small entrance plaza.

The architects redistribute these journeys vertically. Arriving passengers are dropped off at the lower level, while those returning from the waterfall make their way toward an elevated departure area. Shuttle buses circulate between the two along a one-way route. The arrangement gives each pedestrian journey its own continuous architectural space beneath the sweeping roofs.

Reception and ticketing facilities occupy spaces along these routes, and restaurants have been relocated toward the return journey. These enclosed programs sit largely within the hillside. As visitors approach the waterfall, the most prominent architectural elements are the long, sloping canopies overhead and the stone surfaces that extend along the ground.

A similar approach informs the smaller, 1,800-square-meter visitor center at Tianxingqiao, an area known for its natural formations of water and limestone. Here, a four-meter difference in elevation allows the architects to make the roof itself part of the pedestrian circulation system.

While incoming visitors pass through a ground-level hall, those leaving the scenic area walk across the top of the building. An elevated viewing platform offers a place to stop along the return route, with dining facilities that overlook the landscape. Elsewhere, the roof curls upward to shelter the ticketing and waiting areas beneath its extended eaves.

The team retained the site’s mature trees throughout construction and incorporated them into the new architecture. Planted islands punctuate the paved surfaces around the entrance, a landscape composition that draws from the particular arrangement of vegetation and exposed rock within Tianxingqiao.

For both visitor centers, CAUPD and AEPA&TSC look to the vernacular stone houses of Guizhou. These buildings traditionally feature roofs made from overlapping stone slabs, a construction language that the architects adapt to the much larger scale of the tourism infrastructure.

Thousands of slate tiles articulate the curved roof surfaces, while walls of bluestone masonry emerge from the surrounding earth. The texture of the individual tiles is especially apparent where the roofs change direction, and their overlapping edges lend a fine grain to the broad architectural gestures.

From the mountainside, the relationship between the buildings and the river is apparent in their profiles. At ground level, this same geometry is experienced on foot, as visitors follow the curves of the architecture toward the water and back again.
