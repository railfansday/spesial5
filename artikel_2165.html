Ex-pro boxer names England's 5 hardest football firms

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

One of the major shifts to domestic football in the last thirty years comes in the form of football hooliganism, which has received particular police attention and continues to be clamped down by the authorities.

The hooliganism subculture enjoyed its peak between the 1970s and 1990s, as fans throughout the width and breadth of the country organised themselves into factions and travelled in numbers to fight it out with opposition supporters.

It still happens today, of course, but on a far-less-frequent and much more heavily policed scale, with the authorities having taken considerable action against those involved in organised matchday violence over the years.

That doesn't stop punters from debating who the best firms are, though, as not only do some exist to this day, but they also hold a certain level of nostalgia to some.

During hooliganism's heyday, the likes of Birmingham City, Cardiff, Chelsea, Middlesbrough, Millwall, Portsmouth and West Ham were all regarded among the toughest and most violent fanbases in the country. Much has changed since those dark days, however, and where do those firms rank now?

Anthony Phythian, a former professional boxer from Manchester who followed Man City home and away and threw hands both in the ring and on the terraces, was asked to name the FIVE hardest firms he encountered when speaking on Daniel Wright's 'Northern Monkeys' podcast.

Phythian evaded a case of club-rivalry bias by initiating proceedings with a mention of bitter cross-city foes Manchester United, who have always had a bit of a reputation off the pitch as well as on it.

Quite naturally, of course, the ex-boxer will have crossed paths and exchanged blows with those on the red side of Manchester on countless occasions, and despite the fierceness of the rivalry that only ever limits praise across the city's divide, he does believe that the Old Trafford faithful were once among the hardest firms in the land.

Speaking on United, Phythian explained: "Gotta have United, because they've been our biggest rivals over the years.

"To be fair, they are a good lot, they've got a good firm, they're a game lot and they've got numbers."

That's one element that always came to the Red Devils' aid when engaging in matchday warfare, where they could often outnumber opposing firms due to the sheer size of the club and its fanbase.

United hooligans would gain notoriety during the 1974/75 season when the club were positioned inside the old Second Division, and groups such as the Red Army and Inter City Jibbers made quite the name for themselves back in the day.

As previously alluded to, Birmingham City once had one of English football's most notorious and identifiable firms.

Whereas the overwhelming majority of hooligan groups were predominantly white and often included affiliations to far-right factions, the so-called 'Zulu Warriors' earned their name after Man City's lot chanted "Zulu, Zulu" at Bluenoses in 1982.

The name stuck and Birmingham, whose firm obtained a rather unique reputation for having a mixed-ethnicity group courtesy of the city's diversity and multiculturalism, chose to adopt it as their own.

The 'Zulu Warriors' were particularly violent in their pomp and were subjected to numerous police raids and operations, such as the well-documented 'Operation Red Card' relating to disorder against local rivals Wolves, and few firms attracted greater fear from opposition supporters.

That's exactly why Phythian, in his own words, would "have them up there".

Stoke City, meanwhile, were never regarded among the very best firms, but those on pre-2000s terraces across the land who embroiled in organised matchday violence will always sing the Potters' praises in this genre.

Phythian believes that the Potters "definitely" need to be in the conversation and, akin to United and Birmingham, not too many fans will disagree with his claim.

Stoke's more recent reputation, albeit back in the Premier League days under Tony Pulis and the like, revolves around being on the pitch, but, some years previously, was all about being even more formidable away from it.

Their 'Norty Forty' firm certainly lived up to its name, and is believed to have included more than 700 members at its peak. Stoke always travelled in numbers and had no shortage of punters willing to scrap it out on a Saturday, with some particularly-unsavory incidents emerging.

In October 2001, 84 people were arrested following wholesale violence in the Potteries derby between Stoke and Port Vale as around 300 police officers were enlisted on the footballing frontlines that day. That same season would see a staggering 1,000 officers on duty for Stoke's home clash against the aforementioned Cardiff, which was halted for seven minutes owing to widespread fan arrests.

As for Everton, who have also been named as one of Phythian's five picks, the Toffees had the tendency for trouble without being classified as one of the country's leading firms.

The self-proclaimed 'County Road Cutters' had their name from both the long road that led up to Everton's former Goodison Park alongside their deplorable use of Stanley knives in matchday battles, which tells you just about everything you need to know.

Everton's perennial top-flight status means that they represent a group of supporters who Phythian would've had plenty of altercations with, and although they wouldn't necessarily be among everybody's five toughest firms, they're certainly up there as far as he's concerned.

They gained their biggest scalp against Manchester United fans back in 2005, which reportedly left around 100 'Red Army' members bloodied and injured in what is known as 'The Battle of Everton Valley'.

It's moments like those, however violent and unsavoury, that will be in Phythian's thinking.

Phythian rounded off by listing Tottenham Hotspur, which may represent something of a surprise as the only London-based inclusion in the ex-fighter's thinking.

He didn't want to include the Lilywhites, either, as he remarked: "I'd have Tottenham. To be fair, as much as it kills me, they're probably the best (firm) out of London in my time going.

"They've always had numbers, have been pretty organised and are the best lads out of London."

Hooligans in support of Chelsea, Millwall and West Ham would likely disagree with Phythian's claim, and there's no denying that the trio have a greater notoriety for matchday-related violence over the years than Spurs typically do.

But that's not to say that Spurs fans can't get up to all sorts of trouble either, and some would argue that their firm is often overlooked in hooliganism-centred discourse.

Spurs' exploits in Europe, which notably included riots against Feyenoord fans during the 1974 UEFA Cup final, gave the North Londoners a name on the continent, and that particular incident is widely-believed to have introduced hooliganism to the Netherlands.

They're often well up for it when it comes to local derbies against the likes of Arsenal and Chelsea, too, and they have been known to cause lots of trouble over the years.
