Project 2035 sounds good but Scottish women’s football needs more than bold promises

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

A hefty dose of cynicism is always healthy whenever a politician turns up to kick a ball about with a group of kids for the cameras.

John Swinney got to work on his keepie-uppie skills this week at Easter Road as Project 2035 was rolled out in an initiative between the Scottish government and the SFA, among others, with the long-term ambitions of the project comfortably ticking the “ambitious in outlook” box.

It would be hard to find fault with the bold objectives that have been set for the next decade.

There were the immediate headline takeaways; doubling the number of registered female players in the country and quadrupling attendances at matches jumped out. Key, too, is regular qualification for major tournaments – and making an impact when qualification has been secured.

In terms of a manifesto that is exciting and visionary, there was much to be pleased about. But, as ever, the proof will be in the pudding. And when it comes to past behaviours predicting future outcomes, it would have to be said Scotland have excelled at taskforces and not quite so much at outcomes.

The initial outlay from the government is £250,000 for this year. That will cover the first step of their contribution but across the remaining terms until 2035 – by which time Scotland will hope to be co-hosts at the Women’s World Cup – the reality is that if those ambitions are to come to fruition, then pockets will need to be pretty deep.

It will take a lot of work and a lot of joined-up thinking across the board but the key to it all, as ever, is money.

The reality just now is this is a Scotland national team who have not qualified for a major tournament since 2019. There has been a regression in the SWPL both in terms of attendances, media exposure, quality and standard of players.

The league itself is split in terms of those who can afford to offer full-time professional contracts and those who can pay a tenner a week for expenses. A full-time professional SWPL may well be the first major step towards any quest to take the country to a level where it is fulfilling the objectives that were set out this week.

As it stands, that possibility would seem to be light years away.

It is not all doom and gloom. Scotland coach Melissa Andreatta has injected more than just enthusiasm and positivity into the national set-up. Initial impressions of her impact are positive, as witnessed by an unbeaten Nations League campaign and a return to League A.

Brazil next summer is the big one. Getting past Sweden in a two-legged World Cup play-off final next month would be the biggest result in Scotland’s history. It is fair to say it is a monumental challenge that lies ahead.

Caroline Weir has been shortlisted in successive years for the Ballon d’Or while Erin Cuthbert captains Chelsea in the WSL. Kim Little’s time with the national team has long since passed but that trio provide compelling evidence that Scotland have been able to produce world-class players.

But as the game has developed at a rate of knots elsewhere, it has stagnated in Scotland.

Lessons have to be learned from 2019, from the way that 18,555 fans turned up to wave Scotland off to France for their first World Cup to a collapse in interest and exposure within months. To understand how to get it right, there needs to be an understanding of where it went wrong.

The approach from government bodies to work in tandem with the SFA is sensible. The benefits of women retained in sport, not even at elite level but simply a recreational level, have a huge societal knock-on effect.

But it will need more than platitudes in front of cameras. The real work will be done in the nitty-gritty, day-to-day work. And elevating Scotland to the levels spoken about this week ultimately requires significant investment.

Megan Rapinoe has always been Marmite-like; observers either love her frank outspokenness or find their hackles rising on the back of it.

There are some arguments to suggest a woman with strong opinions she isn’t afraid to share will always invite a backlash. In any case, Rapinoe, so central for so long to such a successful USWNT as she claimed two World Cups and an Olympic goal medal, has been consistent that she sees no issue with trans athletes, a conversation that never seems to be too far from the surface in women’s sport.

Rapinoe’s argument centred on the idea there is no fairness in sport, citing the natural advantages of 7-foot-4-inch basketball player Victor Wembanyama as her example.

The problem with that comparison, however, is it fundamentally misses the point that biological males have a distinct advantage when it comes to competing.

Kelly Clark found herself out of Celtic’s starting line-up across recent weeks. The first Celtic women’s captain to lead the club to a title and the first to take them

into the Champions League, one suspects that finding herself on the bench would have been a sobering experience, and novel given the fact

she has been a mainstay of this Celtic team.

Drafted back into the starting XI, she produced a big goal for Grant Scott’s side on Wednesday night as she gave the Parkhead side a win over Hibs at Meadowbank.

Plenty of life in her yet, it would seem.
