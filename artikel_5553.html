How Buyers Can Tell if a Home Price Cut Signals a True Deal

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

More than one in five U.S. home listings received a price cut in August, but a lower asking price does not necessarily mean the property has become a good deal. Price reductions are becoming harder for homebuyers to ignore. In August, 20.4% of U.S. listings had a price cut, matching the rate recorded a year earlier, according to the latest Realtor.com housing report.

The median asking price fell 1.3% year over year to $424,500, marking the tenth consecutive month of annual declines. Meanwhile, pending sales slipped 0.2%, ending an eight-month run of year-over-year growth.

For buyers, the growing number of reductions may look like an invitation to start bargain hunting. However, a crossed-out asking price provides little evidence of the property’s actual value.

“A price cut tells you the seller’s first number did not work. It does not tell you what the home is worth,” says Jeremy Olsher, a principal at Mizner Residential Group. “Some reductions create a legitimate opportunity. Others simply bring an unrealistic price closer to where it should have started.”

Olsher recommends three checks before treating a reduced listing as a deal.

Compare the new price with completed sales. Buyers should begin with recently sold properties that are genuinely comparable in location, size, condition and features. Active listings reveal what other sellers hope to receive, while completed sales show what buyers have accepted.

A $40,000 reduction may look substantial, but it has limited meaning if the home remains $50,000 above comparable sales.

“The percentage reduction is the part buyers notice first because it is easy to understand,” Olsher says. “The more useful question is whether the new price makes sense beside the homes that have actually sold.”

Read the entire listing history. The number and timing of reductions can reveal how the seller is responding to the market. A single adjustment after several quiet weeks may indicate growing flexibility. Several small reductions can suggest the seller is chasing the market downward without fully accepting current buyer demand.

Buyers should also check whether the home was previously listed, withdrawn and returned with a new price or description. A fresh listing date does not always mean the property is new to the market.

“Days on the market need context,” Olsher says. “I want to know whether the seller made one meaningful correction or has spent months making small changes that never addressed the real pricing problem.”

Account for condition and ownership costs. A reduced property can remain expensive once repairs, insurance, taxes, association fees and immediate maintenance are considered. Buyers should compare the home’s total financial exposure with competing properties, rather than evaluating the asking price in isolation.

A lower price may represent genuine value when the cost of resolving known issues has already been reflected. It becomes less attractive when the discount is smaller than the work or ongoing expenses the buyer will inherit.

There may also be room to offer below the revised price, particularly when comparable sales support a lower figure or the property has remained available after multiple reductions. Olsher advises buyers to base that offer on evidence rather than choosing an arbitrary percentage below asking.

“The strongest offer is not necessarily the closest to the seller’s latest number,” he says. “It is the one a buyer can explain with recent sales, property condition and realistic costs. A reduction opens the conversation. The numbers still have to hold up.”
