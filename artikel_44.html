They Defeated a Strongman. But Can They Run a Country?

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Serbia’s student movement was deliberately leaderless and made group decisions to unseat Aleksandar Vucic. Now no one seems quite sure what happens next.

They had no leader, no political party and no conventional campaign. They made decisions by raising their hands after hours of debate in university classrooms, a slow and cumbersome process billed as a unique experiment in direct democracy.

That free-form approach to politics of Serbia’s students proved so effective that, in a general election on Oct. 25, they achieved what established opposition parties had failed to accomplish for more than a decade: routing the well-oiled political machine of Serbia’s strongman leader, Aleksandar Vucic.

Mr. Vucic, nearing the end of his second term as president and barred from running again, had resigned as president a month before the October vote for a new Parliament, gambling that his governing party would win and that he could keep his grip on power by becoming prime minister. He had held that post twice before.

His plan was upended by the election victory of a slate of parliamentary candidates selected by students in freewheeling campus meetings. Their selections and electoral successes marked the culmination of a striking exercise in collective decision making. That approach, some believe, could hold lessons for democratic movements beyond Serbia.

But the principles that carried the students to victory may not, on their own, be enough to build a functioning, coherent and durable government.

The diverse list of candidates chosen by the students, nearly all political novices with a wide range of sometimes contradictory views, reflected a belief that everyone should have a say in a leaderless movement united by the single objective of getting rid of Mr. Vucic and the system he created.
