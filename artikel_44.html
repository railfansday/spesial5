These Ukrainians Aren’t in Combat. But They Deal With War Every Day.

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

This Ukrainian doctor works near the front, trying to save wounded soldiers.

This man has buried more than 1,200 soldiers.

This therapist tries to help damaged veterans heal.

This forensic pathologist once helped to solve crimes. Now he identifies fallen soldiers.

Mykhailo Biliak has spent most of his life in the funeral business, and he no longer remembers how many graves he has dug. But he knows he has buried more than 1,200 soldiers in Lviv, in western Ukraine, since Russia started its full-scale war in 2022. “The worst day was six funerals,” said Mr. Biliak, 62.

Hundreds of miles to the east, in a basement near the front line, Daryna Boiko, a 32-year-old anesthesiologist, prepared to attach an IV drip to a soldier wounded by a drone. “It won’t be scary,” she assured him. “It was scary out there,” he replied. “It’s fine here.”

In Kyiv, the capital, Hanna Buraho, a 53-year-old psychotherapist, made tea after a session with a group of soldiers. More than 5,000 service members have been through her rehabilitation program, which uses horses. “When the war started, everyone rushed to help each other, and every person wanted to do something of their own, so this is my contribution,” Ms. Buraho said.

In Odesa, in southern Ukraine, two morgue workers pushed open the heavy, rusted doors of a refrigerated rail carriage that contained the remains of dozens of soldiers killed in battle. A white bag was placed on an autopsy table, and Ruslan Fedoniuk, 61, a forensic pathologist, turned to his work.

None of these people carry weapons. But like Ukrainians across the country, they deal with the war and its consequences every day.

Lychakiv Cemetery, the oldest in Lviv, has been a place of sorrow for countless families. For Mr. Biliak, it is a workplace. He is the first to arrive at a gravesite, and one of the last to leave after he and his colleagues shape the mound of earth over it.
