Desire, Death, and a Breakthrough Year for Queer Cinema

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

A willingness to die for sex is a familiar bargain for queer people. Born in the twentieth century at the wrong place and time? Beware of Nazis, AIDS, and other mortal dangers. Hollywood has at times depicted these threats in ways that emphasize the humanity of LGBTQ+ people, as when Tom Hanks played a lawyer dying of AIDS in “Philadelphia,” during the height of the epidemic in 1993. The industry also has a history of finding ever more creative ways to kill off and scapegoat queer characters, a trope that as recently as 10 years ago seemed to be running amok.

But a more twisted and nervy dance between desire and death has unfolded in this year’s spate of particularly macabre queer films. After years of what felt like permanent advances in rights and representation, LGBTQ+ people have been stung by backlash, including attacks on gender-affirming care, legal threats to marriage equality, and cuts to suicide prevention. Amid a reduced perception of safety, anxieties about the possibilities and consequences of nonnormative desire are spilling out onscreen like so much fake blood. Even so, their formulation is evidence of social and creative progress. This alarm has played out, perhaps most obviously, in horror films. In Adrian Chiarella’s “Leviticus” and Jane Schoenbrun’s “Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma,” same-sex attraction engenders extreme and potentially deadly terror, be it from detractors or the people it consumes. Both films explore not the evils of queerness — an argument that belongs to the tradition of queer-coded villains — but the horrors of having one’s desires villainized.

The year’s queer history films also subvert conventional narratives around sexuality and deadly fates. In Andrew Scott’s awards-season vehicle “Elsinore,” Ian Charleson’s dying wish to play Hamlet is one many actors would share; the film doesn’t focus on how or why he has AIDS, but what he wants to do before it kills him. The Spanish epic “La Bola Negra” depicts forbidden romance, but desire isn’t what dooms its multiple generations of queer men. The fact that they’re swept up in the currents of history, just like everyone else, is part of the film’s revolutionary power.

The era for combatting the notion that being gay is a no good, very bad thing in a straightforward way finally seems to have passed. Usually such sermons were preaching to the choir anyway. Gone is the teary earnestness of a conversion therapy drama like “Boy Erased,” from 2018, in which much of the trauma wrought on Lucas Hedges’ character derives from the church but is carried out by earthly messengers. In “Leviticus,” when a healer tries to pray the gay away from a pair of lovesick adolescents, he conjures supernatural and murderous doppelgangers.

After rituals of deliverance are performed over the convulsing bodies of a pair of schoolmates (played by Joe Bird and Stacy Clausen), each one is haunted by an illusion of the other that tries to kill them. The concept of “Leviticus,” named for the Old Testament book that forbids man to lie with man, partly functions as a wry play on the idea that you never get over your first crush. It also takes the fear that so many queer people know well — that what you desire most will be your downfall — to literal and horrifying extremes. Imagining the bigotry of organized religion as a senselessly violent supernatural force is a radical reversal. The boys aren’t the problem; the boogeymen that torment them spring from the so-called good book.

Schoenbrun’s meta queer fantasia “Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma” likewise performs a canonical revision. The nonbinary killer in the campy franchise within the film were themselves killed and sunk to the bottom of a lake by their comically gender-panicked peers. A victim seeking revenge, the TV-headed slasher is called Little Death, a nod to a French term for orgasm, la petite mort. Hannah Einbinder’s millennial filmmaker is on a quest to reboot the franchise, but more significantly, to achieve her own “little death.” In order to grow more comfortable with sex, and give into her own desires, her fear of intimacy — one that, again, many queer people know well — needs to be killed.

The movie ultimately intertwines fear of death with the fear of intimacy in a way that demonstrates the stakes — how you can feel so afraid of getting close to someone you could just die. But what must be slaughtered in this case is fear itself — so that same-sex desire, which has so often been pathologized and villainized, can flourish. (The trail of straight people extravagantly dispatched along the way, like so many gays before them, is one of the film’s many deranged and delightful flourishes.)

There is a potent realignment evident in the year’s period films as well, which center the desires and fates of queer characters on their own terms, rather than taking their oppression or disease as subjects unto themselves. “Elsinore” leans a bit heavily on obvious sentiment while leaving much of the thematic richness of “Hamlet” on the table. But Charleson’s determination to play the part before he dies is what drives the film, while the indignities of succumbing to AIDS are mostly a secondary plot.

Contrast that with recent period dramas on TV, like “It’s a Sin” (2021) about young people facing the AIDS crisis in London, or “Fellow Travelers” (2023) about the Lavender Scare in the U.S., and focusing on Charleson’s dream itself rather than the injustice of what threatens it marks a distinct shift.

The broad sweep of the canvas in “La Bola Negra” heralds another major turn of the page. The play of the same name by Federico García Lorca that partly inspires the movie, and appears in one of its several timelines, is about a young man denied access to an elite social club because he’s presumed to be gay. But homophobia isn’t the primary struggle (or a harbinger of death) for the film’s other gay characters. It certainly doesn’t help two doe-eyed young men (played by Miguel Bernardeau and Guitarricadelafuente) brought together and kept apart by the Spanish Civil War. But the forbiddenness of their infatuation is just one current swirling around them, the most pressing of which is, well, the Spanish Civil War.

Weaving the fates of queer characters into the broader scope of history, and suggesting a lineage of ancestry and shared desire, as “La Bola Negra” so marvelously does, feels like a kind of imaginative reparations. And it marks a bolder, more robust tactic in humanizing queer lives; the film traces generational ties and uncovers untold histories, rather than contending with acute crises like religious doctrine or deadly disease.

There is a stranger and more expansive creative palette evidenced in these films, one that suggests the unnerving climate for queer people in the real world is only furthering the reaches of the imagination. In carrying forward the legacy of queer cinema, it seems the gays presumed buried by the trope are finally rising up.
