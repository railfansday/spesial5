Should Sunrise on the Reaping be the last Hunger Games movie?

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

The Hunger Games has never been more back.

Over its opening weekend, The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping topped the domestic box office with an impressive $120 million, making it just one of five movies to open with over $100 million this year. Other titles to achieve that goal are Spider-Man: Brand New Day, Toy Story 5, The Super Mario Galaxy Movie, and The Odyssey.

With Sunrise on the Reaping's massive success comes questions about the future of the franchise. What could our next trip to Panem look like? Which character could author Suzanne Collins focus on in a new novel? Oft-floated options include fan-favorite victor Finnick Odair (Sam Claflin), which would give audiences a look at a Career's experience in the Hunger Games, or Gamemaker Plutarch Heavensbee (Jesse Plemons), which would offer a different perspective on the rebellion against President Snow (Ralph Fiennes).

Alternatively, what if we just let the franchise rest for a little longer? That seems to be the plan for now, as Lionsgate chairman Adam Fogelson told Variety that there would be no more new films until Collins wrote another book. And honestly, if Collins chooses to move on from Panem forever, would that be so awful? The Hunger Games has proven it has staying power, but with Sunrise on the Reaping, it's also begun to show the limits of the books' formula. Not that Sunrise on the Reaping is bad — far from it! However, it does make a pretty solid case as to why it should be the franchise's closer.

When Collins first announced the release of Sunrise on the Reaping, I was skeptical. Film audiences already knew how Haymitch Abernathy's (Joseph Zada) games would end. Book readers had even seen it play out in an abridged version on the page in Catching Fire, when Katniss Everdeen (Jennifer Lawrence) and Peeta Mellark (Josh Hutcherson) watched it to prepare for the Third Quarter Quell.

Still, Collins proved me wrong. The novel deepened our understanding of the Capitol's propaganda machine, revealing that the version of Haymitch's games that Katniss and Peeta watched was edited to omit Haymitch's attempt to break the arena. It also emphasized that characters from the original trilogy like Haymitch, Plutarch, Beetee (Kelvin Harrison Jr.), Mags (Lili Taylor), and Wiress (Maya Hawke) had all been working to undermine Snow since before Katniss was even born. Yes, their sparks of rebellion didn't catch fire at first. But we know that they eventually build and build until Snow is overthrown in Mockingjay. By the end of the book, Haymitch lives to see the sun rise on his birthday with no reaping in site, adding a bittersweet layer to the hard-fought tragedy of his games.

Sunrise on the Reaping, both the film and the novel, closes with the return of Katniss and Peeta, now grown up. Their appearance brings Sunrise on the Reaping full circle, looking to Panem's future while also deepening our understanding of its past. Do we really need to break that circle open again when Collins has already written such a meaningful conclusion?

My biggest complaint about the book and film versions of Sunrise on the Reaping is the same: They both suffer from prequel-itis. That's when a prequel is so focused on linking back to the original title that it becomes bogged down in references and callbacks. In Sunrise on the Reaping's case, that prequel-itis manifests in the nonstop barrage of returning characters, whose presence can almost suffocate the new characters. Collins still weaves a compelling story of rebellion with them, but by the time young Effie Trinket (Elle Fanning) showed up, I almost rolled my eyes at how connected everything was.

If Collins returns to Panem to tell the story of a known character like Finnick or Plutarch, it's possible that novel would suffer a similar fate. The world of the series would grow ever smaller, instead of expanding. Collins could also opt to avoid the Hunger Games entirely and focus instead on the Dark Days before the Capitol created the Games. But without the spectacle of the Hunger Games themselves, how can Lionsgate expect audiences to show up? After all, The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes only featured a rudimentary version of the Hunger Games, and it took in $44 million at the domestic box office on opening weekend, a little over a third of Sunrise on the Reaping's total. The disparity proves that people want to see the Hunger Games on a large scale, preferably with familiar faces. But when it comes to storytelling, those desires might wind up being greater flaws than boons in the long run.

Again, Collins proved my skepticism about Sunrise on the Reaping dead wrong, and I'd like to think that she truly won't write another Hunger Games novel unless she has something new and compelling to say. But given that Sunrise on the Reaping serves as such a fitting bookend to the original trilogy, and that it's beginning to show the warning signs of franchise fatigue, it might be best to let it stand as a strong conclusion, as opposed to a gateway for further meddling in a well-trodden story.
