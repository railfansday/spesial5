Paraguay has become an important location for Bitcoin mining because of the cheap hydropower available near the Itaipu dam. Now a different kind of energy-hungry business is bidding for the same electricity: artificial-intelligence data centres. The country is approaching a turning point as special electricity contracts for crypto miners are due to expire at the end of 2027, potentially forcing operators to renegotiate at higher rates or move their equipment.

The question is no longer whether Bitcoin mining can use abundant renewable power. It is which businesses deserve access when that power becomes more valuable to the wider economy.

CoinDesk reported that Paraguay's state utility ANDE expects to collect about $350 million from crypto miners in 2026. Mining firms have benefited from electricity pricing around $44 per megawatt-hour, a level that helped turn the country into a major global mining location. The revenue is significant, but it does not establish that the arrangement will remain the best option once national demand and competing industrial uses rise.

The contracts matter because mining profitability is highly sensitive to electricity costs. A site can possess efficient machines and substantial computing power yet struggle if its price per unit of energy rises while Bitcoin mining rewards and transaction fees fail to keep pace. Operators make investment decisions years ahead, so uncertainty over access after 2027 can affect capital spending today.

ANDE plans to allow its special crypto electricity arrangements to expire on December 31, 2027, according to the report. A group of utility engineers has separately proposed limiting the combined allocation for crypto mining, AI and data centres to 700 megawatts, with electricity allocated through an auction at higher rates. That engineering proposal has not automatically become binding government policy.

One prominent operator, HIVE Digital Technologies, has a substantial portion of its mining capacity in Paraguay. Its disclosures indicate that continued operations at affected facilities depend on renegotiating power arrangements. That is a business risk rather than evidence that every mining machine is already shutting down. The timetable still leaves room for discussions, alternative contracts and changes in project plans.

The growing competition is more than an abstract forecast. A company connected to the proposed Iguazú AI City project has secured a long-term power arrangement, according to the same reporting. AI data centres generally need stable, predictable electricity supply to support hardware, cooling and customer service commitments. Their economics are different from Bitcoin mining, where machines can sometimes be switched off when prices rise.

That difference can be both a disadvantage and an opportunity for miners. AI projects may justify higher tariffs through long-term contracts and investment promises, but miners can potentially provide flexible demand when the grid has surplus power. The outcome will depend on how regulators value reliable revenue, employment, infrastructure investment and system flexibility.

The energy-versus-computing trade-off is unfolding worldwide. NewsBTC recently examined MARA's Bitcoin sales amid an AI data-centre strategy, and Hyperscale Data's evolving approach offers another example of companies reassessing where computing infrastructure generates the best returns.

For Paraguay, the next meaningful developments will be power-tariff decisions, contract renewals and evidence of actual investment by both mining and AI businesses. The state utility must also meet growing domestic electricity demand. Cheap hydropower created the mining boom; competition for the same resource may now determine how much of that industry stays.
