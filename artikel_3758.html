'Elsinore' Stars Andrew Scott and Olivia Colman on 'Fleabag', Auditions, and Acting Through Illness

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Olivia Colman and Andrew Scott’s friendship pre-dates Fleabag by a decade, though Colman wouldn’t know it. “Was it in the garden for the wedding of Fleabag? Was that the first scene we [did together]?” she asks as the pair sits down in London.

“Oh darling, we met about 10 years before,” says Scott with a laugh. “We did a radio play, which you never remember.”

Colman giggles, and insists she does. It was a BBC Radio 4 adaptation of Harold Pinter's classic play Betrayal, which aired in 2012.

“The big thing that you have to do when you’re playing a radio play is not make the paper make any sound,” says Scott, revealing that another actor once told him a trick to keeping his script silent: “You scrunch it right up, and then you unscrunch it so it’s rigid…so even if you move them, they’re never going to [make a sound].”

The pair have a natural chemistry, which isn’t a surprise after seeing their work on Fleabag. Colman played Godmother, a passive-aggressive, vulgar villain who ate up every scene she was in. And Scott, of course, played the “hot priest” in the second season, the charming and slightly unruly clergy member that became a sensation.

This year, they reunited in the much more dramatic Elsinore, a moving drama about Scottish actor Ian Charleson (Scott) who attempts to perform Hamlet at the National Theatre while secretly living with AIDS. Oscar winner Colman plays Charleson’s doctor, Margaret Johnson, who discourages him from taking on the strenuous role because of the threat it poses to his health.

An exploration of art, ambition, and mortality, Elsinore is centered on a career-best performance from Scott, who had previously played Hamlet (150 times!) onstage in London in 2017.

Earlier this month, Scott and Colman looked back at their favorite Fleabag memories, the way Elsinore brings out comedy within the tragedy, and their best tricks for acing auditions.

This interview has been condensed and edited.

Vanity Fair: What was the first scene you did together on Fleabag?

Olivia Colman: Was that the wedding or was it the dinner party?

Andrew Scott: No, I will never forget it. I had to say that I was pulling out of the wedding and we were in the house and I'd say, you were like, “Oh no, that's absolutely, it's perfectly fine.” And you see me out. And there had to be a door slam and the camera stays in the living room and you just hear the door slam and then we hear you saying—

Scott: Exactly. But I wasn't able to leave because of sound. So I had to crouch down. And then you were, “What a cunt!” And I remember thinking, Hold it together. Do not laugh because I'm going to ruin it. I was in the room and I couldn't laugh. And it was so funny.

Colman: And we had the dinner and the wedding.

Scott: I remember so much about that dinner scene because you'd just come back from Venice, and you just won that big fancy prize. All of these had to be filmed on the weekends.

Colman: Yes, because I was doing The Crown Monday to Friday.

Colman: That's why I can't remember anything. That's in my defense. There was one month, was it September? Where I didn’t have a single day off.

Elsinore opens in theaters beginning Nov. 20.

How did you each get involved with Fleabag?

Colman: Phoebe [Waller-Bridge] and I were doing a play, and she was writing a lot and she'd written Fleabag and she was doing a crowdfunder to get it to Edinburgh. And [English actor] Kevin McNally and I were two of the people who sponsored it to go to Edinburgh. Then I think before [the festival], they did a little read-through of it somewhere. I saw it. Then I also said, just shamelessly, “Can I be in it? Or can I be in anything that you write?”

Scott: So you gave money for the original Edinburgh show? Wow! I never knew that. You must feel so proud of that.

Colman: I’m pretty thrilled with myself, yes. But also just trying to buy Phoebe's friendship.

Scott: A perfect way to buy friendship. That's how you got mine and I'm still getting those direct deposits every month.

Scott: Phoebe and I had done a play at the Soho Theatre about 10 years before Fleabag. And so the first [season] had come out and everybody loved it. And then she called me and said, "I'm thinking of writing this other character. Can we have a sort of talk about it?" We hadn't seen each other in a few years, and we ended up having this incredible walk around Soho and talking about love. And she had this idea of this priest and we talked about religion and what that means. And I remember bringing her into this place in Soho and it's on St. Martin’s Lane, which is the Quaker meeting hall. Have you ever been in there?

Scott: You know that scene where we go into the Quaker meeting hall? Anyway, I brought her into this place and I said, "This is where I come to sort of chill out sometimes and you can go into some really silent room in the middle of St. Martin's Lane." And so we sat in there for about two hours and we just talked about love and life. And then she incorporated it into the story. I sort of signed up to it with no script. So the day before the read-through I was like, "Oh my God, this is incredible." She's so brilliant that she just incorporates things and that amazing mind is always at work.

Olivia, when the “hot priest” situation happened, what was it like seeing Andrew, your friend, suddenly catapulted into this sensational online narrative?

Colman: Well, I remember my girlfriends just going, "Oh my God, the hot priest! I'm having awful thoughts about him!” And I loved it. I thought it was hilarious. All my women friends, just thinking filthy thoughts about you.

Scott: People do get very feral. It's the priest thing. It's the collar.

Colman: It's the taboo. It's the transgression of it.

Scott: Who would have thunk it? I knew that it was kind of funny and sexy and actually very soulful, that relationship between those two characters, but you never think it's going to be…

Colman: I was so cross with Phoebe when I first read it though, and the ending that they weren't together. Why can't you make a happy ending? I want to re-watch it all now.

Scott: We should have a little Fleabag party. That would be so fun.

Colman: Fleabag holiday. Oh my God, let's get a nice big house in a countryside. I want to have dogs and just watch Fleabag every night.

What do you remember about shooting that dinner scene together?

Colman: It took two days to film that scene. And it felt like, “I can imagine film students are going to study this one day.” It's so brilliantly written.

Scott: And I remember at the time I had a 103-degree temperature. I was so sick. But it's that amazing thing about when you're acting, you sort of forget about it.

Colman: I don't want to make this about me, but I had pneumonia when filming Wicker.

Scott: Did you really? How long did you have it for?

Colman: I can't remember. I started to sort of get better as they sort of fell in love, weirdly. Otherwise, I don't think I could have done the marriage dance thing. But it is amazing how you just—and maybe we're sort of a bit old-school, but maybe it's silly.

Scott: Probably it is a little bit silly. You just do those things…

Colman: The fear that you're going to get replaced. Still in the back of my head that someone else will take the job.

Scott: Absolutely. And in a way, it might be a little bit foolish. I had to have a sort of affect having a weird panic attack in [his one-man stage adaptation of Uncle] Vanya, and towards the last two weeks, I was like, "This isn't really stopping." Because I think your brain knows, but your body doesn't know and you sort of continue. It is a sort of corny, nuts thing to do for your life.

Colman: This rather beautifully ties in with Elsinore, doesn't it?

Scott: You're acting and you sort of forget about it for a little while. And in some ways, on a much, much more serious scale, Ian Charleson was really suffering with illness, and he went on and he did this heroic, Olympian part. And then he would just collapse at the end.

Scott: Yeah, six weeks later. I suppose it's about quality and quantity, right? Dr. Johnson basically said, "You can have more time. If you just give yourself a bit more time, then there might be some developments in the treatment. But if you play this part, it's going to mess up your immune system and it's going to mess you up. It could be fatal." And that's their big conflict. And then bit by bit, it's about art and what it means and how we use our time. And eventually—

Colman: They had come to an understanding.

Andrew, how did performing Hamlet onstage back in 2017 inform your performance as Ian Charleson playing Hamlet?

Scott: It was kind of difficult because all the scripts leave my head almost immediately. So I had to relearn Hamlet.

Colman: Do you find, when you're auditioning, the audition scene is the hardest one to film if you get the job?

Scott: Oh my God, totally. Because you're like, “You liked that. Can I do it again?” Because you feel like that must have been what they liked because otherwise I wouldn't be here. You're so right. Did you like auditioning?

Colman: Partly because I really enjoyed forcing them to like me. I'd go in and they'd go, "What the fuck? She's not right for this." I go, "I'm going to win you over. I'm going to do it." And so there was a job where I was playing a mom to some really tough lads and I really enjoyed it and they went, "Oh." Now with everyone taping, I wouldn't get the work I got if I had to do tape. I had to be in the room to win people over.

Scott: And also, I think what's difficult is that if you're putting yourself on tape, you present what you think that your instinct is. But if someone goes, "Oh, actually no, I think a person is much less friendly or more generous or whatever." You go, “ok,” because acting is so much about going, “I can do it a different way.”

Colman: It’s great for people who are far away and can't afford to get the train fare, but I think it's tough for actors now.

Scott: I remember auditioning for Sherlock and exactly the same experience as you had where I think they were like, "Who is this tiny little fella that's coming in?” And I remember thinking, "I'm going to frighten you. I'm going to frighten you." I remember I sort of shouted in the audition and I could tell they were like, "He's a bit creepy actually."

Do you always know when you’ve gotten the part?

Scott: No, you never know after an audition, do you? You sort of have an instinct.

Colman: Occasionally I knew that I didn't get it. I used to do a little thing: I'd go, "Do you want me to leave the script?" And if they went, "Yes, that'd be great. Thank you," I knew I hadn't got it.

Do you prefer dramatic parts or comedic ones, or is there even a difference in your approach?

Scott: If we were to meet for dinner, I don't think we'd go, “We'll do the sad bit. We'll do the funny bit.” I think you've got to be prepared for all the colors. You know what I mean? That is why you are truly exceptional. I don't think you can be a really great, great actor to my mind without having a terrific sense of humor. And that's just what you have. You just have that interest in the lightness of human beings. I don't love overly serious acting, just because I don't think it's real to human beings.

Colman: I think the funny has to be based in realism and if you're trying to lay on funny, play funny. It's not funny. But you are naturally funny and you're naturally feeling and giving.

Scott: I think that's why even though we had some serious scenes to do, but also we should say that the screenplay was just massively funny. And they have a sort of antagonistic relationship and I always think antagonism is a funny thing to play. It's a great fun thing to do. And then they gradually sort of warm to each other.

Olivia, what’s your approach to awards acceptance speeches? Because some have gone viral, like your shoutouts to Lady Gaga, or calling your Favourite costars Emma Stone and Rachel Weisz “my bitches.” What goes through your head in those moments?

Colman: No idea. I can't remember any of it. The Oscars, it's like a wedding day. I remember my dad saying, "There'll be moments, just stop and just look at it. Take it all in. Look at all the faces. Who's there?" And that was amazing advice. And on my wedding day I remembered it. And it was before phones and things—that magic of theater— when it's gone, it's gone. And so that was rather beautiful. But the Oscars was such a head fuck because it’s such a huge occasion. You just sort of can't believe that's happened, and you wake up the next day and go, can't remember any of it.

Scott: I think it's a testament to you because you come on and you're just completely yourself and what's completely yourself is just completely adorable and sincere. That speech is just so incredibly sincere and it's fun and it's also ridiculous, but also meaningful.

Colman: It means so much. And that's why in the buildup when people talk about it, I really understand how it's, “Please don't, please don't” because you can't help but sort of dream about it. And then it happens and then you wish you'd paid more attention.

What role of each other’s would you have loved to try to take on?

Scott: I don't want to do anything that you've done. I'd just be shitter.

Colman: I want to be in more of the stuff that you've done. I want to be in scenes with you.

Scott: Would you do a play? Do you feel you just don't really want to do a play?

Colman: I think until my youngest [child] has left home, I find going out to work just as everyone's getting cozy heartbreaking.

Scott: It's so hard. Or even in the summer when everyone's like, “Oh, we might go to a beer garden,” you think—

Scott: I'm going in the dark. I’m going to scream in the dark.

Colman: The camaraderie is extraordinary. I love that. I miss that. I miss rehearsals, but I might not go back to it until I know my kids are all grown.

Is this something that you’d want to see each other do in the future?

Colman: Well, we talk about doing a little thing together about houses.

Scott: We think we should have a reality show about houses and beach walks. We might not have a huge viewership.

Colman: We walk around and we go, “I wouldn’t put that there.” We'd be a bit bitchy going around people's houses. We thought that'd be fun. Probably no one will see it.

Scott: Nobody would watch that. They’d think, “What awful, horrible people.”
