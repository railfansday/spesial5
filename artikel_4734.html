Consider the following. Toddlers are playing at a sensory table that is full of rubber ducks, water, and bubbles. A child takes the duck out of two other children’s hands. One child calmly picks up another duck, while the other child falls on the floor and proceeds to have an epic temper tantrum. The first child in this example has low emotional intensity. This child may experience intense emotions, though their reaction suggests otherwise. The second child in this example has a high level of emotional intensity.

An emotionally intense child expresses emotions with greater fervour than other children of a similar age. Emotionally intense children express incredible happiness, profound sadness, fierce frustration, and overwhelming surprise. They squeal with delight over every birthday present. They sob uncontrollably if a toy breaks while they are playing with it. They may hit their head repeatedly if they spill a glass of milk.

Emotional intensity is not inherently a weakness. In fact, the degree of emotional intensity is a trait of temperament. It is innate and ingrained. With the right support, children who have a high level of emotional intensity can learn to understand their emotions, develop emotional self-regulation skills, and know that they don't need to change who they are to be happy and successful.

Research suggests that the aspects of temperament have both genetic and environmental influences.1 Differences in emotional reactivity emerge early in life, and many remain at the same level as children grow and develop. Yet development, experiences, and relationships can influence how children manage their emotions.

Emotionally intense children are often described as dramatic, stubborn, difficult, overly sensitive, or exhausting. But these labels are damaging and overlook an important distinction: The intensity of a child's emotional response is not the same as their ability to manage that emotional experience.

How to successfully parent an emotionally intense child:

For example, tell your child, "I understand that you are mad. It's okay to be angry, but it is not okay to hit your brother. Next time, come ask me for help.”

It is important to teach coping skills when the child is calm. Practice labeling emotions, asking for help, problem-solving, and managing frustration during times devoid of conflict or challenges. Yet at the same time, do not automatically rescue children from every disappointment or challenging experience. Provide appropriate support and empower them to navigate challenges. The goal is to help them to trust and believe that "I can experience difficult emotions and still be okay."

Children benefit from knowing their emotional experiences aren't something to be ashamed of. In fact, children need to understand that their emotions are important, valid, and something to be acknowledged. No emotion is inherently good or bad. No emotion is inherently positive or negative. Emotions are simply experiences created in our own psyche. It is important to allow children to experience their emotions fully, without guilt or shame. Yet at the same time, emotionally intense children need to learn, over time, how to express their emotions in a socially appropriate manner.

When is emotional intensity a sign of something more?

Although emotional intensity can reflect normal temperament, persistent struggles with emotional regulation that negatively affect everyday functioning can also occur with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, anxiety disorders, disruptive mood dysregulation disorder, autism spectrum disorder, depressive disorders, and trauma-related conditions.

When emotional reactions are persistently severe, frequent and/or intense, interfere with school performance, raise concerns about safety, represent significant change from the child's usual behavior, or negatively affect friendships, it is important to reach out to your child’s pediatrician.

Emotionally intense children can be explosive. They express their emotions in a dramatic fashion and often possess limited ability to self-regulate. It is important to understand such children. We need to demonstrate patience and compassion. Remember that experiencing emotional intensity is not a choice. Yet emotionally intense children can learn to experience their emotions and express them in an appropriate manner.
