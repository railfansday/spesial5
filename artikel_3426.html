We Officially Ranked the 10 Best Comedy Movies of All Time

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Is there anything more innately satisfying than laughing while watching a movie? It's certainly the simplest and most responsive pleasure of the human body, but it's also arguably the most difficult task to pull off for actors, writers, directors, and crew members. Beyond just the jokes, comedies can also feature impeccable craft, cinematic wonder, and profound commentary via satire. Comedy is generally subjective, but this list of 10 masterpieces of laughter, hijinks, and absurdity sums up the best of the genre over the last 100 years of filmmaking.

While not everyone has seen this 1980s gem, Lost in America's brilliance deserves recognition, so take this as a ringing endorsement. Everything Albert Brooks has done throughout his six-decade career as a comedian, talk show guest, variety show performer, writer, actor, and director is a stroke of genius, and nothing captures the nebbish, subversive, darkly comic Brooks experience better than this satire of yuppie Americans.

Feeling rudderless at their lucrative office jobs, a married couple, David (Brooks) and Linda Howard (Julie Hagerty), abandon their affluent lives to become cross-country drifters in an RV. Failing to live out their Easy Rider fantasy by being constrained by finances and social norms, the couple reveals the inner insecurity of Ronald Reagan-era yuppies who long for a rebellious life despite having everything. Lost in America delivers as a road-trip comedy, featuring a handful of cringe-inducing conversations and signature explosive blow-ups by Brooks, whose chemistry with Hagerty is a sheer delight. The film magically split the atom as a broad comedy of errors and a biting commentary on the upper class before "eat the rich" became omnipresent in media. Lost in America is the serio-comic tragedy for all those who want to upend life under capitalism.

Preston Sturges, another luminary writer-director of the comedy world with incredible insight into the class system and American socialites, is perhaps the sharpest screenwriter in Hollywood history. Defined by their dense dialogue, quick rapport between actors, and manic screwball antics, Sturges constructed his strongest laugh machine with The Lady Eve, starring Barbara Stanwyck and Henry Fonda as the least inspired couple ever.

After being the target of a con, socially awkward brewery heir Charles Pike (Fonda) falls for con artist Jean Harrington (Stanwyck), sparking a chaotic on-and-off-again romance filled with misunderstandings, teasing, and ruthless tormenting. While Sturges has a light touch behind the camera and script, his disdain for those stupidly in love packs a punch, as he runs wild with the hilarious toxicity between Charles and Jean, who somehow can't give each other up after countless betrayals. You can't have an amazing romantic screwball comedy without two dynamic performances, and The Lady Eve puts on a spectacle, thanks to the electricity running between Fonda and Stanwyck. The film is unafraid to be heartwarming, romantic, and even sexy. Sturges was always focused on humor, but his skills were versatile.

During his breakout on the film scene in the 2000s, Edgar Wright did things with the cinematic medium that seemed impossible, if not disobedient to the filmmaking rulebook. The second entry in his unofficial Cornetto Trilogy, Hot Fuzz, starring Simon Pegg and Nick Frost, once again, shows Wright at his best, blending an all-out blitz of cop-thriller action with uproarious, zany comedy. It's a showy film, but when executed this strongly, you can't help but adore it.

After playing slackers fending off a zombie invasion in Shaun of the Dead, Pegg and Frost reunited with Wright as police officers investigating a series of gruesome deaths in West Country in England. Hot Fuzz knowingly salutes classic and contemporary cop movies, everything from Lethal Weapon to Bad Boys II, but it puts all the others to shame with its astonishing craft. The rare parody to match the pedigree of the subject it's targeting, Wright's signature rapid-fire editing and frantic sound design is a tour de force of comedic hijinks and genuinely exhilarating action. As a pure action-comedy, Hot Fuzz pushed the subgenre to unprecedented heights, filling each scene with too many jokes to keep track of. The film, beyond being hilarious, also captures the overlooked mundanity of the police force and our relationship to cops in the modern day.

During a boom period for raunchy comedy on the big screen, it was always considered taboo by many for women to partake in gross-out and crude humor. Until Bridesmaids, most studio comedies pigeonholed female characters as the fussy, stuck-up ones shaking their heads at the crazy boys. Everything changed with Paul Feig's groundbreaking 2011 film, which showed that women could be raw, candid, and, most of all, uproariously funny in the most profane ways.

Despite its reputation as the quintessential female gross-out comedy, Bridesmaids, featuring an all-star cast of Kristen Wiig, Maya Rudolph, Rose Byrne, Ellie Kemper, and Melissa McCarthy, is a surprisingly low-stakes and grounded film with intimately realized character relationships and universal dynamics between long-time friends at a major crossroads in their lives. The humor primarily stems from each actor's respective unique skills and their magnetic chemistry, with the banter between comedy experts like Wiig and Rudolph and relative newcomers like Byrne and Jon Hamm providing the movie with an indelible magic. Everyone knew that Bridesmaids was above the pack, including the Academy Awards, which gave it a Best Screenplay and Best Supporting Actress nomination for McCarthy, who gave one of the funniest screen performances of the century. Gender has no boundaries when writing is this observant, quick-witted, and idiosyncratic.

Mockumentaries are everywhere in movie and television form now, but in 1984, the mode was a stunning breakthrough and revelatory path on how to create comedy. The late Rob Reiner, in his directorial debut, cemented a new formula in This Is Spinal Tap, which would later be expanded upon by master mockumentarian and Spinal Tap star Christopher Guest. Every faux documentary, and even brand of off-beat humor, is indebted to This Is Spinal Tap.

There are quotable movies, and then there is This Is Spinal Tap, a movie that can light up a conversation by reciting any one of its indelible lines, from cranking a speaker to 11 to asking how much more black something can be. Reiner's parody of cinéma vérité documentaries capturing a band before a concert tour has become so cultural pronounced that younger audiences might go into the film assuming that Spinal Tap, the English heavy metal band fronted by David St. Hubbins (Michael McKean) and Nigel Tufnel (Guest), is a real band. At roughly 80 minutes, the movie is a diamond-cut masterpiece that's all killer and no filler, seamlessly creating the aesthetic of a serious, fly-on-the-wall documentary thanks to Reiner's steady hand. What makes This Is Spinal Tap so enduring is its free-flowing comedic rhythm and confidence in its palatable premise.

Even in the most rambunctious and bonkers studio comedies, there had to be a hard cap on jokes, as basic plot setups and character arcs needed to be executed straightly. In 1980, the writer-director trio of Jerry Zucker, Jim Abrahams, and David Zucker defied cinematic conventions by making Airplane!, a spoof of disaster epics and Hollywood's first certified joke machine. Even on rewatch, your body is never prepared to provide enough laughter within you to keep up with this gem.

Airplane!, starring Robert Hays, Julie Hagerty, and Leslie Nielsen in his comedy breakthrough, is an exercise in joke excess. In the film, jokes are found in the dialogue through basic punchlines, non-sequiturs, wordplay, double entendres, and more. When counting all the slapstick gags and background quirks, Airplane! becomes a topsy-turvy experience that must have overwhelmed audiences in 1980. Today, the film is a cherished object that perfected the spoof genre and crystallized a style of madcap comedy often imitated but never matched. The film's only artistic merit is its humor, and the array of jokes and bits replace any semblance of character or story, but ZAZ's writing and execution is undoubtedly a work of pure art.

In many ways, Charlie Chaplin symbolizes the magic of movies. With his distinct blend of eccentric screwball comedy and sincere emotional affection, Chaplin's films have invigorated audiences and empowered them out of the world's darkest times. Released amid the Great Depression in 1936, Modern Times provided catharsis and warmth for the millions of impoverished Americans who needed hearty laughs and tender love. Chaplin's work as a writer, director, and actor was never more urgent.

Although silent films were more or less distinct by the mid-30s, Chaplin proved in Modern Times, his final silent comedy and outing as the Little Tramp character, that dialogue is overrated. The film, in which the Tramp is an overworked factory laborer on an assembly line who becomes smitten with a local woman, played by Paulette Goddard. While the film contains smatterings of dialogue and an extended song sequence, Modern Times retains the trappings of a Golden Age silent comedy, featuring a bumbling but lovable protagonist up against all the odds. Chaplin's physical acting and daring choreography reached an all-time high in this movie, and his cinematic language, with the medium having greatly evolved since his debut, had never been sharper. The film's fast-paced energy and quick-witted sensibilities make you forget that it's 90 years old. Modern Times miraculously jumps between farce and melancholy, a trait that Chaplin made look easy.

A movie that, seemingly overnight, went from a misbegotten flop to an all-time classic, The Big Lebowski might be the source of your most annoying friend's vocabulary, but no matter how obsessed people have become with it, Joel and Ethan Coen's stoner comedy/neo-noir is a precious object. Watch just a few minutes of The Big Lebowski, and you'll fully buy into its ardent cult status, or rather, you'll abide by it.

Most comedies aren't blessed with top-notch cinematography, production design, editing, and sound, but for a movie directed by the Coen Brothers, that's just par for the course. The Big Lebowski's vibrant legacy is partially shaped by its distinct visualization of contemporary Los Angeles as a vast wasteland filled with wandering drifters like The Dude (Jeff Bridges) and curious oddballs like Walter Sobchak (John Goodman). Despite parodying the hard-boiled private eye and cryptic mystery plot, the film is a captivating noir that wrestles with fate and one's purpose in a disillusioned world. Still, you come for The Big Lebowski to laugh your head off, and the film can do that every day at any time of the day. Made up of high-end farce and subtle asides and interjections, the Coens tapped into something beyond our basic comprehension of what humor could be.

To cite the unforgettable final line of Some Like It Hot, nobody's perfect, but movies can be. Look no further than the filmography of writer-director Billy Wilder, a master of absurdist humor, hard-boiled noir, and poignant serio-comic character relationships. The epitome of Wilder's ingenuity came in 1959 with this film, led by the trifecta of Jack Lemmon, Tony Curtis, and Marilyn Monroe. This screwball comedy hasn't lost an ounce of its hysterical mania.

Nine times out of 10, a scenario involving two male jazz instrumentalists dressing up in drag and posing as women to hide from the mob as they become romantically entangled with a gorgeous band singer would feel gross to watch through a contemporary lens. With Some Like It Hot, a film with a deft touch and a shrewd understanding of who and where to poke fun at, these comedic launching pads carry a shockingly progressive register for the time. Wilder imbues this story of two bumbling men caught in an awkward predicament and potential identity crisis with the dramatic sincerity of Sabrina and The Apartment, while the pitch-black screwball antics match the subgenre's golden age. Marked by rapid-fire dialogue, diamond-cut physical timing, and a smart screenplay that ties together all loose ends with the cleverness of a mystery, it doesn't get much better than Some Like It Hot.

Stanley Kubrick perfected just about every genre throughout his illustrious career, so, of course, he also needed to write and direct a comedy film to define all comedy films with Dr. Strangelove or: How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Bomb. It's a movie that established veterans and aspiring filmmakers have been trying to re-create for the next 60 years since its release, and nothing touches the macabre hilarity and profound textual insight of Kubrick's satire on the nuclear arms race.

When you think of a movie satire, Dr. Strangelove is likely the first film to spring to mind. Due to its absurdist yet controlled humor, eerie reflection of the stupidity of the real world, and its prescient commentary, Kubrick laid out the blueprint for the perfect satire that is both enlightening and sobering to the viewer. Strangelove, released in 1964 during the height of Cold War panic, marked the beginning of the Kubrickian vision that we've come to worship, as his precise and calculating command of the frame is a riveting juxtaposition to the comedic showcase put on by George C. Scott, Sterling Hayden, and Peter Sellers in a trifecta of versatile roles. The sheer weight of its bleak text and the virtuosity of Kubrick's craft and mise-en-scene never undermines the comedic spine of the film, which has irreversibly shaped the public's view of government and geopolitical affairs as nothing but a clown show. Never has a film been so easy to laugh at and be terrified of as Dr. Strangelove.
