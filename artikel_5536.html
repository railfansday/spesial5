Court Report: Compass Attempts to Divide and Conquer in MLS War

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Editor’s note: The COURT REPORT is RISMedia’s weekly look at current and upcoming lawsuits, investigations and other legal developments around real estate.

A day after Compass sued 15 MLSs in separate lawsuits, the brokerage again made a move to divide and conquer in its campaign against several MLSs.

Facing a separate lawsuit brought by seven MLSs against Compass—including Arizona Regional MLS (ARMLS), bridgeMLS, Canopy MLS, REcolorado, MLSListings, Hive MLS and Doorify MLS—the brokerage submitted a letter on Nov. 13 to Southern District of New York Judge, Mary Kay Vyskocil, asking for a dismissal, or a motion to sever and transfer.

Within the filing, Compass alleges that the MLSs in question had sought an extension after the brokerage’s initial Oct. 6 letter, demanding that the organizations cease fining and punishing real estate professionals for “publicly marketing office exclusive listings or face an antitrust suit.”

The letter states that Mitch Skinner—an attorney acting on behalf of ARMLS, Doorify MLS and Hive MLS—spoke to Compass’s chief legal officer and asked for an extension to the demand letter’s deadline, on the grounds that “several decision makers were ill and others were busy and needed more time to work on Compass’ demand.” Later the filing mentions that the other MLSs separately sought extensions for time as well.

Compass allegedly extended the deadline, but accuses the MLSs of using that time to organize their Oct. 21 lawsuit against the brokerage instead of complying with Compass’s demands.

Compass’s attorneys argue that because the MLSs “(signaled) that a negotiated resolution was under consideration” by asking for extensions, the case should be dismissed.

Alternatively—should the motion to dismiss be denied—Compass requested that the court “sever each MLS’s claims and transfer them to the district where that particular MLS’s Home-District Action is pending.”

By separating each MLS plaintiff into distinct lawsuits, Compass seems to be holding true to its promise, where the brokerage would “spend millions of dollars to sue CRMLS and other MLSs to accomplish those goals,” as stated in the Sept. 8 letter sent to all the MLSs it sued.

Last week, the mega-brokerage Compass took a major step in the battle over listing restrictions by filing lawsuits against over a dozen MLSs, across eight states. Six of the MLSs are based in California, with three in North Carolina and the rest spread throughout a handful of other states around the country.

In one of Compass’s filed complaints—against North Carolina’s Hive MLS—the brokerage accuses the MLS of “using control of a home listing database that real estate agents in eastern North Carolina and the other areas Hive MLS services cannot work without,” to block Compass and protect the brokerage’s competitors.

Several of the MLSs targeted—including Hive MLS, ARMLS, REcolorado, Canopy MLS, Doorify MLS and bridgeMLS—already sued Compass, asking a judge to preemptively declare their rules do not violate antitrust laws. They have characterized their various policies, which restrict how members can market listing off-MLS, as pro-competitive and pro-consumer, and claimed Compass is merely seeking to use its exclusive listings as a “competitive weapon.”

Compass, on the other hand, has repeatedly accused these MLSs of fighting purely to protect their own interests and claimed the MLS industry has long endured antitrust scrutiny from government regulators. It sent letters to many of these MLSs back in September, explicitly threatening to sue and force them to bear the legal costs if they continued enforcing the policies in question.

“It’s almost as if these MLSs are asking people to ignore the evidence that proves their claims aren’t true,” a Compass spokesperson said. “For years, seven of the top 25 MLSs, covering 340,000 agents across 12 states, have allowed agents to publicly market ‘office exclusives’ without fines, and none of the problems they warn about have happened. If what we are asking for is so bad, why has it worked for years in nearly 30% of the country?”

While the lawsuits are filed against each MLS, the arguments broadly mirror Compass’s accusations against California-based CRMLS, which it separately sued back in October.

Compass moves to dismiss rent-monopoly allegations

Nearly four months since the case was originally filed, Compass now awaits a judge’s decision on its motion to dismiss the antitrust class-action lawsuit accusing the brokerage of monopolizing the rental market in New York City.

The case—initially filed by Peter Castaneda and Haley Gelfand—alleges that Compass monopolized the non-rent stabilized, multifamily residential real estate units (rental units) in its “conquest to seize control over various housing markets,” according to the original complaint, including efforts to remove Compass listings from New York’s StreetEasy portal.

“Compass, which now owns every other competitor real estate brokerage firm (referring to Anywhere Real Estate, which Compass acquired in January 2026), controls over 80% of the rental unit listings available for renters in Manhattan based on 2025 data,” the complaint continues. “Rather than compete in the relevant market on the merits to attain high profits, Compass instead neutralized Competitors by acquiring them.”

On Nov. 10, Compass submitted a motion to dismiss the plaintiffs’ complaint on the basis that they’ve failed to plead the “bedrock” requirement, failed to prove monopoly power and lacks antitrust standing.

Generally, the “bedrock” requirement refers to a plaintiff’s ability to prove a concrete injury was done, along with proving causation and redressability. Compass’s motion also argues that the renters are unable to prove that the brokerage holds monopoly power.

“By (the) plaintiff’s own account, it is the owners and managers of rental units who ‘set rent prices’ in response to supply and demand using various third-party ‘algorithms’ and ‘tools,’” Compass’ argument states. “The complaint does not allege that Compass owns, manages, or leases a single rental apartment, or that Compass sets rents or controls any of the pricing tools that landlords and owners use.”

But, the plaintiff’s original complaint alleges that Compass’ “campaign to have no competitors” is tied to the brokerage’s conduct when it comes to the publication of rental unit listings.

“Compass, led by its chief executive Robert Reffkin, has been engaged in both a litigative and public relations-based war with Zillow,” the complaint states. “The rerouting of Compass’ listings through REBNY Residential Listing Service (RLS) intentionally results in depriving consumers of free-to-access portals to find rental units and instead mandates the use of a broker to find a rental unit.”

The original complaint also argues that with control of over 80% of the listings in Manhattan alone, Compass can choose to delist them from free-to-use platforms like StreetEasy and thus drive up prices for other apartments listed due to the lack of supply.

While the case is still ongoing, all parties await the judge’s decision on the motion to dismiss.

After nearly 40 years of employment, the former chief financial officer of the National Association of Realtors® (NAR) filed a lawsuit against the association, accusing it of withholding his retirement benefit.

John Pierpoint—who retired from his role in 2025—filed the lawsuit against NAR in the Circuit Court of Cook County in Chicago last Wednesday.

According to a release from Pierpoint’s attorneys, NAR “improperly withheld one year of salary in retirement compensation despite having recorded the anticipated payment in its own financial records for more than a decade.”

The release also points out that NAR has maintained the executive retirement compensation practice for approximately two decades, and pays retiring senior executives a year’s worth of their base salary.

The amount Pierpoint was to receive for his retirement was about $541,000, or his base salary, according to his complaint. Pierpoint likens NAR’s refusal to pay his retirement benefit to their recent settlement in the Burnett lawsuit, where the association paid $418 million to plaintiffs.

Weighed down by the burden of the settlement and legal fees, Pierpoint’s attorneys argue that NAR “directed its finance staff to write the Compensation Reserve off its books, eliminating a liability of several million dollars from its balance sheet during the period in which it was funding its antitrust settlement obligations.”

“Pierpoint helped guide NAR through an extraordinarily challenging period in its history, including the financial consequences of the antitrust litigation,” David Fish, an attorney representing Pierpoint, stated. “After nearly four decades of service, he expected the organization to honor the same retirement compensation practice it had followed for other senior executives. We believe the evidence will establish that the financial pressures following the antitrust verdict did not relieve NAR of its obligation to pay compensation earned by its employees.”
