Autoimmunity and the Stories Women Tell About Illness

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

The most trusted resource for mindfulness and meditation.

Sara Hirsh Bordo on chronic illness, inherited expectations, and what it costs to stop being the good girl.

For many women, being good has always meant being useful. Their sense of worth becomes tied to anticipating everyone else’s needs, taking responsibility for problems that aren’t theirs, and making sure no one is disappointed. Their own needs fall to the bottom of the list. Over time, it can become difficult to remember what it feels like to put themselves first.

What are we afraid will happen if we stop being who others expect us to be?

Sara Hirsh Bordo spent 15 years as a documentary filmmaker telling women’s stories before she began examining her own. Her experiences with serious illness led her to question the rules she’d been living by, and the result is her first book, Autoimmunity and the Good Girls. She calls herself a “recovering good girl.”

When we spoke, she kept returning to one question: What are we afraid will happen if we stop being who others expect us to be?

We all know the vocabulary. I should do more. I should be available. I should be able to manage all of it. Bordo thinks the shoulds are not where this starts.

“I don’t think the ‘shoulds’ come first. I think the fears come first,” Bordo says.

She describes the questions that can arise when we begin challenging expectations we’ve lived with for years: What will my family think? Will I disappoint someone? What happens if I become someone new—and what if I don’t like that person?

“I think that if you can survive incremental levels of disappointing other people, all of a sudden, there’s freedom that comes in the place of those pockets,” Bordo says.

For Bordo, recognizing those fears meant reconsidering the rules she’d built her life around. She began questioning the expectations she’d inherited and making room for new ways of thinking about herself and her relationships.

“I think that if you can survive incremental levels of disappointing other people, all of a sudden, there’s freedom that comes in the place of those pockets,” she says.

She also recognizes how deeply these fears can be passed down through generations. They’re not just her own, she explains, but part of the expectations she saw reflected in her mother, grandmother, and great-grandmother.

She said this the morning after a Zoom conversation with a book club of about 17 women of color, where the conversation about permission brought several of them to tears. The discussion prompted reflection on expectations and beliefs that some women had carried for decades.

Bordo’s question started with a pattern. The women around her who had been diagnosed with chronic or autoimmune conditions were all eldest or only daughters, herself included. At first, she assumed it was connected to birth order. So she and what she calls her “crack team of volunteers” started calling the CDC and the NIH. They found research about birth order and health in general, but nothing on autoimmune disease specifically.

She then conducted an informal survey of 300 women in the U.S. with autoimmune conditions, and 70 percent reported being eldest or only daughters.

A few of the others wrote her personal notes describing how they’d taken on significant responsibility for others, despite not being the eldest or only daughter.

The survey was not a scientific study and had no comparison group, so the findings cannot establish a connection between birth order and autoimmune disease. But the notes stayed with her, because they pointed past birth order to something she describes as “heightened responsibility too soon.”

These observations also led Bordo to think differently about the relationship between personal experiences and physical health.

“Our body doesn’t just speak science, it speaks story,” Bordo says.

A doctor she names as Dr. Carmona then introduced her to epigenetics, the study of how changes in gene activity can occur without altering the underlying DNA sequence. She used it to map experiences from different chapters of her life against what her body might have been doing at the time. She is clear that this is her own framework and not a medical explanation. But it gave her a way to think about the body’s immune response in autoimmune disease, and about permission as the story we tell about who we’re allowed to be.

Permission, she told me, “is just the ground floor.”

To describe what it feels like when fear loosens, she reaches for a heist movie. She means it as a picture of how the change felt; she doesn’t claim it explains how autoimmune disease starts or ends.

“I feel like that level of fear and the power of fear is holding the space for freedom. And when freedom lives more than fear does, I think that’s when we are jackknifed into an immune system that actually starts almost unlocking itself. And then it’s like an Ocean’s Eleven scene. They’re opening the safe, and it’s like the inner workings of the safe, and the wheels are spinning, and then the doors are opening, and then that’s the image that I feel is happening in a body. You actually become sovereign enough, and brave enough, and self-permitted enough to choose for yourself first, and other people second. That’s the shift.”

I told her that last line made me think of someone I know. She’s smart, she knows what she wants, and she keeps handing the decision back to the good girl. The story about what she should do wins out over her own happiness more often than not. I can watch it from the outside, going in circles.

Bordo shared an example from earlier that morning. Her best friend’s mother was dying, with perhaps 48 hours left, and Bordo was due to fly out very early the next day. She had been on the road for 12 straight days and had a Reiki appointment booked for 3:30 that afternoon.

She had woken at 4 a.m. with the old instinct: to get on a plane immediately. “Let me just hold that impulse,” she told herself, weighing it against the appointment. “Am I a fraud to my own book?”

Arriving with nothing left to give her friend, she reasoned, wouldn’t help either of them, and she knew what the next five days would demand. She kept the appointment.

The well-worn path of the people pleaser is still there, even for someone who wrote a book about questioning it. At 4 a.m., it was waiting.

“I think we’re all questioning all the time,” she told me. “This is still new. I was a people pleaser for far longer than I have been sovereign.”

The well-worn path of the people pleaser is still there, even for someone who wrote a book about questioning it. At 4 a.m., it was waiting. But we all know that if you do what you always did, you get what you always got.

That doesn’t quiet the voice insisting you should be doing more. When I asked about the harsh inner critic, Bordo tied it to fear and to something she calls “a permission of worth.”

“I feel like if you believe that you are allowed a life that is true and well and free, then that sets the table for a different list. That will yield that list that you are talking about. The list moves. The list changes.”

I know that list well. I was 14 when I was diagnosed with Crohn’s disease. In 2007, in my early 30s, after medical complications, I had my intestine removed, and hoped the hardest part was behind me. Instead came hernias, mesh infections, and more surgery, with complications continuing for years before things finally settled in early 2025.

I assumed my 50s would be easier. Then I got shingles, and with an immune system like mine, an ordinary illness set off six months of fallout. At 51 came a cancer scare unrelated to my autoimmune disease, followed by more surgery and recovery. My body has to recalibrate every time. Each setback is one more timeout, one more delay.

I know how to manage my disease. Watching the plan fall apart yet again, and then living with the aftermath, is what wears me down. It’s a particular kind of fatigue, the kind that comes from having it all on repeat, and underneath it there’s resentment, and sometimes anger. I feel overwhelmed, and quite often defeated, and a voice tells me I should be handling all of it better.

Wanting things to be different is like writing the check for more suffering. Some days I need a leg up, or just a break. When I catch myself in that loop, I know I still have work to do.

This is where I wanted to press, because the territory is slippery. You can notice that you tend to neglect yourself without concluding that the neglect made you sick. Bordo is firm on this.

“We didn’t do anything to deserve what’s happened to us,” she says. “Not ever.”

“We didn’t do anything to deserve what’s happened to us,” she says. “Not ever.”

She also acknowledges the limits of her perspective: “I’m not a doctor and you know that,” emphasizing that she’s speaking from personal experience rather than offering a medical explanation.

Looking back, she sees a pattern of ignoring symptoms because they showed up at inconvenient times, while she kept putting coworkers, family, and friends first. She had watched women exhaust themselves for other people’s needs, and it looked normal to her.

Then the diagnoses arrived. She described the experience of suddenly having to confront her own needs after spending so much of her life focused on everyone else’s.

“That is when the human being actually needs to come online the loudest, not the human doing,” she says.

“I had a disease in my body. I had cancer in my body. I had toxicity in my body.” For a while, it felt too big to tackle. “Self-forgiveness came first,” she says, though she knew she hadn’t caused or earned any of it.

We hear a lot about forgiveness being a gift you give yourself. We hear less about the self-flagellation that can accompany illness and other difficult experiences, particularly when we find ourselves wondering whether we could have done something differently.

Self-forgiveness can be difficult, especially when we’re accustomed to extending more understanding to others than to ourselves. I recognize that tendency in my own life, too, and I understand why Bordo chose to begin there.

“There wasn’t really a Sara,” she recalls. She had spent her life working out who she needed to be to stay “liked, loved, and safe.” She also realized how much authority she’d handed to other people over her own well-being. That pattern, she told me, “boils over from personal to professional to medical.”

These days, Bordo says she’s learning to give greater weight to her own experiences and needs, rather than relying solely on external measures of how she’s doing. For her, this means recognizing the value of her own perspective alongside medical information, rather than treating the two as mutually exclusive.

“Those choices and framework had to be rewritten and re-authored by me,” she says. That work, she added, began when she reached a particularly difficult point in her life.

Recognizing a pattern doesn’t necessarily mean we can immediately break it, particularly when illness, relationships, and circumstances influence the choices available to us.

I told her I believe agency and accountability are part of this too, and I know the word “permission” can be complicated for some readers. She doesn’t pretend it won’t be.

Bordo knew her book wouldn’t resonate with everyone. Some readers find her approach too focused on the mind-body connection, while others interpret her emphasis on personal agency as suggesting that people who struggle to make changes are somehow choosing to remain stuck. She recognizes that her message may be received differently than she intends.

Our conversation left me thinking about how complicated change can be. Recognizing a pattern doesn’t necessarily mean we can immediately break it, particularly when illness, relationships, and circumstances influence the choices available to us.

I do know what she says to the people who keep going in circles: “I want more for you.”
