Pick one of the largest U.S. banks for its branches and ATMs if you want in-person help wherever you live or move. Then set up enough direct deposit to waive the monthly checking fee, and keep your savings in a high-yield savings account, which pays far more than a branch giant’s basic savings account.

Six of the 10 largest U.S. banks run more than 1,900 branches each, according to the Federal Reserve’s quarterly ranking of commercial banks by assets. Savings is where those branch-heavy giants fall short.

U.S. Bank’s Bank Smartly Savings, for one, pays a standard rate of 0.05% APY, or annual percentage yield, a fraction of the 0.39% national average savings rate the FDIC reports.

Two giants with small branch networks are the exceptions. Marcus, Goldman Sachs’s online bank, pays 3.50% APY on savings, and Capital One pays 3.20% on 360 Performance Savings, both with no monthly fee.

The FDIC’s quarterly count of insured banks and savings institutions stood at 4,238 at the end of June 2026, fewer than a quarter of the 18,033 at the end of 1985.

Reach comes first. A bank with branches and ATMs near your home, your job, and your next address saves you trips and out-of-network ATM fees, and a teller can take a cash deposit or sort out a problem in person. That reach is the reason to keep your checking at one.

Keeping your checking, savings, and investments at one giant earns perks. BofA Rewards waives monthly fees on Bank of America checking and savings once your combined three-month average balance reaches $30,000, counting its deposit accounts and Merrill investments. At that level it also adds 25% to the rewards on eligible credit cards, rising to 75% at $1 million, so the perks matter only if your investments or large balances sit there too.

Checking bonuses are another draw. Chase pays new Total Checking customers $400 after $1,000 in direct deposits within 90 days of enrolling in the offer.

Checking fees come first. Chase Total Checking costs $15 a month unless you meet a waiver, such as $500 a month in electronic deposits like a paycheck. PNC’s Virtual Wallet costs $7 a month unless you meet one of its waivers.

Basic savings carries a fee too. Chase, Wells Fargo, and PNC charge $5 a month on it and Bank of America $8, unless you meet a waiver such as keeping $300 in the account ($500 at Bank of America). That’s another reason to keep your savings elsewhere.

Overdraft fees vary. Chase charges $34 per transaction once your balance is more than $50 below zero, up to three fees, or $102, a business day. It waives the fee if your balance is back within $50 of zero when the next business day ends. Citibank, Capital One’s 360 Checking, and Wells Fargo’s Clear Access Banking charge no overdraft fees, so pick one of those if your balance often runs close to zero.

Use the giant for checking. Have your paycheck deposited there to meet the fee waiver, keep about a month of bills in the account, and set a low-balance alert so you see a shortfall before it becomes an overdraft.

Move the rest to a high-yield savings account at another bank, linked to your checking for transfers.

At 3% APY, $10,000 earns $300 in a year, against $5 at 0.05%.
