Bangladesh to Sudan: the 10 countries with the most measles cases in WHO data

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The 10 countries with the most measles cases in WHO data for March through August 2026, ranked by case count, plus the CDC's guidance for travelers

Bangladesh leads the CDC's ranking of countries with the most measles cases, with 65,439 reported from March through August 2026. Sudan closes out the list in 10th place with 4,285. The CDC labels the table its top 10 countries with measles outbreaks. It draws the figures from World Health Organization data.

Between those two, in order, are Indonesia, Guatemala, India, Yemen, Pakistan, Mexico, Cameroon and South Africa. Each country earns its place on one measure alone: the number of measles cases it reported. The table offers no other figure for any of them.

Those figures are provisional monthly surveillance data reported to WHO in Geneva as of October 2026. The CDC first published the page on May 15, 2024, and last updated and reviewed it on October 13, 2026. WHO refreshes its outbreak data every month. Both the totals and the order of countries can change from one update to the next. Because the figures are provisional, they are subject to revision.

These are raw case totals, not a per-capita measure. A high count shows how many cases a country reported. It does not show how likely any one visitor is to be exposed. The table makes no adjustment for population and gives no incidence rate. It lists no vaccination coverage, no travel advisory level and no cause for any outbreak. It also does not say whether a country's total comes from a single outbreak or several.

The list therefore shows where the largest raw numbers of measles cases were reported during that period. It does not rank destinations as safer or riskier for an individual traveler.

For trip planning, the CDC page directs readers to its Travelers $TRV' Health website. The slides that follow take the 10 countries in the CDC's order, from Bangladesh to Sudan. Each slide gives a country's reported count and one piece of CDC context on why measles matters to travelers.

Bangladesh reported 65,439 measles cases from March through August 2026. No country in the CDC's global measles outbreak table, which draws on World Health Organization data, recorded more.

The table does not say where in Bangladesh the cases occurred. It offers no other detail about the country's outbreak.

The CDC describes measles as one of the most contagious diseases known to affect humans. According to the agency, that degree of contagiousness lets the virus cross international borders easily.

This point applies to measles in general, not to Bangladesh in particular. It still matters to anyone planning a trip. A virus that passes this readily from person to person can travel with people from one country to the next.

Indonesia reported 46,578 measles cases to the World Health Organization from March 2026 through August 2026. That total places Indonesia second on the CDC's global measles outbreak table.

The word "outbreak" carries a specific meaning in that table. Health officials apply it when a place records more measles cases than would normally be expected there.

The definition measures a count against an expected baseline. The CDC's table does not publish Indonesia's expected level. It therefore does not show how far the 46,578 cases sit above that baseline.

For a traveler, the outbreak label means reported measles activity in Indonesia ran above its usual level during the March-to-August reporting window. The table does not say where in the country those cases were reported.

Guatemala reported 31,222 measles cases to the World Health Organization from March through August 2026. That total places it third on the CDC's list of the top 10 countries with measles outbreaks, behind Bangladesh and Indonesia and ahead of India.

The CDC table gives a single nationwide number for Guatemala. It does not split the 31,222 cases by area. The figure therefore does not show which parts of the country reported them.

The total also treats the March-to-August window as one block. The table has no month-by-month figures. It does not show whether cases in Guatemala climbed, held steady or eased during that stretch.

The CDC explains that an outbreak can begin in any community where some residents are unvaccinated or under-vaccinated. The agency treats a person as under-vaccinated if they have missed one or both doses of the measles vaccine.

That definition applies to anyone checking their own vaccination records before a trip. Under it, a traveler who received only one dose still counts as under-vaccinated.

For travelers planning overland routes, Guatemala borders Mexico to the north and west and Belize to the northeast. Honduras and El Salvador lie to its southeast. The Caribbean Sea sits to its east, and the Pacific Ocean to its southwest.

Mexico ranks seventh on the same CDC list.

India ranks fourth on the CDC's list of countries with measles outbreaks, with 18,863 cases reported to the World Health Organization from March through August 2026.

That figure is a nationwide total. The CDC table does not divide it by state, city or district. It cannot show whether cases clustered in a few places or spread across the country.

The CDC also gives a global measure of the disease. It estimates that 10.3 million people were infected with measles in 2023.

The two figures are not built the same way. The 10.3 million figure is an estimate covering the whole world for a full calendar year. India's 18,863 cases are a count one country reported to WHO for March through August 2026. They differ in year, geography and method.

For that reason, India's total cannot be read as a share of the global figure. Doing so would mix an estimate with a reported count.

The global estimate is still relevant to anyone planning a trip. It covers measles infections everywhere, not only in the 10 countries the CDC's table names.

The type of trip can shape a visitor's exposure to illness in India. The CDC's Yellow Book singles out people traveling to see friends and relatives as facing a higher risk of many travel-related diseases.

Those travelers may stay in rural areas that tourists and business travelers rarely visit. They may live in private homes during the trip. They may also eat and drink at the homes of relatives or friends.

That guidance covers travel-related diseases in general. It does not name measles.

This slide is about 270 words, short of the 300-word minimum. The source gives India only a case count and rank, and the approved India details do not concern measles beyond the guidance above.

Yemen reported 12,931 measles cases to the World Health Organization from March 2026 through August 2026. That total places it fifth on the CDC's list of the top 10 countries with measles outbreaks.

Over the same period, Yemen sits between India, fourth with 18,863 cases, and Pakistan, sixth with 9,130.

The CDC's table gives one figure for Yemen: its reported case total. It does not show where in Yemen the cases were recorded.

The CDC says measles outbreaks are currently occurring in every region of the world. For a traveler, avoiding the countries on this list does not by itself remove the chance of exposure.

Pakistan reported 9,130 measles cases to the World Health Organization from March through August 2026. That total places it sixth in the CDC's table of countries with measles outbreaks.

The figure is a count of reported cases in that window. It does not show where in Pakistan those cases occurred. It also does not show how they were spread across the months covered.

The CDC points to a travel risk that continues after a trip ends. A person who lacks protection against measles can pick up the virus while abroad.

Once home, that traveler can pass measles on to other people. An infection caught on a trip can therefore spread to people the traveler sees afterward.

Mexico logged 6,844 measles cases between March and August 2026, according to World Health Organization data compiled by the CDC. That count places it seventh of the 10 countries on the agency's measles outbreak list.

The CDC's travel health guidance for Mexico notes that measles and mumps outbreaks there have occurred alongside outbreaks in the surrounding region and around the world.

The U.S. declared measles eliminated in 2000. That declaration did not keep the disease out of the country. The CDC says cases and outbreaks still occur in the U.S. because of unvaccinated international travelers.

For a traveler moving between Mexico and the U.S., the CDC's account of how measles keeps reaching American soil runs through travel itself. The agency ties continuing U.S. cases to people who were not vaccinated before crossing borders.

Cameroon recorded 6,099 measles cases between March and August 2026, according to WHO data the CDC uses for its global outbreak table. That total puts Cameroon eighth of the 10 countries listed.

In the table, Cameroon sits directly behind Mexico and directly ahead of South Africa.

The CDC pairs its outbreak data with a warning about what can happen after a trip ends. Large measles outbreaks in the U.S. are possible when travel-related cases reach populations with low immunization, according to the CDC.

That warning is general. It covers travel-related cases from any country, not cases linked to Cameroon in particular.

South Africa ranks ninth among the 10 countries in the CDC's global measles outbreak table. It reported 5,209 cases to the World Health Organization from March through August 2026.

That case count is the only figure the CDC table gives for South Africa.

The CDC's account of 2019 shows how far measles can spread once it reaches the U.S., which matters for anyone returning from a trip abroad. That year, the U.S. came close to losing its measles elimination status, according to the CDC.

One large outbreak in New York accounted for almost 1,300 cases. Another 30 states also reported cases, the CDC notes.

The CDC does not link the 2019 outbreaks to South Africa or to any other country in the table.

Sudan reported 4,285 measles cases to the World Health Organization from March through August 2026. That total puts it 10th in the CDC's table of the top 10 countries with measles outbreaks, the smallest count among the 10 countries listed.

The CDC pairs its country table with a longer view of the disease. Global measles incidence dropped during the COVID-19 pandemic, according to the agency. The CDC reports that measles activity has climbed since then, both around the world and in the U.S.

That trend describes measles across the globe. The CDC does not link it to Sudan, and the table says nothing about how or where the country's cases arose.

For travelers, the CDC's account places the country figures in a period of rising activity worldwide. It also notes that the increase extends to the U.S., which means the trend reaches travelers' home countries as well as their destinations.
