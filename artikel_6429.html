A college student searching rocky glaciers in Yellowstone finally discovered one of the rarest insects in the world

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

A University of Wyoming student discovered three rare ice crawlers in Yellowstone, marking the first known sightings of these cold-loving insects in Wyoming.

In summer 2026, a college student named Will Anderson found three rare bugs called ice crawlers on rocky glaciers in Yellowstone National Park.

As a student at the University of Wyoming, Anderson had spent the whole previous year looking for them without any luck. Ice crawlers are some of the rarest insects on Earth.

Ice crawlers belong to Grylloblattodea, one of the rarest orders of insects in the world. Only 35 species have been described, and many are known only from single localities.

Anderson is a biology major from Casper and a junior in the Wyoming Research Scholars Program. He works with Audrey Lindsteadt, a third-year Ph.D. student in the university’s Program in Ecology and Evolution.

“I am interested in this insect because there is so much not known about them, with their life history, habitat, and distribution all not being fully understood,” Anderson said.

“I also am passionate about and interested in their alpine habitats, which are understudied,” he added.

Starting in late July 2025, the pair surveyed 14 rock glaciers in the Red Mountains next to Heart Lake, in southwestern Yellowstone. They found no ice crawlers, even though Anderson also checked snowfields and set overnight traps.

Most snowfields had already melted by then. The area also had fewer glaciers than ideal, and most of them were small or inactive.

“Despite the close proximity to their known distribution and the plentiful suitable habitat, Gryllos have never been found in Wyoming,” Lindsteadt explained. “Therefore, Will hypothesized the rock glaciers and nearby snowfields that we are studying could have Gryllos.”

On a cold, snowy six-day backpacking trip in late June and early July 2026, Anderson found two ice crawlers near Antler Peak in northwest Yellowstone. Both turned up under rocks he flipped on a large rock glacier.

In mid-July, a mother grizzly and her two-year-old cub bluff-charged the pair and blocked their planned route.

They scrambled up Grey Peak instead and slid down to its two rock glaciers, where Anderson caught a third one. In August, he searched several alpine glaciers in Grand Teton National Park but came up empty.

Over the summer of 2026, he and Lindsteadt walked about 170 miles (274 kilometers).

“The specimens I collected in Wyoming are so far identified to the species Grylloblatta campodeiformis, which is the most widespread North American species, having populations in Montana, British Columbia, and Alberta,” Anderson noted.

He briefly kept two of the insects alive. He could not tell whether they were feeding on the dead insects and plant matter he gave them.

Ice crawlers are wingless insects that stay active in cold conditions, according to North Carolina State University entomologists. Most species are active below freezing and usually die above 50°F (10°C).

They develop through incomplete metamorphosis, from egg to nymph to adult, and their growth is slow. Some may need up to seven years to complete a single generation.

The University of Wyoming describes them as extremophiles that live at temperatures as low as 21°F (-6°C) in humid alpine settings.

By day, they hide under cool rocks near snow and ice. At night, they scavenge, eating mostly other invertebrates, including insects blown onto the snow, along with some plant material.

Little is known of their diet when they live more permanently under the snow, or in the soil through summer and winter.

The order lives in northeastern Asia, Siberia, the western U.S. and Canada. In North America, small, isolated populations occupy mountains and caves in several western states, British Columbia and Alberta.

Rock glaciers resemble alpine glaciers, but a layer of debris covers their ice, insulating and protecting it.

Lindsteadt suspects that ice crawlers prefer rock glaciers to non-glacial talus fields or snowfields because rock glaciers are colder, more humid and more permanent.

“Obviously, with just three species detections, we cannot confidently identify their habitat preferences,” Lindsteadt said. “But they at least point us in a promising direction.”

Yellowstone holds 58 known rock glaciers, and none had been visited or studied before this project. Next summer, the team plans to survey 17 rock glaciers in eastern Yellowstone and revisit others.

Ice crawlers are generally restricted to high-elevation glaciers or low-elevation caves with permanent ice, according to Purdue University entomologists.

The same entomologists warn that many of these habitats are at risk as glaciers and ice fields recede with climate change.

In Anderson’s view, the insects probably play a small part in these ecosystems because of their rarity, but they still belong to the food chains and nutrient cycles of their extreme habitats.

“More importantly, we can use ice crawlers to better understand how these ecosystems work and be more effective managers of them,” he argued.

He is also working on a paper he hopes to publish in a leading alpine or entomology journal.

Anderson will present the findings at the Entomological Society of America’s 74th annual meeting in Columbus, Ohio, on November 8 to 11.
