Rainforest ferns from an ancient lineage have a surprisingly young family tree

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

An ancient lineage of rainforest ferns reveals how rising mountains and retreating wetlands may have shaped plant diversity across tropical America.

Some rainforest ferns belong to a lineage that stretches back to the age of dinosaurs. Yet roughly half its living species arose within the past six million years, as mountains rose and landscapes changed.

Researchers at the University of Turku and Aarhus University have reconstructed the history of tropical American Danaea ferns.

The findings draw on decades of work identifying ferns and establishing where they grow. That groundwork made it possible to investigate how the group spread and diversified.

“We have conducted extensive research at the University of Turku in order to understand the biodiversity of the Amazon rainforest,” said senior author Hanna Tuomisto, a professor of ecology.

“In the last 25 years, we have discovered and described 47 new Danaea species previously unknown to science, and have clarified the distribution and phylogenetic relationships of the species in the Danaea genus.”

“We have now been able to link this information to the geological and climatic history of tropical America.”

The team combined DNA evidence from 78 species with 2,289 verified records of where the ferns grow. These records helped reconstruct where ancestral ferns probably lived and the elevations they occupied.

The genetic analysis included 11 species that had not yet been formally described.

The researchers’ preferred estimate suggests that the Danaea lineage split from the lineage of its closest living relatives about 128 million years ago, during the Early Cretaceous.

The common ancestor of its surviving species lived much later, around 53 million years ago. The researchers traced that ancestor to Amazonia.

But an ancient lineage need not consist of ancient species. About half of today’s Danaea species originated during the past six million years.

This complicates a familiar explanation for tropical richness: favorable conditions allowed species to accumulate over immense stretches of time.

Persistence mattered, but the landscape also kept changing, creating opportunities for new species.

Fern diversity generally reaches its greatest heights in tropical mountain forests. Danaea follows a different pattern: Amazonia has consistently held its largest pool of species in the reconstruction.

“Mountain rainforests are home in particular to an abundance of epiphytes – plants that grow on top of other plants. Danaea ferns, on the other hand, grow on the forest floor,” said lead author Venni Keskiniva, a postdoctoral researcher at Turku.

“These research results are, in fact, similar to those obtained for trees, which are also the most species-rich in lowland rainforests.”

Where a plant lives within a forest may therefore help explain where its group becomes most diverse. Findings from one fern group cannot automatically describe them all.

The Andes still played an important role. Although Amazonia contained more species, the mountains became the leading source of dispersal to other regions.

Mountain formation affected far more than the slopes themselves. As the Andes rose, they changed rainfall patterns along with the soils developing across neighboring lowlands.

Vast wetlands once occupied much of western Amazonia. Their drainage near the end of the Miocene opened additional land for forest plants to colonize.

The resulting western Amazonian soils were generally richer in nutrients and more variable, than soils in central Amazonia.

Those differences may have encouraged plants to specialize in different environments.

The timing fits the fern history. Most new Amazonian species arising within the past six million years appeared in western Amazonia during the last three million.

New land in southern Central America also offered room for expansion. Arriving lineages frequently gave rise to additional species as the region’s landscapes developed.

The first mountain-adapted Danaea lineages appeared around 20 million years ago, following an intensification of Andean uplift. Several later spread into regions farther from the equator.

Their earlier move uphill may have helped them cope with the conditions they encountered there.

“As the plants had already adapted to the cooler climate of the mountains, it may have been easier for them to spread to areas where the temperature differences between the seasons are greater,” Keskiniva said.

Lineages that remained adapted to Amazonian lowlands tended to stay within that region.

The repeated sequence of mountain colonization followed by expansion suggests that climate tolerance influenced which routes were available.

The mountains could both separate populations and help others reach new habitats. Their role changed as the landscape rose and different fern lineages established themselves.

Assigning dates to this history was difficult. Many relevant fossils are fragmentary, and researchers disagree about where they belong in the family tree.

The team compared three dating approaches, which produced substantially different ages. It favored the reconstruction most consistent with other biological and geological evidence.

The dates remain estimates, and coinciding events do not establish a cause by themselves. Still, the findings connect the survival of ancient lineages with much younger episodes of diversification.

“The biodiversity in rainforests is the result of a long history, but its formation has also been greatly influenced by events that are relatively recent on a geological time scale,” Keskiniva concluded.

The study is published in the journal Frontiers of Biogeography.
