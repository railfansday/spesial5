From Palestine to Gibraltar, 10 cases of home games played elsewhere

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Free daily briefing on global business news.

National teams playing home games abroad usually face war, travel limits or failed stadium checks, and these 10 cases from football and cricket show why

A home game is a line on a fixture list before it is a place on a map. The team named first usually wears its first-choice kit and uses the home dressing room. Nothing in that arrangement requires the match to be played inside the team's own borders. When a country cannot host, the fixture moves abroad and keeps its home label.

That gap between label and location explains why national teams playing home games abroad have become a recurring feature of international sport.

The decision rarely rests with the team alone. In football, FIFA and continental confederations such as UEFA set rules on where competitive internationals may be staged. Those rules cover security, stadium inspections and licensing. In cricket, a home board needs visiting teams willing to tour. Without them, it must find another venue.

Three causes recur across these cases. The first is war or a security threat serious enough that teams, officials and fans cannot be protected. The second is restricted movement, which can stop players from reaching a venue or crossing borders to train together. The third is infrastructure. A stadium can fail an inspection or lack the license a competition demands, even in a country at peace.

The 10 cases that follow are a selection, not a complete list. Nine come from international football and one from cricket. They cover Europe, the Middle East, North Africa, the Caribbean and Central and South Asia. Some relocations followed a single event, such as the 2009 attack on the Sri Lanka team bus in Lahore. Others grew out of long-running conflict or security restrictions, as in Libya and Iraq. Gibraltar's case had nothing to do with security.

A relocated home match still counts as a home fixture in the competition's records. What changes is the setting. The team plays on a pitch abroad, and the size of its crowd depends on which supporters can travel or live nearby. Several hosts in this selection are neighbors, such as Jordan for Palestine, Tunisia for Libya and Tajikistan for Afghanistan. Others sit farther away, such as Malaysia for Syria and Hungary for Israel.

Taken together, the cases show a fixture list responding to events outside sport: conflict, restricted movement and stadium standards.

Palestine's national football team has repeatedly staged home fixtures outside Palestine because of movement and security restrictions. Jordan has hosted some of those matches at the Amman International Stadium.

The team also has a home venue inside the West Bank. The Faisal Al-Husseini International Stadium in Al-Ram has hosted Palestine home matches as well.

That gives Palestine two kinds of home ground. One sits in Palestinian territory. The other is in Amman, the Jordanian capital, on the far side of an international border.

A fixture at Al-Ram is a home game in both senses. The town lies in the West Bank. A match there is played on Palestinian soil.

A fixture in Amman is a home game on the schedule only. Palestine is still the named host. The stadium, however, is in another country.

The venue for a Palestine home match is therefore not settled by geography alone. Over the years, the team's home fixtures have been played both in the West Bank and in Jordan.

Movement restrictions bear on who can reach a ground. A home international needs players, coaching staff, match officials and the visiting team to arrive and leave on schedule. Security restrictions add a second test: whether the host can protect everyone inside the stadium. When those conditions cannot be met in the West Bank, Amman provides a stadium across the border.

Ukraine could not play competitive home matches on its own soil after Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022. In Euro 2024 qualifying, its home game against England was played in Wrocław, Poland. Its home game against Italy was played in Leverkusen, Germany.

The obstacle was security, not facilities. Ukraine had stadiums built to international standards, including Kyiv's Olimpiyskiy Stadium, which hosted the Euro 2012 final. Air-raid alerts and missile strikes ruled out staging a fixture with visiting players, officials and fans inside the country.

Wrocław sits in southwestern Poland, near the German and Czech borders. It was one of the host cities when Poland and Ukraine jointly staged Euro 2012. Its stadium was built for that tournament and already met UEFA standards.

Leverkusen is a city in the western German state of North Rhine-Westphalia. It is home to the Bundesliga club Bayer Leverkusen. The match against Italy was played at the BayArena, the club's ground. Germany was also the host of Euro 2024, the tournament Ukraine was trying to reach.

Ukraine did reach those finals, though not through its qualifying group. It advanced through the playoffs and then played its tournament matches in Germany.

The displacement extended beyond the national team. Ukrainian clubs could not host European ties at home either. Shakhtar Donetsk had already left Donetsk after fighting began in eastern Ukraine in 2014. After the full-scale invasion, it staged Champions League home matches in Warsaw and later in Hamburg.

Domestic football took a different route. The Ukrainian Premier League restarted in August 2022. Matches were played behind closed doors and halted when air-raid sirens sounded. That kept a league running inside the country while the national team relied on borrowed venues abroad.

The invasion also changed the fixture list for the other side of the conflict. FIFA and UEFA suspended Russia's national teams and clubs from their competitions in February 2022. Russia was left without competitive international fixtures, home or away.

Haiti played its home matches in 2026 World Cup qualifying in Curaçao rather than in Haiti. Gang violence at home ruled out hosting, and the Ergilio Hato Stadium in Willemstad served as the team's stand-in home ground.

The move took the national team away from its usual base. Haiti has traditionally played home internationals at the Stade Sylvio Cator in Port-au-Prince, the capital. Armed gangs have taken control of large parts of Port-au-Prince in recent years. The violence has displaced residents, shut businesses and forced repeated closures of the city's international airport.

The Ergilio Hato Stadium is Curaçao's national stadium and the home ground of Curaçao's own national team. It is named after Ergilio Hato, a goalkeeper from the island.

Curaçao is a small island in the southern Caribbean, off the coast of Venezuela. It is a constituent country within the Kingdom of the Netherlands, and Willemstad is its capital. Like Haiti, Curaçao belongs to CONCACAF, the confederation that governs football in North America, Central America and the Caribbean. CONCACAF organizes World Cup qualifying for the region.

The arrangement left Haiti without a guaranteed home crowd. Supporters based in Haiti had to leave the country to watch matches billed as Haiti home games.

The 2026 tournament is the first World Cup to feature 48 teams, up from 32. The U.S., Canada and Mexico co-host it, and all three qualify automatically. CONCACAF received six direct places, including the three hosts' automatic spots. The region also received two places in an intercontinental playoff. That expansion opened more routes to the finals for teams such as Haiti.

Haiti's only World Cup finals appearance before the 2026 cycle came in 1974, in West Germany. At that tournament, Emmanuel Sanon scored against Italy, ending goalkeeper Dino Zoff's long run of international minutes without conceding. Sanon also scored against Argentina, giving him both of Haiti's goals at the finals.

Syria played its home leg of the 2018 World Cup Asian qualifying playoff against Australia in Malaysia. The match was staged at the Hang Jebat Stadium in Malacca and finished 1-1.

Syria's civil war, which began in 2011, left the country unable to host international football. Its national team had to find venues abroad for competitive home matches. It also played its other home matches in that final qualifying round outside Syria.

The playoff was a two-legged tie between the third-placed teams from the two groups in Asia's final qualifying round. Australia finished third in one group. Syria finished third in the other, which also included Iran and South Korea. Away goals would break a tie on aggregate. The winner moved on to an intercontinental playoff for a place at the tournament in Russia.

Syria clinched third place on the final matchday. Omar Al Somah scored a late equalizer in a 2-2 draw away to Iran. The country had never qualified for a World Cup. The playoff left its team two rounds from a first appearance.

Malacca is a historic port city on Malaysia's west coast, southeast of Kuala Lumpur. The Hang Jebat Stadium takes its name from a warrior in Malay legend. Hosting there meant the Syrian squad traveled to Southeast Asia for a fixture listed as its own.

Robbie Kruse put Australia ahead in Malacca. Omar Kharbin equalized for Syria with a late penalty. The result left the tie level but gave Australia an away goal heading into the second leg in Sydney.

Australia won that match 2-1 after extra time. Tim Cahill scored both Australian goals. Al Somah gave Syria an early lead and later hit the post with a free kick in extra time. A goal there would have put Syria through on away goals.

Australia went on to beat Honduras in the intercontinental playoff and qualified for the 2018 World Cup. Syria's campaign ended in Sydney.

Libya's national football team has played home matches in neighboring Tunisia. Years of conflict left Libya without venues approved to stage international fixtures.

Those games keep their Libyan label. Libya is still listed as the home side, even with the match played across the border.

The conflict traces back to the 2011 uprising that ended Muammar Gaddafi's rule. Rival armed groups and competing administrations later fought for control of the country.

Venue approval decides where a competitive international can be played. A stadium must be cleared before it can host one. Libya's grounds lacked that clearance during the years of conflict. The national team needed a host abroad.

Tunisia lies along Libya's western border. Its stadiums gave the team a home base in North Africa.

The disruption reached beyond single fixtures. Libya had been chosen to host the 2017 Africa Cup of Nations. The tournament was moved because of the security situation. Gabon staged it instead.

The same conditions that sent individual matches to Tunisia also cost Libya a full continental tournament.

Afghanistan's national football team has played home matches in Dushanbe, the capital of Tajikistan. Security conditions inside Afghanistan kept the country from hosting those games.

The obstacle was security, not the condition of a stadium. A pitch that meets the regulations is only part of what a competitive international requires. Visiting players, referees and spectators must also be able to travel to the venue and stay there safely.

Dushanbe sits across the border from Afghanistan. Tajikistan shares a frontier with northern Afghanistan, and much of it follows the Panj River, one of the headwaters of the Amu Darya.

The Afghanistan Football Federation runs the national team. It belongs to FIFA and to the Asian Football Confederation. Those two bodies set the rules on where the team's competitive matches may be staged.

A match in Dushanbe still counts as an Afghanistan home fixture in the competition's records.

Pakistan's national cricket team played its home international series in the United Arab Emirates for years after gunmen attacked the Sri Lanka team bus in Lahore in March 2009. Dubai, Abu Dhabi and Sharjah became its main home venues.

The attack took place on March 3, 2009. The Sri Lankan squad was traveling to the Gaddafi Stadium for the third day of a Test match against Pakistan. Several Sri Lankan players were wounded. Pakistani police officers escorting the convoy were among those killed.

International sides stopped touring Pakistan after the shooting. Visiting boards declined to send teams, which left Pakistan with no opponents willing to play on its soil.

The fallout reached beyond bilateral tours. The International Cricket Council removed Pakistan as a co-host of the 2011 Cricket World Cup. The tournament went ahead in India, Sri Lanka and Bangladesh.

The UAE already had grounds built for international cricket. The International Cricket Council has had its headquarters in Dubai since 2005. The Emirates are also home to large South Asian expatriate communities, including many Pakistanis.

Each of the three venues has its own history. Sharjah Cricket Stadium staged one-day international tournaments from the 1980s and has hosted more one-day internationals than any other ground. Sheikh Zayed Cricket Stadium in Abu Dhabi opened in 2004. The Dubai International Cricket Stadium, in Dubai Sports City, opened in 2009.

The UAE was not Pakistan's only stand-in host. In 2010, Pakistan staged a two-Test home series against Australia in England. Lord's and Headingley hosted the two Tests.

Domestic cricket moved to the Gulf too. The Pakistan Super League, the country's professional Twenty20 competition, launched in 2016 with its first season played in the UAE.

Cricket relocations differ from football ones in a practical way. A cricket tour often runs for several weeks and can include Tests, one-day internationals and Twenty20 internationals. That means the stand-in country hosts an entire visiting tour, not a single match night.

Gibraltar played home qualifiers at the Estádio Algarve in Portugal because its own ground, the Victoria Stadium, did not meet UEFA's requirements for some competitive internationals. The move had nothing to do with war or security. It came down to whether a venue passed the governing body's standards.

Gibraltar is a British Overseas Territory on the southern tip of the Iberian Peninsula. It joined UEFA as a full member in 2013. That gave its national team a place in European qualifying competitions. Gibraltar later became a FIFA member in 2016, which opened World Cup qualifying as well. Membership did not come with a stadium ready for every fixture those competitions involve.

UEFA sets infrastructure rules for the venues used in its competitions. Its Stadium Infrastructure Regulations grade grounds into categories. The rules cover areas such as seating, floodlighting, media facilities and the playing surface. A ground that falls short of the required level cannot stage the matches those rules apply to. That holds even if it hosts regular domestic football.

The Victoria Stadium is Gibraltar's main football ground. It sits beside Gibraltar International Airport, on a territory where flat land is scarce. It also stages the territory's domestic league matches.

Portugal provided the alternative. The Estádio Algarve stands between the cities of Faro and Loulé in the country's southern Algarve region. It was built as a host venue for Euro 2004, the European Championship that Portugal staged that year. UEFA accepted it for Gibraltar's qualifiers, and the team played its home fixtures there.

The arrangement meant Gibraltar's supporters had to leave the territory to watch a home match. Spain is the only country that shares a land border with Gibraltar. Portugal does not border it at all. The overland route to the Algarve therefore crosses Spain before reaching the Portuguese border.

For Gibraltar, a home qualifier meant a trip through one country to play in another.

Israel played its home UEFA Nations League matches in Hungary in autumn 2024. The fixtures moved because UEFA matches could not be played safely in Israel during the conflict.

UEFA organizes the Nations League and sets the conditions for its matches. A national association cannot choose any venue it likes for a competitive fixture. The governing body has to accept that a site is safe for players, officials and spectators. When a country cannot meet that test, the match is staged elsewhere.

Israel belongs to UEFA, the governing body for European football, rather than to Asia's confederation. Its national team therefore enters UEFA competitions, including the Nations League. Hungary is also a UEFA member.

Moving the fixtures to Hungary kept them inside UEFA's own membership. The matches stayed under the same competition rules, at a venue that still had to meet UEFA's standards for the Nations League.

The Nations League is UEFA's competition for national teams. It is played during international windows outside the major tournaments. Group results decide standings, as well as promotion and relegation between leagues. A home game in the competition is a competitive fixture, not a friendly. It cannot be dropped from the schedule without affecting the group table.

The matches in Hungary kept their status as Israel's home fixtures in the group. The venue moved, but the fixture's designation did not. Israel's squad traveled to another country to play matches it was formally hosting. Its opponents also traveled to Hungary instead of making the trip to Israel.

The cause sets Israel's case apart from relocations driven by stadium standards or licensing rules. The condition of Israel's grounds was not the reason for the move. The question was whether UEFA matches could be held safely in the country at all during the conflict. A stadium upgrade could not settle that question. The answer depended on security conditions in the country, not on construction work or an inspection.

Iraq played its home football internationals outside the country for years. FIFA had restricted Iraq from hosting competitive internationals because of security conditions.

Those fixtures moved to other countries. Qatar, Jordan and Iran were among the places where Iraq lined up as the home side.

The restriction came from FIFA, football's world governing body, rather than from Iraq's own federation. It applied to competitive internationals, the matches that count toward qualification and tournament standings.

The grounds for the restriction were security conditions inside Iraq. That made the issue one of safety, not a decision Iraqi football officials could reverse on their own.

The hosts differed in their relationship to Iraq. Jordan and Iran share land borders with Iraq. Qatar has no border with Iraq and lies farther down the Persian Gulf.

The restrictions did not end in a single ruling. FIFA lifted them in stages.
