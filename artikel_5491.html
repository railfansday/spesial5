Infill outlier Thomas James Homes ‘landmaxxes’ lots with ADUs

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

TJH leverages attached ADU options across Seattle area floor plans to increase usable square footage on small lots. The company points to state legislation allowing 2 ADUs per lot and local treatment of ADUs as 2 units that can increase allowable floor area.  AI Summary

Where new construction is scarce and lots are tight, luxury homebuilder Thomas James Homes (TJH) has pivoted to offering accessory dwelling units (ADUs) to entice its homebuyers by adding more square footage on parcels with little room to spare.

For the firm’s Pacific Northwest division, which operates in Seattle and nearby suburbs where lots are small and space is at a premium, the approach is a natural fit. While detached ADUs are the more common use case nationwide, TJH’s PNW division uses attached ADUs because they offer cost efficiencies and add functional living space to small infill lots without sacrificing as much yard.

Most TJH floorplans in the region, which typically sell for $2 million to $5 million, include an attached ADU option, according to PNW division president Adam Rohde. More than half of buyers choose to include one.

Founded in 2006, TJH is a high-end, scattered-lot homebuilder that operates in California, Washington and Arizona. The company specializes in building replacement homes on individual lots in established neighborhoods where new construction is limited, either on homeowner-owned lots or through spec home sales. The goal is to bring the efficiency and predictability of production building to the infill market.

ADUs, which TJH began incorporating into more floor plans around 2022 or 2023, nest within that strategy by helping owners get more out of limited space, a trend the builder calls “landmaxxing.” Luxury buyers increasingly want flex space, and as Washington has passed stronger ADU laws allowing more density, TJH has used the new rules to fit more square footage on its buyers’ lots.

For builders operating in dense urban and suburban markets, TJH’s playbook offers a case study in making every square foot of a lot count.

“It’s our job to work with buyers to figure out how to best maximize the building value on their land,” Rohde said in an interview. “Our view of ADUs is that it’s a great way to expand the amount of home you can build, which, in and of itself, is a good proposition.”

TJH’s ADU strategy varies by division and market. While other divisions build detached ADUs, the PNW division favors attached units, which function as independent living spaces connected to the main house or built within it.

Buyers use the extra space in varying ways, from a home office or gym to bonus square footage, a rental unit or a place for multigenerational living.

According to Rohde, attached or integrated ADUs make more sense than detached ADUs for two main reasons.

First, the company builds on smaller infill lots with limited outdoor space. A detached ADU cuts into that space more than an attached alternative.

“We’re typically acquiring lots that…already have limited yard space. So, to put a detached unit in, you’re then eating further into that ability to create a very livable yard,” Rohde explained.

Second, attached ADUs share walls with the primary residence, which reduces the amount of additional construction required. Unlike detached units, they don’t require a completely separate structure, additional siding or separate utility connections. That creates efficiencies and makes them more cost-effective, with a better return on investment.

“With the integrated system, you’re already having some shared wall space, so you’re already getting some economies of scale and efficiency that make it a lot more cost-effective than the detached-style units,” he explained.

Still, that efficiency doesn’t make ADUs a simple project, and Rohde argued against the notion that they are an easy add-on, particularly detached units.

“There’s this perception in the market, especially when the new regulations were hitting to allow the expansion of detached ADUs in particular, that, ‘Hey, it’s really easy. You just pick up a module, plop it in your backyard, and presto, you have additional living space,’” he said. “The reality is, it’s just frankly not that simple.”

He pointed to several reasons why ADUs can be challenging to build, including fire separation and building codes, utility connections, site access and logistics, zoning and permitting timelines and the burden of ongoing maintenance costs.

“What people found is that it’s not that simple. And then it became, ‘How do we integrate ADUs into a more thoughtful development process?’ I think we’re really seeing a lot of traction around that [strategy],” Rohde explained.

Washington House Bill 1337, passed in 2023, helped make TJH’s approach possible. Under the legislation, cities statewide must allow two ADUs per lot. The legislation requires municipalities to allow ADUs with a gross floor area of at least 1,000 square feet. That legislation made it far easier for TJH to offer an attached ADU in most of its Seattle floor plans.

The real density gain, according to Rohde, comes from how Seattle treats a home with an ADU as two units, which increases floor area ratio and lets TJH build more total square footage, including the integrated ADU, on the same lot within zoning limits.

To comply with the law, Seattle updated its ADU rules in 2025 to treat a home with an ADU as two units, which may allow additional floor area ratio (FAR) on the lot. That allows TJH to build more total square footage when including an integrated ADU.

“It allows us to build more home on a given lot,” Rohde said. “But it’s not just building square footage for the sake of building square footage. It’s building thoughtful, value-add square footage.”

Rohde also pointed to Washington House Bill 1096, passed in 2025, which requires qualifying cities to create an administrative process for splitting a residential lot in two. Cities are phasing it in ahead of the 2027 state deadline.

“That will allow existing landowners who want to extract some value from a large parcel that they own to split and sell. It will allow owners of larger lots to put two complete, independent single-family residences that will then have their own opportunities to enact the ADU laws and things of that nature. Those types of things are really going to help us drive value,” Rohde added.
