The Worst Part of the Trail: Shenandoah National Park

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

The one part of Virginia I most looked forward to was Shenandoah National Park. So many people had told me so many great things about the park before I got there. I expected breathtaking scenery; I expected it to be “cruisy;” I expected the waysides to have great blackberry milkshakes. I thought it was going to be a really wonderful time. My wife told me about her fond memories of camping there with her family, and my friend Peaks told me it had been his favorite part of the trail.

On the morning I woke up in Waynesboro at the Quality Inn, I had most of my gear ready to pack, but my clothes were hanging. I had not wanted to pay the $10 to use the coin machines, so I just scrubbed my clothes in the sink and hung them up to dry. Fortunately, everything was somehow dry by the time I woke up, and I got dressed, packed up, and went down to grab free hot breakfast, which included waffles, sausage, coffee, and orange juice. Way better than I was expecting from a budget hotel.

In the lobby, I saw BBC and Dance-On, who were going to hang around town for some extra time because BBC had a video chat for a job interview. He had shaved off his beard scruff and looked clean cut, which was unusual for most of the hikers I encountered, including myself. I wished him luck and grabbed my gear, expecting a pickup from my driver, Kevin, who had brought me in the previous day.

Kevin ran me back to the place he had picked me up at Rockfish Gap, telling me how wonderful Shenandoah National Park would be all along the way. He told me to make sure I took time to look at the signs and take pictures. My wife, wanting to relive fond memories, also wanted me to send her pictures of the park.

Can’t forget to take a picture of the map

I stepped out of the car and bid Kevin goodbye, thanking him for the ride, and I went back to the trail, stopping to look at the map and informational sign at the entrance. Shenandoah required a backwoods permit, which I had purchased online the day before in my hotel, but I had no physical copy of it, so I kept a PDF handy on my phone in case a ranger asked for it. In all my time there, none of them ever did.

The trail into Shenandoah started off with some pleasant views as I passed through sparsely wooded areas with younger trees. The weather was pleasantly cool, and spring flowers that had come and gone in the previous sections of the trail were only beginning to appear there due to cooler heights.

As midday approached, I heard a voice in the distance. It was a woman yelling, “STOP!” I became very concerned and moved ahead toward the sound, thinking I may have to intervene on some sort of abuse or phone for help. As I came closer, the periodic bursts of yelling grew louder, and I heard it again, “STOP!” followed by “Shut up!” and “We’re here for YOU!” I got closer and reached a crossing to another trail where I saw the back of a woman with a large pack and a small dog on a leash. She had turned down the side trail from the AT, periodically screaming angrily, and, as best I could tell, it was directed at her dog. As it became clear that my initial impression of her distress was mistaken, I continued on fast again, this time trying to get away from her and her outbursts.

Not long after, I crossed Skyline Drive, which was the continuation of the scenic Blue Ridge Parkway I had been criss-crossing for several days prior, reaching another scenic overlook which did not disappoint. I stopped there to have lunch and enjoy the view—a habit I had formed while passing similar overlooks in the afternoon every day. Another hiker passed by, pausing to look at the view before continuing.

View from the first overlook on Skyline Drive.

I greeted him and got his trail name, Banshee. He was a small-built young man with a lip ring and an ultralight pack. I asked if he had heard the lady shouting, and we speculated about who she was yelling at. He thought she had been walking a cat instead of a dog, but I was pretty certain I had seen a small-breed dog when I passed her.

He saw me putting a Liquid IV into my water, and asked which kind it was. He recommended the Firecracker flavor, which was his favorite, but said he also liked the lemon lime that I was using. After a few moments of chatting, he got on his way and I finished my lunch.

I hiked on for a while until I reached my goal destination of Black Rock Hut at just over 20 miles for the day. The campsites around the shelter were uneven and a lot of them were already taken. I grabbed one right across from the shelter and put my tent up right away, expecting rain that evening. I was barely set up when the rain started, but it didn’t last long. I lay on my sleeping pad for a bit before I had the motivation to get up and start making dinner at the picnic table next to the shelter.

Though there was a fire pit, the park had an ongoing fire ban. The shelter crowd picked up, and I saw my new friend Banshee along with a guy from New York City named Raja, and an older man who went by Chestnut. Someone asked him how he got his name, and he sighed. He explained that he had told another hiker some facts about the American chestnut tree, and that earned him his name. One of the other hikers made the mistake of asking him what he had told them to get the name, and we all got a full lecture on the history of the tree and how it had been destroyed by an invasive fungus, but they were trying to cross-breed it with another species of chestnut tree to get a resistant breed.

The crowd was good—a mix of section hikers with a few thru-hikers. I enjoyed the conversations and got plenty of unsolicited camping advice from Chestnut, but when the rain sprinkles picked up, I ended up going back to my tent to call it a night.

The next morning was foggy, cold, and damp. I packed up my wet tent, which had leaves and debris stuck to it, and I put my Frogg Toggs suit on before making coffee and breakfast. I accidentally burned a hole in the sleeve of the rain jacket with my stove, so I fished the duct tape out of my pack to patch it before I went on.

The fog remained for most of the morning, and the rain continued on and off throughout the day. Every time I came to a scenic view or an overlook, there wasn’t much to see but a wall of cloud. The cool weather was no fun, but at least it only bothered me when I stopped moving. Despite my best effort, my socks and shoes got wet, and my morale dropped from there.

Around midday I reached one of the Waysides with a camp store that sold food and snacks, boasting a bathroom, plumbing, and other amenities. I got there and saw someone inside, so I thought it must be open, but I was informed that they were preparing the facility to open the next day. I was able to fill up my water and throw away my trash in their bear-proof cans, so it wasn’t a total loss, but it was still unfortunate timing on an already rough day. Banshee showed up behind me and decided to hang around for a while so he could recharge his phone on the outlet that was accessible outside.

At the end of the day I reached another 20+ mile day at Hightop Hut, opting to stay in the shelter to avoid messing with my dirty, wet tent. A man hiking with his preteen child stopped by the shelter while I was eating and said hi, asking if I knew of a good place for them to set up their tent. Once they left, I didn’t hear from them for the rest of the night. The rain continued in sprinkles and picked up again during the night, leaving everything soaked when I got up in the morning.

I got ready under the shelter’s overhang as best I could, not seeing the father and young son who had camped nearby the night before. Once I got going, wearing my full rain suit, the rain continued to pick up. I had my gloves on, and they helped keep my hands warm, but they got wet along with everything else I was wearing. My Frogg Toggs rain suit kept me warm and insulated, as I’d already discovered, but it did little to keep me dry; I would either sweat from the extra heat or the rain would get through at the cuffs and neck and pool in the sleeves. I found it was usually best to wear fewer layers under it.

As the day continued, I found that most of the views were obscured by clouds and there was not much to see. I trudged through muddy ground, and between the rain and the fog, I found very little to enjoy that day, but I knew I was going to run across the Big Meadows Wayside and that drove me on. I had banked on being able to resupply there and I really wanted to try one of the famous blackberry milkshakes.

While most scenic overlooks that day were a bust, I did come to one where I had a nice view from above the clouds with the mountain peaks poking out over them. I paused to snap a pic before pushing on.

For all the complaining I did about the fog, at least this was a cool scene.

Eventually, I came to the wayside. I was drenched, so I stood outside, peeling off my layers, and I set my gear by the door, hoping it would dry out a little before I had to put it all back on. There were already wet floor signs posted, assuaging my guilt for dripping as I walked the aisles to shop.

The resupply at the Big Meadows Wayside was not as good as the FarOut comments had made it sound. Having lived in Japan and tasted good ramen for several years, I was reluctant to eat instant ramen, and it was the one popular trail food item that I had avoided. Unfortunately, it was the only carbohydrate option at the wayside. And without tuna packets, Spam singles were the lone packable protein. I loaded up with the best food items I could find and bought some postcards to send to my grandparents. I also got one for my wife, thinking if I wasn’t going to get good photos during my rainy walk through Shenandoah, then I would at least send her a postcard of a nice spring view. The older lady at the counter said they could send the postcards for me once I had them filled out.

I ordered lunch at the restaurant and filled out postcards, apologizing for all the water I dripped everywhere. My stamps had somehow gotten wet in their protective plastic bag and a few of them were ruined, but I had just enough to put on all the postcards. My food and milkshake came out, and the blackberry milkshake, though pricey, proved to be worth the stop, even if the resupply was disappointing.

When I finished my food, I went to the checkout counter and asked the clerk if she could mail my postcards. She seemed uncertain. “The other lady I spoke with earlier said it would be no problem,” I said. She went to ask her manager, and then came back out and took the postcards. I wasn’t confident that they would get sent or arrive after the younger clerk’s hesitation, but I later found out that everyone had received them.

With a heavy heart, I walked back out and started putting my wet layers back on. Nothing had dried at all. Now that it no longer had the warmth of my body heat on it, it was especially awful to put back on.

No sooner had I got situated, shivering in my cold, wet gear, than Dance-On and BBC walked up to the door of the wayside. “We got a hostel for the night,” Dance-On said.

I had not been aware that there were any good options for hostels in Shenandoah. I didn’t see any listed on FarOut at least, and I had not expected to leave the park until I walked out of it. As cold and miserable as I was, though, I considered this new information. “Do you think they have room for one more?” I asked.

Dance-On checked with the hostel and also with the shuttle driver, and before long I was no longer going to stay another cold wet night in the park.

After Dance-On and BBC got some food, I followed them to the pickup spot, where we met Nelson, their shuttle driver, and got a ride to Small Axe Farms.

The hostel was a bit of a drive from Shenandoah National Park, and the accommodations were humble. It was simply a barn, and you could hear the sheep and goats on the lower level, who got much more vocal at their feeding time. Kelly and Alec, the owners, showed us around and got us situated. I was able to set out my tent to dry, and I was hoping to brush off some of the dirt and leaf litter after it dried. Unfortunately, another rain shower hit right after I set it out.

Kelly collected laundry from all the guests, promising to return it by the following morning. I threw my gloves in with everything else, hoping to get it all dried more than washed. When it was my turn for a shower, I had been warned that the hot water was going out, but I actually got a good hot shower, which made an incredible difference.

There were several other guests I recognized at the hostel, including Raja and Chestnut. Most of them gathered at dinner, which Kelly made with heaping portions of pork chops and potatoes, allowing us all to eat our fill, and she even served dessert after.

When it was time for bed, the arrangement had been for everyone to use their own sleeping pads and blankets, so I was prepared for that, but one of the sofas was open, so I ended up sleeping on that. I missed the part where Kelly had told us to use sheets and blankets from their chest in the barn if I was going to sleep on their furniture, but one of the other guests was quick to let me know when I started putting my own sleeping quilt on the sofa. Once settled, I drifted off to sleep, warm, snug, clean, and fed, barely noticing that I was in a barn with animals and no climate control.

The next morning, I got up and checked on my tent, only to see that it was gone. I was afraid it had blown away, but I asked Alec, who told me that Kelly had brought it in because of the rain. Kelly served breakfast on her own schedule, which was much later than I liked, but, again, the meal was good and plentiful. The laundry bags began to come out, and I gathered my gear as best I could.

When Kelly gave me my tent back, it was clean and neatly folded. She had washed off all the dirt and debris for me, and dried it in her basement. I was incredibly grateful as I packed everything back into my pack and prepared to take off back to Shenandoah with my friends Dance-On and BBC.

After another bit of a drive with Nelson, we got dropped off at the same spot where he had picked us up. There, it came down to the classic debate of AT thru-hiking purity: Dance-On and BBC were already planning to road walk, which many hikers recognize as the best way to experience Shenandoah. I, however, wanted to get back to the white blazes of the official Appalachian Trail. I knew that I was going to end up skipping a small section since we had gone to the parking area to meet the driver, and I had no intention of backtracking to the wayside and finding the trail from there. The way I took ended up being about a mile shorter than if I had stayed on the trail. The way Shenandoah had been treating me, I actually didn’t mind skipping a small section of it. I didn’t even mind missing the mileage; I more than made up for it when I did the stairs of the approach trail twice back at Amicalola Falls.

The best part about getting back to Shenandoah was that the rain finally let up. I was starting to see some of the views and scenery that the park was well known for. However, it was still cool, and I realized I had forgotten to find my gloves in the laundry pile. I tried to make arrangements with Kelly to get them mailed home so I could maybe get a hold of them later. As of this writing, I still have not received them.

At the hostel, someone had told me that this would be a historic day for the park because King Charles and Camilla were going to tour the park as part of their official visit. Their schedule did not interfere with my hike, but I did run into some people whose planned day hike section was closed off.

With the rain gone, I got to see some great clear views, and there were plenty of spring flowers in bloom. I came to another wayside for lunch, meeting with Dance-On and BBC again. We planned to eat at the wayside restaurant, but it wasn’t going to open for an hour or so, so we all got coffee and sandwiches at the kiosk in the lobby.

I split from them after I finished eating, and got back on my way, planning to stop at a campsite where the AT crossed the Thornton River Trail because it was good mileage, falling between two shelters that were not far enough or too far. However, I saw from FarOut that there were some nuisance deer in the area of the campsite. The comments were actually kind of funny because there seemed to be a back-and-forth between people who favored chasing off the deer vs people who thought the deer were a delight.

Having lived in Japan for several years, I had memories of my visit to Nara Park with my wife. One of the tourist attractions at Nara Park is buying rice crackers to feed the deer, who will bow to you to get a cracker. However, as with any wild animals habituated to being fed by humans, the deer became aggressive if you weren’t giving them food, and after my wife ran out of crackers, one of the deer actually bit me because it thought I had more crackers in my pocket.

The situation at the campsite was similar, but fortunately it was a lone deer instead of hundreds of them like at Nara. However, the deer was persistent and aggressive. I set up my tent and the deer appeared shortly after. As soon as I opened my food bag, it was trying to stick its head in, so I chased it off, yelling and clapping. The deer did not go far, though, and it came back as soon as it saw that I was relaxed, hanging around and begging like an obnoxious dog.

Banshee walked by soon after, noting the deer and tsk-tsking over its obvious habituation. He went a little farther down the trail and found a campsite just out of sight of mine, but I don’t think he had the deer problem.

I finished eating and tucked in for my last night in Shenandoah. The next day I had a reservation for Mountain Home B&B, where I hoped to get a ride into town for a more suitable resupply before I started the final section of Virginia.

The deer was gone when I awoke, but it materialized, as if summoned, as soon as I started making breakfast. I got back onto the trail and ran into Dance-On and BBC again, telling them about my B&B reservation. They had been unable to reserve online because there seemed to be a shortage of bunks in the hiker room. I suggested that they come along, and we could see if they could get rooms in person.

I found my way out of Shenandoah National Park in the afternoon, reflecting that, despite how much I’d looked forward to it, I was incredibly relieved to be done with it. Between obnoxious hikers and campers, bad weather, and pesky animals, all the hype going in turned out to be a major letdown. Yet a part of me felt that maybe I had unfinished business with the park; that I would have to come back and see it all again on a better day. Reflecting on some of the cool people I met and looking through my photos of Shenandoah, I am all the more certain that it deserves another chance.
