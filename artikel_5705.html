Energy Drink's Surprising Ingredient Likely Sparked Severe Reaction

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

— The drink contained fish collagen, which may be an under-recognized allergen

A 15-year-old boy with eczema, asthma, idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis, and allergic rhinitis developed widespread hives, wheezing, and difficulty breathing within minutes of trying a new energy drink.

The energy drink contained 5 grams of fish collagen derived from cod, which is suspected to be the culprit behind the teen's anaphylaxis, reported Annabelle Rowland, MD, a resident physician at the Emory University School of Medicine in Atlanta, at the American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology (ACAAI) 2026 Annual Scientific Meeting in Phoenix.

The patient first presented to the emergency department where he was treated with two doses of intramuscular epinephrine as well as albuterol, cetirizine, and isotonic fluids until his symptoms subsided. He told doctors that he was in his usual state of health earlier that day and hadn't exercised or taken nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.

In the past, the teen had noticed mild throat itching and lip swelling when he ate canned tuna, but he was able to eat salmon with no issues. He reported tolerating the "big 9" allergens. Of note, the patient developed localized hives after previous exposure to chlorhexidine wash.

After the emergency department, the teen had skin prick testing in the allergy clinic. He was negative for tuna, cod, and flounder but positive for environmental allergens. Rowland noted the patient's working diagnosis is an allergy to fish collagen, though the patient was lost to follow-up, and therefore confirmatory with testing with fresh cod was not done.

"Standard fish allergy tests mostly look for a different fish protein and contain very little collagen, so a person can test negative and still react," Rowland told MedPage Today. She explained that most commercially available serum testing for fish allergy is parvalbumin-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE), whereas "fish collagen is a cross-reactive protein across multiple fish species."

She noted that some studies have shown that most patients who are sensitized to parvalbumin are also sensitized to fish collagen, but there was a small subset of patients who only showed sensitization to the latter. Other research has demonstrated that some fish-allergic patients may have significant symptoms to as little as 1 mg of protein.

Fish collagen is not water-soluble and may not be present in sufficient amounts to elicit a positive test from standardized skin-prick extracts, the researchers noted. Thus, patients with fish collagen allergy may be at risk for false-negative testing with both standard serum and skin-prick tests.

"This case shows how tricky fish allergy can be," Rowland said in a press release, adding that fish-based collagen is popping up in a growing number of foods and supplements.

Rowland said the takeaway is that it's important to read ingredient labels closely as more unexpected protein sources are added to products.

Zachary Rubin, MD, a pediatric allergist who practices near Chicago, told MedPage Today that most would not associate energy drinks with fish allergens, but with fish-derived collagen popping up in drinks, supplements, and more, there are increased opportunities for unexpected exposure.

To Rubin, this case illustrates that "allergists sometimes need to investigate ingredients that might not be immediately obvious" and that "a negative standard allergy test doesn't always tell the whole story."
