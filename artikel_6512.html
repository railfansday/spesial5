Security Footage Catches Two Zoo Gorillas Forming a “Living Ladder” To Outsmart an Electric Fence

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Two zoo gorillas repeatedly teamed up for food despite rarely spending time together, showing that a shared effort does not always end with a shared meal.

A leafy branch was out of reach for two gorillas at the Ramat Gan Safari, and an electric barrier around the trunk blocked a direct climb. The female western lowland gorillas, half-sisters Amelia and Lisha, formed a “living ladder,” with Lisha providing a steady back for Amelia to stand on.

The most extensively documented event came to light in September 2024, when keepers noticed the pair peacefully eating bark and leaves from a large branch that had previously been beyond their reach. Staff reviewed security camera footage to find out how they had obtained it.

The recording showed Amelia walking over and climbing onto a rock beneath a Peltophorum dubium tree. Lisha joined her and stood firmly on all fours on the rock. Amelia then climbed onto Lisha’s back, stood upright, and reached up to grasp and break off a branch approximately 4.5 meters, or 15 feet, above the ground. The rock, Lisha’s stable posture, and Amelia’s reach together made the branch accessible.

Researchers documented three events involving the same pair in 2021, 2024, and 2025, with each gorilla performing the same role every time. Neither had been trained to perform the maneuver. The team describes their behavior as a rare example of spontaneous cooperation between adult gorillas, with a clear division of roles.

Published in Primates, the study was led by Michael Levitzky and Prof. Israel Hershkovitz of the Department of Anatomy and Anthropology at the Gray Faculty of Medical and Health Sciences and the Dan David Center for Human Evolution and Biohistory Research at Tel Aviv University.

Yehonatan Samocha, Head of the Primate Section at the Ramat Gan Safari (Zoological Center Tel Aviv-Ramat Gan), said, “At the Ramat Gan Safari we have observed and studied animal behaviors for years, closely following their relationships, social bonds, and fascinating capabilities. Particularly among great apes, including gorillas, we repeatedly encounter behaviors that demonstrate high intelligence, learning ability, problem-solving, complex communication, and cooperation.

“This is why we are so thrilled, though not necessarily surprised, by the cooperation between Amelia and Lisha. We are familiar with their abilities and understand how intelligent and curious they are, and how capable they are of learning from one another and working together. Sometimes, our surprise tells us more about our expectations of animals than their actual abilities. The study’s great importance lies in providing scientific documentation and analysis for observations and behaviors we encounter in our daily work, allowing us to enhance our knowledge of gorillas, their social world, and their cognitive abilities.”

In the June 2025 footage, Lisha positioned herself with her back toward Amelia, who was not observed using force. Levitzky, who initiated and led the study, explained how the recording helped the researchers interpret Lisha’s participation.

“When I first watched the footage, it was clear to me that this was something extraordinary. What I find fascinating is that Leisha did more than simply serve as a ‘step’ for Amelia. She appeared to participate actively and willingly in solving the problem, approaching on her own initiative and positioning herself in exactly the right place, without Amelia physically guiding her. This is the difference between using another individual as a tool and true cooperation, in which both individuals work together toward the same goal.

“For years, gorillas were considered less cognitively sophisticated than other great apes. As more observations like these accumulate, we will be able to deepen and refine our understanding of their social and cognitive abilities. Future studies involving systematic documentation and audio recordings could help us decipher how they coordinate with one another, and perhaps also teach us something about the roots of cooperation in our own species.”

The sisters generally spend little time together, sleep in separate night quarters, and rarely groom one another. Their teamwork also had different outcomes at feeding time. Although keepers reported peaceful sharing after the 2024 event, Amelia alone ate the vegetation in the observed 2025 sequence, showing that cooperating to obtain food did not necessarily lead to sharing it.

Hershkovitz said, “Our observations reveal how two gorillas can join forces and assume complementary roles to solve a problem that neither can solve on her own. It is particularly intriguing that this cooperation recurs even though the two do not usually display much closeness, and even when the food is not shared between them afterward. These observations are important for understanding human evolution because they offer an explanation, albeit a partial one, for how we survived on the savanna – through social support and a high level of cooperation that can occur even without language or preplanning. Although the number of observations is small, they offer a window into gorillas’ ability to learn from experience, coordinate their actions, and draw on one another and their surroundings to achieve a shared goal.”

The study involved only two gorillas and three documented events. The available footage could not establish whether they communicated through vocalizations or subtle gestures, or whether they planned their actions in advance.

Reference: “Cooperation in adult female western lowland gorillas: complex problem-solving through learned spatial affordances and mutual agency” by Michael Levitzky, and Israel Hershkovitz, 24 October 2026, Primates. DOI: 10.1007/s10329-026-01297-6
