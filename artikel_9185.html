How to Motivate Children for Success in School

Parents in America and around the world agree on this: We want our children to succeed in school. Intense pressure for academic achievement now dominates the lives of many American families.

American students, however, are not doing well. Perhaps even more concerning, they seem to have less interest in learning. High school and college teachers across the country now assign less reading to their students, and students still complain that this is more than they can manage. Many American high school and college students no longer read books!

There are several possible causes for our children's loss of interest in learning, and different causes for different children. The impact of screens is certainly important. Children no longer have the attention span for reading. Instead, they are scrolling and watching short videos. They may even believe that, in this way, they are learning.

1. Express enthusiastic interest in your child’s interests, even if these are not the interests you would choose. Learning begins with interest. Children's instinctive interest and curiosity about the world are the starting point for their motivation and enjoyment of learning. As soon as children begin to talk, they are constantly asking questions, seeking information, and wanting to know “why.” Too often, in our understandable effort to help improve their skills, we neglect this essential aspect of children’s motivation.

Make note of moments of interest and talk with your children about what interests them. Ask questions. Do "research" together. Help them develop their interests into projects and goals.

The promise of rewards or the threat of punishment may lead to some short-term improvement in a child’s effort. But rewards and punishments cannot create interest, enjoyment, or long-term goals

2. Find the source of their anxiety, frustration, and discouragement. Parents often ask, "Why doesn’t he put more effort into his schoolwork? Why doesn’t he care?” The answer to these questions is almost always, “Because he is discouraged.”

Children may mask their discouragement with defiant or rebellious attitudes, and they may pretend that they don’t care. But they do care. If we look hard enough, we will find in every child some interest and a desire to do well.

Undiagnosed attention and learning disorders are a common source of discouragement and lack of sustained effort in children and adolescents. It is essential for parents and teachers to understand the impact of these difficulties. Even mild or moderate learning problems can be a source of anxiety, frustration, and low self-esteem.

3. Make note of every improvement, not every mistake. To solve the problem of discouragement, children need more encouragement and less criticism. Of course, to improve their skills, children need criticism. But they need encouragement even more. Encouragement is not false praise. We all need encouragement, even as adults, with every new challenge and skill we attempt to master.

4. Help them cultivate a growth mindset. Praise effort, not intelligence or talent. Praise learning, not grades. Then, on this foundation, we can set high standards and challenge children to work harder. But we will have to do this less often, because they will more often challenge themselves.

5. Appreciate their strengths. Students who seem unmotivated are often focused on their weaknesses, not on their strengths.

When I talk with “unmotivated” children, I often find qualities of intelligence, creativity, effort, and character that their parents and teachers have overlooked and that they may also fail to appreciate. When I point out these qualities, many children and adolescents feel deeply understood. For some, this is a transformative moment. Years later, these affirming statements are what they remember and value most about our work.

6. Make learning meaningful. Individual achievement alone—getting good grades in school—is a fragile source of motivation and effort, with a high cost in anxiety and stress. If we define achievement, however, as making a contribution to our families, our communities, our country, and the world, we offer children a much better goal and standard for success. We should help them become involved in projects that help their community, and we should be involved with them.

7. Have conversations. Every moment of dialogue with our children and grandchildren adds meaning and relevance to learning. Talk with your children about what they are studying—about civics and history, science, literature, and religion—and the importance of these subjects to our current lives. Talk with them about how others help us, about the good work that many people are doing, and what they can do to help others.

Conversation is a (partial) antidote to the addictive pull of screens. Before you ask, “Have you done your homework?” ask them about what they have been learning in school.

8. Establish "library hours" in your home. Even when children are highly motivated to do well in school, they still need to establish good work habits. They need to put away their devices, do their homework, and get enough sleep.

I encourage parents to set aside a specified time, early in the evening, as a homework or “library” hour. During the library hour, all electronics should be turned off (except for computers or tablets children need for schoolwork) for the entire family. No television, no video games, no phones.

Homework should be done in a communal place (as in a library), at the kitchen or dining room table. Especially with younger children, parents are present and available to help, offer encouragement, and answer questions. Then, when homework is done, there is time for play.

Most families have found these guidelines helpful for students of all ages. The result is an important lesson in self-discipline, fewer family arguments, and more success in school.
