5 things to watch for in Tuesday’s midterm elections

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Midterm elections are almost always referendums on the party in power. That’s especially true this year, when President Donald Trump has repeatedly directed Republican voters to “pretend that I’m on the ballot.”

But Trump’s sagging poll numbers, driven by cost-of-living concerns and an unpopular war, have helped create a political environment that favors Democrats.

Inside Elections with Nathan L. Gonzales projects Democrats could net four to six seats in the Senate, a scenario considered highly unlikely at the start of the cycle. To succeed, Democrats need to flip seats in several red-leaning states, among them Alaska, Iowa and Kansas. The party would also need to hold its own vulnerable seats in New Hampshire, Minnesota and Georgia.

On the House side, the question seems to be not whether Democrats will take the majority, but rather how big their margin will be. The party needs a net gain of three seats; Inside Elections projects Democrats could wind up winning 20 to 30 seats, a significant number of them in districts that voted for Trump by double digits two years ago.

Here are five things we’ll be watching for as the votes are tallied:

Republicans appeared to win the redistricting wars that defined much of the 2026 election cycle, especially after the Supreme Court weakened the Voting Rights Act, which led to new maps in several Southern states. Democrats gained favorable lines in California and Utah, but Republicans redrew more seats in their favor. The overall GOP effort put new districts on the board in places such as Florida and Louisiana but also sought to boost Republicans’ chances of ousting longtime targets, including Reps. Marcy Kaptur of Ohio and Henry Cuellar and Vicente Gonzalez of Texas.

While some redrawn districts are clear pickups, many redrawn seats are considered Toss-ups, forcing the GOP to deploy significant resources. Still, if Republicans make the gains they hoped to make in places such as Florida and Texas, it could go a long way in helping them either somehow keep the majority or narrow a Democratic one.

Historic gains among Latino voters helped Trump win the White House in 2024 and boosted GOP candidates in swing districts across the nation. Those victories also influenced Texas Republicans’ redraw of the state’s congressional map, betting that Latino voters would stick with the party. But anger over the president’s immigration policies, coupled with economic anxiety, has driven Trump’s approval numbers among Latinos to historic lows.

Across the South, the Trump-led redistricting push that dismantled House districts with sizable Black populations has fired up Black voters in states such as North Carolina and Louisiana. Black voters could also be key to determining the winner of Michigan’s hard-fought Senate race.

Democrats’ initial midterm target list, released in April 2025, included a few Republican incumbents who represented seats that Trump had carried by double digits the previous year. The number of “reach” battleground races in traditionally red terrain that fit that description has only grown since then. One of the biggest questions heading into election night is how many of those districts Democrats will be competitive in. Wins in those districts would translate into a larger Democratic majority or, at the very least, could help offset losses by Democratic incumbents in redrawn seats.

But flipping these districts would put a bull’s-eye on the Democratic winners, since their seats would likely rank among Republicans’ best pickup opportunities in 2028.

At the start of the midterm cycle, few could have predicted that the Senate battlefield would run through states that Trump carried by double digits in 2024. But concerns over rising prices driven by Trump’s tariff policy and the war in Iran have put several red-state seats in play. In the farm belt states of Iowa and Kansas, soaring diesel and fertilizer costs have GOP candidates on defense. In South Carolina, a divisive Republican primary and an untested first-time candidate have made the race unexpectedly competitive. And in Ohio, the return of a Democratic former senator, coupled with a national environment that is increasingly inhospitable to the GOP, suggests the state could shift from red to purple.

Recent surveys have shown Democrats with the advantage heading into Election Day and Trump’s job approval rating underwater, meaning more voters disapprove of the job he’s doing than approve. All signs point to Democrats retaking the House and potentially winning the Senate majority. That wouldn’t be unexpected — after all, the president’s party almost always loses seats in midterm elections. But polls have been wrong before. In August, some surveys predicted progressive Abdul El-Sayed would notch a double-digit win over Rep. Haley Stevens in Michigan’s Democratic Senate primary, but El-Sayed wound up winning by less than a point. Throughout the campaign, Republicans have cautioned against reading too much into the polls, asserting that they typically undercount GOP voters.
