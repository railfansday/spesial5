Small businesses drive the economy. Who is financing their growth?

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Small businesses drive much of U.S. job growth but often struggle to qualify for bank loans. Colin Kilgour sees an opening for private credit.

This article has been reviewed by the Quartz editorial team. The content may be licensed for reuse.

When a business looks to expand its operations or invest in new equipment, its growth often depends on access to the right financing. Across America, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that from 2013 to 2023, small businesses contributed 55% of the country’s total net job creation. Between March 2023 and March 2024, they accounted for 88.9% of net job growth, according to the U.S. Small Business Administration’s Office of Advocacy. These figures highlight the important role small businesses play in supporting the economy. Yet, these small businesses that increasingly help the economy grow are the ones still struggling to get the support that they need.

According to a report released by the U.S. Chamber of Commerce, small businesses account for between 43.5% and 50.7% of U.S. GDP and generated nearly $5.9 trillion in 2014, the most recent year available. This further illustrates how essential small businesses are to the broader economy of America. However, their economic importance may not always translate to easier access to financial opportunities.

Traditionally, one of the main barriers to small business lending is the regulatory requirements that govern how banks allocate their lending capital. Different types of loans carry different risks. Small businesses may be at a disadvantage, since their loans generally carry higher regulatory risk ratings than other forms of lending.

Collateral is another challenge. Many small businesses may not be equipped with the collateral banks typically require, such as commercial property, expensive equipment, or outstanding receivables. Processing these loans can also be costly. Since smaller loans often generate less revenue but require almost the same amount of work as larger loans, banks may find them less profitable to offer.

For Colin Kilgour, co-founder and principal of Kilgour Williams Capital, these challenges present an opportunity for investors within the private credit market. Through his work in credit and structured finance, Kilgour has seen how traditional lending practices can leave many small businesses neglected and disregarded.

Colin Kilgour, co-founder and principal of Kilgour Williams Capital.

He views the main challenge as helping small businesses secure the funding they need. “Many businesses need money to expand, renovate, or grow their operations, but struggle to qualify for traditional bank loans,” he says. He adds that some do not have the assets banks require as security, while others need smaller loans that banks may be less willing to provide.

Kilgour identifies these barriers as an opportunity for private credit to serve businesses that are often overlooked. He noted that relying solely on conventional lending requirements ought to be reconsidered. Private credit providers can look into assessing the business’s cash flow and ability to repay. Overall, his view is to offer another perspective that creates a potential source of financing for business owners who need capital to support their growth.

“Technology has changed how these businesses can be assessed,” he says. Kilgour points to open banking, which allows lenders to review a business’s financial activity. Modern financial technology, in addition, makes it possible to evaluate records and inspect business operations remotely. He emphasizes that technological advancement allows lenders to assess a business’s financial position remotely and more easily. These tools can help lenders make faster and more informed lending decisions.

In Kilgour’s view, loans can help fund expansion, equipment, or renovations while allowing owners to retain control of their businesses. The opportunity, however, also comes with risks. He recognizes that small businesses do not carry the same credit profiles as larger, investment-grade companies, which means lenders must account for potential losses and build diversified portfolios.

Kilgour sees these challenges as part of why the small-business private credit market still has room to grow. He believes the market may expand significantly as more business owners become aware of financing options beyond traditional banks. For him, the opportunity is not limited to large companies or major transactions. It may also lie in supporting the smaller businesses that contribute to the wider economy but have historically had fewer financing options.

“Private credit connects businesses with needed capital while opening opportunities for investors in an underserved market,” he says. In his view, investors should look beyond large corporate lending to Main Street businesses. By assessing risk carefully and finding more effective ways to provide capital, private credit can help connect underserved businesses with the financing they need to grow.
