The Law of Reversed Effort

Posted November 4, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

For the better part of this decade, I suffered from a severe case of insomnia. Initially, it had biological origins. After coming down with COVID early in the pandemic, the virus triggered a rare autoimmune condition in me called Morvan syndrome. Morvan’s gift to its victims is profound, treatment-resistant insomnia (agrypnia excitata), and the syndrome can be fatal in up to 20 percent of reported cases. Fortunately (though it’s hard to diagnose), once it’s identified, it’s quite treatable. Unfortunately, prolonged insomnia can develop a learned psychological component, even after the original biological problem improves, so my sleepless nights continued even after IVIG immunotherapy resolved most of the other consequences of the condition.

So, as behavioral scientists often do, I was determined to solve my problem through intellect and science. I bought an Oura Ring and started tracking my sleep. I learned everything I could about sleep hygiene. I hired a sleep coach, therapist, and hypnotherapist. I got a silly pair of glasses and bought all the supplements. I even started injecting myself with peptides purported to help with sleep. Like so many who suffer from insomnia, I just wanted out of it. I jumped the shark when I bought a machine to freeze my prefrontal cortex, a machine so loud my poor wife was now not able to sleep either.

After more than five years of suffering, one little trick made it stop: I decided to stop caring. To be clear, I didn’t suddenly abandon my healthy sleep habits; I just stopped treating good sleep as something I needed to achieve.

If you follow my work, you know Alan Watts has been a significant influence in what I do. Although the law of reversed effort didn’t originate with him, he helped popularize similar ideas through his writing about Taoism and effortless action. Watts even described Lao-tzu as a master of the law of reversed effort.

Despite the name, the law of reversed effort is not a scientific law per se, in the same sense as something found in empirical disciplines. Rather, it’s a psychological idea that there are certain outcomes where increasing conscious effort begins to work against us. Sleep has emerged as the textbook example of this concept, but it shows up in a lot of places. For instance, the paradox of hedonism is the idea that the best way to become unhappy is to chase happiness (the foundation of my book, The Fun Habit). Or the ironic monitoring process, a topic extensively explored by psychologist Daniel Wegner, where in the pursuit of peace of mind, you inadvertently train yourself to subconsciously always scan for threats, therefore making peace of mind elusive.

Here’s how Wegner explains the monitoring process: When we deliberately try to create or suppress a mental state, two processes get activated. One tries to produce the outcome we want; the other monitors whether we are succeeding. Unfortunately, that monitoring process must keep looking for evidence of failure. For instance, if your goal is to stop feeling anxious, some part of your attention must continually ask, “Am I still anxious?”

In my case, with the goal of desperately wanting to fall asleep, my brain (unintentionally) was trained to keep noticing whether I was still awake. In fact, there’s even a sleep treatment that uses this paradox and reverses it. “Paradoxical intention” asks people with insomnia to let go of the effort to force sleep and, in some versions, gently try to stay awake instead. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that paradoxical intention improved important insomnia outcomes and reduced sleep-related performance anxiety (although the authors acknowledged that stronger research in this area is still needed).

For those of us like me who enjoy living in health metrics and data, optimization runs the risk of making problems worse. With sleep trackers now becoming ubiquitous, the problem has become so prevalent that researchers now have a term for it: orthosomnia. But hyperfocusing on a personal problem is hardly unique to sleep.

Both of my parents were professors who lived through the publish-or-perish era. Now that I better understand the pressures of that time, I’ve forgiven them for never being around. However, because there is a lot of residual pain from their absence, I’ve always felt a sense of duty to ensure that my children have all the access to me they need.

So, I read all the good parenting books and followed all the parenting influencers on social media; I tried to be the best parent I could be at every step. But beneath all those good intentions, a practice of rumination and a small “sub-agent of evaluation” began to emerge, framing our interactions as performative. In other words, every interaction with my kids was also an opportunity to collect data. Did I handle that correctly? Was I present enough? And even though I hate to admit this one: Do they like me?

Serendipitously, when my sleep miraculously improved, it dawned on me pretty quickly that I could apply the law of reversed effort to other areas of my life. So, I stopped worrying about being a good father (important to note, I didn’t stop being a good father). I was able to pull this off because I benefited from the confidence of knowing I had enough reps in to trust that the one-off disagreements and difficult afternoons were not a referendum. And once I stopped keeping score, something magical happened: I became more available to what was actually happening. The result is we all started having a lot more fun.

I can already see fans of Ryan Holiday’s work roll their eyes, so let me state the obvious: The law of reversed effort can be problematic if taken too far.Folks who truly know me understand that my argument is never for passivity or living a life of whimsy. On the contrary, direct effort works well for countless things. If you want to become stronger, you need to lift weights. If you want to learn a musical instrument, you need to practice. If you want deeper relationships, you need to put in the time.The important distinction to be aware of is between actions we can perform and states we’re trying to force, as well as becoming comfortable with not being wedded to an outcome. I can create a supportive environment in which to go to bed. I cannot command myself to sleep. I can listen carefully and be present with my children. I cannot force them to be endearing at every turn. I can flex my agency and autonomy to create time for enjoyable experiences. I can only order myself to become happy at my own peril.

Here’s a question I find myself asking more and more these days: Is this something I can directly affect? Or is this something more likely to emerge if I create the right conditions?

If you’ve gotten this far, I hope you see it was never about caring less. It’s about recognizing when your level of caring has quietly turned into an anxiety trap, because you’re trying to control something that is uncontrollable.

Some things respond beautifully when we push harder. Others seem to require us to create the right conditions, loosen our grip, and then simply allow them to happen. Learning this hasn’t just helped me improve my sleep. It’s made my life better by freeing up energy to be fully present with those I love and cherish. I’m excited to see where else I can benefit from the law of reversed effort as I apply it to other areas of my life. Stay tuned.
