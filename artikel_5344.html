Revolut Tokenized Funds: 4 Private-Market Funds Head On-Chain. What Retail Investors Should Know

Revolut chair Martin Gilbert said on Tuesday, November 17, that the fintech plans to launch four tokenized funds, letting its customers buy small slices of private-market investments on a blockchain. He spoke at Solana’s Breakpoint conference in London.

The funds would hold assets such as private equity, which normally can’t be sold quickly, and use a blockchain to cut them into what Gilbert called “marketable sizes.”

Revolut has more than 80 million customers, so tokenized funds in its app could reach far more everyday savers than most tokenization projects do today. But the timing is awkward. We tracked the share prices of the four firms that already manage Revolut’s private-market funds. All four are down this year, by a median of 28%, while the S&P 500 is up 7%.

So can tokenized funds fix the problem Gilbert describes, getting money out of assets that are hard to sell?

Gilbert framed the plan around one problem: getting money back out. “In this world, there is liquidity until there is no liquidity,” he said. “If you can help people get liquidity in illiquid assets, you’re going to solve a lot of problems for a lot of people.”

Liquidity here means being able to sell quickly at a fair price. Private equity funds usually lock money up for years. Splitting a fund into tokens on a blockchain could, in theory, let investors trade those tokens with each other instead of waiting for the fund to pay them out.

The plan builds on a product Revolut launched on July 27. Its Private Markets section lets eligible European customers invest from €1 in funds run by Apollo, Ares, Hamilton Lane and Partners Group. Those funds use the EU’s ELTIF 2.0 rules, a framework that lets retail investors buy long-term funds. Subscriptions are generally monthly and redemptions quarterly. Holders can’t move them to another broker or sell them to other investors.

The four managers Revolut picked are some of the biggest names in private markets. Their share prices show how investors feel about the business right now.

Part of the worry is exactly the liquidity Gilbert talked about. In June, Partners Group capped withdrawals from an $8.6 billion fund after investors asked for 9.8% of its assets back, nearly double its 5% limit, Bloomberg reported. Apollo and Ares have also limited redemptions on some funds that promise investors regular access to their money.

That’s the case for tokenized funds, and also its limit. A token can make a fund easier to trade, but only if someone wants to buy it. When many holders want out at once, a token changes how a share is sold, not whether a buyer shows up.

Revolut wouldn’t be first. Apollo and Hamilton Lane already offer tokenized credit funds through Securitize, a company that issues fund shares as tokens on blockchains. Those products are mostly sold to institutions and wealthy investors with high minimums.

There is even a token linked to Revolut itself, though Revolut has nothing to do with it. $CREV, issued by Swiss firm Colb Finance on BNB Chain, tracks the value of Revolut shares before any stock market listing. It launched on May 28 with a $25,000 minimum for professional investors, and holders get price exposure only, with no voting rights. Revolut has not announced a date for listing its own shares.

What Revolut could change is scale. Moving funds like these into an app with 80 million users, at small ticket sizes, would test whether tokenized funds can work for ordinary savers.
