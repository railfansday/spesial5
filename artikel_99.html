The event is rolling into Bangkok, with performers in feathered capes and sequined corsets. Will it appeal to fans in a vast new market?

The Eurovision Song Contest will crown its first Asia champion in Bangkok on Saturday, in an event that will test whether a high-camp singing competition with an ardent fan base can appeal to audiences in a new market.

The inaugural Eurovision Song Contest Asia comprises artists and original songs from 11 countries: Bangladesh, Bhutan, Cambodia, Laos, Malaysia, Mongolia, Nepal, the Philippines, South Korea, Thailand and Vietnam. It will be live-streamed on YouTube at 8:30 p.m. local time (8:30 a.m. Eastern) and broadcast by television stations across the region.

Eurovision has been a fixture of the European cultural calendar since it began in 1956. Past winners include Sweden’s ABBA and the Canadian chanteuse Celine Dion, who represented Switzerland in 1988.

Asian artists have readily embraced the kitschy outfits and spectacular sets that are part of Eurovision tradition, with costumes that include traditional dress, as well as feathered capes and sequined corsets. Malaysia’s performer plans to enter the stage in a giant teardrop.

But the Eurovision format has not been tested in Asia. Fans and regional partners have expressed concern in recent weeks that budget constraints, production issues and weak audience demand could imperil the spinoff. Organizers say the contest could take time to build an audience.

“It would be profoundly arrogant to think that just because we have a heritage brand and a huge global audience for the European show, that we would walk in and demand it to be the same here,” said Martin Green, the European Broadcasting Union’s director of the Eurovision Song Contest. “Like any new thing, it needs to find its roots.”

Two previous attempts to take the competition to Asia were abandoned by the European Broadcasting Union, which owns Eurovision, and its partners in the region. A Eurovision spinoff in the United States, NBC’s American Song Contest, ran for one season in 2022.

The Asian edition kicking off on Saturday is far smaller than its European counterpart. Eurovision organizers said they were in talks with more countries to join future seasons. They include China, Japan and India, whose music industries have global reach.

“Bringing the world together through music is a fundamentally good idea,” Mr. Green said. “Europe shouldn’t keep it to itself.”

Inside the theater, fans are jostling for spots in front of the stage and near the so-called green room, where the artists will sit after their performances until the results are announced.

It’s less than an hour before showtime at the One Bangkok Forum, and crowds of fans have gathered outside the theater, taking photos, buying memorabilia and sneaking in a quick bite of dinner. Organizers have handed out cardboard signs with the performers’ images — I’ve been told that they’ve run out of South Korea and Thailand.

Francesca Regalado reported from Bangkok, where she will cover the Eurovision Song Contest Asia on Saturday.

The Eurovision Song Contest, Europe’s long-running singing competition, is expanding into Asia for the first time on Saturday, with singers from 11 countries vying for the title. The New York Times is covering it live.

This is the third attempt by the European Broadcasting Union to develop an Asian spinoff of the high-camp competition. With Bangkok as host city, it will test demand in Asia for an international competition based on original songs.

The Eurovision Song Contest Asia could either be Eurovision’s first successful foray into another region — or a one-off flop like NBC’s American Song Contest.

The Asian edition will have a far smaller pool of competitors than its European counterpart, which typically includes over 30 countries. But it will include South Korea and Thailand, whose pop songs have found international audiences.

The contest will be live-streamed on YouTube on Saturday night local time (Saturday morning Eastern) and broadcast by government-owned and commercial television stations in the 11 participating countries.

As in Europe, the winner will be determined by votes from a panel of judges and viewers from around the world.

Eurovision, which began in 1956 as an experiment in synchronized programming by public broadcasters across Europe, has grown into a weeklong spectacle with an international fan base. The competition’s 70th edition in May was won by Dara, a singer who represented Bulgaria, with the dance-pop hit “Bangaranga.”

Organizers have unsuccessfully tried to replicate the contest outside Europe. The American Song Contest, which pitted singers from 56 states and territories, ran for only one season in 2022. An Australian public broadcaster, SBS, called off its yearslong attempt to bring Eurovision to Asia in 2021.

This time, the Asian project got off the ground partly because pop music from countries like South Korea, Japan and Thailand has global appeal, said Assaf Blecher, the executive producer of the Eurovision Song Contest Asia.

“It’s the right time for something to happen, with the evolution of music from Asia and the curiosity of Eurovision fans to see what’s happening beyond Europe,” he said.

The 11 countries, in order of performance, are Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos, Mongolia, Bhutan, the Philippines, Bangladesh, Thailand, South Korea, Nepal and Malaysia.

Asia’s three largest media markets are conspicuously missing. Mr. Blecher said that broadcasters from China, India and Japan had been in talks to join the competition, but did not sign on in time to meet the deadline for entries.

To stage the competition in Asia, Eurovision had to overcome a fraught relationship with the continent’s broadcasters. In 2018, the European Broadcasting Union canceled a contract with China’s Mango TV, a provincial-government-owned streaming platform, after it censored elements of that year’s Eurovision show, including a gay love story depicted in an Irish singer’s performance.

Last year, China, India and Vietnam sent singers to Intervision, a Cold War-era song contest that Russia revived after it was expelled from Eurovision in 2022 over its invasion of Ukraine. A Vietnamese singer, Duc Phuc, won the contest with a song inspired by a mythical folk hero.

Each country will send a singer who won a national contest with an original song. For emerging singer-songwriters like Nepal’s Pratik Himal Rai and Bhutan’s Nyendra, winning their national contests was life-changing.

Others, like Malaysia’s Aina Abdul and Vietnam’s Toc Tien, are established pop stars with decades-long careers. Bangladesh’s entry, Beatmosphere, is a six-member a cappella group that formed in 2009.

The 11 acts will perform a wide range of genres: love ballads, a hip-hop track, a rock song and dance anthems. Some artists, like Thinla of Laos and Junhee of South Korea, will sing in English.

Each act will have three minutes to perform onstage at the One Bangkok Forum, a newly opened auditorium in central Bangkok that can seat around 5,000 people.

Once a performance is complete, a jury in each country will rank the songs. Those rankings will be converted into point scores for each singer, from zero to 12.

Public voting — in each country and among YouTube viewers watching internationally — will also be used to allocate points. The public and jury scores will then be combined to work out the winner.

Viewers from participating countries cannot vote for their own nation’s act.

A QR code will be shown onscreen to direct viewers to the Eurovision Asia voting website. Each user will have eight votes for free to distribute to at least three songs. They can purchase up to 10 extra votes.

The organizers said that they expect most viewers to watch the show from their phones and personal devices, not on television. Last year, the European contest's final competition received more than five million views on YouTube.

The winner of the Asian contest will be invited to perform at the European edition next year, though not as a competitor. And unlike in Europe, the winning country will not automatically be chosen to host next year.

Alex Marshall contributed reporting from London.
