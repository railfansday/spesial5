The NFL injury report explained: what questionable, doubtful and out mean

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

The NFL injury report explained, from questionable, doubtful and out designations to practice participation, the Friday report and game-day inactives, and why the labels matter

The NFL injury report is a league-mandated public disclosure. Every team must file one for each regular-season game. The result is that everyone sees the same official information at the same time. It shows which players are hurt, what is wrong with them and whether they are expected to take the field.

Opposing coaches read it while preparing for a game. Fantasy football managers read it to set lineups. Bettors read it before placing wagers. Reporters read it to explain why a starter is missing. Each group works from the identical official list.

The report has a simple structure. Each entry names a player, the body part or illness involved and how much he took part in practice that day. Teams submit the report to the league, and the league makes it public. Later in the week, the report adds a game status for players whose availability is in question.

The game statuses each carry a specific meaning, and they are not interchangeable. Questionable signals uncertainty. Doubtful signals that a player is unlikely to play. Out means the player will not play in that game. A fourth label, probable, once sat at the top of the scale, but the league dropped it starting with the 2016 season.

The labels do not tell the whole story on their own. Practice participation — full, limited or did not participate — adds context that a game status alone does not provide. Timing matters too. For a typical Sunday game, teams release reports on Wednesday, Thursday and Friday. Only the Friday report carries game designations. The final answer arrives on game day, when teams submit their inactive lists about 90 minutes before kickoff.

Injured reserve sits outside this weekly system. It is a roster move, not a game status, and it works under its own set of rules.

The 10 slides below walk through each piece of the system. They cover who files the report, what each label means and how the weekly schedule unfolds. They also explain where injured reserve fits and why the league requires accurate information. Read together, they explain how a single injury listing moves from a Wednesday practice note to a game-day decision.

The NFL injury report is a required public disclosure that every team must file for each of its regular-season games. It names the players who are hurt or ill, identifies the body part or illness involved and records how much each player took part in practice.

The requirement covers every club in the league. No team is exempt. The obligation attaches to each game on the regular-season schedule rather than to the season as a whole. A team therefore produces a new report for every matchup it plays.

Both clubs in a game carry the same duty. Each matchup therefore produces two reports, one from each side, covering the players on each roster who are dealing with a problem that week.

Each entry links a named player to a specific problem. An injury is listed by the part of the body affected, such as a knee, an ankle or a hamstring. A player who is sick rather than hurt is listed with an illness instead.

Listing by body part keeps each entry brief. It also means an entry does not reveal a diagnosis or its severity. A knee listing alone does not show whether the problem is minor or serious.

The second core element is practice participation. Alongside the injury itself, the report shows how fully each listed player practiced. That pairing gives readers two separate pieces of information about a player's condition in the same entry.

Teams do not publish the report through their own channels. Each club submits it to the league, and the league then makes it public. The information reaches fans, media and opposing teams through a single official route.

The report is a statement about players, not about the whole roster. A player who is healthy and practicing normally does not need to appear on it at all. Appearing on the report does not mean a player will miss time.

Practice participation is the part of the NFL injury report that records how much work each listed player did on a given day. Teams describe it with one of three labels: full participation, limited participation or did not participate.

These labels describe what happened at practice. They do not say whether a player will suit up for the game. Game-status designations sit in a separate part of the report and serve a different purpose.

Full participation means the player took full part in practice. It is the strongest signal of health this section offers. A player can still appear on the report with a full listing because the injury itself remains on file.

Limited participation means the player practiced, but not fully. The label covers a wide range of workloads. It does not show which drills a player sat out or how much of the session he missed. Two players with the same limited listing may have done very different amounts of work.

Did not participate means the player stayed out of practice that day. The label on its own does not explain why. The body part or illness listed beside the player's name usually supplies that context.

Some absences have nothing to do with health. Teams can mark an entry as not injury related. A rest day or a personal matter falls into this group. A did-not-participate entry marked this way says little about a player's physical condition.

Teams do not always hold a real practice. When no real practice takes place, the report lists estimated participation instead. In that case, the team reports the level of work it expects each player would have managed in a normal session.

An estimate is a judgment, not a record of work done. A limited listing on an estimated report reflects the team's assessment rather than a session that actually happened. That distinction matters when a reader compares one day's entry with another's.

Questionable is the NFL injury report's label for a player whose availability for the upcoming game is uncertain. It does not predict that the player will sit or play. It signals only that the team has not ruled him in or out when the report is filed.

Older versions of the report attached a percentage to each game-status label. The league no longer does that. Questionable now carries no official numerical chance of playing. Any figure attached to it today is an outside estimate, not league policy.

Without a number, questionable covers a wide range of situations. A player managing a minor problem and a player facing a real chance of missing the game can carry the same label. The designation itself does not distinguish between them.

Players working through the league's concussion protocol can carry the questionable tag late in the week. A player in the protocol cannot return until both the team physician and an independent neurological consultant clear him. That clearance can come after the team files its final weekly report.

Practice participation helps narrow the picture. A questionable player who closed the week with full participation presents a different case from one who did not practice on any day. A player who moved from did not participate to limited also shows a different trend from one who stayed limited all week.

The designation reflects the team's view when the last report of the week is filed. A player's condition can still change before kickoff. Teams can downgrade a questionable player to out after that report. A downgrade means he will not play.

The inactive list settles the matter on game day. A questionable player left off that list is available to suit up. One who appears on it will not play, whatever the earlier report said.

For fantasy managers and bettors, questionable marks an open question rather than a final verdict. The practice record, any late downgrade and the inactive list together carry more information than the label alone.

Doubtful means a player is unlikely to play in the upcoming game. On the NFL's game-status scale, it sits between questionable, which signals genuine uncertainty, and out, which means the player will not suit up.

The label shows which way a team is leaning. A questionable player could go either way. A doubtful player carries a clear lean toward missing the game, but the team has not ruled him out.

Doubtful is not a final decision. The team keeps the option of playing him. He stays on the roster and can still be named active on game day. The definitive answer comes with the inactive list the team submits shortly before kickoff.

A team can also settle the question earlier. If it decides before game day that the player will not play, it can downgrade him from doubtful to out. That change tells everyone the absence is confirmed.

Doubtful appears only on the week's final injury report, the one that carries game designations. Earlier reports in the week list a player's injury and practice work but no game status. A player can therefore be on the report all week and pick up the doubtful tag only at the end.

Doubtful is a game-status designation, not a practice label. Reading it next to the player's practice entries gives a fuller picture. A doubtful player who sat out every session that week presents a different case from one who was limited late in the week. A late-week limited session shows the player was back on the field in some capacity.

The designation applies to one specific game. A player listed as doubtful this week starts fresh on the next week's report, where he may carry a different label or none at all.

Like the other game designations, doubtful can be attached to an illness as well as an injury.

For anyone planning around the game, the label works as an early signal of a likely absence. A doubtful listing is the team's public notice that it does not expect the player on the field.

An out designation means the player will not play in that game. It is the most definitive game-status label on the NFL injury report. It leaves no room for a late decision.

Questionable and doubtful both describe a degree of uncertainty. Out removes it. Once a team lists a player as out, his availability for that game is settled.

The label covers one game at a time. On its own, it does not say how serious an injury is or how long recovery will take. A player can be ruled out with a minor strain one week and practice fully the next. Another player can carry the same label for several straight games while a longer injury heals.

Each new game brings a new report. The status does not carry over automatically. A player listed out this week has his availability assessed again for the next game.

The entry also shows why the player is out. The report names the body part or illness involved, such as a knee, a hamstring or a concussion. Readers see the status and its cause on the same line.

Practice participation sits alongside the designation. The report records whether a player practiced fully, in a limited way or not at all during the week. Those entries describe how the week went. The out designation records the outcome for the game.

Out does not change a player's roster status. He stays on the team's roster even though he will not play. That separates the label from roster transactions, which move a player into a different category altogether.

The certainty matters on both sidelines. A team that rules a player out can build its game plan around his absence before kickoff. The league publishes the report. The opponent therefore works from the same information.

The label is read closely outside the stadium as well. An NFL player who does not appear in a game earns no fantasy points. Fantasy managers treat an out designation as a signal to change their lineups. Bettors read it the same way — as a confirmed absence rather than a possibility.

The NFL stopped using the probable designation starting with the 2016 season. Probable had told readers that a player was likely to play. Its removal left the injury report with three game-status labels instead of four.

Before the change, the report used a four-step scale. Probable sat at the top, followed by questionable, doubtful and out. Each label signaled a different level of confidence that a player would be available on game day.

With probable gone, the scale has three steps: questionable, doubtful and out. Questionable is now the least severe label a team can assign.

Players who once would have carried a probable tag did not disappear from the report. A player who is expected to play but still has an injury is usually listed with his practice status and no game designation. His name, the injury and his participation level still appear. The game-status column next to him stays blank.

That blank space carries meaning. A player on the report without a designation is generally someone the team expects to have available. A player with questionable next to his name is someone whose availability is still uncertain.

The change shifts more of the reading work onto the practice entries. Under the old system, one word summed up the team's outlook for a lightly injured player. Now readers build that outlook from the participation notes logged across the practice week.

Older news coverage, archived reports and past fantasy football advice can still mention probable players. Those references describe a system that ended before the 2016 season began. They do not reflect how current injury reports are written.

Anyone comparing injury reports across seasons should keep the switch in mind. A probable listing in 2015 and an entry with no game designation in 2017 could point to the same expectation that a player would suit up. The two entries record it under different reporting rules.

For a typical Sunday game, NFL teams file three injury reports during the week: one on Wednesday, one on Thursday and one on Friday. The Friday report is the one that carries game-status designations such as questionable, doubtful and out.

The Wednesday and Thursday reports serve a different purpose. They record which players are hurt or ill, the body part or illness involved, and how much each player took part in that day's practice. They do not assign a game status. A player listed on Wednesday is not being ruled in or out for Sunday at that point. Teams submit each of these daily reports to the league, which then makes it public.

Each report captures a single day. Read together, the three releases form a sequence. A player whose practice participation rises from Wednesday to Friday shows a different trend from one whose participation stays flat or drops. A name that appears on Thursday but not on Wednesday points to an injury or illness that surfaced midweek.

Friday changes the nature of the document. Alongside the practice information, teams attach a game designation to players whose availability is in doubt. That makes the Friday release the main public statement of a team's expectations before the weekend.

The Friday report is still not the last word. Game status can shift between Friday and kickoff. A team can still update a player's status after the Friday release, such as downgrading him to out. The final list of players who will not suit up comes later, on game day itself.

Not every game falls on a Sunday. Teams that play on a Monday, a Thursday or another day follow a shifted version of the same cycle. The specific release days move with the date of the game. The Wednesday-to-Friday pattern does not carry over unchanged.

For a non-Sunday matchup, the timing of that week's reports differs from the standard Sunday pattern.

Every NFL team must submit its list of inactive players about 90 minutes before kickoff. That list is the final, official word on who will not suit up. It can also differ from the game designations a team gave on its Friday report.

The inactive list answers a narrower question than the weekly injury report. The injury report describes health and practice work across a week. The inactive list simply names the players on the active roster who will not dress for that specific game.

Those two documents do not always line up. A player listed as questionable on Friday has no settled status until inactives arrive. He either appears on the list or he does not. That ends the uncertainty for that game.

Change can run in either direction. A player who seemed likely to play can suffer a setback over the weekend or in pregame warmups and end up inactive. A player carrying a game designation can also be cleared and take the field.

Not every inactive player is hurt. Teams cannot dress their entire active roster, so some healthy players sit out each week. Coaches decide who stays in uniform based on depth at each position and the needs of that matchup. A name on the inactive list does not, by itself, signal an injury.

The timing matters to anyone tracking player availability. Fantasy football managers and sports bettors watch for the list closely. It is the last official update before the game begins. For a player listed as questionable, it is often the first definitive one.

A player's status can remain open until that deadline. As a result, a Friday questionable tag can leave fans waiting into game day for an answer. The requirement applies to every game, whether it kicks off on a Sunday afternoon, a weeknight or in prime time.

The inactive list is also separate from roster transactions. A player on it remains on the team's active roster. He can be available the following week without the team making any move. That distinction separates a one-game inactive from longer-term absences handled through formal roster changes.

Injured reserve is a roster transaction, not a game-status label. A player marked out on the weekly injury report is ruled out of one game. A player placed on injured reserve must sit out a minimum number of games before he is eligible to return.

The two terms describe different things. Out is a designation tied to a single upcoming game. It tells the public that the player will not take the field that week. It says nothing about the weeks after that. The following week, the player goes through the reporting cycle again, and his status is set fresh.

Injured reserve runs on a different timeline. Moving a player there changes his roster status rather than his availability for one game. Once the move is made, the player cannot come back simply because he has healed. He has to wait out the required minimum first.

That minimum has not stayed the same. The NFL has changed the number of games an injured reserve player must miss over the years. To work out how long a specific player will be sidelined, check the rule in force for that season.

The two terms also surface in different places. Out appears on the weekly injury report, next to the player's practice participation. A move to injured reserve is announced as a roster transaction. A player on injured reserve is already ineligible to play. He does not need a weekly game designation to confirm it.

The terms carry different expectations as well. An out designation leaves open a quick return, sometimes as soon as the next game. Injured reserve signals that the team expects the player to be unavailable for at least the minimum stretch. A team can also list a player as out for several straight weeks without ever moving him to injured reserve.

For anyone following a team, each term answers its own question. Out answers whether a player will appear this weekend. Injured reserve answers a longer question: the earliest point at which he is allowed to return to the field.

The NFL requires teams to report player injuries accurately, and the league can discipline a club that fails to do so. That requirement turns the weekly injury report from a courtesy into an obligation.

Accuracy applies to every element a club files. A game status has to reflect the team's honest read of a player's availability. A practice entry has to reflect the work the player actually did, not a softer or harsher version of it. The obligation holds for each report a team releases during the week, not only the one that carries game designations.

Misreporting can run in either direction. A club might hide an injury to keep an opponent guessing about a key starter. It might also list a healthy player with an injury to create doubt that does not exist. Both versions mislead the opposing staff and anyone else relying on the report.

Competitive fairness is the main reason the rule matters. Both teams in a game prepare with the same official information about each other. A club that shades its report gains an edge its opponent cannot see or correct before kickoff.

Public trust is the second reason. The report reaches a far wider audience than the two coaching staffs. For readers outside the building, the report is the league's official record of who is hurt. Their confidence in the report depends on the league holding clubs to it.

The rules also give teams an honest outlet for absences that have nothing to do with injury. A player can be listed as not injury related. A club therefore has no reason to invent an ailment to explain a missed practice.

Enforcement rests with the league itself. The NFL receives each club's report, publishes it and can act when a filing falls short. Penalties can include fines against the club.
