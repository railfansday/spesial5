<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/providers/hhs-medicaid-state-directed-payment-savings-primarily-stem-slowing-growth" hreflang="en">HHS: Medicaid state-directed payment savings 'primarily' stem from slowing growth</a>

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

HHS: Medicaid state-directed payment savings 'primarily' stem from slowing growth" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Rural hospitals claim a relatively small portion of the state-directed payments being squeezed in coming years by 2025’s H.R. 1, with much of the legislation’s projected spending reductions the result of holding funds for those hospitals from growing beyond fiscal year 2025 levels, Department of Health and Human Services policy heads wrote in a new report.

The analysis, released Monday, paints a softer picture of the rate limits Republicans imposed on a supplemental Medicaid payment stream in last year’s broad budget reconciliation law, also referred to as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act or the Working Families Tax Cut Legislation.

Hospitals and other healthcare industry groups, as well as advocates and Democrats, have said the law’s billions in projected savings—from state-directed payment changes as well as other policy areas like Medicaid work requirements—are likely to devastate providers already operating at thin or negative margins.

Specifically, the Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation (ASPE)’s analysis showed that an estimated 7.8% of Medicaid state-directed payments reached rural hospitals in FY2025, or $11.5 billion out of $148.3 billion. That rate is slightly above rural hospitals’ 7% share of total Medicaid hospital spending for that same fiscal year, as estimated by the CMS Office of the Actuary.

Scaling from that baseline in line with H.R. 1’s multi-year payment rate phasedown, with some cumulative adjustments, showed that total nominal cumulative spending on rural state-directed payments would decline by 42.9%, or $81.6 billion, from 2026 through 2035 under the bill. This rate is again slightly higher than the CMS Office of the Actuary’s 40.8% estimate for all affected hospitals, which ASPE said is the result of the higher average dollar weighted payment rates rural hospitals currently receive, “which means that rural providers have more room to reduce rates to meet the new limits of 100% to 110% of Medicare.”

The ASPE report’s bottom line, however, is that $75.2 billion of the $81.6 billion reduction rural hospitals face, or 92.1%, reflects projected increases over the FY2025 baseline of $11.5 billion. The remainder, about 7.9% of the projected change, is a reduction below FY2025’s estimated state-directed payments to rural hospitals.

HHS, in a release highlighting the report’s findings, said that the language used in government reports describing the full $81.6 billion 10-year spending reduction as “savings” misses the government’s primary goal of capping future growth.

“Counting every dollar of projected savings as a cut below existing funding gives the public the wrong picture. Most of these savings are not reductions below what the program spent in 2025,” HHS Chief Economist and Chief Regulatory Officer Casey Mulligan, who was one of the report’s coauthors, said in the release. “They reflect future spending increases that will no longer occur. Our analysis makes that distinction clear.”

H.R. 1’s multi-year scale down in state-directed payment rates is set to begin in 2028, and are expected to slash some hard-hit states’ Medicaid spending by up to a quarter of their total annual Medicaid spending, per one analysis. Hospitals have broadly criticized the policy changes while spending much of this year adjusting their operations to contend with the future limits supplemental revenue, expectations of increased uncompensated care costs and year-to-year expense pressures like wage and supply inflation.

Additionally, CMS earlier this year proposed state directed payment caps substantially more extensive than what was outlined in H.R. 1 and used as the basis for Monday’s ASPE report. That proposal would extend rate limits beyond the bill’s four outlined service areas (inpatient hospital services, outpatient hospital services, nursing facility services and qualified practitioner services at an academic medical center) to “all [state-directed payments]s for all services in all states.” Hospital groups have flatly opposed the proposal, which more than triples the 10-year estimated savings.

Lawmakers last year, when seeking votes, blunted H.R. 1's funding reductions for rural hospitals by inserting a five-year, $50 billion rural health fund into the legislation. Those funds have come with usage restrictions for states and providers, with critics also noting transparency concerns and the fund's relatively small scale compared to the bill's total projected impact on hospitals.
