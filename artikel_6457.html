Scientists Trap a Single Antimatter Particle for Months in Quest To Solve the Universe’s Biggest Mystery

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

A single particle of antimatter, suspended for months in a laboratory trap, could help physicists unravel why matter survived to build the universe around us.

Northwestern University physicists have brought a positron, the electron’s antimatter counterpart, into a precisely controlled quantum state for the first time. After capturing the energetic particle from radioactive decay, they cooled it to nearly absolute zero and suspended it in a near-perfect vacuum.

Their achievement, described in Physical Review Letters, creates a “one-positron quantum cyclotron.” Like an artificial atom, the trapped positron has distinct quantum energy levels that researchers can manipulate and observe.

That control opens a path toward an exceptionally precise comparison of matter and antimatter. By measuring the positron’s magnetic properties and comparing them with an electron’s, the team hopes to test a fundamental prediction of particle physics and search for signs that current theory is missing something.

The Standard Model describes fundamental particles and three of nature’s four fundamental forces (electromagnetic, strong nuclear, and weak nuclear forces). Although experiments have repeatedly confirmed its predictions with extraordinary precision, it cannot explain why the observable universe contains overwhelmingly more matter than antimatter.

“Antimatter gets its popular charm from science fiction,” said study leader Gerald Gabrielse. “If you take an electron and an anti-electron (or positron) and collide them together, they both disappear. We describe that as annihilation, a concept that fuels science fiction writers’ imaginations. The big mystery of the universe is the Big Bang should have created equal amounts of matter and antimatter. But, if that were true, it would annihilate, and we’d be left with nothing. There’s clearly something wrong with that theory, and that’s what we’re trying to figure out.”

The unresolved question is how matter escaped annihilation to build stars, planets, and people.

“We know the Standard Model has to be wrong somewhere—or at least incomplete,” Gabrielse said. “For example, it cannot predict why the universe exists, so that’s a pretty big loophole. We’re very precisely examining the most fundamental and simple systems to find where the theory breaks down. We hope to find an unambiguous answer.”

Nearly everything on Earth consists of electrons, protons, and neutrons. An antimatter world could instead contain positrons, antiprotons, and antineutrons. Gabrielse says that if everything here were made of antimatter, the difference would be extraordinarily difficult to recognize. The trouble begins when matter and antimatter meet, which makes an ordinary container unsuitable for holding a positron.

“If we want to study antimatter, we must handle it carefully,” Gabrielse said. “To be clear, it’s not dangerous. We don’t have enough antimatter to make an explosion.”

The Northwestern team used specialized electric and magnetic fields to suspend the particle away from the walls of an advanced trap. A near-perfect vacuum minimizes encounters with ordinary matter, allowing the positron to remain confined for months.

Capturing it required first overcoming its enormous initial energy. The researchers obtained positrons from the radioactive decay of sodium-22, a rare isotope of sodium, then slowed a particle dramatically and guided it into the trap.

Inside the trap, the temperature is about 300 millikelvins, just 0.3 degrees Celsius (0.54 degrees Fahrenheit) above absolute zero. Cooling allows the researchers to bring the positron’s cyclotron motion, its circling movement in the magnetic field, into its lowest quantum energy state.

“We cool it down so much that it’s in its quantum ground state,” Gabrielse said. “We actually coaxed this very fast positron to sit there and behave for months at a time.”

The team then used carefully tuned microwaves to deliver tiny amounts of energy and detected the positron’s transitions between individual quantum states. Being able to both trigger and observe those changes provides the control needed for a highly precise magnetic measurement.

The experiment combines two strands of Gabrielse’s career. His group previously developed techniques to isolate electrons and measure their magnetic properties, while his pioneering work on slowing, capturing, and cooling antiprotons helped establish low-energy antimatter research. The new study extends the quantum control achieved with electrons to a single antimatter particle.

In earlier work, Gabrielse’s team used a one-electron quantum cyclotron to measure the electron’s magnetic moment to about one part in 10 trillion, among the most precise measurements ever made of an elementary particle. The magnetic moment describes how strongly a particle interacts with a magnetic field.

The next goal is to make a comparable measurement of the positron. Electrons and positrons have opposite electric charges, and theory predicts magnetic moments equal in magnitude but opposite in direction. An unexpected mismatch would challenge the Standard Model and could offer clues to the universe’s imbalance between matter and antimatter.

“Current theories suggest that there will be no difference in the two measurements,” Gabrielse said. “If it turns out they are not equal, that would be the biggest discovery in physics in the last century.”

Reference: “One-Positron Quantum Cyclotron” by T. G. Myers, L. Soucy, B. A. D. Sukra, B. Sinha and G. Gabrielse, August 20, 2026, arXiv. DOI: 10.1103/gbkc-6nlj
