How Kindness Unlocks Happiness In Our Brains

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Small acts of kindness and self-compassion can interrupt a cycle of self-criticism and release neurotransmitters that create a sense of wellbeing.

Some years ago, my daughter was beaten up at a kids’ gathering. I asked myself as a psychologist, “What happened to kindness? When did we lose it?”

I started to dig into the research. In the World Happiness Report, researchers looked at kindness as a factor contributing to happiness, and they developed a novel way to study the prevalence of kind acts. They tracked “lost” wallets to see if they were returned. They found that more people were helping strangers than was reported before the pandemic, which may be surprising.

Other social science studies found that measurably happier people live in areas where community members tend to donate more, volunteer their time, or help strangers. In other words, helping inspires more helping.

What explains this kindness-happiness connection? In short, giving or receiving kindness kickstarts a “helper’s high” in our brains.

Research shows that acts of kindness engage certain brain networks that release good chemicals like an internal pharmacy:

The dopamine from a helper’s high is different from the endorphin hit associated with running, gaming, or gambling, which keeps you wanting more. The dopamine generated in a helper’s high continues to produce positive feelings over time. The release of these neurotransmitters is especially helpful for people with ADHD who may struggle with emotional control, motivation, and stress.

Studies have shown that volunteering, even for one hour a week, can reduce inflammatory and stress cortisol responses, and even increase longevity. Volunteering is also considered a preventative action for loneliness and depression. Teens who volunteer outside their homes are more resilient and less likely to become depressed, according to research.

Helping people learn to be kind to themselves is my lifelong challenge as a clinical psychologist. We tend to prioritize other people’s needs over our own. But the research is clear on self-compassion: When you talk to yourself in a kinder way, or do kind things for yourself, you’re learning how to quiet that inner critic.
