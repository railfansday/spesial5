The Bivo Trio Mini Keeps Finding a Place on Our Bikes When the Temperatures Begin to Dip—Here’s Why

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

In a crowded category, the Bivo Trio Bottle ends up in our bottle cages for colder rides.

Your water bottles have probably frozen at least once if you ride outside in cold weather. Usually, it’s only a thin layer of ice on top or a clogged nozzle, but that’s enough to be annoying. If you ride at low enough temperatures with standard plastic bottles filled with plain water, it will inevitably happen. However, it’s important to stay hydrated on winter rides.

There are proven ways to keep your bottles from freezing—or carry hot beverages on rides. Hot chocolate, coffee, tea, or Skratch Labs’ apple cider will warm your soul and lift your mood. And nothing hits better than a coffee stop in the middle of a cold winter ride, especially when conditions are dreary or sloppy.

Stainless insulated bottles broke into cycling about five years ago and have since exploded in popularity. They’re a proven way to keep water and hydration mix cool on the hottest summer days, and they can also keep hot drinks warm for many miles on winter rides.

The Bivo Trio Bottle, available in raw stainless steel and silicone-covered stainless steel versions, has become a bottle of choice on cold-weather rides. Read on to find out why, plus tips to prevent your bottles from freezing this winter.

Cyclists have many different methods (often handed down from one generation of riders to the next) to help prevent frozen bottles. One simple solution is to start your ride with hot water. However, depending on the temperature and ride length, this can also freeze.

Using insulated plastic bottles, adding a high-sodium drink mix, and wearing a hydration pack or vest under a riding jacket are all tricks I’ve used (with varying degrees of success) to keep my bottles from freezing. And some riders still swear by adding a shot of booze to their bidon. However, while these methods may prevent icing, they still result in a cold beverage during your ride.

It’s hard to miss the growing popularity of stainless steel water bottles in cycling. Their use has boomed in the last few seasons as more riders have become concerned about microplastic contamination. Plus, these metal bidons, particularly the Bivo bottles, are in the cages of many cycling influencers.

Stainless bottles typically feature a vacuum-sealed double-wall design with a coated outer and a bare stainless inner layer. Since metal bottles are rigid, you cannot squeeze them like plastic bottles. (It takes some getting used to if you haven’t ridden them before.) And while stainless bottles are much heavier than plastic models, they are heavy-duty and almost indestructible—they dent and scratch but rarely break.

Bivo is the trend-setting stainless option and commonly available from retailers like REI, Backcountry, Amazon, or your local bike shop. The Insulated Mini we tested is 17 ounces and costs $44, or opt for the 21-ounce model for $5 more.

I have ridden with insulated bottles at several points in my cycling life. But until last summer, I usually stuck with non-insulated plastic bottles for most of my rides, regardless of air temperature.

Generally, I prefer the feel of a regular bottle; 22-ounce Specialized Purist bottles are my go-to, and I occasionally carry a 10-ounce Purist Maker mug for a mid-ride coffee break. But I often skip stainless insulated bottles because they aren’t squeezable and weigh noticeably more than plastic bidons.

However, before a recent ride on a cold and blustery day, I filled an insulated bottle with hot chocolate and was pleased to have a warm treat throughout my 90-minute ride. So, like my insulated bottle test last summer, I applied a little science to see which insulated stainless bottles performed best in cold temps.

For this test, I gathered several popular metal insulated bottles. First, I evaluated the bottles by measuring water temperature over three hours in subfreezing conditions. Then, I used them on rides in cold weather to determine which worked best from a functional perspective.

While the intent was somewhat scientific, I also strove to be practical—these are tests anyone can do at home. This testing was not performed in a controlled environment or with professional-level, calibrated equipment.

Since it was below 32 degrees outside, I was able to perform outdoor freeze testing. Like my summer insulated bottle tests, I filled the selected bottles with 400 grams of tap water (heated to 212 degrees for this test instead of chilled water used in summer). Then I placed the filled bottles on my front porch in subfreezing temperatures; I did this after sunset so sunlight would not warm the bottles. Every 30 minutes, I checked and recorded the water temperature of each bottle, in the same order each time, with a digital food thermometer.

Following the initial three-hour test period, the bottles remained overnight in sub-freezing temperatures for an additional nine hours. This tested whether the contents would stay warm for a full 12 hours (as per some bottle makers’ claims). Additionally, for reference, I subjected a Purist Maker tumbler (with 200 grams of water), an insulated plastic bottle, and a standard plastic bottle to the same tests.

The following day, I evaluated the bottles’ on-bike performance on a short loop in my neighborhood. I tested their use with standard and lobster-claw gloves (such as holding, opening caps, removing them from cages) and their nozzles’ function while drinking hot coffee (a medium-roast pour-over with some oat milk for my tests 😉).

The results were clear: Insulated stainless bottles work very well in cold weather. They keep your beverage hot for an hour-long ride in 30-degree temps. For longer rides, your drink will experience some heat loss but will remain drinkably warm for three hours. The insulated metal bottles performed notably better than plastic bottles (insulated and non-insulated) for the full duration of the test.

After about three hours of exposure in the cold, the Bivo Insulated Trio Mini kept my coffee at a warm 119.8 degrees. This is only slightly below coffee expert James Hoffmann’s recommended coffee-drinking temp of 122 to 129 degrees. (Although I’m not sure Hoffmann would be pleased drinking three-hour-old brew.) In 12-hour testing, none of the metal bottles froze. Most bottles finished at room temperature after 12 hours outside in freezing temperatures.

On the bike, the Bivo valve works great, and the bottle texture isn’t overly slippery. The bottle shape is compatible with most cages, though it fits best in the Arundle Sport cage recommended by Bivo.

Nozzle Shield: Look for bottles with a cap that protects the nozzle. Typically, I’m not a fan of these shields when riding in warm weather—they flop around and get in the way when drinking. However, in the winter, caps keep road salt and slush off the bottle’s nozzle, and they also help prevent icing. Ones with a removable plastic or rubber shield are easier to clean; plus, you can use the bottles without the cap on warm days.

Vapor Pressure: Many of the tested bottles carry a warning about using liquids hotter than 180 degrees. This is to avoid scalding and burns from pressurized hot water when the cap or nozzle is opened. With insulated bottles, hot liquid heats the air inside the bottle enough to spray potentially near-boiling water into your mouth or onto your body.

Avoid this by letting your hot beverage cool slightly before pouring it into your bottle. For long rides (when using two bottles of hot drinks), save the hotter bottle for later in the day. This way, it has time to cool to a drinkable temperature.

Cage Compatibility: Since they do not deform or flex, metal bottles don’t pair with every bottle cage. Some stainless bottles have a loose fit in cages (bottles can even eject on rough roads), and some cage shapes do not allow some metal bottles to sit correctly, causing annoying rattling and buzzing while riding. Other cage-bottle pairings are so tight that it’s hard to remove the bottle while riding.

My advice: Check that your cages work before dropping up to $100 for new bottles that won’t fit. Some brands have specific cage recommendations on their sites. You can also read user reviews for the compatibility of different bottles and cages. Also, be sure to check the overall bottle height to be sure it fits in your frame.

Dropped Bottles: I do not recommend using metal bottles in mass-start situations; use plastic bidons for big group rides or races. When a metal bottle is ejected from a cage on a bump, or you drop one (as is prone to happen with cold hands or when wearing bulky gloves), it ricochets off the road surface more than a plastic bottle. And while metal makes these bottles more durable, they do not burst open on impact or crush if hit by other riders.

Bivo shook up the bottle market when it launched its stainless steel bottles. The small Richmond, Vermont-based company launched insulated bottles shortly after, and cyclists have fallen in love with them. They work well, look good, and don’t have a funky taste.
