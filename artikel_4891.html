Why Autistic Employees Are Targeted By Bullies

Workplace bullying damages health and drains productivity. For autistic employees, it perpetuates the cycle of abuse that starts much earlier. A 2026 review confirmed that autistic students experience disproportionately high levels of bullying, with most estimates falling between 40% and 70% (Heredia-Oliva et al., 2026), and it is a major source of stress for them.

The painful pattern then follows people into work. And many work environments prove fertile grounds for both overt (threatening, shouting) and especially covert (backstabbing, sabotaging) types of bullying (Praslova et al., 2022).

In experiments, neurotypical observers reported liking autistic people less than other neurotypical people, with vague explanations like perceived “awkwardness” (Alkhaldi et al, 2021). Workplace stressors have the potential to magnify the dislike. Autistic employees, with their spiky profiles of abilities, are more likely to be on extreme ends of performance. High performers often experience veiled violence like sabotage or having crucial information withheld due to coworker envy; struggling performers face overt hostility, which makes their performance further decline (Jensen et al., 2014). Add autistic tendencies of direct communication, including possibly challenging inefficiencies and misconduct (Hartman & Hartman, 2024), along with a focus on work over socializing, and the target is set.

Autistic people's nervous systems often have less capacity to recover from social stress (Cheng et al., 2020), and autistic adults are already at elevated risk of developing mental and physical health issues due to a mismatch between their needs and the environments, sensory stress, effortful masking, and other forms of chronic stress (Botha & Frost, 2020; Dhanasekara et al., 2023). Past bullying also leaves a psychological trace. My school expereince was such that the sound of laughter made me shudder, and in a research study, 87.4% of autistic adults showed signs of fear of being laughed at, compared with 22.6% of non-autistic adults (Leader et al., 2018).

Multiple vulnerabilities create compounding effects. Years of mistreatment result in hypervigilance and keep the body's threat response switched on. Each new injury is a blow to a system that never fully recovered from the last. To make things worse, the lack of resources often associated with unemployment, with only about 3 in 10 working-age autistic people in the UK employed (Buckland, 2024), leaves fewer options for recovery, seeking help, or leaving a toxic job.

Reporting bullying at work is generally a thankless task. Reports are often ignored or dismissed: in a nationally representative U.S. survey, 60% of employers reacted negatively when bullying was reported (Namie, 2021), and targets describe HR as regularly siding with bullies (Boddy & Boulter, 2025). Moreover, skillful manipulators, which many bullies are, can present themselves as victims.

Autistic people, however, face additional barriers to successfully reporting bullying and being heard.

Most organizations say they are committed to healthy workplaces, but their methods for handling bullying often make things worse. Those methods fail autistic employees and others dealing with trauma or overwhelm in at least three ways.

They are reactive. Action typically begins only after bullying is proven. But the first line of defense against bullying and any other hazard at work is prevention (Praslova et al., 2022). Preventing harm is both more ethical and more practical than repairing it.

They put the burden on the target. Targets are told to document what was said and done, when, and who was present, then discuss it calmly with a supervisor. How many people can realistically do this while doing their jobs, under stress, and dealing with trauma? The process appears to be designed to make it easy to reject the complaint because of the “tone” or the target “being emotional.”

They try to fix personalities. Bullies may act out their entitlement, fragile egos, and poor self-control, but bullying flourishes where the environment lets it go undetected and rewards it with resources or advancement (Praslova et al., 2022; Salin, 2003). Trying to change personalities is not an effective way to produce sustainable organizational change.

Fixing environments is a more productive way to positively change norms and behavior than attempting to change humans (Praslova, 2024). Here are a few mechanisms, described in more detail in Praslova et al., (2022) and Praslova, (2024).

Remove the rewards for bullying. Avoid internal competition for scarce resources. Distribute resources fairly and transparently, and balance individual rewards with team rewards. Realistic workloads reduce bullying used to offload work onto others, and transparency may take some of the fuel out of envy. Transparent decision-making makes rumors and backstabbing less likely to pay off (Praslova et al., 2022).

Set standards and train for them. A clear policy should state that bullying is not tolerated, and name its covert forms, not just its overt ones (Praslova et al., 2022). Train everyone in respectful communication, and train new managers specifically for complex interpersonal situations. Ideally, facilitate professional support from psychologists trained in workplace well-being.

Build civility and voice together. Some people mistake authenticity for a license to be cruel. Others try to buy “calm” by silencing people. Neither works. Bullying of autistic people is a predictable outcome of systems that fail to check assumptions, reward sameness and conformity, and make targets prove their own reality. Workplaces that are healthy by design, with transparency, civility, and voice, on the other hand, not only support autistic people, but make work better for everyone (Praslova, 2024).
