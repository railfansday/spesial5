Muse Is Unleashing a World of Pettiness on Instagram

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Khalil Roberts had heard horror stories of people getting kicked off Instagram for using third-party apps to purge follower counts, so when he came across an X post claiming that Muse—Meta’s AI assistant—could mass-unfollow people who didn’t follow him back, he didn’t believe it.

“I’ve never gone through my exact list to vet those people out, but I’ve always been curious,” the 31-year-old New Yorker tells WIRED. But after realizing that by using Muse, his account wouldn’t get flagged as spam, his mind was made up. “I was like, I actually will be a little petty.”

After downloading Muse, Roberts, who had over 7,600 followers, entered the prompt: “Can you show me a list of people (non celebrity) that [don’t] follow me back on instagram and can you unfollow them for me. ”

Within minutes, Muse identified 151 accounts. It attached an Excel sheet with a breakdown of everyone cataloged—separated by categories identifying their username, real name, classification, and profile link—and asked if Roberts wanted to proceed with his request. Shockingly, some of the names were people he thought of as friends. “It was a little surprising, but also a reminder that we don’t have a relationship anymore.”

Following the success of his November 7 cleanse, Roberts posted about it on X, offering instructions to his followers. Over the past several weeks, parallel scenarios have played out across X, Instagram, and Facebook, with users sharing tips on how to automate their unfollows with the help of Muse.

X user @justdrmj posted about the trend the same week as Roberts, and nearly every reply shared a taste for trivial grudge-holding. For him it came down to one simple rule: “Why be a fan?” “i agree. reciprocity is key,” one person said in response. “The way I just cleaned house,” added another. In a seeming outburst of celebration, one X user posted: “Just unfollowed 1000+ people on IG.”

Even tech worker Kimberly Munoz, who has 33,200 Instagram followers, said she wasn’t about to be “anybody’s fan,” noting in an Instagram video that she used the feature to suss out “people that I know in real life that have unfollowed me.” “I downloaded Muse just to do this only,” one person responded.

With social media rapidly evolving, there is no longer a unified consensus around etiquette. For some people, following others is an indication of friendship or simply a way to keep tabs on your latest crush. Others view it as more of a social obligation—you follow someone if they follow you, but the opposite is true as well.

Natalie Pennington, an associate professor who researches social connection at Colorado State University, wonders about the consequences of people gradually outsourcing small social decisions to technology.

“It is probably at least partially a question of not wanting to deal with emotionally uncomfortable things,” Pennington says. “This also strikes me as another example of information we don’t really need that social media can give us. There’s this data that exists at the platform level or would take a lot of effort to find out on your own, but now you can learn with the click of the button. But it doesn’t mean it is something you need to know.”

In October, despite the seeming popularity of the feature, Meta removed an advertising campaign that promoted Muse with the promise that users could “see who unfollowed you each week,” Business Insider reported.

When WIRED asked about the reasoning behind Meta’s decision to remove the ad, the company did not respond to a request for comment.

But Muse’s capabilities aren’t just being employed to even scores. Some people are using the assistant to streamline feeds that have become extremely cluttered over the years.

Elizabeth Brooks is a self-proclaimed “sweeper”—the term for a hobbyist who routinely enters sweepstakes and promotional contests—who, over the course of 10 years, had followed various accounts offering giveaways. Recently, she started prompting the assistant to bring her five profile links every few hours, no more than six times a day, for her to manually unfollow one by one. “Your following list can finally work for you instead of the other way around,” she wrote on Facebook.

Roberts, who joined Instagram in high school 15 years ago and calls the platform “my app of choice,” says people are becoming more intentional about their digital relationships and who they let into their online lives. But he doesn’t plan on letting Muse make any more decisions about his social life, he says.
