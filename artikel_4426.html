European Union Cracks Down on Predatory Gaming Practices

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Modern games have found plenty of ways to make a small purchase more complicated than it should be. You buy in-game currency instead of the item you want, spend that currency on a random reward, and sometimes end up with just enough left over to tempt another top-up.

The studio is giving Kiln, its pottery-themed multiplayer game, another shot.

Now, the European Commission wants to put limits on some of these practices. On November 11, it unveiled the proposed Digital Fairness Act, a set of consumer-protection rules covering loot boxes, premium currencies, subscription traps, and manipulative design, and although games aren't its only target, they're very much part of it.

Under the proposal, games could no longer sell paid loot boxes to minors. Adults would still be allowed to buy them, but publishers would have to show the possible rewards and their odds, and give players an accessible way to turn the system off.

Pay-to-win and pay-to-progress mechanics would also be prohibited for minors. That second term is important because it could cover purchases that let players skip a grind, not just items that give them an advantage over someone else. Where the law draws that line will matter to publishers whose games rely on paid progression boosts.

Premium currencies are another target. Games would have to show what an in-game purchase costs in real money. Players shouldn't have to do the math just to find out what an item costs. Paid virtual currencies would also be switched off by default for minors unless a parent or guardian approves them.

Brazil passed a law against loot boxes for children last year, but the EU proposal covers more ground. It also doesn't mean adults are about to lose access to every microtransaction.

Outside gaming, the proposal targets "dark patterns" – misleading prompts and interfaces designed to push people into choices they didn't intend to make. That includes fake urgency, confusing options, and other tricks meant to keep people spending.

Subscriptions would get stricter rules, too. A free trial could only become a paid subscription with explicit consent, and canceling online should be straightforward. The Commission also wants renewal reminders instead of leaving customers to discover another charge after it's happened.

Another part deals with addictive design. Businesses would have to let users disable certain features that encourage excessive use, such as unwanted notifications, autoplay, and streak rewards. The proposal also calls for clearer influencer advertising and tighter limits on personalized ads and prices aimed at minors.The Commission also highlights the difficulty of reaching a human customer-service representative. Earlier this month, a Battlefield 6 player won a court case against EA after an unexplained ban and unsuccessful attempts to appeal. That's a separate dispute, but the frustration of getting nowhere with support will sound familiar to plenty of players.

The gaming industry has already objected. In a joint statement, Video Games Europe and the European Games Developer Federation described the proposal as 'unworkable, unjustified and disproportionate.' They argue it could hurt European studios, particularly smaller ones, and leave players with fewer games to choose from.

The groups say the rules around in-game currencies are too broad and don't account for existing safeguards. They point to PEGI ratings, parental controls, and spending restrictions, and warn that the proposal could create more legal uncertainty. The industry groups have also suggested smaller currency top-ups and refunds for unused currency as alternatives.

The definitions of pay-to-win and pay-to-progress will be worth watching. They sound straightforward until you apply them to a game where almost everything can be earned through play or bought to save time.

For now, the Act is only a proposal. It still needs to go through the European Parliament and Council, where parts of it could change. No publisher is being ordered to pull loot boxes from its games today.

The Commission has separately proposed stronger consumer-protection enforcement, including possible fines of up to 6% of a company's worldwide annual turnover in major cross-border cases. Those powers aren't in effect either.

If the legislation passes, publishers will have to rethink at least some of their usual monetization systems in the EU. What survives the negotiations, particularly the rules for minors, will decide how big that change is.

After years of near radio silence, Fairgames has been canceled and its staff redistributed throughout Sony games.
