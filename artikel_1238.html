Carrick apology to United fans ominously similar to Solskjaer’s final match

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

There have been some dark days in the post-Sir Alex Ferguson era but this was up there with the very worst of them: the kind of performance that gets a manager sacked, the kind of display that points to a fundamental disconnect, the kind of occasion that leaves you thinking Manchester United are as far away as they have ever been.

When players seem so averse to doing the very basics, such as running, and the club habitually have such trouble attracting the right profiles and personalities, it is hard to escape the feeling that saying goodbye to another manager is going to do little more than paper over the many cracks at Old Trafford.

But, watching this, you had to think Michael Carrick will do well to limp on as far as Sunday, when United face Bournemouth at Old Trafford.

And that was before we had heard the United captain, Bruno Fernandes, appearing to call out the manager’s tactics just a week on from the last show of public dissent from a player, when Lisandro Martínez attacked the Old Trafford board. If only these players were as good at talking on the pitch.

At the final whistle, Carrick walked over to the 700-strong group of United supporters, held up both hands in apology on no fewer than seven occasions and then walked off down the tunnel without looking in the direction of any of his players.

There were shades of Ole Gunnar Solskjaer at Watford in November 2021, when he also held up his hands in apology before being sacked 24 hours later.

In that instance, United fans booed Solskjaer. Carrick at least received some polite applause from the fans, who are a smart bunch and know the club’s problems extend far beyond the man in the dugout. Still, it is always the manager who pays the price. Solskjaer said he knew at half-time he was toast and Carrick is now under real threat himself – and seemingly at loggerheads with his captain.

Fernandes’s criticism of United’s set-up and approach was cutting and a direct attack on his manager and backroom team. “It’s a decision from the staff to not press man to man and sometimes leave space behind and try to get the ball, wait for them, but sometimes when you give space to these teams they get confidence,” the United captain said.

There may be some truth in that, but Carrick’s response felt the most accurate assessment of the lot and will certainly chime with supporters who have long questioned how and why so many United sides, regardless of the manager, reach a point where running, intensity and urgency seem optional. “Whatever tactics you play, if you don’t run, and you don’t run forward, and you don’t run back, you ain’t going to win,” Carrick said.

Carrick has been telling us for weeks that he and his squad are together, that the team are not too far away, that it is all about small margins. He even described Sunday’s 1-1 draw at Leeds as a “fresh start”. But Como blew the lid off any pretence of that being the case. Even by the standards of this season to date, though, what unfolded here in the rain in Lombardy was shocking to witness.

Como, inspired by the outstanding Nico Paz, were every bit as good as United – who have won just two of 11 games this season – were a pitiful mess.

Having conceded after just 47 seconds to Atlético Madrid last week, United were behind after only 118 seconds this time. That in itself told you a lot about these players and this team and it set the tone for what was to follow: a masterclass in incompetence and indifference. There have only been four worse results in United’s European history, although you will struggle to find a poorer performance than this.

Michael Carrick has insisted there is not a “toxic culture” at Manchester United in the wake of scathing criticism from former manager Erik ten Hag of the Old Trafford hierarchy and player power at the club.

The current United manager – who is under pressure after the club’s worst start to a season for 40 years – also believes he has the backing of the United board, despite Ten Hag accusing Sir Jim Ratcliffe’s Ineos regime of a series of “broken promises”.

In an interview with The Athletic, Ten Hag blamed Ratcliffe and his executives for fuelling a “toxic culture” by giving power to players and failing to back him on disciplinary matters relating to Marcus Rashford, Jadon Sancho and other first-teamers.
