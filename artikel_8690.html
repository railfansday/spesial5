Self-publishing pays you up to 70% of each ebook’s price, but that rate only matters if your book sells. Treat the book as a product by writing for readers who already buy books on your topic, paying for a copyedit and a cover, and planning the launch before you publish.

Most authors’ first few books earn little. In Written Word Media’s 2025 survey of 1,346 indie authors, about 80% of those with one to three books out made under $100 a month from writing.

Yet each sale pays well. Amazon’s Kindle Direct Publishing (KDP) pays 70% of an ebook’s list price in the US, minus a small delivery fee, when you price it from $2.99 to $12.99, and 35% outside that range. A paperback earns 60% of its list price at $9.99 and up, or 50% below that, minus printing costs.

Pick a topic whose readers already buy books, then spend on what they notice, the editing and the cover. Copyediting nonfiction runs a median 3 to 4 cents a word in the Editorial Freelancers Association’s 2026 rate chart. KDP’s free Kindle Create tool handles formatting, and a paperback gets a free ISBN, the number stores list it under, usable only on KDP.

Say a nurse writes a guide for new nursing students, prices the ebook at $5.49, and spends $1,200 on a copyedit and a cover. After the average 6-cent delivery fee, her 70% royalty comes to about $3.80 a sale, so 40 sales a month repays the $1,200 in about eight months. The IRS has self-employed writers report royalties as business income on Schedule C, where costs like hers are deductible and the profit can owe self-employment tax.

Before you pay anyone, divide the budget by your royalty per copy. That’s how many copies the book has to sell before it turns a profit.
