Your credit score helps set your mortgage rate, and the fixes that raise it take weeks to show up on your reports, so start three to six months before you apply. Pull all three credit reports, dispute any errors, pay card balances well below their limits before each statement closes, and keep your old cards open.

On a $300,000, 30-year fixed loan with 20% down, FICO’s loan savings calculator puts the annual percentage rate, the loan’s yearly cost with fees, at 8.06% for a 660 score and 7.76% for a 720. Raising your score from 660 to 720 would cut the payment it shows by $63 a month, or more than $22,000 over the life of the loan.

A mortgage lender pulls all three of your credit reports, each with its own score, and usually sets your rate by the middle one. Those scores typically come from older FICO versions mortgage lenders have used for decades, so the free score in your app may not match them. On loans sold to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, lenders can now use another model, VantageScore 4.0, instead.

Either way, an error or a high card balance on any one report can lower your middle score.

Pull all three reports at AnnualCreditReport.com, where each is free every week. Dispute any error with each bureau that lists it. A bureau has 30 to 45 days to investigate, so starting six months out leaves time for a second round.

Card issuers usually report each card’s balance on its statement closing date, so pay each balance down well below its limit a few days before the statement closes, not just by the due date.

Catch up on any past-due account now, since your score counts how far behind it is today. And keep old cards open, even unused ones. Closing a card removes its limit from your available credit and raises your utilization, the share of that credit your balances use.

A lender sees only what’s on your reports the day it checks your credit, so pull them this week and give each fix time to show up.
