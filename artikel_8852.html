Here’s How to Train From Now Through the New Year to Maintain Your Hard-Earned Marathon Fitness

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

A run coach and an exercise physiologist break down the weekly mileage and workouts you need to preserve your speed, strength, and endurance.

You’ve spent months training for your fall marathon, so the thought of losing that hard-earned fitness after race day probably stings. But at the same time, you can’t simply continue logging miles and workouts like you’re in race mode. Trying to stay razor-sharp year-round can lead to injury and burnout; your body needs rest after training for peak fitness.

So, instead, put your marathon fitness on ice. Freeze the adaptations you’ve gained and the endurance you’ve built. Then, you can pick up where you left off on, say, January 1, or whenever it’s time to lock back in for a spring marathon.

If you train right in the weeks between now and the new year, you won’t feel like you’re rebuilding fitness from scratch. Instead, you’ll preserve the key aerobic, muscular, and endurance adaptations you’ve worked so hard to develop.

To do that, we explain the minimum miles you need for maintenance and how speed, strength, and cross-training keep you in prime condition. Expect to return to race training feeling well-rested, confident, and ready to tackle another marathon cycle this spring.

In the first two weeks immediately following your marathon, you’ll want to focus more on rest and easy time-based jogging. Once you’ve recovered, however, you can return to tracking your miles more closely.

The bare minimum mileage you’ll need to maintain will vary from runner to runner, Heather Milton, CSCS, lead exercise physiologist at Primary MD and cofounder of Master My Run, tells Runner’s World. Much of it depends on how many miles you regularly put in over the weeks of your marathon training cycle.

“Science does not give us an exact number of what percentage of your marathon volume to decrease to that still allows you to maintain the same amount of aerobic fitness,” Milton explains. From her coaching experience, however, she’s found this number is likely around 50 percent of a runner’s typical weekly mileage.

David Hall, an RRCA- and USATF-certified run coach and founder of Running Coach CT, agrees. When planning his athletes’ schedules for the off-season, he looks at their typical running volume during marathon training and reduces their weekly mileage to no less than half. Every fourth week, however, mileage can drop to as low as 20 percent of their average to help facilitate recovery.

“A runner who was running 60 miles per week during their marathon build could definitely cut that in half,” Milton says. Or, take a runner who averaged around 40 miles per week. This individual could reduce their weekly mileage to 20, add in some cross-training, and expect to maintain much of their progress.

In fact, Milton adds, every runner can benefit from incorporating more of those cross-training sessions in the off-season. “Other forms of exercise can decrease the repetitiveness of the running motion and help you develop more well-rounded fitness, so I do prefer more variety from cross-training in the down-season,” she explains.

At the same time, Milton wants people to do the exercise they enjoy and, of course, runners love running. “I have to be reasonable about what a runner is willing to do and what keeps them healthy while they’re doing it,” she says. If you want to keep running four days per week in the off-season, that’s still workable—with one important caveat: You need to prioritize strength training.

When you’re in the thick of marathon training, running is your main focus. So while you’re still doing strength work during this time, it mostly helps maintain the strength you already have, rather than increase it, Milton explains. The off-season provides an ideal time to really build up and strengthen your muscle mass and bone health as much as possible.

Each week, Milton says to aim for two to three resistance training sessions that focus on heavier weights with a low to moderate number of reps. (You can do more than three weekly sessions as long as you’re not working the same muscle groups two days in a row.) This sets you up for a stronger return to your spring training cycle because it helps improve running economy, your overall neuromuscular health, and your fast-twitch muscle fibers, which help power you through fast bursts of running or hill climbing.

“Plus, when you start training for your next marathon, you’ll be in a stronger position to increase your running volume again because you’ve strengthened all of the areas of your body that were impacted by your previous race, which can help prevent injuries,” Milton adds.

During the off-season, you certainly don’t need to run every day to maintain your fitness. In fact, you can preserve key fitness markers—such as your VO2 max—with as little as three workouts per week.

“The goal is to maintain at least three to four aerobic days per week to maintain your fitness level,” says Milton. You can opt to run as little as once per week, as long as you continue to cross-train on the remaining days, she adds.

Aim for 30 to 60 minutes of whichever aerobic exercise you most enjoy—hit the gym for the elliptical or indoor pool, or get outside for skiing and cycling.

Similarly, the number of running days per week for Hall’s athletes varies, but many have success with three because it allows more time for strength and flexibility work. He also adds a second rest day to the schedules of his runners who typically train six days per week during a marathon block. But in both cases, consistency is crucial—you don’t want to go a week without doing anything. “Do something every other day at a minimum to stay active,” Hall says.

One of your runs each week should be a bit longer than the others, but it doesn’t need to match the lengths of your true marathon long runs. Milton typically recommends a long run between five and 13 miles, depending on the individual’s training volume.

If you don’t want to worry about tracking mileage in the off-season, that’s fine, too. Running for time instead of miles provides the same endurance benefits, Hall says. Around four weeks postmarathon, his athletes will typically start with one-hour long runs. “Then, they’ll go up to 75 minutes, then to 90 minutes, and then maybe up to an hour and 45,” he explains.

Over several weeks, this gradual progression sets his runners up with a solid base to build from when they start their spring marathon training. “But don’t push the pace,” he emphasizes. “These are easy, conversational efforts.”

Whether you run for time or miles, make sure you’re not increasing either too quickly. “Keep the same rules in mind as when you’re building mileage in marathon training,” Milton says. “Aim to increase by no more than 10 percent each week.” Recent research suggests that, to reduce injury risk, you can apply this rule to the distance of any single run, as well.

What does this look like in practice? Let’s say a runner is doing five aerobic sessions per week, which includes about 20 miles of running. Two are cross-training days and the remaining three are running days. They might choose to run three to five miles one day, five to seven miles another day, and then five to 13 miles for their long run. You also don’t need to increase distance as quickly as you would in marathon training, Milton explains.

Over three weeks, she says a runner might balance their mileage as follows:

Week 1: 4 miles, 6 miles, 10 milesWeek 2: 5 miles, 6 miles, 9 milesWeek 3: 3 miles, 7 miles, 11 miles

In the four weeks immediately following your marathon, don’t worry about fast workouts or hitting paces, says Hall. During those first two weeks postrace, he advises his athletes to go for 20- to 30-minute daily walks. In the third and fourth weeks, he reintroduces easy running and a touch of speed.

“The general rule I apply is: no hard running for the number of days that’s equal to the number of miles in the race. So for a marathon, we’re talking about 30 days of walking and conversational effort running—maybe dipping into tempo pace a bit—but certainly nothing above lactate threshold.”

After that time, Hall says runners can start to add back intensity. In fact, he encourages it, suggesting you incorporate either a 20-minute run at threshold pace or an interval session for the same amount of time. He also recommends alternating one mile at your threshold pace, followed by one minute of easy jogging or brisk walking, until you reach 20 minutes.

“Training at lactate threshold pace can make conversational effort feel easier and can help you run faster while still remaining mostly in an aerobic zone,” Hall explains. In other words, your body adapts so running at any pace starts to feel like it requires less work.

Another way to get in some speed during the winter season? Sign up for a holiday race like a Turkey Trot or Jingle Bell Jog, he says. “It can get boring if all you do is train. Racing a 5K keeps the interest alive and lets you put in a hard effort.” Keep in mind, though, you did just run a marathon, so you don’t need to race all out or cross the finish line with a PR.

According to both Hall and Milton, the golden rule that will help you maintain your hard-earned gains is consistency. Once you’ve recovered physically and mentally from 26.2, get back into a routine that keeps you moving. “Doing something is always better than doing nothing,” Hall says—because you can lose 8 percent of your VO2 max in four to six weeks if you pull the plug on activity completely.

Similarly, you’ll want to avoid “stop-and-go” training. “If you’re running two to four days per week, keep running two to four days per week, every week—within reason, because life gets in the way sometimes,” Milton says. But running 30 miles one week, doing nothing for three weeks, and then trying to get back into it can lead to a real struggle, she adds.

As long as you do your best to stay consistent—and don’t stress when the holidays, travel, or other engagements inevitably interfere—you’ll find your fitness quite close to where you left it after your fall marathon. All that’s left for you to do? Thaw it out with a fresh marathon training cycle this spring.
