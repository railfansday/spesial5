8 Types of Financial Advisors – Hire for Your Problem, Then Ask Who Pays Them

A financial advisor can be a planner, a broker, an insurance agent, or a tax pro, each trained and paid differently. Name your problem first, whether it’s taxes, an estate, investing, insurance, debt, or a whole plan. Then hire the professional built for it, and find out how they’re paid, since their pay can affect what they recommend.

The title on the business card won’t tell you which one you’re dealing with. FINRA, which oversees brokers, calls “financial advisor” a generic job title, one that investment professionals without any specific credential or license sometimes use.

The license, registration, and credential behind the title tell you what you’re getting. FINRA’s BrokerCheck and the SEC’s Investment Adviser Public Disclosure site are free, and they show whether a broker or an investment adviser is registered, the licenses they hold, and any disciplinary history.

Then find out who pays them. A commission pays a professional when you buy or sell, so it rewards a sale, while a fee you pay directly doesn’t depend on which product you choose.

A financial planner looks at your money as a whole, from saving for college and retirement to taxes, insurance, and your estate, and gives you a plan to follow. Look for the Certified Financial Planner, or CFP, mark, which takes coursework, a bachelor’s degree, an exam, and thousands of hours of experience. It also commits the planner to act as a fiduciary, in your best interest, whenever giving you financial advice, and CFP Board’s verification tool shows who holds it.

A fee-only planner takes no commissions, while a fee-based one earns fees and commissions, so part of their pay can depend on what you buy. Hire one when a decision or change affects several parts of your money at once, such as a new baby, an inheritance, or the move from a paycheck to retirement income.

An investment adviser manages your investments and advises you on them over time, usually for a yearly fee based on your account’s value. At 1% a year, a $500,000 account pays $5,000, and the fee rises as the balance does. Advisers generally must register with the SEC or their state and must act in your best interest, and the SEC’s site shows the registration and any disciplinary history. Hire one when you’d rather hand the investment decisions to someone who keeps advising you.

A robo-advisor is an online service that builds and manages a portfolio for you from your answers to a questionnaire. Most are registered investment advisers too, and their fees are often lower than a human adviser’s, usually a yearly percentage of your balance or, at some services, a flat monthly fee. Compare robo-advisors on that fee plus the expenses of the funds they buy, since both come out of your balance. A robo-advisor is a simple way to start investing when you don’t need help with taxes, insurance, or a plan.

A broker places trades for you and recommends what to buy, sell, or hold, earning a commission, or a markup added to the price, on each trade. Brokers must act in your best interest when they make a recommendation, but that duty doesn’t extend to monitoring your account afterward. BrokerCheck lists a broker’s licenses, such as the Series 7, which qualifies a broker to sell all kinds of securities.

Use one when you’ll make your own decisions and trade only now and then. Some professionals are both broker and adviser, so ask which role they’re in for each recommendation.

For taxes, look for a certified public accountant, licensed by a state board of accountancy, or an enrolled agent, licensed by the IRS after a three-part exam. Either one, like an attorney, can represent you before the IRS in any matter, including an audit, and the IRS’s directory of preparers with credentials shows who holds which.

Few bill by the hour. Most charge for the return, starting from a base fee that rises with its forms or complexity, and the IRS warns against any preparer whose fee is a percentage of your refund. Pay one when your return needs more than tax software can handle, such as a rental, a business, or an IRS letter you don’t understand.

An estate planning attorney drafts the documents that say who gets your property and who decides for you if you can’t, such as a will, a trust, and powers of attorney. Attorneys are licensed by their state’s courts or bar, so check with your state bar before you hire one. Many charge a flat fee to write a will and an hourly rate for other work, so get the fee agreement in writing. Hire one when you have children under 18, own a home or a business, or have a blended family.

An insurance agent sells policies such as life, disability, home, and auto coverage, and the insurer pays the agent a commission on each sale. A captive agent sells for one company, while an independent agent can sell policies from many, so you can compare quotes in one place. Make sure the agent is licensed in your state, which you can check with your state insurance department.

Talk to one when you’re buying coverage you’ll keep for years, such as life insurance or disability insurance. Price the same coverage directly from insurers too, since a policy bought direct may cost less without an agent’s commission.

A nonprofit credit counselor reviews your income, bills, and debts and helps you build a plan to pay them. If a debt management plan fits, you make one monthly payment to the agency, which pays your creditors, who may accept less interest or drop some fees.

Counseling is often free or low cost, though a plan can carry fees, so get any one-time or monthly fee quoted in writing. Look for a counselor certified by an outside group, such as the National Foundation for Credit Counseling. Call one when credit card or medical bills have grown past what you can pay down on your own.

Ask every candidate which license, registration, or credential they hold. Look up the record of a broker or an investment adviser on BrokerCheck or the SEC’s site, and check a planner’s CFP mark with CFP Board.

Last, ask whether they or their firm earn anything when you buy what they recommend. A yes doesn’t rule them out, but get a second opinion from someone you pay directly before you buy.
