Why the Word “Boundaries” Makes Boomer Parents Mad

When the topic of boundaries comes up, a Millennial woman (born 1981-1996 according to Pew Research) will vigorously nod in agreement. But when that same daughter tries to install those boundaries with her Baby Boomer mother, (born between 1946-1964, according to Pew Research), the reaction can be swift and negative: Mom gives a raised eyebrow, a long silence, or an extreme and snarky reply.

How did a word meant to build healthier relationships become one that is so triggering? Why do boundaries make your family members feel rejected? The issue isn't the new behavior and delineation itself, but what the word signals.

For some adult daughters (Gen X were born 1965-1980; Millennials born 1981-1996; and even adult Gen Z (born 1997-2012, according to Pew Research), boundaries represent a way to honor yourself, not give up on the elements in a relationship that matter to you, and hopefully reflect your evolving emotional maturity. For a Baby Boomer parent, boundary talk sounds like withdrawal, rejection, and pulling away from the family (or particularly from them).

Part of the problem is that the paradigm of “Boomers vs. Millennials” has become a ready-made cultural storyline. In a 2026 study of Instagram memes about the two generations, media researchers Centorrino and Ferrigno found that the largest group of memes did one thing above all: draw lines between generations. That is, sow division between the Millennial and the Boomer. These memes ranged from dismissive jokes like “OK Boomer” to side-by-side comparisons of what each generation was like at the same age, but with a negative twist. The researchers argue that generational labels work less like neutral categories and more like symbolic boundaries, sorting people into “us” and “them.” It can become a crass classification system or a way of saying "they're not like me and my peers."

With this culturally sensitive division in mind, when a daughter deploys the language of boundaries, her mother may not hear a healthy limit. She may hear one more line being drawn between “your generation” and “mine.” In short, she feels pushed away and made fun of for her behaviors and ways of doing things. That's a pretty big barrier to hearing their adult child in a neutral or healthy way.

Research on adult development helps explain why this mismatch runs so deep. Karen Fingerman, a leading researcher on parents and adult children, and her colleagues draw on socioemotional selectivity theory. This theory suggests that as people age, they become more invested in keeping their longest-standing close ties than in forming new ones. That means they get really zoned-in on their adult children, their own siblings, and lifelong close friends.

In a 2024 study of more than 300 adults aged 65 and older, Fingerman’s team found that nearly three-quarters of participants’ long-term relationships were with family. That means most of what an older person wants is from their adult kids. They're invested in these long-term relationships with a deep intensity. However, the parent-child ties were also complicated. The time older folks spent with their family members was linked to more stressful conversations and intense moods (both positive and negative). All this means that Boomer parents are putting an intense lens on their adult child and it's kind of stressful.

And here's the rub. That intensity only goes one way. Researchers have described the concept of “intergenerational stake”: parents tend to be more invested in their children than children are in their parents. In a 2015 study of more than 600 middle-aged adults, Kira Birditt, Fingerman, and colleagues found that people rated their relationships with their children as both more important and more negative than their relationships with their own parents. Friction, in other words, can be a sign of how much a relationship matters. In sum, the more an adult child matters to their parent, the more likely there's tension as they seek connection.

Why does that friction occur? Fingerman’s earlier work points to an answer. In a 1996 study of 48 mothers over 70 and their middle-aged daughters, she introduced the term “developmental schism” to describe tension that arises because mothers and daughters are at different points in adult development. That's the sandpaper feeling between mothers and adult daughters, even when there's a lot of love. Two of the biggest sources of that tension were independence and how much importance each person places on the relationship. Many of today’s Boomer mothers were once the daughters in research like this, navigating the same tug-of-war with their own mothers. They haven't forgotten that. They're trying to discern how to manage things well with their own daughters, but sometimes it still comes off poorly.

That is the boundary conversation in a nutshell. A daughter may be focused on protecting her time, energy, and independence. Her mother is at a stage of life when she is intensely focused on family ties. She's laser-focused on keeping the relationship close, but maybe doesn't have the skills to make it happen, just the desire for it to be so. Both daughter and mother are responding to real needs. They just aren’t the same needs.

And nowhere does that mismatch become more visible than during the holidays, when traditions, expectations, and family identities are on full display.

As a family communication researcher, I’ve spent more than a decade studying the invisible work of daughtering: the task, emotional, cognitive, and identity labor adult daughters do to keep families connected. Lately, I’ve become especially interested in what happens when daughters begin to renegotiate that work.

Boundary conflict isn’t really about what a daughter is willing to do or a wall she's putting in place with her mother. The tension is about what these boundaries end up meaning to everyone else. A daughter's work (or lack thereof) ripples to every corner of the family. When she decides to prioritize some needs more than others and set down some of the unnecessary labor she's been doing, expect the family to shift, either seismically or gently, but it will change nonetheless. But you should know, daughter, you have every right not to abandon yourself and your needs. Set the boundary, but don't be shocked when others dislike it or feel hurt. Wait it out with empathy and gentle understanding. A new paradigm is on the horizon.
