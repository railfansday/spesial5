Stuck in a Rut: Escaping 'The Vicious Cycle' Through Reading

Even children can get stuck in a rut, spinning their wheels in ruminating thoughts or maladaptive behaviors that don’t serve their mental health and well-being.

Enter Newbery Honor-winning author Kyle Lukoff’s latest picture book, The Vicious Cycle, about a unicycle who gets stuck in a rut and the friend who inspires it to try something new, at least for a little while. Lukoff shares the inspiration behind this insightful book that speaks to the courage it takes to change, and how literature has played a role in his own mental health journey.

Q: Share a bit about your background and what inspired you to write The Vicious Cycle.

Kyle Lukoff: I've spent a lot of my professional life thinking deeply about picture books. I was a lead bookseller in the children's section of a Barnes & Noble in a neighborhood with a high population of young children, and that made me decide to get my master's degree in library science with a focus on children's literature and youth services.

From there, I got a job as an elementary school librarian, with students aged 2 through fifth grade, and spent many hours a day sitting cross-legged on the floor reading to kids. My first published works were all picture books, and even now, with many novels and speeches and short stories under my belt, I consider myself a picture book author who sometimes writes other things as well.

The inspiration for The Vicious Cycle is truly underwhelming. "You know what would be funny," I told a friend, "is if there was a picture book about an angry unicycle, and it was called 'The Vicious Cycle.'" That idea percolated in my subconscious for a year or two before I finally figured out what the story there was.

Q: In this moving picture book, a unicycle finds itself stuck in a rut until it finds the courage to break free from the well-worn path it dug for itself. How do we find ourselves stuck in our own vicious cycles in life? What role might trauma play in this process?

Kyle Lukoff: I have said, jokingly, that "this is a picture book about trauma," but it really is. It's about how we can be hurt so badly that we can't find a way out, even though others might see it clearly laid out in front of us.

I know I have gotten stuck in quite a few bad patterns, and even now I have to refuse to go down those same paths, and at least try to find new mistakes to make and new problems to create for myself. I am not a therapist or a psychologist (that's my husband's job), but I have been in therapy for most of my adult life, and have worked very hard to build a life for myself that isn't just going around in circles.

Q: In the story, how does a new friend inspire the unicycle to find courage? What can we learn from her example about being a friend to someone who is “stuck” in regards to their mental health?

Kyle Lukoff: You know, the story itself isn't sure what makes the unicycle take that first step (wheel?). It just happens one day, because there's no grand moment when all of your problems go away; there's just a day where you feel better and want to try something different.

I think the most important thing about Emily's role in this story is that she doesn't allow herself to become trapped alongside the unicycle. It is her decision to join it in its path. When it's time for her to leave, she leaves. When she wants to come back, she comes back. She helps the unicycle only in ways that serve her, not in ways that are codependent or unhealthy or self-sacrificing. And she's not even trying to fix it. She just likes being with it, and hopes that her presence might help, but isn't behaving as if she owes the unicycle anything, or [vice versa]. I, personally, have a lot to learn from Emily.

Q: The little girl doesn’t pull the unicycle out of its rut—the choice to take a new path belongs to the unicycle alone. How does this mirror our own self-determination and healing process?

Kyle Lukoff: Without going into too much personal detail, in my early 20s, I was suffering from some very severe and dangerous mental health issues. I had thought that if I got hospitalized I'd be surrounded by people who had the secret knowledge that would make me better, but all they did was help me stay alive until I was a little more medically stable, and then I was discharged.

The most important thing I learned from that experience was that no one was going to save me, and that I had two choices: I could do the hard work it would take to get better, or I could be miserable and unwell for the rest of my probably-shorter life. I decided to go with the former, because no one was going to do it for me, and because the latter seemed unpleasant.

Q: How do you hope The Vicious Cycle helps young readers, and their grown-ups, to take a healthy perspective on mental health and healing?

Kyle Lukoff: I wanted to point out something about the ending. The unicycle gets out of its rut, yes, but at the end of the story it goes back to it, because "the outside was unfamiliar. And a little scary."

There is no one simple straight path to healing, or to a better life. You try something, maybe it works, maybe it doesn't, maybe it works for just long enough for you to find something else. Maybe you relapse, or retreat back into unhealthy patterns, but the next time you try to get out, it might be a little easier.

Healing is no more linear than the unicycle's rut, but at least there's the chance for something new around the bend.
