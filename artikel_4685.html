The Courage to Disagree

At 23, Mike Soutar was appointed editor of the British teen pop magazine Smash Hits. It was an amazing opportunity at such a young age for someone who had left school and started life as a journalist just six years earlier.

Soutar was much younger than editors working on other titles; in fact, he was younger than almost everyone on his own team. Feeling the need to be seen as a strong, decisive leader, he modeled himself on the powerful authority figures he had seen in Hollywood movies.

His leadership was falling flat, and after a couple of weeks, the organisation’s CEO, Sir Robin Miller, visited him. Sir Robin gave Soutar some advice that has stuck with him to this day: “A great day in the office is when I lose more arguments than I win."

What initially seemed counterintuitive—how can you assert authority if you are constantly shown to be wrong?—became a central mantra for a career that took Soutar to the pinnacle of the media world.

Encouraging disagreement lies at the heart of Soutar’s definition of a great leader. Why, if disagreement can be so valuable, are we often so reluctant to challenge each other?

Many of us naturally like the easy life. It’s tempting to keep our heads down, get on with our work, and avoid being marked as a troublemaker.

In her research into speaking truth to power, Professor Megan Reitz, leadership expert and author of Speak Out, Listen Up, has explored why people remain silent, even when they have something important to contribute.

Reitz told me about a leader who was frustrated that employees weren't speaking up and assumed they needed to become more assertive or articulate. But when she and her colleagues spoke to those employees, one response revealed a rather different problem: "Last time somebody spoke up around here, they disappeared."

The issue wasn't a lack of courage. It was what people believed might happen if they challenged authority. As Reitz explained, our willingness to express an opinion depends not just on our own confidence, but on how the person listening behaves and how safe they make us feel.

Growing awareness of the need for psychological safety has led leaders to tell their teams it’s OK to speak up and challenge. But telling people they have a safe space isn’t enough; you have to demonstrate it. Trust develops through repeated interactions and consistent behaviour.

When Mike Soutar reflected on the advice he took from Sir Robin Miller, he explained how he saw a culture of positive dissent in the workplace evolving.

“It starts with trust so that everybody knows that they can voice their feelings, their doubts, their own opinions.

From there, it moves into constructive dissent, where ideas are challenged rather than individuals attacked. Then it moves into decision-making, where the leader weighs the arguments and reaches a conclusion.

Finally, you move to accountability, where the leader parcels out the jobs, timelines, and deadlines, with everybody getting behind the decision that's been made.

When I understood this, it transformed my capabilities as a leader because it meant I could take people with me.”

Building constructive dissent on a foundation of trust creates an environment where people actively seek alternate opinions to create better outcomes, rather than spiraling into a destructive culture of blame and competition.

Disagreement that leads to better decisions ultimately strengthens working relationships, creating a positive cycle that reinforces a safe space for dissent.

The ability to challenge and dissent doesn’t mean you will always get your way, but you should always be heard. That only works when the leader genuinely engages, listens, and is seen to welcome challenge.

Soutar later moved into his first managing director role at the London dance radio station Kiss FM, where one of his first moves was to change the station’s playlist policy. He wanted the station to focus on playing only "new" music—nothing more than two years old. To mark the shift, he planned a symbolic bonfire of old records in the station’s car park.

The proposal provoked strong opposition among both staff and listeners, who felt that Soutar was destroying a culture they cared deeply about. People warned him, but he dismissed their criticism.

“We didn't end up having a bonfire. I’m glad this happened before social media existed, because I think I would have been hounded out of a job.

All around me, I had people telling me in advance, ‘This is a really bad idea’. And I didn't listen to them because I took it personally.”

Knowing that constructive dissent matters is one thing, but welcoming it when your own judgment is being questioned is quite another. A culture of dissent has to be source-agnostic, focusing on the idea itself rather than the person who suggested it.

Professor Reitz, whose research explores speaking truth to power, warns that leaders often underestimate the signals they send when challenged. They may genuinely believe they are approachable, yet their colleagues experience something quite different.

Reitz calls these behaviours "shut-up signals," the often-unintentional gestures and responses that discourage people from speaking openly. She describes how her own "thinking face" can become a frown when she's particularly interested in a conversation. Someone who doesn't know her might interpret that expression as disapproval.

The danger is particularly acute when someone challenges a leader's judgment. As Reitz warns, "If you respond poorly, you can just forget about ever getting difficult feedback again."

Leaders need to respond to dissent with curiosity rather than judgment. Seek to uncover why someone disagrees and what they might see differently.

We can often react too quickly, shutting down debate without taking time to reflect. Even if the challenge isn’t ultimately accepted, a good leader will return to the person who flagged an alternative approach, acknowledge their contribution, and explain what they have decided.

Demonstrating that their perspective was considered and valued reinforces trust and encourages them to try again.

Sir Robin Miller made a huge impact on the young Mike Soutar when he said, “A great day in the office is when I lose more arguments than I win.” He helped Soutar recognise that effective leaders don’t need to win every argument, but that better decisions come when you invite dissent.

Constructive dissent requires challenge on both sides: the courage to challenge and the courage to invite challenge.

When was the last time someone challenged your thinking, and what did your response teach them about whether they should do so again?
