How to Deal With People Who Always Excuse Their Bad Behavior

You’ve met them; I’ve met them: folks who seem always critical or controlling, forgetful or unreliable, and often good at apologizing later. But the apology often ends with a comment like, “I guess it’s just the way I am; I’ve been this way my whole life.” Or, “Because I’ve been doing _____ for so long, I guess my brain is wired to think in terms of ___.” Or, “Because I struggle with—ADHD, depression, anxiety, etc.—I can’t always do _____—e.g., not be late or follow through.”

You want to be sympathetic and understanding, but over time, it all gets old. In your heart of hearts, it starts to feel like a default excuse—their way of dismissing your feelings, of not considering or caring about you, and of leaving you not only frustrated but, at times, even emotionally manipulated.

What’s going on? Why do some people lean on personality weaknesses or mental health diagnoses to explain or excuse what is often simply bad behavior? Here are five common sources:

Your father-in-law always seems critical of you, but in his mind, he is just expressing his opinion, making conversation, or even being helpful by offering suggestions. Pointing out that you feel criticized doesn’t help—he either thinks you’re being too sensitive or rationalizes it by blaming his ingrained habits: Because I’ve been supervising people my whole life, I guess I’m just wired to see the negative.

For some, it’s understandable to be exhausted from trying to manage their depression, anxiety, ADHD, trauma reactions, etc. They've been through dozens of treatments, dozens of drugs that make minimal change at best, but no real relief. They are left feeling that this is what they have to deal with and, often, that they're sorry it affects you as well.

This takes resignation to a higher level, where their struggles have solidified into their overall self-concept: I’m not just a person who struggles with high anxiety, but I am an anxious person, or You don’t understand—I have ______. Sometimes these folks can, understandably, seem overly assertive and rigid, or, once they've informed you, expect you to be automatically accepting and accommodating. If you are not, they can quickly get upset.

Sorry I can’t come into work today, show up for the party, finish my work assignment, or so on because I’m having a flare, I forgot to take my meds, or my _________ is really bad. This goes from explanation to manipulation because they are lying, and their motive is clear: to use their real condition to get what they want. This is often a characterological problem where they lack empathy and use people as tools for their own ends.

So how should you respond in these situations? At a basic level, it’s about shaping a two-sided relationship where you can only control your side. Here are some suggestions.

If your father-in-law keeps brushing off your comments about his criticism, even though you’ve gently explained how it affects you, it’s time to drill down: Ben, I know your comments come from good intentions, and you probably think I’m being overly sensitive, but I don’t understand how, if you care about me—and I believe you do—you don’t make an effort to stop doing what feels hurtful to me.

One obvious path is to have a conversation about that—the giving up—not as a therapist or as someone trying to fix them, but simply as someone who cares about them and wants to understand why they are so discouraged. Your deeper understanding can help you feel less frustrated and more accepting, and the conversation may help them be more sensitive to you.

Time to set boundaries. If you have the clout, call them out on their behavior—require a doctor’s note when they are out sick, or hold them to deadlines at work. If the relationship is more peer-to-peer, do what you need to stop feeling repeatedly taken advantage of: Speak up and tell them how you feel, but more importantly, stop making excuses for them, stop doing the heavy lifting in the relationship, and maybe end it.

Whether it's your dismissive father-in-law, your overly assertive or discouraged friend, or your manipulative employee, they may react negatively to your response, whatever it may be. This is about repairing communication. Here, you work on your side of the relationship by acknowledging their feelings and clarifying what you were trying to convey, without responding in kind or feeling the need to back down. Be sensitive and calm, yet assertive.

Relationships are no longer healthy when they feel out of balance—when you’re not getting enough back for what you give, when you feel used, abused, or simply dismissed. Step back, assess the relationship climate, and advocate for yourself to get what you need.

Is it time to stop making excuses for them and accepting what you get?
