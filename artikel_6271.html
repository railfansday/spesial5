Quantum energy harvesting could turn an unpredictable source into a predictable charge

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

Three physicists show on paper that quantum energy harvesting can take power from a jittery source and leave the jitter behind every time.

Electronic devices generally need a stable supply of electricity, and energy picked up from the surroundings is rarely tidy. Smoothing it out first wastes much of what was collected, especially at low voltages.

Three physicists have shown on paper that a tiny quantum system can harvest energy from a jittery source and end up fully charged every time, with none of the jitter.

Oscar Dahlsten, an associate professor of physics at City University of Hong Kong, led the work. His co-authors were Zihan Wang in the same physics department and Fei Meng of the University of Glasgow.

Dahlsten also holds a post at Southern University of Science and Technology in Shenzhen.

Nothing was built. The result is a set of mathematical proofs with one worked example.

The proofs show that the method works with a much wider range of sources than the original proposal considered.

“We initially set out to show that such a protocol might be impossible. To our surprise, our efforts led us to a quantum solution that could achieve exactly the deterministic energy transfer we were looking for,” Dahlsten said.

A rectifier turns a wobbling current into a steady one, and at low voltages it loses a lot. Part of that loss can’t be engineered away.

A perfect rectifier could turn the random jiggling of warm matter into usable current, and that would break the second law of thermodynamics. That law says you can’t get useful work out of heat alone.

Dahlsten’s group proposed a different quantum route to energy harvesting in a 2025 paper.

Instead of smoothing the source, put a quantum system with two energy levels, a qubit, between the source and the device being charged. A qubit has many paths from its low level to its high one.

Many different source behaviors can push the qubit along different paths and still land it on the high level at the same moment, charged, with the source’s randomness left behind.

In that first version, the source was treated as an ordinary wave. Whatever point in its cycle the wave started at, the qubit ended up charged at the same time.

A simple oscillator, such as a pendulum or a spring, responds differently depending on the wave’s starting point and cannot achieve the same predictable result.

Which sources the trick works for was the open question.

The new paper answers that in two steps. First, suppose you don’t know which of several source states you have, only that each one on its own would charge the qubit.

Then the uncertain mix charges it too, because the physics of the mix is the average of its parts.

The second step is where quantum theory does something classical physics can’t.

In quantum theory, one and the same uncertain mix can be described as a blend of entirely different pure states, and no measurement can tell the descriptions apart.

So every pure state in any of those alternative descriptions must charge the qubit as well, and so must any superposition of the original states.

As a result, the family of usable sources widens considerably. The team also calculated how many parameters describe these usable source states.

A plain mix of a given set of states has about as many adjustable knobs as there are states. Their superpositions and mixtures together have roughly that number squared.

A real source is unlikely to match a textbook state exactly, so the team also asked what a slightly wrong source does to the qubit.

They proved that two qubits charged by two slightly different sources can end up no more different than the sources were to begin with.

In classical physics, that guarantee offers little useful information.

That is why classical systems can be so sensitive to their starting conditions.

Two quantum states can be nearly indistinguishable, so a small difference between sources guarantees a small difference in the qubits’ final charge.

The worked example is the simplest quantum model of light meeting matter, a single atom-like qubit and a single mode of the electromagnetic field.

The field states that behave most like a classical wave are called coherent states.

Every coherent state of a given strength, whatever its phase, charges the qubit at the same moment.

So do mixtures of them, and so do superpositions of a wave with the same wave shifted by half a cycle. Physicists call those cat states, and making them has become a central theme of experimental physics.

What can’t work shows up in the same example. A field in thermal equilibrium, warm light at some temperature, always carries some chance of being empty, and an empty field can’t lift the qubit at all.

So heat on its own fails, which fits the second law.

Every result in the paper is a calculation, and the paper names superconducting circuits and single atoms in cavities as the platforms where someone could try it.

Nowhere does the paper estimate how much energy such quantum harvesting could deliver or how quickly.

So there is no figure yet to set against the rectifiers in real devices that gather stray energy from their surroundings.

The authors wrote that the next steps are to extend the argument to systems with more than two levels, and to systems described by continuous variables. New transfer regimes may turn up there.

The full study was published in the journal Physical Review Letters.
