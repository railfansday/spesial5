Córdoba’s famous orange trees face a test in one of Spain’s hottest cities

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Olive and holm oak leaves kept working at temperatures that damaged orange and tipu, a 17-species test finds, a guide to heat-tolerant trees.

Orange trees line the streets and plazas of Córdoba, in southern Spain, and a tree from a warm climate seems like a safe bet for a hot city.

In a test of 17 common street and park trees in Córdoba, the orange was among the first to fail in the heat.

The orange tree’s leaves had lost 90% of their photosynthetic function by the time they reached 123°F (50.6°C). Olive leaves held on to 141°F (60.6°C), and holm oak kept working longer than any other species at the halfway mark.

Native Mediterranean and temperate trees tolerated more heat than exotic species from the subtropics, the study found.

The trees were sampled during one week in one city, so the rankings are a starting point rather than a verdict.

Sonia Vega-Rosete and Cristina Bastias of the University of Córdoba ran the test.

Córdoba’s summers already pass 104°F (40°C) on more than 15 days in some months, according to records from AEMET, Spain’s weather agency, and the count is rising.

Street trees cool a city with shade and with the water their leaves release. If heat damages the leaves, both services shrink.

The researchers picked 17 species that are common in Córdoba and span a range of origins. The native Mediterranean trees included olive, holm oak, cork oak and carob.

Ash, white poplar and elm stood in for native temperate trees. The exotics from the subtropics included orange, jacaranda, chinaberry, tipu and catalpa.

In the last week of June 2025, the team cut one sun-exposed branch from each of five mature trees per species, all growing in irrigated city parks.

Back in the lab, they punched leaf discs about 0.6 inches (1.5 centimeters) across and sealed them in bags.

The bags went into a water bath for 30 minutes at each of 11 temperatures, from 77°F to 140°F (25°C to 60°C), six discs per temperature.

Afterward the team measured how well each disc’s light-harvesting machinery still worked.

From those readings, they fitted a curve for each species and read off three thresholds.

The first was the temperature where damage began, and the other two were where half and 90% of function was gone.

Every species held up to about 108°F (42.5°C). Above that, the damage threshold ranged from 108°F for tipu to 125°F (51.5°C) for holm oak.

The halfway point separated the groups most clearly. Native species lost half their function at an average of about 125°F (51.5°C), exotics at about 121°F (49.6°C).

The gap between the two groups was unlikely to be chance.

For 90% loss, the averages were about 135°F (57.2°C) for natives and 130°F (54.4°C) for exotics.

Grouped by climate of origin, Mediterranean and temperate trees reached about 136°F and 134°F (57.6°C and 56.4°C) before losing 90% of function, and subtropical trees about 128°F (53.5°C).

Whether a tree keeps its leaves in winter made no difference. Evergreen and deciduous species were spread across the rankings.

“This allowed us to identify the temperature thresholds at which the photosynthetic performance of these species begins to be impaired, which could compromise their ability to ‘cool their surroundings,'” Vega-Rosete said.

The orange tree, chinaberry, catalpa and tipu are planted across Córdoba’s parks and streets, often for their looks or their history there. All four were among the least heat-tolerant trees.

The authors offer one explanation. A subtropical climate pairs warmth with humidity and plenty of water, and Córdoba supplies the warmth without the other two.

To measure that gap, the team mapped each species’ home climate from occurrence records in GBIF and other databases, matched to climate data from WorldClim.

For the subtropical species, the farther Córdoba’s climate was from the climate of their native range, the lower the temperature at which leaf damage began. Native species showed no such drop.

Holm oak, whose native range includes places as hot as Córdoba, had the highest damage threshold of all.

Leaf temperatures matter here. The authors note that leaves can run 14°F to 18°F (8°C to 10°C) hotter than the air around them, and up to 36°F (20°C) hotter in full sun.

A 104°F afternoon can put a leaf past the damage threshold of several of these trees.

The heat-tolerant trees shared a build. Their wood was denser, their leaves were thicker, and each leaf packed more dry matter into its area.

Species with heavier leaves per unit area lost half their function at higher temperatures, and species with denser wood resisted the onset of damage longer.

Such traits were most pronounced in the native Mediterranean evergreens.

The subtropical exotics, and a few temperate trees such as white poplar and black locust, had the opposite build: bigger leaves with more nitrogen, geared to fast growth.

All of the trees grew in watered parks, so the thresholds may differ for trees on dry streets or in other cities.

The leaves came from a single week of a hot summer. The five trees of each species were pooled, so the test gives one curve per species rather than a range.

The thresholds also capture only one part of how a tree copes with heat. Heat-shock proteins and other chemical defenses weren’t part of the study.

The authors wrote that the same test needs to run in other cities, climates, seasons and years, and on more species, before the rankings can guide planting broadly.

The full study was published in the journal Urban Forestry & Urban Greening.
