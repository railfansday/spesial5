Ancient Egyptians Revered Cats as Sacred. Then 131 Skulls Revealed What Really Happened to Them.

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Fractured skulls, healed wounds, and signs of strangling suggest sacred cats lived—and died—brutally.

Not every Egyptian necropolis was meant for humans. Across Egypt, the cemeteries of Bubastis in the eastern Nile Delta, the Bubasteion in northern Saqqara, and Speos Artemidos near Beni Hassan, along with others in Giza and the Dakhla Oasis, were meant for animals, especially cats. Cats were seen as the earthly embodiment of the feline goddess Bastet. Yet although they were often mummified as votive offerings, their treatment did not always match their status.

Early archaeologists usually ignored animal mummies in favor of human mummies, which were often showcased at theatrical unwrapping parties. Some animal mummies were used as ship ballast for the journey home after an expedition, while others were powdered for questionable medicine, burned for fuel, or ground into fertilizer. During the 19th and early 20th centuries, however, some animal mummies were unwrapped (without a party) so that their skeletal remains could inform studies of Egyptian fauna. Many of these skulls remain in museum collections in Egypt and abroad. Zooarchaeologist Wim Van Neer of the Royal Belgian Institute of Natural Sciences and his research team studied one such collection: 131 exceptionally well-preserved skulls of small cats from Beni Hassan, now housed at the Natural History Museum in Vienna. Their goal was to find out how the cats lived before they died.

“Attention was also paid to the presence of pathologies and marks on the skulls, which could provide information about the conditions in which the cats were kept and their cause of death,” Van Neer and his colleagues wrote in their study, recently published in the Journal of Archaeological Science. In addition to carbon and nitrogen isotope analysis to determine diet, “characteristics such as dental abnormalities, infectious or metabolic disorders and trauma were recorded.”

Most of the skulls turned out to be from domestic cats (Felis catus), with several jungle cats (Felis chaus) among them. Many of the animals apparently suffered from conditions such as gum disease and infections, and some skulls had puncture marks and fractures that could indicate fights between cats. How the cats met their end, however, was more disturbing. Evidence suggests that cats were deliberately killed so they could be turned into offerings to Bastet. Fourteen of the skulls show traumatic injuries, including blunt-force and sharp-force trauma. Sharp-force trauma is thought to have possibly killed the humans who were sacrificed to join First Dynasty pharaohs in the afterlife. Beyond fight wounds, some injuries were inflicted long enough before death to show signs of healing, which suggests that the animals may have endured physical violence from humans while still alive.

When Van Neer and his team took a closer look at one skull, they found marks consistent with blows from a sharp metal weapon. Wounds on some of the other skulls were thought to have been made by strikes from a heavy wooden implement. The cats’ treatment before death also seems to have been deplorable. Although no ancient records describe how the cats were kept, the researchers inferred that they were probably held in crowded enclosures. This could explain the fights, as well as the attacks on domestic cats by jungle cats, which were caught in marshlands or along the banks of the Nile when Egypt was lusher than it is today. Isotope analysis showed that the jungle cats’ diet clearly differed from that of the domestic cats, further suggesting that this species was unlikely to have been bred in captivity.

Even among the wild-caught jungle cats, six of the seven skulls came from animals with conditions similar to those of their domestic relatives. This was probably a sign that all of the cats, the overwhelming majority of which were adults, were kept in conditions that caused illness and injury before death. Radiocarbon dating revealed when they died.

Cats are generally thought to have first been domesticated in the Fertile Crescent of the Near East roughly 10,000 years ago, although some recent genetic studies suggest it happened later, in Egypt or elsewhere in North Africa. In Egypt, the earliest clear evidence of domestic cats dates to the Middle Kingdom (c. 2000–1700 B.C.E.). However, demand for mummified cat offerings was greatest much later, in the Late and Ptolemaic Periods. Older feline mummies do exist, and one of the nine dated skulls goes back to the Third Intermediate Period. The other eight, however, date to between 380 and 40 B.C.E., from the end of the Late Period into the Ptolemaic era.

As for how the cats were killed, blunt- or sharp-force trauma appears to have been the main cause of death. Some cats were strangled, but a blow to the head was apparently the more common method. “Skull fractures were the most common cause of death, whereas evidence of strangulation occurred less frequently,” the researchers said. “Our observations of traumatic lesions on the skulls provide evidence of fatal injuries, as well as cases of animals that survived violent interactions with humans.”
