Pedro Almodóvar Didn't Think He Could Write A Novel. But Once He Started, The “Story Began To Pour Out”

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

A woman who might, or might not, be Penélope Cruz stares down at us from the wall. For now, I’ll accept that it is Cruz because the image is on a poster for Broken Embraces, a 2009 film she starred in. My hesitation in believing it’s her, however, is because finding a resemblance between the woman on the poster and Cruz requires a lot of generosity. It’s as if the artist had painted her based on a detailed description he’d heard, but he’d never actually seen his subject onscreen. “We were given this in India,” explains Pedro Almodóvar’s brother, Agustín, the head and owner of El Deseo, the production company behind Almodóvar’s films. “Specialized artists still hand-paint their movie posters, and the results are images like this.” The work’s imperfections give it a charm that no poster created by AI could ever match.

This brings to mind one of the stories in Almodóvar’s first novel, The Man Who Only Wrote on Airplanes. It depicts a Blade Runner–style dystopian future where replicants, jaded by digital perfection, are fascinated by the rough edges of human culture. The book has just been published in Spain (it is scheduled to be published in the United States, in Spanish, in February 2027), following the release of Almodóvar’s latest film, Bitter Christmas (which will be in select US theaters on November 13, and released nationwide on November 25).

Both works have much in common: They are works of autofiction with protagonists—the filmmakers played by Bárbara Lennie and Leonardo Sbaraglia in the film, and a very multifaceted character named Flavio Guijarro in the book—who appear to be alternative versions of their creator. At the same time, they are clearly different from Almodóvar, like the difference between Cruz and the woman on the Indian movie poster.

I ask Almodóvar why he decided to recount Flavio’s story in the form of a novel rather than a movie, but he appears to have not heard my question and answers instead as if I’d asked him why it took him so long to publish a novel, which is undoubtedly much more interesting: “I think the reason I hadn’t written a novel until now was that I wasn’t sure I’d be able to, even though I’d wanted to write one since I was a young man. At first, however, I didn’t even know myself that this story would be a novel. I started with a simple anecdote about an acting coach and an actor, and that opened the floodgates; the story began to pour out. When it was already over 30 pages, I sent it to my editor. He told me there was a novel there and that I should keep going.”

The 77-year-old director from La Mancha poses in a jacket, polo shirt, pants, and sneakers by Loewe.

He will answer many other questions that I haven’t asked but should have: “I didn’t know, for example, that I’d include my story ‘Addiction,’ which is a little treat for fans that had already appeared in Pain and Glory. It was going to be a monologue that Carmen Machi would perform in a small theater, but I ended up giving it to Asier Etxeandia in that film. I also wrote Pain and Glory very quickly. And in it, I inserted, like little snippets, more pieces that I’d already written. For example, there’s another story, just five pages long, about a chance encounter with an actor with whom I’d had a very bad relationship in the past [Eusebio Poncela, who starred in Law of Desire 40 years ago] and with whom, in the story, I ended up doing heroin. In real life, the fact that he was using heroin while we were filming was the reason we had a falling out. Thanks to those preexisting elements, which fit into the story, I was able to write that movie so quickly. And the same is true of my new book. What defines it is a sense of absolute freedom.”

And maybe that sentence is, in the end, the answer to all my questions for Almodóvar.

Vanity Fair España: Is that sense of freedom why you decided to write a novel instead of a screenplay?

Pedro Almodóvar: In Bitter Christmas, Leonardo Sbaraglia’s character says he needs absolute freedom to work. Just like me throughout my entire career. That freedom feels more intense with a novel because it’s a format with which I have less experience. I’ve woven it together from different stories, but they all revolve around the protagonist and it’s been very satisfying. It wasn’t a whim: The book has the same rigor to it as everything else I do, but that freedom is deeply rooted in my style. That’s why I wrote it so quickly. Everything flowed naturally.

There is a tradition of using a novel as a container for many stories, as with Don Quixote or Jan Potocki’s The Manuscript Found in Saragossa. On the other hand, speaking of influences, it’s interesting that when you wrote your Patty Diphusa stories in 1983 [included in Patty Diphusa and Other Writings, published by Faber & Faber in 1992], Anita Loos and pornographic photo novels are influences that appear in the mix, but in The Man Who Only Wrote on Airplanes, you turn to, let’s say, more distinguished names: Henry James, Nabokov, and Colm Tóibín, who even appears as a character.

I’m not trying to compare myself to Cervantes or Potocki, but yes, that’s what the book is. That’s precisely why this novel can’t be adapted for the screen: You’d have to choose one part of it and develop it, but as it stands now, that wouldn’t be possible. Those earlier influences are still with me—Anita Loos is certainly among them—and they continue to inspire me with both specific phrases and broader themes. In the novel, I name authors who inspire me and with whom I connect organically, such as Joan Didion and Virginia Woolf. But Patty and Flavio have something in common: a sense of urgency that drives them to write at a breakneck pace, out of a physiological need. Neither of them sleeps much, though in Patty’s case it’s because she can’t stop doing cocaine. Beyond that, other differences set them apart, including the differences between what life was like in the ’80s and what it’s like now.

What sort of differences are there, and where are they most apparent?

Take sex, for example. Patty Diphusa is an archetype of the 1980s, a time of absolute freedom in Spain, and her character can only be understood in the context of the utterly carefree and hedonistic lifestyle we led back then. If she sucks a guy's dick in a bathroom, she’ll then record her wild thoughts because she considers herself a sort of philosopher of eroticism. Although Flavio is a very free-spirited person, just like the book itself, he belongs to another era and another generation. Here, sex is part of a love story, and his attitude toward it is very different. I devote less space to the erotic scenes in this book because I assume people already know what it’s like to sleep with someone.

Almodóvar, in another room at El Deseo’s headquarters, wearing a Louis Vuitton jacket and a Prada knit polo shirt.

You’ve argued on different occasions that in the 1980s, many people in Spain chose that hedonistic and seemingly frivolous lifestyle as a reaction to the previous era, and that this was a political statement. Now we see a new generation adopting a different kind of stance. For example, all across Europe, people are taking to the streets to demand their right to housing and decent living conditions, and there’s not much frivolity in that. How do you interpret this change?

I completely empathize with and support the people filling the streets. They are demanding fundamental rights that are enshrined in the Spanish constitution and which are now in jeopardy: housing, health care, and education. I went to the encampment on the Puerta del Sol [in Madrid] only a few times, because I was in New York presenting Bitter Christmas at the New York Film Festival and also because I think a famous person doesn’t really fit in at these demonstrations—if we go, we distort the spirit of it. But I did go, and I bought them food because that’s what I thought they’d need, even though they told me they’d be better off with some tables. I don’t just support their initiative; I’m glad it happened. One thing I’d heard about young people, a generation I don’t know much about, was from a news article that said that 25% of young Spaniards wouldn’t mind living under a dictatorship like Franco’s. I shuddered. What happened in the Puerta del Sol shows that there’s a whole crowd of young people beyond those who go to Ferraz to pray the rosary [since November 2023, a group of Catholic protesters has gathered daily to pray outside the headquarters of the PSOE, Spain’s socialist party, on Madrid’s Calle Ferraz]. They aren’t rich, and they don’t have their lives figured out; they can’t work in the professions they studied to pursue, and they don’t earn enough money to buy homes. That’s terrifying. And the political parties have shown their true colors when it comes to housing. Of course, the PSOE is late to the game with the so-called Maricarmen decrees [a series of measures named after Maricarmen Abascal, whose eviction from her home and subsequent death in October galvanized activists], but at least it has reacted. The starkly different reactions from the Partido Popular [Popular Party] and Vox [center-right and far-right parties, respectively] already tell you what they think about this issue.

Would you agree today with that line from Gloria Muñoz in The Flower of My Secret: “With so much reality, the country’s ready to explode”?

She was talking in literary terms, but it’s true that this phrase has taken on a life of its own over time and has spilled over into other areas. The same thing has happened to another phrase: “A woman is more authentic the more she resembles the person she has dreamed of becoming,” as Agrado said in All About My Mother. It was a line written for a transgender woman, and one that makes perfect sense for her. But now it can be understood in relation to any kind of character: It speaks to authenticity, to staying true to oneself and to one’s own dreams. In the case of young people in our country, we’re talking about a social reality. The country has changed a lot, and we’re very polarized—though France is even worse off than Spain. Europe is in very bad shape overall. The kind of news coverage being produced plays a major role in this rise of the far right. Since the images promoted by the right are so jarring and sensationalist, TV stations pick up on them, and in doing so, they’re effectively promoting figures like Vito Quiles [a far-right activist and influencer]. Damn it, don’t put Vito Quiles on TV—it’s better if we don’t even know where he is or what he’s doing!

Are you particularly concerned about the attention that the far right is getting?

People like [Santiago] Abascal [president of the Vox political party] and [Javier]Milei [president of Argentina] want to do away with the state. Milei, the biggest loudmouth of them all, even aims to destroy the state from within the state itself, which is diabolical. I cite Milei as an example because he says so himself. And on top of that, people elect him for that very reason: He’s someone who has come to power democratically, just like [Donald] Trump. So we’re putting the tools of democracy in the hands of someone whose main goal is to destroy it. Furthermore, in a world so polarized—and with a far right that has no qualms about calling itself fascist—the emergence of AI creates a perfect storm.

One of the stories in your book is, in fact, a dystopian tale inspired by Blade Runner, in which large corporations with shadowy agendas seek to create a less human world. You have contributed so much to human creativity. Are you concerned about how that creativity is being taken over by AI?

I’m always open to change. It’s necessary for society to move forward. But I think the encroachment of AI on human agency is terrible. They should start regulating it now. I’m concerned about the jobs it will replace and also the synthetic culture it will create. In fact, a synthetic culture based on data already exists, and it lacks a soul. The genesis of a screenplay must be human, because AI doesn’t have the sense of doubt, the patience, or the capacity for stories to build up layers of meaning, as in Talk to Her and Bad Education. We need a society that cares about culture, promotes it, and supports it, and not a soulless culture focused only on the immediate. I haven’t used AI creatively, nor do I really know how to. But I’m concerned that we’re now unable to tell whether an image is real or not, given the level of perfection this technology has reached. And moreover, behind it all are very powerful people who know no limits.

On another topic, standards of beauty have changed, with cosmetic surgery and fillers now widely prevalent. For someone who places such importance on the expressions of actors that must also be a cause for concern.

Well, I wouldn’t mind if an actor in one of my movies had had plastic surgery. For example, I wouldn’t turn down Nicole Kidman for that reason—she’s not only a great actor, but she has also had plastic surgery, and she looks better than ever. Because, these days, actors who’ve had plastic surgery are part of the landscape. It’s so common that you no longer have to explain that a character has had it done; it’s accepted as a matter of course. What’s more, cosmetic surgery has advanced so much that high-profile figures who’ve had work done—like Donatella Versace, Lindsay Lohan, or Nicole Kidman herself—have completely erased the signs of their previous procedures with splendid results. [Editor’s note: Lindsay Lohan has denied having a facelift, while Kidman has never confirmed undergoing any plastic surgery.] The big problem is that, if you make films set in the past, you can’t cast women with plastic surgery or lip fillers; it would be anachronistic, just like putting a piece of furniture from the 1980s in a film about the Spanish Civil War. But it’s true that in my films, plastic surgery hasn’t usually been an option, for aesthetic reasons. Fortunately, there will always be people like Frances McDormand and Carmen Maura. We need older women who look their age.

What interests me most about your new novel is the protagonist’s relationship with an actor, Alez Mazado—a sex symbol who isn’t very intelligent but has a certain natural talent and is open to being molded. Flavio is his Pygmalion. There’s even an explicit reference to Hitchcock’s film Vertigo, which also features that kind of relationship. Do you often have that kind of dynamic with your actors?

I’m like Jimmy Stewart in Vertigo: With actors, I get involved in their hair, lip color, outfits—everything. When I watched Vertigo for the umpteenth time, I thought that character was a director: “He knows exactly what he wants,” they tell him when he takes Kim Novak to try on outfits at a store. The actors I work with and I have adapted to each other, just like in a marriage—in my films, they speak differently; the actors themselves say so. Aitana Sánchez-Gijón told me that she felt she could do anything after playing her role in Bitter Christmas. But I work differently with each actor. With Penélope, for example, I rehearse a lot, and the more we rehearse, the better she gets; then I shoot many takes, and the 10th one is better than the third. And if I can shoot 12, even better. But others don’t like to rehearse, like Antonio Banderas, because he’s a different kind of actor. He has extraordinary intuition and instinct. I don’t need to talk to him as much or push him so hard.

People talk about Los Javis [the moniker for actors turned directors Javier Ambrossi and Javier Calvo] as your natural heirs, in the wake of the success of La Bola Negra, which you coproduced through El Deseo. Do you think they will continue your creative legacy?

They definitely treat me like their mentor. They know entire films of mine by heart, even lines that I don’t remember. They’ve been deeply influenced by my films, just as I’ve been influenced by [Luis García] Berlanga, [Rafael] Azcona, [Luis] Buñuel, and [Carlos] Saura. Even if you don’t want to admit it, everyone has role models. I find it flattering, but to say they’re my heirs is to minimize their work. They have a style all their own. In fact, if I were to write the screenplay for La Bola Negra, it would be very different. I don’t feel like their father. I take responsibility for my films and nothing else.

How do you view their film’s unstoppable run at the awards and at the box office?

Los Javis are the big thing right now. Their film is going to be the highest-grossing Spanish movie of all time, although I don’t remember how much The Last Torch Song (1957), starring Sara Montiel, made back in the day. At El Deseo, we’ve had two fantastic years—first with Oliver Laxe’s film [Sirāt], which we also coproduced, and now this one. And I hope the Javis can keep on going and make it through the rest of their promotional tour and then the awards season. From my experience as an Oscar nominee, I know the best moment isn’t when they call your name, but when the ceremony ends. That’s when your life is your own again—whether to start on the next project or simply sleep if that’s what you prefer.

Have you shared that experience with the Javis? Do you give them advice?

Just yesterday I texted Javier Ambrossi, and he told me they’ve already had enough. And they have no idea what’s still in store for them! We’ll meet next week, and I’ll explain it all to him. At the Oscars, they’re the favorites in the best-international-feature-film category; they’re a sure thing. Although I experienced that curse of being the front-runner when Women on the Verge of a Nervous Breakdown was the favorite and then Pelle the Conqueror ended up winning—a film no one even remembers now. But anyway, everyone now expects La Bola Negra to be nominated in other categories as well, which is historic for our film industry. It’s great for us.

On a recent podcast, one of the actors from La Bola Negra, Yenesi, described the film as Federico García Lorca fan fiction—which is actually quite fitting, since its entire narrative is built on elements related to Lorca’s life and writings. Roland Barthes had already spoken of the death of the author, and Eugenio d’Ors famously said, “There is no true originality outside tradition. Everything that is not tradition is plagiarism.”

I’ve written that phrase down! I read it in one of [journalist and writer Francisco] Umbral’s columns; I suppose he got it from d’Ors.

In other words, Umbral appropriated it for himself, and in doing so, he proved its enduring relevance.

It’s inevitable that the things you like will influence your work. And the things you hate too. When you write, you have a general idea of the story, but as you develop it, what you create is rooted in things that are part of your life. And your life always resembles the things you’ve seen before. I’m fascinated by the phenomenon of fan fiction because of its contradictory nature; it’s a miracle that an entire culture has developed around this phenomenon, which exists outside the mainstream market yet at the same time feeds off the most mainstream works, such as Star Trek or Star Wars. Fan fiction is a declaration of love for the original and proof that great stories never end: There will always be someone to continue them. Just as people don’t die as long as there’s someone who remembers them.

Of course I have to ask you, with the promotional tour for Bitter Christmas coming to an end, what are you working on next?

Well, it was going to be a comedy, but after I finished it, even though I was 90% passionate about it, I realized there was something about the supporting characters that I didn’t like. So I’ve put it on hold, as I’ve done before with other projects. And now I’m writing something else. I’m well into it, but I can’t say anything. They’ll kill me if I do. It’ll be based on a book, and we’re currently working out the rights. This one will have plenty of humor too. Because I know for sure that, after Bitter Christmas, I don’t want to talk about myself anymore. I need different characters. And humor too.

Is there any movie you wish you’d made but that’s missing from your filmography?

There are several recent films I would have loved to have made. For example, Drive My Car by Ryusuke Hamaguchi and Anatomy of a Fall by Justine Triet. Or Sentimental Value by Joachim Trier. I mean, I should have made that movie! It has everything: the cinema, a house, a father and his daughters. Everything.

Should we just accept, by the way, that you won’t make your own movie about the Spanish Civil War? Or was Parallel Mothers already your take on it?

When I read Cosamala, Alana S. Portero’s latest book, I really wanted to adapt it. But it’s such a wonderful work of literature that translating it into images would greatly diminish the wonder and surprise of her writing. If the book proves anything, it’s that Alana is a tremendous writer. I’d have to find another angle from which to tell the story of the civil war. But I do want to do it.
