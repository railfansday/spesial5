IF YOU WANT to gain muscle, your first instinct may be to head to the gym and hit the dumbbells. And while lifting will never go out of style (in our humble opinion), we're here to tell you that's not the only way to build strength. If you're short on gear—or even just want to shake things up—it's time to add some of the best resistance bands to your training arsenal.

Resistance bands are relatively affordable, lightweight, and compact. Most importantly, they're one of the most versatile strength accessories you can buy. Adding a resistance band into your routine can be as simple as integrating it into your bodyweight warmup, or looping it onto a kettlebell to add a little extra heat to a set of swings.

"Because of the elastic properties, resistance bands recruit muscle fibers differently than free weights," explains Juan Guadarrama, CSCS, one of the creators of Men’s Health’s "7-Minute Workouts for Fat Burn".

Of course, there are tons of different types of resistance bands (and resistance band exercises) out there, from closed loop bands, thick latex bands, and bands with handles to ones that use anchors to attach to your door. Depending on the thickness, they also provide different levels of resistance, meaning that not every band workout is for light weights.

We've spent years testing dozens of the best exercise bands of nearly every type and thickness. Your most important consideration should be how you plan to use your resistance bands—for solo workouts, to add resistance to exercises, to help you with dips or pull-ups, or to warm up before your lifts. No one band will perfectly check every box.

Most resistance bands are made from latex and rubber (or some combination of the two) and use plastic parts for accessories like handles and door anchors. (If you have a latex allergy, there are latex-free resistance bands.) So we’re not going to list materials as a significant consideration.

Likewise, the bands in this guide are all similar in price, with no major outliers. Premium and/or unique band designs cost more, but expect to pay between $20 and $70 for bands. The price scales based on the band resistance and whether or not you’re buying a set. Below are the top three things you should consider when shopping around for a new resistance band.

The type of band you buy dictates the kind of workouts you can perform. Here’s a quick breakdown of the band styles you’ll see, save for a few unique models, most of which are on this list.

Also referred to as super bands, these are the quintessential resistance bands that most manufacturers sell. Because the band is a single loop, you have to get creative in performing specific exercises, but you can target most major muscles with a looped band. You can also wrap them around pull-up bars, barbells, and machines to change the resistance profile of an exercise.

Popular in physical therapy offices, mini bands are an excellent tool for activating the smaller muscles in your hips, legs, upper back, and shoulders. They can’t support your bodyweight and they don’t stretch far enough to allow for squats, presses, or curls. Think of these as a warm-up tool, not a workout replacement.

These bands are defined by a single tube of rubber with a handle on each end (usually). These bands give you the most freedom of movement, and the handles allow you to press and row more efficiently (compared to looped bands).

Some bands cite a single poundage versus a range. If a band lists its resistance as between 20 and 60 force, you’ll experience 20 pounds of resistance when the band is least stretched and 60 pounds at the most stretched position. Squatting with a 50-pound band doesn’t feel the same as squatting with a 50-pound dumbbell, either, since the force you’re resisting is literally changing with every inch you move.

To track your progress effectively, find a band that has you reaching within one to three reps of failure for your desired rep range. Stick with that band until the reps feel easier, and then move up to the next heaviest band you have. Progressing with bands isn’t as easy as with free weights and machines, but you'll make gains as long as you’re basing your effort on training intensity and not weight.

Resistance bands can be dangerous. If they snap while doing assisted pull-ups or a face pull, you’ll fall or injure your eyes or face. No band is wear-proof, so it’s essential to use them responsibly: don’t overstretch, inspect them regularly for tears, and avoid anchoring them against textured surfaces like the knurling on a barbell, which creates friction that weakens the material.

Manufacturers use different strategies to improve longevity. Some bands are built from layered latex for added reinforcement, while others encase the elastic in a protective sleeve that reduces wear and shields the user from a whip-like snap mid-rep. These features can’t eliminate risk, but they can extend the lifespan of your bands and make training safer.

We asked Strength in Diversity member Eric Sung about his favorite ways to incorporate resistance bands with clients and in his own training.

"The banded rear delt fly is great exercise to do as a warm up to prepare your body before an upper body session," says Sung. "Grab the band, hold it out in front of you, stretch it across your upper chest and try to resist the band as much and slowly as you can."

If you're looking to get started in training lower body plyometrics, put band-assisted squat jumps on your radar. "Tie a band to a high anchor, grab each end of it, assume your squat stance, and focus on jumping as high as you can with the assistance of the band. This is a great entry point," he adds.

Similar to free weights, resistance bands help create tension and place stress on the muscles, explains Sung. However, resistance bands assist in efforts to build muscle and strength in a few unique ways.

Because they deliver more resistance as you stretch them further, they offer a different challenge than free weights. This means your muscles face the most load and tension at the top of every exercise. For example, the top of a resistance band pushup is a challenge; the top of a bodyweight pushup is a position of rest.

Another way resistance bands help build strength is by assisting in movements that an individual cannot yet complete at baseline standards, explains Guadarrama. "It allows athletes to move through the full range of motion and acquire neuromuscular capacity," he explains. "Over time the body can adapt, learn the movement, and build the strength needed for the unassisted effort."

Bands can also be attached to plates and kettlebells to add more resistance to the eccentric or concentric phases of a given movement, explains Guadarrama. "Each of these methods has benefits to biomechanical advantages and providing variance in stress," he says.
