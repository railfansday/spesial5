Maladaptive Daydreaming and ADHD

Posted November 12, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

One of the greatest things about the internet is finding out you’re not alone. I feel sorry for those Victorian souls who had a weird thought and simply had to live with it. Instead of going on Reddit and searching, “Does anyone else pretend to be a chicken to get to sleep at night?” they sat there in their little nightcaps by candlelight, feeling like the biggest weirdos on Earth, when they could have been doomscrolling for three hours about how many people had the exact same fantasy and, even more comforting, even weirder and more bizarre ones. Which brings me to my most recent discovery. It not only made me feel less of an oddball, but it also gave me that now-familiar feeling: Has anyone on this planet ever had a truly unique experience?

According to subreddit threads, no. We are all infinitely bizarre and confused creatures, bumbling around and trying to look like we’ve got our sh*t together when we clearly don’t. That brings me a great deal of comfort, knowing I’m not alone.

As a woman with ADHD, I’ve spent my life feeling like an alien who has landed on Earth while everyone else received the manual on how to be human except me. It turns out that even that alien feeling is common among people with ADHD. Through my research into women with ADHD, I keep finding stories I thought were as odd and unique as that chicken dream, only to discover they’re not.

One of the most shameful aspects of ADHD for many of us is how much of our mental landscape is taken up by living in entirely fictional versions of our lives. Some of these are so vivid that they intermingle with reality, and it becomes hard to tell the real life from the fantasy. This might seem like a very strange concept to a neurotypical person, but many ADHDers will resonate with it on several levels. I used to do it so much that I truly didn’t realise it was a thing. I thought everyone lived in a parallel universe of epic proportions.

This is known as maladaptive daydreaming (MD), a proposed condition in which someone gets absorbed in vivid, story-like fantasy for so long that it takes over their life. The term was coined in 2002 by Eli Somer of the University of Haifa, who described it as extensive fantasy activity that replaces human interaction or interferes with academic, interpersonal, or vocational functioning (Somer, 2002). The fantasies usually have characters, plots, and settings, and people with MD can tell their fantasies aren’t real, which separates it from psychosis. It isn’t an official diagnosis, but as researchers like myself uncover more about the lived experiences of people with ADHD, it appears to be especially common in this population (Somer et al., 2017; Theodor-Katz et al., 2022).

What is it about ADHD that makes maladaptive daydreaming so appealing? From my own anecdotal research, it can be a form of escape from a world that often feels unkind and full of rejection. From early childhood, many people with ADHD experience increased levels of bullying and negative feedback because of their behaviour (Abregú-Crespo et al., 2024; Becker et al., 2017), which can create deeply felt low self-esteem and a sense of unworthiness. One way of coping can be to escape into a fantasy of “One day…I’ll show them all!”

There is something about the ADHD mind that tends to wander off into other dimensions, but when it does, what are the most common fantasies people engage in? Getting people to open up about the innermost workings of their minds is notoriously difficult, even in non-judgmental research settings, but a quick subreddit search throws up some revealing insights. Nowacki and Pyszkowska (2024) found that Reddit users described fantasies about being a perfect version of themselves, undoing past mistakes, and having imagined companions who offer constant support. Others imagined being a confident leader, the opposite of their real-life selves, or went back to painful memories to have their younger selves rescued. One poster dated the start of their excessive daydreaming to being bullied by siblings. While these accounts are not specific to ADHD, they show how fantasy can serve as both comfort and escape from a world that feels unforgiving.

So if your mind is off in another universe right now, don’t beat yourself up. You’re in very good company, and with a little kindness you can enjoy the daydreams without letting them run the show. In my book of ADHD hacks, How to Get Your Sh*t Together, I devote a whole chapter to forgiving yourself and all your ADHD quirks, and daydreaming is just one of them (Murphy, [2026]).
