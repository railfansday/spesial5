Tennis is Aces for Healthy Aging

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

There’s good reason why the United States Tennis Association calls it “the world’s healthiest sport.”

If we told you tennis has been shown to boost heart health, balance, strength, mental acuity, and mood plus add nearly 10 years to your life, you might think we also had a bridge to sell you. But it’s true: Research shows that even recreational tennis can have a seriously impressive impact on your health—now and for years to come.

“Tennis is a whole-body sport,” says sports medicine surgeon Alexis Colvin, MD, chief medical officer of the US Open. “It offers cardiovascular benefits, muscle strengthening, flexibility—all the things you need to deal with any physical challenges later in life.”

You also don’t have to be a star tennis player to reap the longevity benefits, Dr. Colvin adds. In a 2020 study she co-authored, researchers surveyed over 10,000 tennis players, most of them recreational; about 70 percent played just one to three days a week. The players scored higher than the general population on measures such as physical strength, mental and emotional health, and social engagement. “You don't have to be playing at a very high level to get those benefits,” Dr. Colvin says.

And those benefits appear to extend one’s life: When researchers followed 8,577 people for up to 25 years, they found that those who played tennis an average of 103 minutes a week lived 9.7 years longer than their sedentary counterparts. They also lived significantly longer than cyclists (who get an extra 3.7 years), swimmers (3.4 years), and joggers (3.2 years).

Even if you’ve never played tennis before, it’s never too late to start. Keep reading for five reasons to pick up a racket, hit the court, and do your future self a favor.

Tennis involves bursts of high intensity movement—sprinting to the net, running backwards to lob a volley, stopping and then lunging to the opposite side of the court to hit the ball—all of which can do wonders for your cardiovascular health. It boosts heart rate, maximizes your body’s use of oxygen, strengthens heart muscle, improves circulation, and can lower blood pressure and cholesterol levels. As a result, women who play tennis an average two to three hours a week have a 44 percent lower risk of developing cardiovascular disease and a 66 percent lower chance of dying from it.

When you sprint across the court, swing a racquet, or come to a sudden stop, you’re working your leg muscles, core, shoulders, back, and arms—in other words, strengthening nearly every part of the body. A recent study of more than 5,000 women between the ages 63 and 99 found that those who had greater muscle strength lived the longest. And because you’re also supporting your own body weight as you run and land, the sport also helps increase bone density, Dr. Colvin says. Stronger bones mean a lower risk of fractures and osteoporosis in the future, which is what you need if you want to age well.

Tennis requires coordinated movement and muscle control to maintain fluid footwork and smooth, continuous swings. Your core helps make that possible and is key to good balance. Both core strength and balance can help you avoid falls and fractures as you get older. Another part of balance is visuospatial skill—the ability to recognize environmental cues that help you stay upright. Tennis may help sharpen that, too. In a 2022 study, older tennis players who faced a disruption that could throw them off balance were able to react and steady themselves as well as younger players.

Tennis demands intense focus and decision making. “There’s a lot of active thinking going on,” Dr. Colvin says. “You’re making a lot of split-second decisions while you’re playing, plus thinking about the overall strategy of the game. That kind of mental engagement is important as we get older.” According to research, engaging in that kind of quick and strategic thinking while being physically active may be particularly good at promoting neuroplasticity and boosting cognitive function—both of which can stave off the cognitive decline that can come with age.

Studies have found that people who play tennis regularly tend to be less stressed or anxious than people who do lower-intensity exercise. They also report feeling more optimistic, less depressed, and a greater sense of well-being. Like other high-energy sports, tennis may boost mood by prompting the release of brain chemicals such as endorphins, dopamine, and serotonin. It’s also a fun game to play with friends. “The social aspect of tennis is one of its key benefits,” Dr. Colvin says. Numerous studies show that those kinds of community ties are essential for a long, healthy, happier life. With tennis, that’s built into the game.
