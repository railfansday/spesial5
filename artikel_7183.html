Want to Sell an Agency? Launch a Creator Marketing Firm

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

This story was originally published in On Background with Mark Stenberg, a free weekly newsletter that explores the key themes shaping the media industry. You can sign up for it here.

In recent months, the market for creator and social agencies has been, to put it lightly, brisk.

In September, the Madrid-based social agency Samy acquired Get Engaged, a U.S. social and influencer shop, in a deal valued in the low nine figures.

In October, Billion Dollar Boy Group bought Creator Authority, a business-to-business influencer agency, and the private equity firm Shamrock Capital, which owns ADWEEK, made an eight-figure investment in the social-first agency Saylor.

Earlier this month, the social-first agency LV8 merged with its sister company Mirage Digital on the back of a “high eight figures” investment from Velocity Capital Management. And last week, the private equity firm Falfurrias Management Partners took a majority stake in Freeman & Forrest, another B2B creator agency.

The deals are part of a record year, according to James Creech, founder of the creator M&A advisory firm Quartermast. There have been 30 agency acquisitions in the creator economy so far in 2026, up from 20 in all of 2025 and above the previous high of 28 in 2022, according to Quartermast data.

Overall, there have been 124 M&A creator-adjacent deals this year, the busiest on record, and roughly half of the agency deals have come since July, per Quartermast.

Behind the surge is a crop of boutiques founded during the pandemic-era influencer boom that have matured into businesses worth buying, which are run by founders who need more resources to keep up with what clients are asking for.

A specific set of firms—social-first creative shops, podcast agencies, TikTok Shop specialists, and B2B creator plays—are responsible for the majority of this interest, while the deals themselves are increasingly financed by a new cohort of buyers, including private equity firms, strategic roll-ups, and international firms. All of it is unfolding in a frothy market for creator economy deals, which is just now beginning to consolidate in response to urgent demand across a fragmented space.

“Creator marketing works,” Creech said. “Brands and their agency partners are going to continue to invest there.”

The current rush of creator agency activity has its origins in the pandemic, according to LV8 chief executive Griffin Haddrill.

Lockdown acted as a digital accelerant, pulling budgets toward creators at a moment when television and film had dried up and out-of-home advertising had stopped working.

Because acquisitions typically take nine to 12 months to come together, the resulting deal volume spiked in 2021 and 2022, slowed over the following two years, and has since normalized, Creech said.

The same period produced a wave of new agencies, many of which were founded by executives who saw the emerging relevance of the creator ecosystem and launched startups to service it. Five years later, those boutiques have matured into businesses worth buying, according to Haddrill.

For founders, selling offers the resources to keep up with clients, according to Get Engaged co-founder Ben Hiott.

“Social is becoming the center of how brands build their businesses, and clients are asking us to do more every year,” Hiott said in October.

The wave of dealmaking that followed the pandemic was primarily influencer agencies, according to Creech, while this year, the variety of targets has expanded.

In 2026, buyers have gone after social-first creative agencies, such as Get Engaged, OK Cool, and Sway Social; podcast and audio shops like Ad Results Media and Listening Party Agency; and TikTok Shop specialists including Orca, Uptickk, and Yada.

Private equity is also getting more involved in the space, investing capital after years of financing strategic acquirers, as Bridgepoint does with Samy, according to Creech. This year, Shamrock Capital took a majority stake in Saylor, Velocity became a near-equal partner in LV8, and Falfurrias bought Freeman & Forrest, among other deals.

Serial acquirers are consolidating the field as well. Influencer agency Ykone has made four acquisitions in 2026, and creator company network Residence has made two, while Samy and Publicis remain active, according to Creech. After large platform deals for Influential and Captiv8, Publicis has turned to regional acquisitions, including the French creator agency Point d’Orgue, to enter new markets, according to Creech.

For investors, the opportunity lies in stitching these shops together, according to Haddrill.

“The consumer demand was there, but the agency space was fragmented,” Haddrill said. “Now, investors are seeing this fragmentation and working to bring them together.”

B2B creator marketing has also emerged as its own category.

Creator Authority, whose clients include Amazon, Adobe, and Mastercard, was Billion Dollar Boy Group’s first acquisition, an all-cash, multimillion-dollar deal with an earnout component, according to Business Insider.

Freeman & Forrest, founded in 2024 by Emily Freeman and Forrest Brazeal after they ran influencer programs at Microsoft and Google Cloud, helps tech companies like Amazon and Google reach technical customers through creators.

And more of the deals cross borders: Get Engaged gave Samy a U.S. base after the agency built its business in Europe and Latin America, and dealmaking between the U.S. and the U.K. has picked up in both directions, according to Creech.

As the ecosystem matures, the next wave of dealmaking may come from the middle of the market, according to a creator management executive who asked not to be named.

The largest independents, such as Whalar and Influential, have already sold to Accenture Song and Publicis, respectively, which leaves room for midsize independents, many of which will come to market over the next 12 months, the executive said.

Creator management firms, which run creators’ businesses rather than simply booking their brand deals, are likely the next category to consolidate, the executive added.

The buying, in other words, is far from over, according to Creech.

“It’s mature, it’s scaled, it’s professionalized. The work is not done,” Creech said. “We’re maybe entering middle innings.”

Hearst CMO Heads Out (SCOOP): The chief marketing officer at Hearst Magazines, Sherri Chambers, will be leaving the organization in a few weeks, according to sources. Chambers, who joined the digital media firm in December 2024, confirmed the news, while a Hearst Magazines spokesperson expressed their gratitude for Chambers in a statement. The departure is the latest in a series of executive shake-ups. In July, Hearst Magazines appointed Chris Berend as its first chief content and experience officer, and in October, it named A+E executive Juliana Stock as its new president, replacing Debi Chirichella, who will become chair of the group. The legacy media firm has worked hard to cultivate a reputation for calm and consistency in an otherwise turbulent industry, but it certainly looks as if the company is aiming to turn some kind of corner.

Bunny Boss Bolts: The editor in chief of Playboy, Phillip Picardi, has left the organization after less than a year, Puck reported earlier this week. According to a company spokesperson, the move was a personal decision, with Picardi returning to Los Angeles after Playboy set up headquarters in—where else—Miami. But Picardi only began the job officially in March, meaning his tenure at the publisher only lasted a brief eight months. Given all the fanfare surrounding his appointment, I have to imagine something went seriously awry. Or Picardi, who did not return my request for comment, landed an otherwise irrefusable job elsewhere. This is disappointing because I liked the direction Playboy was heading under Picardi, reporting on the state of sex, dating, and relationships in this fraught moment—but maybe the loyal fanbase did not.

BuzzFeed Taps Influential (EXCLUSIVE): On Monday, BuzzFeed named its first chief revenue officer under its new ownership, tapping Chris Poydenis, co-founder of the creator marketing firm Influential, to lead its revenue division. The appointment comes as BuzzFeed attempts an ambitious shift in direction, positioning itself as a source of free, ad-supported television, bundled with the rest of the Allen Media Group portfolio. The decision to name a creator marketing executive as the chief sales lead clearly telegraphs the manner in which BuzzFeed would like to evolve, shifting focus to its creators and video franchise talent. Will it work? The odds are long.

Sara Kehaulani Goo is president of The Washington Post’s creator network, a new division that sits outside of the newsroom and makes sponsored short-form video series with independent creators. Goo, formerly the editor in chief of Axios, launched the first show in April, and the network has since released six series in categories ranging from personal finance to career advice.

Publishers increasingly want to work with creators, but the harder question is why creators would want to work with publishers. In our conversation, Goo and I discussed how The Post’s model works, why its creator content goes through Post fact-checking, and what a media company can offer talent who could go it alone.

Mark Stenberg: You specifically left Axios to head up this new creator division. Can you tell me about the role and how The Post is working with creators?

Sara Kehaulani Goo: I spent 12 years of my career at The Post as a reporter and editor, so it’s been nice to return in a very different role. The theory is simple: Look at where people spend their time, where they get information, where trust is growing, and where revenue is growing across media. The answer to all of those questions is creators. So, we are working with knowledge creators, people who have built big audiences around their expertise, and treating them as equal editorial and business partners.

Sara: We mostly work with creators who’ve built audiences with short-form video in lifestyle topics like personal finance, health and wellness, and career advice. Then, we develop the shows together and publish them together. Last week, we launched Her Next Move with an executive recruiter based in New York. She posts a two-minute video to her audience of a million people as a collab with us, so it reaches both audiences. We amplify it on The Post’s social accounts and publish it on our website and app. That show is sponsored by Uber.

Mark: Why would a creator want to work with the Post? Do they share in the revenue?

Sara: I won’t get into the details, but we pay them for their time. It’s similar to a contributor model, though not exactly like freelancing, because they’re entrepreneurs. A lot of them want to focus on the content, which is what journalists want too. We help build the business around them, and we provide distribution and editorial support when they need it.

Mark: Does their content go through Post fact-checking?

Sara: Yes, through my team. The standards are the same. There’s a lack of consistency in the creator world when it comes to disclosing sponsorships, and to quality in general, and we can bring that. Trust in creators is growing, but people still wonder whether they should trust what they’re watching on Instagram and who paid for it. We can help answer that.

Mark: It’s early, but how big is this now, and where is it headed?

Sara: This year has been a proof of concept. We’ve done six shows with six creators, across health and wellness, personal finance, travel, career advice, and entertainment. Now we’re deciding which ones to renew, and next year we want to do more always-on content. Brands are very interested, because every brand is thinking about its creator strategy, and finding the right creator takes a lot of effort. We take that headache off their shoulders.

Mark: Creators often relish operating on their own. How do you convince them to work with you?

Sara: Having led newsrooms, I’ve always seen my job as spotting talent, developing it, and creating an environment where people can do their best work. This is the same. Creators are entrepreneurs, and the platforms give them plenty of ways to make money on their own. But being an entrepreneur isn’t for everybody, at least not at every level. Media companies have always worked with outside experts. The structure just looks different now.
