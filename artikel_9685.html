Survival Under the Allowlist: A Sharded, Agent-Assisted Design for Censorship-Resistant Networks

How internet control moved from blocklists to allowlists, why today's circumvention tools struggle with that shift, and a design sketch that combines local AI agents, sharded relay discovery, and a thin web3 coordination layer.

For a long time, the mental model of internet censorship was a blocklist. An authority decided that certain domains, IP addresses, or keywords were forbidden, and the network enforced it. Everything else worked. Circumvention was a race: find a path the list did not cover.

That model is eroding. In 2025 and 2026, several things happened at once:

This article does two things. First, it gives a unified technical picture of how modern network control works, drawing on published measurement research and reporting, so that readers who are not specialists in this field can see the whole system. Second, it proposes a design direction, built from existing research, that tries to answer a specific question: what would a circumvention network look like if it assumed that allowlists, per-ISP differences, and AI-assisted blocking are the normal case?

A note on scope. Iran, China, Russia, and North Korea are used here as points on a spectrum rather than as targets of an indictment. Iran is a particularly instructive hybrid, because its infrastructure draws on both Chinese and Russian technology (more on that below). The focus is technical: how the systems work, where current tools fail, and what might do better.

Nothing in this article is a finished invention. Most building blocks come from published research. The contribution, if there is one, is the composition, and an honest list of what remains unsolved.

This design also sits in the decentralized-AI stack. The agent runs small models on the user’s device (edge inference) rather than calling a central inference API, route intelligence is shared through a ledger with no single operator, and the aim is an AI-assisted network that is hard for any single party to switch off.

The shift can be summarized in four overlapping eras. This is a simplification, since all four coexist in different places, but it is a useful frame.

DNS poisoning, IP blocking, HTTP blockpages

OONI's 2014–2017 Iran measurements found DNS injection and transparent proxies serving blockpages, with circumvention-tool sites themselves blocked and Tor blocked on many networks

Inspect traffic and block what looks suspicious

The Great Firewall's passive detection of fully encrypted traffic (first reported in November 2021); SNI-based QUIC blocking from April 2024; Russia's TSPU boxes

Blocked-by-default networks, approved-user tiers, whitelisted IP ranges

The key observation is that each era is harder to circumvent than the last, for a different reason. Era 2 breaks tools that rely on looking like something else. Era 3 breaks tools that rely on being reachable at all, unless they can hide inside the allowed set.

A useful way to understand what a censor can see and do is to go layer by layer.

Two examples show how these layers interact.

The Great Firewall (GFW) is the reference system. Research from Wu et al. (USENIX Security 2023) found that in early November 2021 the GFW deployed a technique that passively detects fully encrypted traffic, such as that produced by Shadowsocks, VMess, and obfs4. Rather than defining what fully encrypted traffic is, the censor applies crude heuristics (protocol fingerprints, the fraction of set bits, and the number and position of printable ASCII characters) to exempt traffic that is unlikely to be fully encrypted, then blocks the rest. The authors estimated that such rules could block roughly 0.6 percent of normal traffic as collateral damage. A key finding is that randomness itself became a signal. The study's artifact repository also records that the GFW had stopped this dynamic blocking at least since 15 March 2023, a reminder that these systems change on their own schedule.

In 2024 the GFW began another capability. Zohaib et al. (USENIX Security 2025) report that since 7 April 2024 it blocks QUIC connections to specific domains, even though QUIC handshakes are encrypted, by decrypting QUIC initial packets at scale. The Register's summary notes the system cannot block all QUIC traffic and that its success rate fluctuates by time of day, and UMass researchers explain that the computational burden of inspecting those packets can overwhelm the system under modest load. The lesson for designers is that censors have capacity limits and collateral costs. (Such limits are not an invitation to attack national infrastructure: doing so would also harm ordinary users.)

Russia's approach is built on a different architecture. A 2019 law required operators to install DPI equipment. Measurement research on TSPU (IMC 2022) found that the boxes are deployed close to users while remaining centrally controlled. An earlier NDSS 2020 study found significant differences in the type and amount of blocking between ISPs.

Since then, the system has moved toward allowlisting. Global Voices describes whitelists applied during mobile shutdowns in 2025, and the Moscow Times quoted a ministry source describing shutdowns in Moscow as a test of the ability to block sites not on the whitelist.

Iran's system combines elements of both. A FOCI 2020 study documented a protocol whitelister that permitted only a small set of protocols (DNS, HTTP, HTTPS). More recent measurement work, IRBlock (USENIX Security 2025), finds that Iran's blocking strategies resemble the GFW's in many respects. A 2025 preprint describes a "stealth blackout" in which global routing appeared intact while users were isolated through DPI and protocol-based filtering, which is exactly the pattern that makes outage dashboards misleading.

The January 2026 shutdown is documented in unusual technical detail. FilterWatch's report, built on IODA, Kentik, and Cloudflare Radar data, describes a phased operation. In the days before 8 January, interference showed up as "zombie connections": devices looked connected at the IP layer while higher-layer performance was sabotaged, and VPNs showed "connected" but passed no data. On 8 January, 98.5 percent of Iranian IPv6 address space was withdrawn hours before the full cut, which the report interprets as herding traffic onto IPv4 infrastructure where inspection tools are more mature. Connectivity then fell to under 1 percent of normal. Restoration followed a blocked-by-default pattern in which services such as Google Search returned while the wider internet stayed dark, and, as an Iranian digital-rights researcher told Global Voices, whitelisting was enforced at the infrastructure level and not only at the service level. Its February 28 investigation describes "white" SIM cards and tiered pricing for international traffic. Iran International describes a related scheme branded "Internet Pro," in which access is organized around approved users and services.

Censorship infrastructure is increasingly a commercial product. In September 2025, more than 500 GB of source code, work logs, and internal documents from Geedge Networks and a related Chinese Academy of Sciences lab leaked. Tom's Hardware and the Jamestown Foundation describe deployments in Ethiopia, Pakistan, Myanmar, and Kazakhstan, as well as Chinese provinces such as Xinjiang and Fujian. The data suggest that a censorship system can now be bought, configured, and operated by an ISP without building it from scratch.

Iran shows the same thing from the other side. ARTICLE 19 documents Chinese support for Iran's infrastructure over more than a decade, including equipment from ZTE, Huawei, Tiandy, and Hikvision. On the Russian side, Global Voices recounts Citizen Lab's 2023 finding that Protei, a company with Russian roots, was selected to provide core network components, including DPI, to the Iranian mobile operator Ariantel, and notes Foreign Policy's reporting of Russian help in building Iran's control architecture. The same article is explicit that it remains unclear exactly what Protei supplied.

For designers, the implication is simple: the problem will not stay local. Techniques that are developed against one system will meet its commercial relatives elsewhere, which is an argument for designs based on principles rather than on tricks for one country.

One recent episode illustrates how network control reaches the naming layer. On 28 October 2026, Iran’s cybersecurity authority Afta urged organizations to migrate from foreign domains to .ir, citing the risk of sanctions-driven domain suspension. This shifts the threat model. For a platform hosted abroad on a foreign domain, the state’s main lever is blocking at the network level, and the site may remain reachable through circumvention. A domain under a domestic registry, by contrast, places namespace-level control with the state.

Two further details from the 2026 shutdowns matter for what follows. First, circumvention was itself targeted. Chatham House reports that Starlink use is criminalized, with penalties of up to two years, and that the state reportedly uses jamming. Second, the shutdown decision was described as a security-institution decision: Iran International quoted a government spokesperson saying that security policymakers had decided the national network had to be cut and that the government could not override that decision. For design purposes, this means the people operating in-country infrastructure are a compliance point, and a design that depends on their cooperation is fragile.

Existing tools such as Tor with Snowflake, Psiphon, Lantern, and a long list of VPN protocols have done real work. This section is not a critique of them. It is a catalogue of the pressures that no single tool handles well.

A design that treats the censor as a single adversary will be wrong on at least some networks. Each ISP should be treated as an independent adversary.

When only approved destinations are reachable, one tactic is to tunnel through approved IP ranges. Community reports on the net4people forum describe exactly this in Russia, and also describe a crackdown in January 2026 in which Roskomnadzor reportedly demanded that cloud providers separate the address pools used for whitelisting from those used for other customers. In March and April 2026, as noted above, whitelisted platforms were told to block VPN users. A related failure mode is worth naming: in Iran, circumvention developers reported a "no-ping" pattern in which a VPN shows as connected but cannot pass data (see the FilterWatch report above). A tool that only checks whether a handshake succeeded will mislead its user. The pattern is the allowlist era's version of the old arms race: every trick that rides on collateral dependency invites a countermeasure that raises the cost of that dependency.

Russia's arms race is not one-sided in tooling. TechRadar, citing Forbes Russia, reports that Roskomnadzor was set to allocate about 2.27 billion rubles to build an AI-powered traffic-filtering mechanism aimed at prohibited content and VPN connections. This is a secondary report and should be treated with care, but the direction is plausible: classification is a natural fit for machine learning, and any circumvention approach should assume it.

Direct-to-satellite connectivity avoids the national network, but Chatham House reports that its use is criminalized in Iran and that jamming has been reported. It is a valuable tool but not a universal answer.

Mesh messengers deserve a short note. The Electronic Frontier Foundation's guide to circumventing shutdowns says apps such as Briar are hard for most people to use and rarely installed, while Bitchat saw some success in Iran. A Bloomsbury Intelligence and Security Institute report notes the claimed range of around 300 meters, which fits dense gatherings rather than cities, and calls the technology immature. Mesh does answer a real need in a total shutdown, but it requires nearby participants, preinstallation, and some technical comfort. For the average user, that is a high bar.

Before proposing anything, it is worth stating what the literature supports. These are the design principles the rest of the article leans on.

Diversity beats mimicry. The GFW findings above show that being random is itself detectable, and the parrot-style approach of imitating another protocol tends to fail when imitation is imperfect. The robust alternative is not one clever disguise but many options, chosen according to what works.

Cheap, ephemeral endpoints. Snowflake uses temporary WebRTC proxies run by volunteers, which makes it hard to enumerate and block by address, since proxies come and go.

Hide in collateral, carefully. Conjure uses unused address space belonging to cooperating ISPs (refraction networking), so that a censor who wants to block proxies has to block address space that might hold legitimate services.

Automate strategy discovery. Geneva uses a genetic algorithm to evolve packet-manipulation strategies, and it discovered strategies effective against censors in China, India, and Kazakhstan. A follow-up, Come as You Are, found strategies applied only on the server side, helping unmodified clients, and tested against censors including Iran's at the time. These results are from 2019 and 2020 and say nothing definitive about today's systems, but they establish that strategy search can be automated and that evasion does not have to be client-only.

Distribute access with care. Lox is a bridge distribution system that combines trust levels, invitations, open entry for new users, and anonymous credentials so that the social graph of inviters stays private. Its design goal is that a censor cannot enumerate bridges just by joining. The authors also identify a scaling concern: the cost of the blockage-check protocol grows with the number of blocked bridges, which a patient censor could exploit to disrupt migrations during a critical event. Note that Lox, as published, relies on a single central authority that issues and verifies credentials, and the authors present it as a proof of concept with demonstrative parameters.

Censors have limits. As shown in Section 3.2, inspection costs compute. This is a design observation, not an attack recommendation.

Many readers will be thinking: if strategy search can be automated, why not let AI agents manage the whole thing? There is real momentum behind that idea in enterprise networks. A Cisco and Omdia survey of 1,000 IT and network operations leaders found 84% expect an AI-led operating model within 12 months, though 56% still prefer a human to approve actions before an AI takes them and 24% favor full autonomy.

But agents bring a cost that matters here. Cisco's 2026 report AI Impact on Wide Area Networks, summarized by SDxCentral, finds that an AI agent generates about 450 percent more traffic for a task than a human doing the same task, with roughly 70 percent of that traffic being inference. The report projects enterprise traffic growing about nine-fold by 2035 with widespread agent adoption, compared with about 2.5-fold without, and AI inference reaching about a quarter of total traffic.

Two cautions apply. These are vendor-produced figures for enterprise WANs, and an analyst commentary notes that the headline multiplier is not a general estimate for all agent tasks. And the traffic cited is mostly agents talking to models, not agents configuring networks. Still, the direction of the lesson is clear and it transfers to censored networks, where it bites harder:

This section describes a hypothesis, not a tested system. It assembles known components into an architecture aimed at the pressures in Section 5.

Architecture overview: a local agent, VPN app, transport pool and anonymous credential on the user's device; sticky relay shards on the operator side; and a thin coordination layer with an on-chain registry and a findings ledger. The data path never touches the chain.

The agent is a small program on the user's device. Its job is not to carry traffic, only to decide how to try. It is event-driven rather than always running (Section 8.9).

A back-of-envelope illustration of the control-plane budget (assumptions, not measurements): if the network tracks 50 transports across 20 coarse network classes with a 2-bit success bucket for each, a full status summary is 50 × 20 × 2 bits = 2,000 bits, or about 250 bytes. Even with metadata, that is orders of magnitude smaller than a single inference call. The point is to make the budget explicit and then enforce it.

The heart of the proposal is how users get relays. Two meanings of "sharding" are in play, and it is worth keeping them apart:

An earlier version of this sketch re-assigned every client to a fresh shard each epoch. A toy simulation (Section 8.10) showed why that is a mistake: re-randomizing lets even a few quiet infiltrators see new relays every epoch and enumerate the whole pool over time. Persistent shards cap what any one identity can ever learn.

Blockchain sharding has real deployments and also real lessons.

What transfers is the idea of partitioning to limit blast radius and parallelize load. What does not transfer is cross-shard consensus machinery, which this design avoids by keeping data paths off-chain and shard membership soft. The known cost of sharding, that smaller groups are easier to attack, appears here as the infiltration problem described above, and it should be stated plainly.

When preferred paths fail, the agent walks down a ladder rather than failing outright:

Mesh and satellite appear only as complements, outside this design's scope.

A user on ISP A can connect on Tuesday through one transport. On Wednesday, a block lands. The local agent sees repeated resets, classifies them as probable injection, and tries alternatives from its pool, using a few kilobytes at most. Meanwhile the same user's cousin on ISP B, where the block never appeared, keeps using the original. Neither user does anything technical, and neither sees a wallet. Both keep their shard until it is blocked. A censor operating a fake client sees only the relays in its own shard, and each identity it adds must earn trust slowly, so infiltration cannot scale quickly.

None of this has been tested in the field. The scenario shows the intended behavior, not a result.

The agent does not have to be a VPN. It can be a separate layer whose only product is a ranked list of paths that currently work on the user’s network, handed to a tunnel app that does the actual connecting. This is not a new pattern: the Outline SDK’s Smart Dialer already searches a configured set of strategies for one that unblocks DNS and TLS for a list of test domains, and its documentation notes that obtaining a Psiphon config may require a contract with the Psiphon team. The proposal here extends the idea with shared, sharded intelligence about what works where.

Two ways to ship it. The reference experience is the agent as a module inside the project's own VPN app. Third-party VPN apps can integrate the same layer through an API or SDK under agreed terms (the contractual and legal side is out of scope here). In both cases the interface is narrow: the agent returns ranked paths with parameters and a time-to-live, the VPN app connects, and the VPN app reports only success, failure, and coarse timing. The agent never sees user traffic.

An event-driven lifecycle, not continuous monitoring.

This keeps control-plane cost bursty and small, and it avoids a measurement problem: on Android, only one VPN connection can be active at a time, and probes sent while a tunnel is up would measure the tunnel rather than the underlying path. Measuring only when no tunnel is active sidesteps that. It does not shrink the data plane: carrying user traffic remains the VPN's job and cost. Platform limits matter, too. Android exposes network and VPN state changes to apps, while iOS gives far less background freedom, so on iOS the logic would likely live inside the VPN app's own lifecycle rather than a separate always-on app.

Guided discovery, not blind scanning. The agent should find new open paths by exploring a structured search space (fronting combinations, transport parameters, new relay candidates from its shard) with a per-run probe budget. Sweeping arbitrary third-party address space from a phone would be slow, noisy, and easy for a censor to flag.

Candidate confidentiality. Candidate lists can be distributed encrypted under per-epoch keys, with a memory-hard key-derivation step or a time-lock-style puzzle so that bulk decryption is costly. This hides lists at rest and in transit and raises the price of mass enumeration. It is not access control: a censor running a copy of the agent holds the same decryption capability as a real one, and a datacenter has far more compute than a mid-range phone. The real gate remains the trust-level credential from Section 8.4.

A shared findings ledger. Agents with credentials can sign reports of what worked on which network class. A permissioned ledger (for example, proof-of-authority, run by validators from diverse jurisdictions) can serve as an append-only log: commitments and small summaries on-chain, bulk data off-chain. A candidate is promoted only after confirmation by several independent agents from different network classes, and old confirmations decay. Two cautions apply. A small validator set is a coercion and seizure target, and a signed but malicious agent can still try to poison the log. If a chain is more than the problem needs, a Merkle-tree transparency log with signed gossip is the minimal alternative.

Permanent anchoring. One place where permanent storage fits is as a public anchor for the ledger's history. Arweave asks uploaders for a one-time, upfront contribution to a storage endowment, priced to cover roughly 200 years of replicated storage at present prices (Arweave documentation). A signed checkpoint of the ledger (a Merkle root plus validator signatures, a few kilobytes per day) can be written there by an operator outside the censored region, and the one-time fee for something that small should be modest; check the current price before relying on that. This does not stop validators from being coerced, but it makes silent rewriting of history detectable, and it gives researchers a durable archive of aggregated, anonymized results. It must not hold relay addresses, per-user reports, or credentials: data that can never be deleted would become a permanent enumeration list and a permanent record of who reported what. Nor is it a bootstrap path, since reads go through HTTP gateways that can be blocked like any other, so clients should fetch it through the tunnel.

To check the central claims, I wrote two small simulations (code and results). They simulate a simplified model with assumed parameters. They are not measurements of any real network, and the numbers should be read as how the toy behaves, not as predictions.

Per-ISP transport selection. Six simulated ISPs block 12 transports independently and change over time. Averaged over 12 seeds, a client with one fixed transport failed to connect about 61 percent of the time. Random failover failed 7.6 percent and needed about 2.3 tries per connection. A single global ranking failed 2.6 percent. A per-ISP local agent failed 1.2 percent with 1.5 tries, and a per-ISP agent that also used pooled reports from other users on the same ISP (with a one-day lag) connected on the first try about 90 percent of the time. This assumes the pooled reports are honest; poisoning is not modelled.

Relay distribution under infiltration. The simulated censor creates fake identities, learns whatever relays they are given, and blocks them, either immediately ("naive") or after staying quiet and then striking at once ("patient").

Sticky shards + eviction of repeat offenders

The sentence to design toward is: "It works like a VPN app." If the user has to understand epochs, shards, bonds, or keys, the design has failed, because the lesson of mesh networking and wallet-based naming is that technical-user-only tools do not spread.
