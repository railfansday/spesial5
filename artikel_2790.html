Highs and lows of Fernando Mendoza’s starting debut for Las Vegas Raiders

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Fernando Mendoza's first NFL start in the Las Vegas Raiders' Week 10 matchup against the Seattle Seahawks was a tale of two halves.

In the first half, he completed 11 of 13 passes for 90 yards, with both incompletions being throwaways, and had five rushes for 21 yards and a touchdown. According to Sumer Sports, that resulted in a total EPA of 6.56, an EPA per play of 0.328 and a 55 percent success rate — pretty good numbers. However, the second half was a different story, going six-for-16 with 53 passing yards and one rush for one yard to post a negative 13.49 total EPA, negative 0.642 EPA per play and a 14.3 percent success rate.

For the entire game, the No. 1 pick completed 17 of 29 passes (58.6 percent) for 143 passing yards, along with six rushes for 22 yards and a touchdown. That netted a negative 6.93 total EPA, negative 0.169 EPA per play and a 34.1 percent success rate.

For more context, Pro Football Focus credited him with a 66.7 percent adjusted completion percentage (accounting for the two throwaways and one drop), a slightly below-baseline (60.0) 59.8 passing grade and a slightly above-baseline rushing grade (65.2) to sit at a 62.2 overall mark.

While the numbers above help paint part of the picture, let's dive into the tape to see the full story.

To help get the rookie going, Klint Kubiak dialed up bootlegs on Mendoza's first three throws. The clip above is the third one and is a great decision by the quarterback.

His first read is to see if the go route from the outside receiver is available for a one-on-one opportunity. However, the Seahawks have that covered with the corner underneath and the boundary safety on top of the receiver to execute the Cover 2 side of the Cover 6 call.

Mendoza's next two options are either tight end Michael Mayer, who runs a chip release into a curl route, or wide receiver Jalen Nailor on a crossing/drag route. The MIKE linebacker is all over Mayer to take that away, and the WILL linebacker turns to ROBOT and help the corner against Nailor. Meanwhile, fullback Connor Heyward is working across the formation to sell the run fake and run a flat route.

Heyward gets contacted by the edge rusher, who ends up getting pressure on Mendoza. However, Mendoza does a good job of going through his reads, finding Heyward and throwing around the free rusher to dump it to the fullback, who picks up five on second and six to set up a third-and-short situation.

This isn't a sexy throw by any means, but it's a good process to put the offense in a favorable third down situation.

Here, we'll get a look at the touchdown run that got Allegiant Stadium rocking and is eerily similar to the run the Indiana product had to win the National Championship over Miami last January.

It's third and 10 as the Raiders come out in an empty formation where the Seahawks respond with a five-man box and both safeties deep pre-snap. That's the perfect look to run a quarterback draw into, and Mendoza sells it well by taking a drop with his first step and eyeing wide receivers Jalen Nailor and Tre Tucker post-snap, keeping the safeties in the end zone.

Then, he takes off and does a great job of setting up left guard Spencer Burford's block by getting downfield and pressing the five-yard line. That allows Burford to position himself between the linebacker and ball carrier. Finally, Mendoza shows off some impressive vision to see the field safety overpursue, cut back to the other hashmark and put the ball in the painted area.

If the previous clip was the No. 1 pick's best play of the game, the clip above is the best throw he made on the afternoon.

Seattle starts with a two-high look pre-snap before rotating the boundary safety into the hook-to-curl area to play Cover 3. That takes away the curl from Nailor on the curl-flat concept. Maybe Mendoza can hit Mayer on the flat route, but the linebacker manages to avoid the pick and stay on top of the route. So, that's a fine decision to hold onto the ball. But the problem is that the pocket starts to break down with Trey Zuhn III getting bull-rushed by Byron Murphy II.

Mendoza manages to get out of trouble, but he can settle down and pick his eyes up at the 10-yard line instead of continuing to scramble. That would have allowed him to find slot receiver Cody White earlier — who is wide open on the corner route with a coverage bust from Devon Witherspoon — and hit White with some room to run. Regardless, Mendoza eventually finds White and delivers a strike on the run for a 22-yard gain near the sideline.

This is where things started to get a little messy for the rookie, as the play above comes with about a minute left in the second quarter. Mendoza had a few missed opportunities for explosive plays in the game, with the one above being the biggest.

The Seahawks show a two-safety coverage pre-snap but rotate into Cover 3 with the boundary safety spinning down. Since the Raiders are in a three-by-one formation, the boundary corner is locked in man coverage against Tucker, the Silver and Black's best deep threat.

Tucker beats press coverage and has a couple of steps on the corner, while the field or deep safety isn't in a position to help the corner on a pass outside of the numbers. Mendoza gets to Tucker in his progression but doesn't pull the trigger. Maybe left guard Spencer Burford losing control of Murphy discouraged the quarterback from letting it rip, but he has enough time and a clean enough pocket to make that throw.

Instead, Mendoza opts to scramble and only picks up one yard. To be fair, this was the play that had the 4th and 1/first and 10 confusion (see the markers on the field and the scorebug on the top of the screen). So, maybe that played a factor in his decision, but this was certainly a missed opportunity.

Part of the reason Mendoza had such a low EPA is that he took six sacks on the afternoon. That was the result of some great play calling from Seattle's defensive mastermind/head coach Mike Macdonald, and the rookie quarterback's subpar feel for pressure.

This is a perfect play call by Macdonald. Vegas runs a play-action pass where the entire offensive line and the full back work left to sell the run fake, while running back Mike Washington Jr. inserts into the protection scheme on the right edge. Meanwhile, Seattle slants the defensive line to the strong side of the formation as the MIKE and WILL linebackers blitz, on top of the cornerback coming off the edge.

So, the Seahawks end up with a numbers advantage, where the Raiders have three blockers (Zuhn, right tackle DJ Glaze and Washington) to account for four pass-rushers (the edge, two linebackers and the corner). Also, Mendoza's back is turned to the defense, and Kubiak calls a shot play with two deep routes.

That being said, he gets turned around well before Witherspoon gets there and doesn't sense the pressure coming, failing to move off his spot. Again, this is a tough look — especially for a quarterback making his first start.

But if we back up to the pre-snap look, the safety playing outside the hashmark with the corner in press coverage was the tell that Witherspoon was blitzing, especially since the receiver is in a reduced or cut split (meaning between the numbers and hashmark). That creates a learning opportunity for the young QB.

Another sack that highlights how Macdonald tested Mendoza's ability to read the defense when the picture changes post-snap and handle pressure.

Seattle comes out in a double A-gap look with a seven-man front, where four pass-rush threats are on the weak side of the offensive line (in this instance, the offense's right side). So, the quarterback changes the protection, sliding it to the right. However, Macdonald doesn't run a blitz but rather a simulated pressure, where all four defenders on the right side drop into coverage, and the nickel cornerback rushes through the C-gap for a four-man rush.

Since the line is sliding right and there's an edge rusher outside of the tight end, left tackle Kolton Miller stays with Murphy in the protection call, and Mayer sticks with the edge rusher, leaving Witherspoon unblocked as a free rusher. Mendoza doesn't see or feel the pressure and ends up taking another big hit for a sack on third down.

To be fair, it looks like he was trying to throw hot to Ashton Jeanty. But Witherspoon closed so quickly that he had already started making the tackle by the time Jeanty got his head around. Still, this is another instance where Mendoza needs to expect pressure with the nickel as close to the line of scrimmage as he is pre-snap.

Finally, Mendoza had a few throws where his accuracy got away from him, and the one above really hurt.

On this play, it's 2nd and 10 with about 2:45 to play and the Raiders trailing by 11. Seattle keeps the coverage pretty vanilla (for Macdonald's standards, at least), playing Cover 6 with quarters to the wide side of the field near the goal line. Mendoza makes the right read since the safety steps inside while wide receiver Jack Bech runs an out route. That's open for a potential touchdown.

However, Mendoza doesn't finish the throw, so the pass sails on him and falls incomplete. Granted, the pocket is collapsing, but he had enough room to deliver an accurate pass and just missed the opportunity.
