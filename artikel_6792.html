Are Zen Hotels Now Putting Us to Sleep?

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Rich, layered maximalism is the latest form of escapism—historically inspired decor spiked with creative contemporary flair. Get ready to Instagram every nook.

Above: One of the cottages at Little Saint in Healdsburg, CA.

On the top floor of a Gilded Age McKim, Mead & White mansion in New York, now reincarnated as the Fifth Avenue Hotel, Martin Brudnizki recently put the finishing touches on a two-bedroom suite dubbed the Flâneur Penthouse. With his hallmark exuberance, the Elle Decor A-List designer arrayed the space with distinctive flourishes: a spirited Scalamandré wallcovering trimmed in grosgrain ribbon, bespoke furnishings mixed with French Art Deco pieces, India-inspired inlaid nightstands, Japanese-style lamps, and Murano glass ceiling lights. Each element connects to an imagined narrative that shaped Brudnizki’s vision for the hotel: “this cinematic idea of the home of a flâneur who has traveled the world, has seen and acquired a lot, and put it together here,” the designer says. “It’s a multicultural journey.”

Hotels in this vein tend to be -owner-operated properties looking to carve out a niche. One could trace a baroque family tree that links mold-breaking 1980s boutique hotels like Ian Schrager and Philippe Starck’s Royalton in New York to Jacques Garcia’s interiors for the Hôtel Costes and L’Hotel in Paris to Ilse Crawford’s Ett Hem in Stockholm to Kelly Wearstler’s Proper Hotel in Austin.

Fulk’s hospitality design, like Brudnizki’s, embraces spaces inspired by history and storytelling, with an emphasis on artistry, material richness, and character. Both men’s work is rooted in tradition, with idiosyncratic twists that feel fresh. And they have plenty of kindred spirits working in a diversity of styles, including such designers as Lorenzo Castillo, Kit Kemp, and Laura Gonzalez. While far from a coherent movement, the sumptuous, high-impact aesthetics practiced by these designers just might be today’s most zeitgeisty current.

Take Fulk’s Cottages at Little Saint in Healdsburg, California, each one an elevated boho mashup of influences. “Sixties and ’70s rock and roll, and a sort of Laurel Canyon meets Northern California spirit,” explains the designer. “The cottages feel utterly like nowhere else.” And that, of course, is the point.

For his part, Brudnizki doesn’t care much for maximalism as a label. In the case of his glamorously moody interiors for the Broadwick Soho in London—shimmering surfaces, lavishly upholstered furnishings, a riot of pattern—everything began with an idea he describes as “Studio 54 meets your grandmother’s Soho townhouse, a disco granny kind of thing.”

Then there are the roughly dozen memorable properties created by Kit and Tim Kemp under their Firmdale Hotels group. The most recent is the Warren Street Hotel in New York, with the forthcoming Bedford Place Hotel slated to open next year in London next to the British Museum. “It’s about personality, a sense of arrival, and fun,” says Kit Kemp, who outfits each of her hotels with a more-is-more mix of her ebullient textiles and wallpapers in splashy colors, animated by art and craft.

Kimberley Cohen, the creative director behind Maisons Pariente, says her family’s small portfolio of hôtels de charme are about creating a residential-feeling experience that is also “something you have never seen, that you would not do in your own home.” She has worked with Brudnizki on Le Grand Mazarin in Paris, with Charles Zana on two hotels in the South of France, and with Pierre Yovanovitch on a Méribel chalet. Her latest project, opening this winter, is a Courchevel retreat named Saint Roch, with interiors by Friedmann & Versace that channel the chic romanticism of traditional dachas.

Next up for Brudnizki, meanwhile, is the Knox Hotel, set to open soon in Dallas, new territory for him. As for what one should expect of the decor, he sums it up as “100 percent unapologetic glamour. It won’t be boring.”
