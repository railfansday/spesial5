Can AI Really Make Us Feel Seen?

MIT professor Sherry Turkle’s new book Artificial Intimacy explores what happens when people turn to chatbots for the kind of understanding we used to seek from each other.

In one exchange she describes, a man asks his chatbot whether his former girlfriend was right that he was emotionally unavailable. He brings a painful judgment about himself to a machine that is likely to offer a fluent, sympathetic response, instead of asking another person whose reply might be more unpredictable.

Turkle has studied our relationships with technology for decades

Today people use chatbots as companions, therapists, confidants, and romantic partners. “Pretend empathy" is the term Turkle uses to describe the sympathetic replies of the chatbots. Through her own experiments, she acknowledges just how powerful their responses can feel. She argues that because talking to a chatbot feels so easy, people may come to prefer it to human relationships, which take more work and can sometimes be uncomfortable.

Most people like hearing that their reactions make sense, especially when they are upset or hurt. It's harder to hear that we contributed to a problem, misunderstood something, or missed what someone else was trying to tell us. Another person who knows us may notice a pattern we have missed or remember an encounter differently. It can be unsettling to be shown something about ourselves that we couldn't see for ourselves. We often learn more about ourselves from knowing how our words and actions landed with someone else.

A chatbot's reassurance can soothe us when we are upset while leaving our account of events unchallenged. Validating a person's feelings doesn’t necessarily mean endorsing their version of what happened. But with a sympathetic reply, the two can easily blur. That's what’s called the sycophantic pull. This is when the chatbot’s agreement feels like insight, even when the response only reinforces the user's version of events. This helps explain the preference that Turkle describes. Of course, we want to return to the listener who always seems to agree with us.

One thing that can make the pull even stronger is that the chatbot can sound like an objective observer. But the chatbot knows only what we tell it. For instance, when we describe an argument, we choose the details and frame what happened, and the chatbot works only from the information we provided it with.

A therapist also starts with one person's account, but then brings independent judgment and experience to it. A therapist or confidant may notice what we leave out or question how we tell our story. A chatbot's polished answer can sound like a second opinion, but it’s really just our own account handed back to us in more confident language.

There is also a difference in who has to live with the consequences of the advice given.

A chatbot's sympathetic replies can sound like concern, but the program has no feelings about us and won't be affected by the outcome.

If the man acts on its answer, whether by reaching out to his former girlfriend or by deciding she was wrong about him, the chatbot won’t wonder how things turned out or bear any responsibility for the outcome. Professional ethics guide therapists. A friend has lived with the advice they gave, and with us, afterward.

When someone who has to face us afterward tells us something we don't want to hear, they are risking their relationship with us to say it. But a chatbot's agreement costs it nothing. That’s why the difficulty and awkwardness of human relationships is so essential. Another person may see us differently from how we see ourselves. If we turn to a chatbot's soothing agreement instead of risking disagreement and repair in real relationships, we miss chances to see our behavior from another’s perspective.

I explore these ideas in my forthcoming book, Being Seen: The Psychology of Visibility – Its Promise and Peril. Other people can help us recognize parts of ourselves that we can’t see for ourselves. Another person’s view can be painful or mistaken, but it comes from someone with an independent mind and one who cares what happens to us. A chatbot can soothe us with an answer that feels personal, but it has no independent point of view from what we tell it and nothing to lose if its advice goes wrong.
