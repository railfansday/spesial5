E-bike safety for kids: What pediatric ER doctors want parents to know before gifting one

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Home / Health & Wellness / Children's Health

A new JAMA study shows e-bike injuries in kids climbing quarter after quarter. Two pediatric physicians break down who’s ready to ride, which bikes and helmets to choose and when a crash means a trip to the ER.

By Sara Goldstein Updated Nov. 19, 2026

E-bikes and e-scooters have become a hot item on holiday and birthday wish lists. They’ve also become a hot topic in online forums and parenting circles, where people constantly worry about kids on e-bikes going too fast, losing control or making poor choices. I’ve seen it myself on my city’s bike path—kids flying by and recklessly weaving past pedestrians and other cyclists.

If you’re trying to decide whether or not to give your kid an e-bike, it helps to know what pediatricians are seeing in their emergency rooms. A JAMA study published in November 2026 looked at more than 13,000 injury visits for kids under 18 at six large U.S. children’s hospitals between 2023 and early 2026. E-bike injuries grew 33% per quarter over that stretch and e-scooter injuries grew 15% per quarter, compared with 2% for traditional bikes and 4% for traditional scooters. About a quarter of the e-bike injuries and nearly half of the e-scooter injuries involved kids under 12.

I talked with two physicians who’ve treated these kids, Dr. Beth C. Natt, system medical director of pediatrics at Atlantic Health, and Dr. Zebulon J. Timmons, division chief of emergency medicine at Children’s Nebraska in Omaha, about what parents should weigh before buying one and how to keep kids safer if one is already in the garage.

“It mostly comes down to speed,” Natt says. “Doubling the speed roughly quadruples the force in a crash.” Some e-bikes can top 25 mph, and Natt notes that e-bike crashes are far more likely to involve a car.

Timmons points to the rider as much as the bike. “Kids do not have the frontal lobe capacity and reaction time to make judgments at that speed,” he says. “You don’t take your young child to the batting cages and have them start at 80 mph.”

In the JAMA study, 17% of e-bike injuries led to a hospital stay, the highest share of any group of injuries, and 10% involved a brain injury, double the rate for regular bikes. Severe injuries, like those needing ICU care or surgery to fix a fracture, were twice as common on e-bikes, too (8% vs. 4%). Natt mostly sees broken wrists, arms and legs, plus head, face and dental injuries. Timmons sees a lot of open fractures, where the bone breaks through the skin. “Kids’ heads are bigger relative to their bodies, so they tend to hit head-first,” Natt says, “and a fracture through a growth plate can affect how a bone grows later.”

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) says kids under 16 shouldn’t ride e-scooters and, citing Consumer Product Safety Commission guidance, no e-bikes for kids under 12. Timmons says kids 12 to 15 can ride a Class 1 e-bike with proper safety training and gear, and teens 16 and up can consider a Class 2, though many will still be safer on a Class 1. Natt is more conservative. “Most pediatric injury experts would say no throttle-powered e-bike or e-scooter before about 16, and some argue for 18,” she says.

A child is ready, Natt says, if they can handle a regular bike confidently in traffic, follow the rules when you’re not around, wear a helmet without reminders and judge how fast cars are coming. “Most kids don’t have that judgment until at least their teens,” she notes.

Class 1 e-bikes are pedal-assist only, up to 20 mph. Class 2 adds a throttle, still capped at 20 mph. Class 3 is pedal-assist up to 28 mph, and many states restrict it to riders 16 and older. For kids, choose Class 1 and skip the throttle, Natt says, and avoid “e-motos” sold as e-bikes, since they go much faster. Both doctors note a regular pedal bike is still the safest choice for most kids.

There’s no federal minimum age for e-bikes, and more than half of states set age limits for Class 3 models, so check your state and town’s rules before you buy.

Timmons says a standard bike helmet is fine up to about 15 mph. From 15 to 25 mph, he recommends a helmet certified to the NTA 8776 e-bike standard, and above 25 mph, a motorcycle or moped helmet. A full-face helmet adds protection for the face and jaw, Natt says, and any helmet should be replaced after a crash.

Keeping it on is another matter. Timmons says fewer than 25% of kids treated in pediatric emergency departments were wearing a helmet when they crashed, and many take them off once they’re out of a parent’s sight. Make wearing a helmet a non-negotiable, and let friends and neighbors know to keep an eye on your child. If they’re spotted without the helmet, the bike goes away.

After the helmet, wrist guards and gloves help most because broken wrists are so common, Natt says. Front and rear lights plus reflective clothing help drivers see your child. Timmons recommends that kids wear closed-toe shoes (“No Crocs”), and for teens 16 and up on a Class 2, motorcycle-style long pants and sleeves.

Any head injury or neck pain after an e-bike or e-scooter crash should be checked in the emergency room, Timmons says. Natt says to go if your child passed out, even briefly, or keeps vomiting, or has a worsening headache, confusion, unusual sleepiness, a seizure, weakness or numbness, belly pain, a limb that looks bent or can’t bear weight, or a deep cut or facial or dental injury.

Watch for “handlebar injuries,” too, when the handlebars turn sideways and strike the chest or belly. “This mechanism causes all the force to be delivered to a small area and can cause significant internal injuries,” Timmons says. A handlebar-shaped bruise on the chest or abdomen means a trip to the ER. Once there, tell the care team what kind of e-bike your child was riding and whether they wore a helmet, Natt says.

If the e-bike or e-scooter isn’t right for your child’s age, consider exchanging it, Timmons says, or look for a community buy-back program. “This last trauma season was the worst one in my career and that was largely driven by e-bike/scooter injuries,” he says.

“These are motor vehicles, not toys,” Natt says. “A crash at e-bike speed can look more like a motorcycle injury than a bike injury.”
