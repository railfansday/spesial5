How to Design a Garden That Solves Problems and Invites You In

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

People often ask how I approach the garden design process and what information I gather before developing a plan. While every client and garden is different, I usually start with the same basic questions.

I like to create purpose-driven designs. For example, I’m often called on to solve a problem, even if it is simply “I have a new house and a blank slate.” It may be just a section of the garden, the front yard, or something specific like repairing a garden after a new septic system installation or after a new house is built next door.

Design is all about solving problems and connecting clients to their gardens, usually beginning with a questionnaire to bring all those unspoken needs to light. Questions help people consider things they would not normally think about. As a result, these conversations define their needs and desires, helping me combine different elements into a cohesive design.

The same approach applies to projects in my own garden. I don’t give myself a questionnaire, but I do ask myself the same questions. First, I assess the challenges, the location of the area in relation to the rest of the property, and the environmental conditions that will influence the planting.

Because the sidewalk was already there when I moved in, I had to incorporate it into my design, but I could still make it disappear visually.

Looking at the space, I could see a definite focal point within my property. With the neighbor behind me, the view naturally led the eye toward the water. Therefore, I designed a one-sided border rather than an island bed meant to be viewed from multiple directions. Making the border as deep as 20 feet in places gave me enough room to continue screening the neighboring property while also providing some sound buffering.

Understanding the environmental conditions was the next step. The back edge is on the north side of my property. The large trees and shrubs would not shade the rest of the bed. This meant the bed would be in full sun all day, all season long.

What a challenge for this PNW native who grew up mostly in the forest (or woods, as we called it). I had to learn about plants that liked full sun, drying winds, and dry soil so they could thrive. Rather than trying to re-create the shaded woodland conditions I knew best, I needed to work with what the site naturally offered.

Growing up in the forest, I longed for wide-open sky and long views. Above all, I wanted to immerse myself in the garden. A long row of hedging plants that I could only look at wasn’t what I had in mind. I wanted to become part of the blowing, rustling movement of the grasses. While experiencing the plants, I also wanted to connect with insects, pollinators, birds, frogs, and even a few rabbits.

I consider the challenge met. The amount of wildlife activity surrounding me in the garden never ceases to amaze me. I feel as if I’m in a different world while surrounded by all the action and movement of the grasses and perennials, far away from big cedar trees, moss, and ferns. Sun, wind, fragrant flowers, and the smell of warm earth are all-encompassing there.

Even during the winter, this garden pulls me out, bundled up and coffee in hand, to see what is happening. Early spring bulbs push through the soil, mice shelter under the evergreen sedges, and birds search for a breakfast of grass seeds and gather bits and pieces to build new nests.

The ultimate goal of any designer is finding the right outdoor elements to pull people outside and connect them with nature. Those elements are individual to each client. They could be an eating area, outdoor kitchen, hot tub, play area, pond, fountain, or hidden sitting area. Maybe the goal is plant-driven, with a vegetable garden, cutting garden, or collection of conifers.

When these features are thoughtfully integrated with immersive, naturalistic plantings, the result is an outdoor space that invites people to experience their gardens throughout the year.
