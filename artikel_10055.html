Inspiring FOSS project Crosspoint Reader has lessons about tech, communities, and LLMs

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Cheap tiny e-readers and the perils of LLM-generated drive-by pull requests – plus much more

The Ubuntu Summit isn't all about Ubuntu, or even Linux. The second day opened with a talk from CrossPoint Reader project lead Justin Mitchell, with both good and bad news for all kinds of FOSS.

The talk, EPUBs, ESP32s, and Everyone's A Developer Now, was all about a FOSS project that isn't Linux-based – because the devices it targets are much too small to run even the smallest, most cut-down distro.

Recently, a new generation of roughly credit-card sized ebook readers has been thriving. Some have integral magnets, which let them cling to the back of iPhones – or to models with magnetically attached wireless chargers, anyway. The idea is that instead of waking your phone and doom-scrolling, you flip it over and read a book. They are bringing about what Mitchell calls "the Tiktok-ification of reading."

Many of them, though, come from their various manufacturers significantly limited by their original OSes. The strong sales are linked to the availability of free, open-source, cross-platform alternative firmware, which removes many of the restrictions that the devices ship with. For most of them, that means Crosspoint Reader. Some devices are now shipping, new, with versions of Crosspoint Reader.

This runs on multiple different models of compact e-readers, and this small community project has seen impressively widespread adoption: over 300,000 installations. Early in his talk, project lead Mitchell mentioned more than 1,600 forks on GitHub, but at the time of writing, that's up to 2,300 and counting. He estimates nearly half a million users. This kind of success forced Crosspoint Reader to change from being FOSS code to a product. A peripheral example is the Brick Club List, a list of what versions work with what devices – an Excel spreadsheet that grew into a website.

If you haven't been paying attention, you may not have noticed just how much e-readers have shrunk – in size and in cost – in the last few years. The Reg FOSS desk first noticed this trend in 2024, when we read about the smartphone-sized Onyx Palma, which sold for around $300 (about £225). As it happens, this was a decade after The Register first looked at one of Boox's devices. Mini e-readers – and their prices – have continued shrinking since. The $99 (£75) Xteink X4 Classic has a 4.- inch (10.9 cm) screen, and the $79 (£59) Xteink X3 is even smaller: it's the size of a credit card, with a 3.7-inch (9.4 cm) screen. As a result of their strong sales, there are now multiple similar devices on sale, such as the Boox Picco.

Mitchell's talk is the first one – about 14 minutes in.

The project's GitHub page lists 11 devices it currently supports, all of which are based around the ESP32-C3 system on a chip. The ESP32-C series chips are more powerful than the earlier models of ESP32 microcontrollers: they have a single RISC-V core, but running in the low hundreds of MHz and with some 400 kB of RAM, of which about 380 kB is available. It's very little space, but enough to have fun.

Mitchell talked about some of the problems of being a FOSS project that is used by nearly half a million non-techie people. For many of Crosspoint Reader's users, it's the first time they have ever used open source. Some want tech support. Many demand features that can't fit into these tiny devices. Even when the requests are modest and feasible, such a large user base means that people want a wide range of functionality – and thanks to inexpensive cloud LLMs, even totally non-technical users can try to add new functions for themselves. The result is multiple code submissions every day. Many might fit on their own, but it's impossible to fit them all in. So many queries and submissions puts considerable stress on the volunteer maintainers.

Mitchell mentioned a possibility that they – and we – had never dreamed of: that of pull requests (GitHub parlance for code submissions) from people using such extensive automation that the submitters don’t realize that they’re sending them. One submitter sent 10,000 pull requests in a single month. The problems aren't just technical, or workload-related: there's also a delicate balance of how to refuse well-intentioned and technologically viable submissions that just won't fit for non-technical reasons, such as being too niche for the typical use-case of a sub-£60 gadget.

The problem is that this FOSS firmware is making these cheap gadgets so much more useful that re-flashing them is an implicit part of the purchase. That introduces all kinds of unexpected side-effects, for the users and for the developers. Crosspoint Reader is a microcosm of the rapid growth of Linux and FOSS in general – and a taste of what will happen if it ever goes truly mainstream with mass-market consumers. One of his "what I got wrong about this job" points we particularly liked was:

“The scarce resource isn’t code. We have more code than we know what to do with."

Instead, it's… "Judgement. Trust. Responsibility."

There are lessons for the whole IT industry in there, we reckon. Even for the biggest players.

In the meantime, though, these tiny cheap e-readers are an interesting new thing. As Mitchell put it, paraphrasing The Atlantic, they're:

"Fun in a way gadgets haven’t been for a long time."

Just being fun is a goal that is far too often forgotten… Especially in FOSS. ®
