Every Season of 'The Bear,' Ranked Worst to Best

I have never wanted to hug a group of fictional people and tell them to shut the hell up quite as badly as I do with the staff of The Bear. These people can turn a perfectly normal conversation into a personal insult in under twenty seconds. Somebody asks for help, somebody else hears criticism, and suddenly three generations of unresolved family trauma are being discussed over a pan of burning food. I adore them. I also find them exhausting.

And that's why I'm so particular about this show. I don't need everyone to become emotionally healthy or start speaking politely. I need their mistakes to lead somewhere. I need the affection underneath all that anger to feel earned. Across five seasons, the series has sometimes tested my patience, occasionally broken my heart, and delivered a few hours of television I would defend against practically anything. Christopher Storer made this series for FX on Hulu and clearly let us know from the beginning that the restaurant was an extension of this family's emotional wreckage. Let’s further dig in.

Carmy Berzatto (Jeremy Allen White) has spent so long trying to build the perfect restaurant that he's nearly destroyed his relationships with the people helping him. Season 4 finally forces him to confront that contradiction. Sydney (Ayo Edebiri) is considering an opportunity outside the restaurant, Uncle Jimmy's financial pressure is becoming increasingly difficult to ignore, and the kitchen desperately needs Carmy to stop treating every imperfect detail like a personal catastrophe.

I wanted to love this season more. There are beautiful moments, particularly Sydney's conversations with her father and the uncomfortable honesty of Carmy's decision to leave the restaurant behind. But too much of the storytelling feels hesitant, as though everyone is approaching an important realization without quite getting there. Even the strongest emotional exchanges sometimes arrive after hours of familiar frustration. I respect where Carmy's story goes. I just wish the journey there had been considerably more exciting.

I'll defend “Napkins” against almost any criticism you throw at this season. Tina (Liza Colón-Zayas) loses her job, spends a miserable day searching for another, and eventually wanders into the Beef. There, Mikey (Jon Bernthal) offers her something she's been denied everywhere else that day: a little kindness. Their conversation is painfully ordinary, which is precisely why it hits so hard. Ayo Edebiri directed the episode, and that quiet meeting accomplishes more than some of the season's elaborate restaurant scenes.

Then “Ice Chips” puts Natalie (Abby Elliott) in labor with her deeply unreliable mother Donna (Jamie Lee Curtis) beside her. Two women who've spent years hurting and misunderstanding each other suddenly have nowhere to hide. I love these episodes desperately. Unfortunately, Carmy's obsessive pursuit of perfection grows repetitive, while the restaurant's larger story often struggles to move forward. Season 3 can be maddeningly self-indulgent. Yet those two intimate stories remind me just how extraordinary this show can be when it allows somebody other than Carmy to occupy the emotional center.

Four Netflix characters, four completely different ways of showing up for somebody. This is the one who fits how you actually do friendship, and what having them in your life would be like.

You do not do friendship at half volume, and neither does he. What you want is somebody who says yes to the plan before you have finished describing the plan.

You are the friend who makes things happen, and so is she. Together you would be mildly exhausting to everyone around you and completely delighted with yourselves.

You have no patience for pleasant lies, and she has no ability to tell them. What looks like coldness from the outside is the most reliable friendship on this list.

You look after whoever is having the worst day. So does he. It is the least strategic way to move through the world and the best possible quality in a friend.

An enormous storm, a booking disaster, dwindling supplies, and an overloaded dining room. Oh, and the chef who helped build the restaurant has announced he's leaving. Season 5 gives Sydney, Richie (Ebon Moss-Bachrach), and the remaining team one monstrous service to prove they can manage without Carmy. I love that premise. After spending years watching these people discuss the possibility of becoming better, we finally get to see whether they've actually learned anything.

The pressure is magnificent, particularly when improvisation replaces Carmy's obsession with absolute control. Tina gets opportunities to demonstrate her confidence, Sydney starts trusting her own leadership, and Richie increasingly resembles somebody who genuinely belongs in hospitality. There's a wonderful sense that this restaurant has become larger than the man who created it. The season's final gathering is affectionate enough to make me forgive some of its more sentimental impulses. And Carmy's last message to Mikey, simply saying “All good,” is devastating in its simplicity. Five seasons of grief, anger, and impossible expectations, reduced to two words that finally sound like peace.

The Original Beef of Chicagoland is a disaster. The kitchen is filthy, the accounts are a nightmare, the employees refuse to cooperate, and Carmen has inherited the entire operation following his brother's suicide. He knows how to cook at an extraordinary level. Unfortunately, knowing how to run a prestigious kitchen doesn't prepare him for managing people who loved Mikey and resent practically everything Carmy represents.

I'll never forget “Review.” Online orders keep pouring from the printer, Sydney's attempts to improve the operation collapse into chaos, and Carmy finally loses control. It's an agonizing episode because everybody is contributing to the disaster, yet nobody has enough perspective to stop it. And underneath all that shouting is a grieving younger brother desperately trying to understand why Mikey left him behind. When Carmy eventually finds Mikey's note and the money hidden inside those tomato cans, I feel relief every single time. For a brief moment, these exhausted people have something worth celebrating together.

Finally, my favorite. Richie is assigned to polish forks at an elite restaurant, and I genuinely believe the resulting episode, “Forks,” belongs among the greatest hours of television in recent memory. Here's a man who's spent years defending his own importance through arguments, resentment, and sheer stubbornness. Then somebody teaches him that taking care of other people can be an extraordinary profession. Ebon Moss-Bachrach makes Richie's growing pride almost unbearably touching. That moment when he drives through Chicago singing along to Taylor Swift? I could watch it a hundred times.

And that's only one part of an astonishing season. The old sandwich shop is being transformed into a fine-dining restaurant, with Sydney, Marcus (Lionel Boyce), and the others pursuing skills they never imagined possessing. “Fishes” takes us into a Berzatto family Christmas dinner so hostile that Donna eventually drives her car into the house. Watching it makes every argument in the present more understandable. Then the restaurant finally opens, and Carmy ends up trapped inside the walk-in refrigerator, unable to participate in the success he's sacrificed everything to achieve. That contradiction breaks my heart. Everybody else is discovering what they can become, while Carmy still cannot imagine happiness without finding a way to punish himself. Season 2 understands these people completely. I wouldn't trade a single one of its extraordinary emotional highs for a tidier, more comfortable story.
