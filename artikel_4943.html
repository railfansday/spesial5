The 10 Best Smartwatches for Men, Tested by Gear Editors

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Our product picks are editor-tested, expert-approved. We may earn a commission through links on our site. Why Trust Us?

The Samsung Galaxy Watch9 and Suunto Race 2 came out on top in our testing.

AT MEN'S HEALTH, we love a good fitness tracker—but let’s be honest, they’re not exactly stylish. Most of them look great with running shorts and a workout tee—but less so when you’re in a crisp dress shirt and tailored trousers. That’s where the best smartwatches for men come in. They bridge the gap between performance tech and actual style—tracking your heart rate and steps without clashing with your fit. A great smartwatch can go from your 6 a.m. run to your 8 p.m. cocktail hour without missing a beat. Some, like the TAG Heuer Connected, look so sleek you could wear them for a black-tie event and look like you belong on the cover of a magazine. We’ve tested dozens to find the ones that deliver that perfect balance of design, durability, and data.

With the Galaxy Watch9, Samsung didn’t mess with a good thing. The aluminum case, “squircle” shape, and bright 3,000-nit screen all carry over from the Watch8 that was our previous favorite—so at a glance you’d be hard-pressed to tell them apart. The key changes are under the hood: a bigger battery, a more power-efficient processor, and a smarter set of health tools that turn your sleep and workout data into advice you can act on.

One of the most notable new additions is the Vitals tool, which learns your normal range for things like heart rate and blood oxygen, then flags you when something’s off. A jump in your resting heart rate or skin temperature, for instance, can signal a cold before you feel it—keep in mind these should be anecdotal. The other new features are for the data-driven crowd. A Heart Health Score rolls your sleep, activity, and blood-pressure trends into a single read on how your lifestyle is affecting your heart, with tips to improve it. My sleep schedule has been all over the place lately, making me feel lethargic at certain parts of the day, and the Watch9 has been recommending I go to bed earlier. Finally, a Fitness Index charts your body composition, cardio, endurance, flexibility, and strength on a spider graph so you can see where you’re strong and where you’re lagging.

There are carryovers from the Watch8 too, including the Running Coach that builds a plan off a 12-minute test run, with well-structured workouts, and the Antioxidant Index, which reads the carotenoid levels in your skin for a rough sense of your fruit and vegetable intake (to be taken with a grain of salt).

In a brief head-to-head test against the Apple Watch Series 11, the Watch9’s heart rate held within a few beats on runs and walks. I’ve been following a Couch to 5K program for the past four weeks, and the heart-rate readings during each run are reliable enough to pace by. Daily Cardio Load has also been a big help, nudging me to dial it back instead of pushing myself too hard, too soon.

Samsung dropped the older Exynos chip for Qualcomm’s Snapdragon Wear Elite, and everything feels snappier, from starting a workout to pulling up a stat mid-run to talking to Google’s Gemini. Now you just lift your wrist to say a command, rather than first saying a wake word.

The Watch9 keeps the excellent Dynamic Lug System, making it comfortable enough to sleep in, and its premium-yet-friendly design moves from a morning run to a business-casual meeting without looking out of place. It comes in 40mm and 44mm sizes with a wider band lineup, including Sport, Fabric, and a two-tone Misty. The battery is bigger on paper—390mAh on the 40mm, a 20 percent bump, and in my testing it still lands around a day of use. At $379.99, Samsung’s newest model costs a bit more than the Watch8 did at launch, but you’re getting the smartest, most feature-packed version yet.

The Apple Watch SE used to be the Apple Watch you bought because you couldn’t justify spending more. Apple changed that with the SE 3 by adding an always-on display, the S10 chip, double-tap and wrist-flick gestures, 5G cellular on cellular models, and health features like Sleep Score and breathing irregularity detection through the Vitals app.

Over several weeks of testing, the SE 3 held up as a daily driver without feeling like a compromise. The always-on display made a real difference while exercising, and performance felt snappy across the board—from launching workouts to setting timers with on-device Siri. Fast charging is the other underrated upgrade. A 15-minute top-up while showering in the morning was enough to carry the watch through the rest of the day and overnight for sleep tracking.

After a rough night (67 out of 100), I could see exactly how that Sleep Score was calculated and adjusted accordingly, scoring a 90 the next night. The Vitals app runs in the background and tracks wrist temperature and respiratory rate without any input. It hasn’t flagged anything yet, but that kind of passive monitoring adds value over time.

The new Series 12 is also available at $399. For the extra $150, it adds an ECG app, a Blood Oxygen app, and hypertension notifications, none of which the SE 3 has. It also takes a background heart rate reading every five seconds, and its Readiness app turns recent activity, training load, vitals, and sleep into a single score.

The Series 12’s display reaches 2,000 nits (the SE 3 gets to 1,000), the bezels are thinner, and the screen area is larger. Battery life is rated at 24 hours compared to the SE 3’s 18 hours, and five minutes on the charger gives up to 10 hours of sleep tracking, where the SE 3 gets up to eight hours from eight minutes. The Series 12 also adds a water temperature sensor and a depth gauge to six meters for snorkeling.

We recommend the Series 12 if you use a watch to follow training load and recovery, if you swim or snorkel, or if you want more advanced health tracking features. But the SE 3 is the call if you want notifications and standard workout and sleep tracking on an Apple Watch that still has an always-on display, and you’re on more of a budget.

If you want a GPS running watch that nails the fundamentals and is relatively affordable, the Coros Pace 4 should be at the top of your list. At $249, it competes on accuracy, battery life, and display quality with watches that cost significantly more, and at 40 grams with a silicone strap, it’s light enough to forget you’re wearing it.

The biggest upgrade from the Pace 3 is the AMOLED display, which is sharp and easy to read in bright sunlight. Battery life actually improved despite the brighter screen, jumping to 31 hours in max GPS mode with all satellite systems active, or 24 hours with always-on display enabled. A quick charge while you’re showering or eating a meal should top it off.

GPS tracking is where Coros built its reputation, and the Pace 4 delivers. Dual-frequency satellite support promises clean, accurate tracks that hold up even alongside buildings and under tree cover. The Pace 4 also features voice logging, so after finishing a run, the watch prompts you to record your thoughts, which get transcribed and saved in the Coros app. The watch also uses breadcrumb navigation rather than full maps, so if you rely on turn-by-turn directions while exploring new routes, you’ll want to step up to the Coros Apex or a Garmin with offline maps.

Our contributing editor and watch expert Ted Stafford, who styles every Men’s Health cover, was seriously impressed by the TAG Heuer Connected Calibre E5. “I was impressed by how well the E5 straddles the line between traditional style and modern innovation,” he says. “It wears beautifully on the wrist, with a sleek, stylish design and a comfort-first build. Its intuitive interface also makes it refreshingly simple to navigate, even on the move or during high-intensity activities.”

In fact, Stafford recently met with Tag Heuer’s team in Boston to test the Connected Calibre E5. During his 5K run, he appreciated how easy it was to glance at messages, check his heart rate, and accurately track his activity—all without slowing down. He also noted that the stainless bracelet stayed comfortable and secure the whole time. Another standout feature is the ability to customize the watch face. “Whether you’re in the mood for a sporty Carrera or a bold Formula 1 look, the E5 lets you effortlessly switch styles to match your day,” he says. “This and all the other unique tech offerings make having a connected watch a win-win for me.”

What really makes the E5 shine, though, is how much it packs under the hood. You get dual-band GPS for pinpoint tracking, heart-rate and blood-oxygen sensors, and full sleep and stress tracking that rivals top fitness-focused wearables. The new TAG Heuer OS runs smoother than ever, with cleaner visuals and tighter iPhone integration for those in the Apple ecosystem. Plus, it’s built like a tank with 50 meters of water resistance, a sapphire crystal display, and your choice of steel or titanium cases. Battery life clocks in at about two days of regular use, and fast charging gets you back to full in around 30 minutes.

The Apple Watch Ultra 4 keeps the 49mm titanium case of the Ultra 3 and replaces the sensor on the back. The new Health Sensing System adds a circular optical sensing array, larger and more efficient LEDs for heart rate readings, and more surface area touching the skin for ECG readings. Earlier models took background heart rate readings every five minutes. The Ultra 4 takes one every five seconds and shows it in a new heart rate complication, which is a data point you pin to the watch face. Faster readings also let the watch measure heart rate variability (the variation in time between heartbeats) as often as every five minutes while you’re at rest.

The Ultra 4 also adds Readiness, which combines Fitness, Vitals, and Sleep Score into a rating from 1 to 10 for how prepared your body is for the day. Readiness needs several consecutive nights of sleeping with the watch before it starts scoring. The S11 chip makes the watch feel quicker and powers a new step counter and better Siri, which our tester used to set timers more often than ever.

Apple rates the battery at up to 50 hours of normal use and up to 84 hours in Low Power Mode, with up to 18 hours of continuous workout tracking. In testing, the first charge lasted two nights of sleep. When the battery dropped to about 10 percent one night, our tester was almost three-quarters charged by the time he had brushed his teeth, gotten into bed, and set an alarm. Apple says 15 minutes on the charger gives up to 18 hours of use. Adventure watches from other brands can last weeks between charges, so the Ultra 4 still trails them.

The Ultra 4 costs $799 and the Series 12 costs $399. Both have the same sensors and health features, so the Ultra 4’s advantages are the bigger battery, the 49mm case, and adventure features like precision dual-frequency GPS, emergency SOS via satellite, and diving to 40 meters.

When it comes to premium running watches, the latest Suunto Race (the Race 2) packs a serious punch at $499—or $599 for the Titanium Trail version. The display is larger and brighter than the Race 1 while being thinner. That said, at 49mm, the Race 2 isn’t a small watch. Men’s Health Senior Gear and Reviews Editor Ryan Brower put it to the test, noting: “While I’ve enjoyed testing it, the Race 2 would be nice to have in a smaller size.”

That bigger size, though, makes room for a faster processor and an impressive 55 hours of battery life in GPS mode (or up to 30 days with daily use and no training). “The battery claims really hold,” Brower says. “I’ve only had to charge it a couple of times throughout months of testing. To be fair, I don’t wear it every day, but I did for a few stretches to get a sense of it.” Another big improvement is the redesigned charging clip, which Brower found far more effective and reliable than previous Suunto models that didn’t always connect easily.

Performance-wise, the Race 2 delivers more than 115 sport modes. Brower put many of those programs through their paces—treadmill running, running, trail running, strength training, hiking, climbing, rowing (technically paddling), and even surfing—and each one worked smoothly, serving up plenty of useful metrics. GPS tracking proved fairly accurate, and features like training load and recovery provided deeper insights when synced with the Suunto Coach or other training apps. Building a regimen and letting the watch help implement it, Brower says, “can be a great tool for runners.”

The Race 2 leans into a rugged aesthetic with its large round case and durable polymer or titanium build. Its style clearly favors sport and outdoor fits, so the 49mm case can feel oversized or out of place in formal or office clothes. The standard strap is a breathable silicone, perfect for sweaty workouts or trail runs, and the Titanium Trail model adds a premium case finish for those who want a more elevated look in casual settings.

The Pixel Watch 5 looks almost identical to the Pixel Watch 4. It comes in the same 41mm and 45mm sizes, with a domed Actua 360 display under scratch-resistant Gorilla Glass that reaches 3,000 nits at its brightest. Most of the changes are inside and in the software. Google says the new watch is 20 percent faster than the Pixel Watch 4, and it’s built around Gemini. Raise your wrist to talk to the assistant, and the watch face brings up things like flight info and your ETA when you need them.

The Pixel Watch 5 can detect a loss of pulse and prompt a call to emergency services. Satellite SOS lets you contact emergency services and update family from remote areas without cell service, and Google includes it free for two years after activation. Fall Detection, Car Crash Detection, and Safety Check are back, too. Safety Check shares your location with emergency contacts if a timer runs out during a solo run or a late walk home and you don’t respond.

New this generation, the Pixel Watch 5 can show your blood pressure trends and track signs of insulin resistance over the course of a month. Google is clear that neither is a medical reading or a diagnosis, but both can flag patterns to bring up with your doctor. You also get the ECG app, irregular heart rhythm alerts, SpO2, HRV, skin temperature, and a nightly Sleep Score from what Google calls its most accurate sleep tracking yet. Each morning, Morning Brief combines your sleep recap, readiness, and the weather on one screen.

Google calls the watch’s dual-frequency GPS its best yet. It uses machine learning to keep runs through tall downtown buildings and hikes under thick tree cover on track. The watch tracks more than 50 activities. You can build strength sessions with exact reps, weights, and rest timers, and get audio and visual guidance as you go. A Google Health Premium subscription, which includes three free months, adds a Gemini-powered coach that adjusts your plan around your schedule.

Google rates the battery at up to 30 hours for the 41mm model and up to 40 hours for the 45mm. That means charging most days, but the included Quick Charge Dock adds 15 hours in 15 minutes, so a short top-up in the morning gets you through a night of sleep tracking. The watch only works with Android phones, and at $399.99, it costs $50 more than the Pixel Watch 4 did at launch. If you’re an Android user who wants the strongest safety features on any smartwatch, it’s still the one to beat.

The Fēnix 9 is Garmin’s premium multisport GPS watch, built for people who take their training and outdoor adventures seriously. It comes in 43mm, 47mm, and 51mm cases, with the 43mm model being styled for smaller wrists. Every version has an AMOLED display with a titanium bezel and a scratch-resistant sapphire lens, and it offers a much brighter screen than the Fēnix 8’s. The watch is tested to U.S. military standards for heat, shock, and water resistance, and it’s rated for recreational dives to 40 meters.

Navigation has always been Garmin’s forte, and it’s where the latest model stands apart. Full-color topographic maps come preloaded, and the Fēnix 9 has twice the RAM of the Fēnix 8 and an updated map engine that pans 30 percent faster. Storage doubles to 64GB, which holds twice as many maps as before plus up to 7,000 songs from Spotify, Deezer, or Amazon Music. Tell the watch how far you want to go, and dynamic round-trip routing suggests a loop that brings you back. If you go off course, it maps a new route with the same mileage. NextFork shows the distance to the next trail junction and the name of the trail, ClimbPro shows current and upcoming climbs on downloaded courses, and SatIQ adjusts GPS accuracy to save battery.

Garmin rates the 43mm model at up to 10 days in smartwatch mode. That's a different category from any Apple Watch or Samsung Galaxy Watch, and it means far less battery anxiety day to day. You don’t have to be quite as strategic when you charge as you would with other smartwatches.

The training tools go deeper than ever before, too. New stamina features show how your endurance holds up over different distances and help you manage your effort during a race. Garmin Epic combines several days of activities into one summary in the Garmin Connect app, which is useful for a backpacking trip or a training camp. Rowers get VO2 max and power metrics, and rucking workouts let you enter your pack weight.

Returning features include Training Readiness, which scores your sleep, recovery, and training load each morning, along with HRV status, sleep coaching, PacePro race pacing, and an ECG app. A built-in speaker and microphone let you take calls from your wrist when the watch is paired with your iPhone or Android phone, and there's a built-in LED flashlight.

The 43mm Fēnix 9 is pricey at $999.99, and more advanced connectivity features like inReach satellite messaging, SOS, and LTE calling require the more expensive Fēnix 9 Pro, so the standard model relies on your paired phone to send your live location if it detects an incident. But for athletes who log serious miles across multiple sports and want one watch that handles all of it, the Fēnix 9 is the way to go.

We got our hands on the G-Shock Move, and this thing is a beast—in the best way possible. Built with G-Shock’s signature toughness, it feels indestructible yet surprisingly comfortable on the wrist. I ran, lifted, and hiked with it, and it handled everything without a hiccup. Inside, you’ve got serious hardware: GPS, heart-rate monitoring, altimeter, compass, thermometer, and a gyroscope that tracks every stride, step, and elevation change with precision.

What really impressed me was how capable it felt in different conditions. I tracked a few long runs with GPS fully active, and the data stayed consistent and accurate the whole time—no weird pace spikes or drop-offs. The battery life held up too; even when I pushed it hard, I still got several days before needing a recharge. And when it does run low, the solar-assisted charging converts sunlight into extra power, adding several hours—or even a day or two—of runtime if you’re outdoors. It’s rated for 200 meters of water resistance, so swimming, rain, and sweat are all fair game.

Living up to its name, the G-Shock Move supports more than a dozen sports, from trail running and cycling to gym training and open-water swimming, while also monitoring sleep, recovery, and blood-oxygen levels. The display’s bright and easy-to-read mid-workout, and the interface feels intuitive enough to switch modes on the fly. For guys who demand a watch that’s as tough and versatile as they are, this one’s the real deal.

The Fitbit Edge is a fitness tracker first, with just enough smartwatch features to cover the basics. You can read notifications, send quick replies, set alarms, control your music, get turn-by-turn directions from Google Maps, and find your phone. But there are no calls, no contactless payments, no voice assistant, and no app store.

That limited feature set is why the battery lasts up to seven days and charges back to full in 90 minutes. The rounded display is also 45 percent bigger and more than 40 percent brighter than on previous Fitbits, and it shows your stats and coaching cues at a glance.

Built-in GPS and an altimeter let you map outdoor runs, rides, and hikes and track how much you climb without bringing your phone. The Edge also recognizes workouts automatically, so a walk or run still counts if you forget to start it. The tracker records heart rate, HRV, SpO2, breathing rate, skin temperature, and resting heart rate. Each night it also tracks your sleep stages and gives you a Sleep Score. High and low heart rate alerts and Irregular Rhythm Notifications flag potential heart issues.

A Google Health Premium membership, which comes with three free months and then costs $9.99 a month, unlocks a Gemini-powered coach that builds a training plan around your schedule. It also adds blood pressure and insulin resistance trends. Google says neither is a clinical reading nor a screening tool, but both can flag patterns to discuss with your doctor.

At $179.99, the Edge works with both iPhone and Android phones and comes with small and large bands in the box. It’s a good fit if a full smartwatch is more than you need but you still want to see texts and directions on your wrist. If you want calls, payments, and apps, look at the Pixel Watch 5 or Apple Watch SE 3 instead.

For texts, calls, and notifications, you need a cellular model. The Apple Watch Ultra 4 comes with cellular built in, while the Apple Watch SE 3 and Series 12 offer it as an upgrade. Watches without cellular, along with fitness trackers like the Fitbit Edge, stop getting notifications once you're out of range of your phone. Some options like the Ultra 4 also add satellite SOS, which lets you reach emergency services when there's no cell service at all.

Smartwatches are more accurate than ever, thanks to newer optical sensors, more LEDs, and more sensor area touching your skin. Most of today’s flagship watches from Apple, Google, Samsung, and Garmin are reliable enough for everyday tracking and most workouts. A chest strap is still the most precise option, though, so pair one with your watch for hard intervals or races. The Garmin Fēnix 9, for example, works with Garmin's HRM 600. Over time, trends in your heart rate are more useful than any single reading, so wear your watch consistently.

Most full-featured smartwatches last one to two days on a charge, while fitness-focused watches can go a week or more. The Google Pixel Watch 5 is rated at up to 30 hours for the 41mm model and up to 40 hours for the 45mm, and the Apple Watch Ultra 4 is rated at up to 50 hours (up to 84 hours in low power mode). Garmin rates the 43mm Fēnix 9 at up to 10 days, the Fitbit Edge lasts up to seven, and the Suunto Race 2 can go up to 30 days with daily use when you’re not tracking workouts.

Your actual battery life depends on how you use the watch, since GPS tracking, continuous heart rate monitoring, and an always-on display all drain it faster. If you track your sleep, look for fast charging, too. Apple says 15 minutes on the charger gives the Ultra 4 up to 18 hours of use, and 15 minutes on Google’s Quick Charge Dock adds 15 hours to the Pixel Watch 5.

Fabric and silicone bands are best for workouts because they’re lightweight, breathable, and dry quickly. Woven nylon and elastic straps adjust easily and won’t dig into your wrist mid-run, though they can hold onto odor and need an occasional wash. Metal bracelets in stainless steel, titanium, or aluminum give a smartwatch a polished, traditional look for the office or a formal event, but they feel heavy during workouts and can trap sweat. Leather bands dress up a watch for work or dinner and mold to your wrist over time. They’re not a great option for workouts, though, because sweat and water can warp or stain them.

A lifestyle or hybrid smartwatch is the best fit for most offices, because it’s sleek enough for business casual and still tough enough for the gym. The Samsung Galaxy Watch9 is a good example, with a slim aluminum case that works with casual, sporty, and business-casual outfits. If you dress up more often, a luxury smartwatch like the TAG Heuer Connected Calibre E5 pairs a stainless steel case and sapphire crystal with performance tracking, and it looks right with a suit. Adventure watches like the Suunto Race 2 and Garmin Fēnix 9 have larger cases built for trail running, hiking, and multisport training, while rugged watches like the Casio G-Shock Move are shock- and water-resistant for tough conditions. Both are better suited to the weekend than a client meeting.

Every smartwatch should handle notifications, music controls, and heart rate and sleep tracking. Built-in GPS is worth having if you run, ride, or hike without your phone. Beyond the basics, the features worth paying for depend on how you train. Runners and outdoor athletes should look for dual-frequency GPS and offline maps, like those on the Garmin Fēnix 9 and Suunto Race 2. If you want guidance, look for training plans that adapt to your progress, like Garmin Coach or the Gemini-powered coach in Google Health Premium. For recovery, look for HRV, sleep scores, and readiness scores, which the Apple Watch Ultra 4, Pixel Watch 5, and Fēnix 9 all offer. If you want to leave your phone at home, look for an onboard voice assistant like Siri or Gemini, contactless payments, and LTE for calls and texts.

Bracelets (or metal bands) are best for work, formal occasions, and everyday wear. They give your smartwatch a more polished, traditional-watch look—great for dressing up or blending your tech into a sleek outfit. Stainless steel feels classic and weighty, while titanium offers a lighter, more premium feel with added durability. Aluminum links are affordable and comfortable for all-day wear. However, metal can feel heavy during workouts and may trap sweat, so they’re better for the office than the gym.

Fabric bands are great for casual wear, fitness tracking, and comfort-first users. The straps are lightweight, breathable, and soft against the skin, making them great for long wear or sweaty workouts. Woven nylon or elastic options adjust easily, dry quickly, and don’t dig into your wrist mid-run. Fabric bands can hold onto odor or discolor over time, so they’ll need a wash if you’re wearing them to the gym often.

Leather bands work for everyday and office wear, and dressed-up casual looks. This material instantly elevates a smartwatch—ideal if you want tech that doesn’t scream “tech.” A good leather strap molds to your wrist over time and works seamlessly from the office to dinner. However, leather doesn’t love sweat or water, so it can warp or stain after a tough workout or shower.

To pick the best smartwatches, we didn’t just look at specs—we constantly put them to the test in real life. Our contributing editor and watch expert Ted Stafford, who styles every Men’s Health cover shoot, weighed in on how they feel on the wrist and how versatile they are for everyday wear. Ryan Brower, our Senior Gear and Reviews Editor, tested the watches’ performance, accuracy, and tech features over weeks of workouts and training sessions. Our fitness editors logged miles, tracked recovery, monitored sleep, and pushed each watch through tough workouts to see which ones could actually keep up. After combining all their hands-on notes, we narrowed the list to the models that deliver on style, durability, comfort, battery life, smart features, and reliable health and fitness tracking.

Smartwatches won't replace your smartphone, but they can be a great complementary accessory. Most of today's smartwatches can provide alerts for calls, texts, emails, calendar notifications, and more. The best smartwatches also add outdoor-centric features like GPS-enabled maps with route-tracking. Plus, many people like smartwatches for advanced health and fitness tracking, as many premium options can monitor sleep patterns, track stress levels and heart rate throughout the day, and even provide accident or emergency warnings (as with Apple's Crash Detection feature).

Depending on the make and model, most smartwatches last about two to four years—but it's entirely possible for yours to last longer or much shorter depending on use. Today's models are durable enough to handle years of daily wear, so longevity isn't usually the issue. What tends to make your smartwatch feel outdated is old software that can't keep up with the latest updates or app integrations.

If you wear your smartwatch every day and rely on it for calls, texts, and fitness tracking, you'll likely want to upgrade every couple of years to stay current with the newest software and performance features. If you wear your smartwatch a couple of days a week or just for fitness purposes, then you'll be okay with sticking to the watch you have for a longer period of time. After all, you're still getting the same high-quality performance tracking metrics regardless of the software you're running.
