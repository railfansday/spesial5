Helsinki puts trust at the heart of its smart-city model

Benjamin Plackett is a science journalist based in London.

Helsinki Central Library Oodi is just one example of a building in the Finnish capital that uses real-time data to optimize energy efficiency.Credit: Sergei Gapon/Anadolu Agency via Getty Images

Urban planners — and curious residents — can go online and click on almost any building in Helsinki to see a host of details including its age, height, heating source and estimated energy use. The information sits in the various maps that make up Helsinki 3D, an open-data digital twin of the city built by the City of Helsinki from aerial photographs, light and detection laser scanning (LiDAR) surveys, housing registers and geographical data. The project’s Energy and Climate Atlas estimates heating demand for nearly every building, models how it would change in response to different climate conditions and calculates the solar-panel potential across every roof and wall in the Finnish capital.

Helsinki’s technical prowess in these kinds of projects is underpinned by a substantial research base; the city is ranked 100th among the world’s top 200 cities in the Nature Index, with particular strength in health sciences (ranked 69th), biological sciences (80th) and Earth and environmental sciences (83rd). It produced 55% of Finland’s total Share in 2025, a ratio that is among the highest for cities in the overall top 200.

The city has treated itself as a laboratory, too. Kalasatama, a brownfield site in the harbour district of Helsinki, has been designated as an urban testbed for researchers, city officials and companies to try out novel urban infrastructure ideas.

Helsinki’s Nordic climate is also an advantage when it comes to handling the large amounts of data involved with smart cities. Cooler air reduces the energy needed to chill large data centres and the excess warmth created by such facilities is redirected to residents using heat pumps. One of the largest data-centre operators that publishes its waste heat recovery data says it recovers up to 90% of its waste heat, which is fed into the district heating network — enough to warm 14,000 two-room apartments.

One of Helsinki’s most distinct smart-city features is the way it uses data.

“We have to be responsible when we use AI. We must do it in a transparent way,” says Pasi Rautio, a service manager for the City of Helsinki’s AI and optimization unit.

The city’s AI register, for example, allows residents to keep tabs on AI projects the city’s government is running. It shows what data are being used and why, and offers the opportunity for public feedback.
