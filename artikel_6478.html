The Exiled Thanksgiving Dishes You Forgot Were Once on the Table

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

There was a time when Thanksgiving dinner involved gelatin—and lots of it.

Thanksgiving is a holiday that doesn’t take kindly to change. Suggest replacing the mashed potatoes or leaving the stuffing off the menu, and you’re likely to hear about it until Easter. Yet for all our devotion to tradition, the Thanksgiving table has changed quite a bit over the years. Somewhere along the way, entire dishes disappeared, and most of us barely noticed.

Some were beloved family favorites that gradually fell out of fashion. Others were products of an era when gelatin was considered an essential ingredient, even in dishes that had no business holding that much shape. Looking through old Thanksgiving menus, cookbooks and holiday advertisements reveals a rather wonderful assortment of foods that once had a place alongside the turkey. A few deserve to come back. Others might be better left to history, although I would very much like to meet the people responsible for them.

“Aspic” is a lovely word. It’s French, sophisticated, and just mysterious enough to suggest that whatever arrives at the table will be very impressive. You could put it on a formal dinner menu and nobody would think twice. But what you’re actually discussing is meat Jell-O.

And not some unfortunate kitchen experiment, either. Savory aspics were perfectly respectable additions to holiday meals well into the 1950s, with suspensions of meat, vegetables, seafood and even hard-boiled eggs presented in elaborate molds. The finished creations could be sliced into neat portions, allowing guests to admire every carefully arranged ingredient through a translucent wall of solidified broth.

There’s something almost admirable about the confidence required to make one of these. Imagine spending hours arranging shrimp and vegetables in a decorative mold, then carrying it proudly to the dining room as though everyone had been waiting all year for a slice of cold, wobbly meat.

Not every gelatin creation merits as harsh a judgment as aspic. I have a certain affection for the brightly colored Jell-O salads that used to appear at holiday gatherings. There was something cheerful about a ruby-red cranberry gelatin salad or a fluffy lime concoction studded with pineapple, even if calling either one a “salad” required a fairly generous interpretation of the word.

These dishes were enormously popular in the mid-20th century, when molded gelatin desserts offered home cooks an opportunity to get creative with fruit, nuts, cream cheese, whipped topping and whatever else happened to be in the refrigerator. Some combinations were genuinely delicious. Others involved shredded carrots, celery or mayonnaise, at which point the distinction between salad and practical joke became rather difficult to maintain.

I’d happily make room for a cranberry Jell-O salad at Thanksgiving. The ones made with canned tuna can remain in retirement.

There was a time, particularly in the 1890s, when mincemeat pie was every bit as deserving of a place on the Thanksgiving dessert table as pumpkin or apple pie. Its rich filling of dried fruits, spices and citrus had a long history in English cooking, and early versions also contained actual meat—often beef or mutton—along with suet.

The meat gradually disappeared from many recipes, leaving behind the sweet, deeply spiced filling that became a holiday tradition in its own right. Even so, the name has never done the pie any favors. Tell someone you’re serving mincemeat for dessert, and there’s a reasonable chance they’ll assume you’ve been sampling too many Thanksgiving cocktails.

That’s a shame, because a well-made mincemeat pie has plenty going for it, particularly for anyone who loves the warm flavors of cloves, cinnamon and nutmeg. It may be a harder sell than pumpkin pie, but I wouldn’t object to seeing it make a modest comeback.

Waldorf salad has much more glamorous origins than most other dishes on this list. It was created at New York’s Waldorf Hotel in the 1890s, originally as a simple combination of apples, celery and mayonnaise. Walnuts became a familiar addition later, and by the 1960s, the salad had found its way onto holiday tables across the country.

I can understand why. The crisp apples and celery provide a welcome contrast to the rich, heavy dishes that make up Thanksgiving dinner, while the walnuts add enough substance to make it feel like more than just a bowl of chopped fruit. It’s refreshing, pleasantly crunchy and especially good alongside roasted turkey.

Of all the dishes that have slipped off the Thanksgiving menu, Waldorf salad might be one of the easiest to bring back. It requires very little fuss, and a fresh, crisp side is hardly unwelcome at a table already crowded with casseroles.

At some point in the ’50s, we became convinced that nearly every food could be improved by pressing it into a loaf pan.

The holiday vegetable loaf was one expression of that enthusiasm, turning assorted vegetables into a tidy, sliceable centerpiece with the help of binders like eggs, breadcrumbs or gelatin. Some versions were baked, while others emerged cold from the refrigerator, ready to be unmolded and presented with considerable ceremony.

I understand the practical appeal of preparing a dish ahead of time, particularly for Thanksgiving. What I struggle to understand is the insistence that vegetables should be made to resemble meatloaf. Carrots, peas and green beans are perfectly capable of sharing a serving dish without being pressed into a rectangular block.

Hot Dr Pepper sounds like something you’d find in a cookbook published during a national shortage of good judgment. But it was a genuine promotional idea, introduced by Dr Pepper in the 1960s as a way to enjoy the soft drink in colder weather.

The instructions were simple: Heat Dr Pepper, pour it into a mug and add a thin slice of lemon. The company promoted it as a warming drink for winter gatherings, making it an intriguing candidate for the sort of holiday refreshment that now seems to have vanished almost completely.

I can’t decide whether this is a terrible idea or one that deserves a fair trial. Dr Pepper already has a distinctive blend of sweet and spicy flavors, and warm spiced drinks are hardly unusual during the holidays. The lemon is an interesting touch, too. I wouldn’t necessarily replace the hot apple cider, but curiosity might get the better of me.

There are questionable vintage recipes, and then there are the recipes that suggest the author had finally snapped. Cranberry mayonnaise candles belong firmly in the second category.

The idea was to turn cranberry sauce and mayonnaise into a molded holiday creation resembling a candle, complete with a decorative wick or flame effect depending on the version. It was the sort of elaborate presentation that food companies once promoted with an entirely straight face, apparently confident that a candle-shaped mixture of fruit and mayonnaise was just what the Thanksgiving feast had been missing.

I have several questions, beginning with why the cranberry sauce needed to look like a candle and ending with why mayonnaise was invited to the party. There’s also the matter of placing something resembling a candle in the food and expecting guests to understand they’re supposed to eat it.

If you’ve ever wondered what to do with leftover turkey, the sensible answers include sandwiches, soups, potpies and perhaps casseroles. Vintage recipe developers, however, were not always interested in sensible answers.

Enter frozen jellied turkey vegetable salad, a 1970s creation that manages to combine several questionable ideas into a single dish. Turkey, vegetables and a gelatin-based mixture were assembled into a cold, molded preparation that was perhaps more ambitious than necessary.

It’s difficult to imagine anyone looking at a plate of leftover turkey and deciding that what it really needed was to become a frozen, sliceable salad. Yet recipes like this reflect a period when casserole popsicles made perfect sense.

Creamed pearl onions were a familiar sight on Thanksgiving tables in the 1920s, and I think we may have been too quick to let them go. Tender little onions in a rich, buttery cream sauce are exactly the sort of thing that belongs alongside roast turkey, mashed potatoes and stuffing. They’re comforting, a little old-fashioned, and wonderfully suited to a holiday when nobody is particularly concerned about their butter intake.

They also have the advantage of feeling special without requiring an elaborate presentation. Peeling a mountain of tiny onions is admittedly nobody’s idea of holiday fun, but frozen pearl onions make the whole business much easier. If your family has never had creamed onions at Thanksgiving, this may be a tradition worth borrowing from an earlier generation.
