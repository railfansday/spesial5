Carbon removal could reverse economic damage, but some countries would benefit more than others

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Removing carbon dioxide could help economies recover from warming, but models suggest richer countries would benefit the most.

Removing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere could help reverse some economic damage caused by global warming.

But a new modeling study suggests that richer countries would gain the most, while many places would still face losses left over from warming.

Many climate assessments already count on carbon removal to help meet the Paris Agreement targets. This study asks what a cooler climate might mean for economies around the world.

Wenting Hu of the Institute of Atmospheric Physics, part of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, led the work with Wenguan Wang of Zhejiang University.

Their team combined climate simulations with an AI model of the world economy.

The model estimated that removal would boost output in almost every region, but only Asia and Europe would recover at least as much as they lost during warming.

The researchers stress that their simplified scenarios do not directly predict any economy’s future.

Earlier research has linked economic growth in states and provinces to daily changes in temperature and rainfall, including extreme weather.

Some methods describe those links with straight lines or simple curves, which the authors say may miss much of the complexity.

Studies of carbon removal have also tended to focus on the cost of removing the gas. Less attention has gone to what economies might gain from a cooler climate.

To explore that question, the team built EconClimNet, a deep learning model. This type of AI learns patterns from large amounts of data.

The researchers gathered economic records from 1,554 states, provinces, and similar regions across 77 countries.

They matched those records with 111 local weather measures from 1979 to 2016, including the number of wet days each year.

The model also accounts for national borders. It connects regions within the same country more closely because their economies tend to rise and fall together.

The team trained it on data from 1979 to 2008, then tested it on unfamiliar data from 2014 to 2016.

For total economic output, it scored 0.77 on a scale where one means a perfect match, compared with 0.59 for the next-best AI method.

The researchers then used six climate models from CMIP6, an international project that compares models using shared experiments. Each simulation began with carbon dioxide at its 1850 level.

The gas increased by 1% a year until it reached four times that level, then followed the same path in reverse. Each phase lasted 140 years.

Temperature and rainfall changes peaked about 20 years after carbon dioxide did. Even when the gas returned to its starting level, the planet stayed warmer and wetter, with smaller temperature swings from day to day.

The researchers measured how closely four weather features returned to their earlier state: average temperature, daily temperature swings, the number of wet days, and the heaviest two-day rainfall.

A recovery score of 100% meant a full return.

Across most of the globe, all four scored above 80%. Daily temperature swings recovered best, while average temperature and wet days recovered less fully on nearly every continent.

Northern North America and central Eurasia showed poorer recovery in average temperature and remained warmer. In these simulations, removing carbon dioxide did not return the climate fully to its earlier state.

When the team fed these climates into EconClimNet, global economic output generally fell as carbon dioxide rose.

Output improved in almost every region once removal began, and rainfall measures ranked among the weather factors the model relied on most.

Oceania had the steepest losses during warming, with a median decline in output of about $1.53 million a year. It also had the largest gains during removal, about $1.41 million a year, but those gains did not cover its earlier losses.

Asia gained about $1.23 million a year during removal, more than the $1.13 million a year it lost during warming. Europe roughly broke even, with gains and losses of about $1.22 million a year.

Every other continent recovered less than it had lost. These figures describe trends in the model, rather than forecasts for real economies.

The team divided countries into richer and poorer halves using economic output per person. Both groups lost output as carbon dioxide rose.

Richer countries saw larger losses, with a median decline of about $1.57 million a year, and every sector shrank. In poorer countries, manufacturing grew slightly, but losses in farming and services outweighed that growth.

The authors suggested that manufacturing in poorer countries may cope better with extreme conditions.

When carbon dioxide fell, both groups improved, with richer countries gaining more: about $1.56 million a year at the median.

Services, including retail, banking, and tourism, gained the most during removal. The longest stretch of wet days in a row was the weather measure that mattered most to the model’s results for this sector.

Earlier studies have found that services respond strongly to rainfall. Services also make up more of richer countries’ economies, which the authors say likely helps explain their larger gains.

Farming changed little overall and lost output in richer countries during removal. Those countries cooled more sharply, which the authors suggested could bring more crop damage from frost and cold fronts.

The researchers called for removal plans to support vulnerable nations in the interests of climate justice.

They pointed to climate finance, including carbon markets and loans on easier terms, as ways to help places that would gain less.

The study followed a deliberately simple path: carbon dioxide rose and fell steadily, while everything else affecting climate stayed at 1850 levels.

Removing so much gas so quickly would create major technical and practical challenges with current technology.

The authors cautioned that their conclusions do not “represent a near-term implementation roadmap.”

They did not model any particular removal method, such as planting forests, capturing carbon dioxide directly from the air, or adding alkaline minerals to seawater.

Real projects would also need to account for their costs and energy demands.

EconClimNet assumes that past links between weather and economic output hold for nearly three centuries of simulated time.

The authors acknowledged that some apparent gains may partly reflect quirks of the model.

Its economic records cover only 77 countries, and the team left out regions without data. The model also gives limited insight into why it produces particular answers or what causes the changes it identifies.

The researchers plan to make its reasoning easier to inspect. They say further climate experiments will also help distinguish real economic gains from effects created by the model itself.

The study is published in the journal The Innovation.
