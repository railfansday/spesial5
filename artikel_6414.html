A forgotten 1967 LSD pamphlet warned first-timers what could go wrong, and today’s users mostly agree

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Lisa Bieberman's 1967 LSD pamphlet urged trust, patience and no mirrors. A survey of 496 experienced users backed 18 of her 19 tips.

The 1960s LSD scene is mostly remembered through Timothy Leary, the Harvard psychology lecturer who urged a generation to “turn on, tune in, and drop out.”

One of his former followers spent those same years on a less famous question: what goes wrong on a trip, and how to stop it.

More than half a century later, experienced psychedelic users still agree with nearly all of the advice she gave beginners. Advice that history largely forgot has, by this measure, aged well.

Daniel Kruger, a research associate professor at the University at Buffalo, surveyed today’s users with Paul Gillis-Smith of Harvard Divinity School’s Center for the Study of World Religions.

Co-authors came from the Michigan Psychedelic Center at the University of Michigan and from community groups in Ann Arbor and Southfield.

The team asked people whether they agreed with the advice, not whether following it had kept anyone safe.

Lisa Bieberman studied at Radcliffe College, then the women’s sister college to Harvard. After graduating in 1963 she went to work for a group that Leary and Richard Alpert had founded.

In 1965, she started what she called a Psychedelic Information Center in Cambridge, Massachusetts. She published regular, nationwide newsletters on LSD use.

The problem on the scene, she wrote, was “one of widespread misinformation and not knowing who to believe,” and her publications were part of her effort to counter this.

In 1967 Bieberman published a book titled Session Games People Play: A Manual for the Use of LSD. It cost $1 by mail and covered drug laws, gatherings and practical tips on the use of psychedelics, especially for first-timers.

“This book then is no tourist’s guide through paradise, but a down-to-earth discussion of the sorts of things that can go wrong in an LSD session and how to prevent them,” she wrote. The first thousand copies sold out quickly.

Her “games” were habits to avoid during a trip, like running away from your companions, or deciding you’ve unlocked the deepest truths of the universe and need to tell the world.

The team turned 19 pieces of her advice into survey statements. Ten covered getting ready, and nine covered the session itself. People rated each one on a scale running from strongly disagree to strongly agree.

Recruiting ran online and at Entheofest, a psychedelic advocacy event in Ann Arbor, Michigan, in September and October 2023.

The analysis covered 496 adults who had taken a full dose of a psychedelic, and left out people who had only microdosed.

Nearly all of them had used psilocybin mushrooms in the past, about half had taken LSD, and most had used a psychedelic within the past year. And 58.5 percent held a role that called for psychedelic expertise, such as guide or sitter, therapist, or educator.

On average, participants agreed with 18 of the 19 statements. For getting ready, the strongest support went to “Be with people who all know, like, and trust each other,” and to “Be aware that even with a guide, you have to do most of the work yourself.”

During the session, two statements did best. One was “Recognize that the experience will eventually end.” The other was “Recognize that you may experience anxiety, which does not mean that something is wrong.”

On the first point, Bieberman had been blunter. Asking friends to bring you down, she wrote, “is as practical as asking your fellow passengers on a transatlantic jet to stop the plane and let you off in mid-flight.”

One tip drew neither agreement nor disagreement on average, and had the lowest rating in the survey.

“Shun mirrors,” Bieberman wrote. “On LSD you usually look awful to yourself in the mirror, probably because your pupils are dilated, and you see all your pores. You don’t really look that bad.”

Leary and his co-authors had said the opposite in their own 1964 guide. They recommended that guides have people look in a mirror when they were struggling to return to ordinary reality.

Experiences since then have been mixed. In a 2023 study of Reddit posts about mirror-gazing on psilocybin mushrooms, about one in ten people reported feeling good. About one in twenty reported feeling bad.

Kruger’s team suggested that Bieberman and Leary may have weighed the same trade-off differently, one putting more value on preventing harm and the other on gaining benefits.

Psychedelics are being studied as treatments for severe PTSD and depression, Kruger said. Classic psychedelics are still illegal in the United States, but Oregon and Colorado have set up state-regulated programs for supervised therapeutic use.

Most psychedelic use still happens outside those settings, the authors wrote.

In the 2017 Global Drug Survey, the most common reasons people gave for seeking emergency care after using psychedelics were the “wrong setting” and the “wrong mindset.”

Several of Bieberman’s tips, from picking a familiar room to clearing your mind of negative emotions, dealt with exactly those two things.

Agreement isn’t evidence that advice works. Everyone answered once, about tips they might give a first-timer, and the study didn’t follow anyone who used the advice.

The survey was online and in English, and most people who took it lived in North America. They were recruited through psychedelic networks, so they may not represent users worldwide. However, the authors wrote that they do resemble the readers for whom Bieberman wrote.

Stronger evidence could come from trials that test specific practices, the authors wrote, as long as no participant is denied essential safety information.

“It’s a freight train coming down the tracks,” Kruger said of the psychedelic medicines he expects to reach the market. “So, we better have a train station to safely load passengers into this train or else they’re just going to try to jump on board while this train is moving.”

Bieberman’s pamphlet said little about the days after a trip, beyond keeping the next one free. Future surveys could ask how today’s users see her other writing on that stage, the authors wrote.

The full study was published in the journal Psychedelics.
