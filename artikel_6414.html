New underwater discoveries in Australia show how First Peoples lived before the ocean swallowed their land

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Divers found 21 stone tools on the seabed off Australia, including some made of stone carried 14 miles to a freshwater spring that is now 40 feet under water.

The seabed off a coast looks like the last place an archaeologist would dig. Yet when the last Ice Age ended and the oceans rose, the coast people had lived on for tens of thousands of years went under, stone tools and all.

Off northwestern Australia, divers have now found 21 stone tools on the seafloor. They lie at two spots that were dry land until about 9,000 years ago.

Almost half the tools at one site were made of a rock from an island at the far edge of the archipelago.

That rock outcrops only about 14 miles (23 kilometers) away. Someone carried that stone across a landscape that is now open water. Its destination was a freshwater spring that now lies 40 feet (12 meters) down.

Jonathan Benjamin, a marine archaeologist at Flinders University, led the work, with colleagues at the University of Western Australia, James Cook University and the University of York.

Rangers from the Murujuga Aboriginal Corporation dived alongside them, and two are co-authors on the research paper. The scientists state that they can’t date the tools directly, so 9,000 years is a floor, not an age.

The Ngarda-Ngarli people have cared for Murujuga, also called the Dampier Archipelago, for at least 50,000 years. It was listed as a World Heritage site in 2025. On land it holds more than 4,000 recorded sites, from stone scatters to rock art.

The first underwater finds there came in 2020. The two new sites extend the record offshore and down.

One is a submerged freshwater spring in Flying Foam Passage, about a quarter mile (400 meters) from today’s shoreline. It is a depression 525 feet (160 meters) long, with rock walls up to 8 feet (2.5 meters) high.

The other is a boulder-strewn knoll called the Maitland River Hummock. It is on the bank of a drowned river channel, nearly a mile (1.3 kilometers) from the nearest island.

Both sites were drowned by the same rising sea, roughly a thousand years apart. The knoll would have been a small, rocky island by about 10,000 years ago and was under water soon after.

The spring went under around 9,000 years ago, when rising seas spilled over the sill at the passage’s northern end.

Divers worked the sites on neap tides, when currents in the passages are weakest, for about 40 minutes per dive.

Every rock is coated in marine growth. A diver had to judge shape and angle by eye, lift a candidate stone tool, and send it up.

At the spring, eight dives produced 158 rocks hauled to the surface. After a soak in diluted acid to strip the growth, nine proved to be artifacts: three whole flakes, five broken ones, and a core with at least 12 flake scars.

Most of what a diver lifts turns out to be just a rock.

The knoll was richer. Fourteen dives there produced 12 artifacts, including two cores from which 16 and 19 flakes had been struck.

Murujuga Ranger Malik Churnside, trained as a scientific diver, found one of the knoll’s artifacts himself. Two of the 21 pieces are not pictured in the paper, at the direction of Murujuga Elders.

“The collaboration demonstrates the value of working beside trained Indigenous Rangers who actively contributed to this challenging scientific research,” Benjamin said.

The team read each stone tool’s chemistry with a handheld X-ray fluorescence scanner. Two elements, titanium and zirconium, were plotted against the known rock types of the archipelago. The signal survived thousands of years under the sea.

At the spring, 56 percent of the tools were the local granophyre, a granite-like rock found on both sides of the passage. The other 44 percent were a volcanic sedimentary rock that, today, outcrops only on or near Rosemary Island, about 14 miles (23 kilometers) away.

The knoll told a different story. All 12 of its tools were local rock, probably quarried from the outcrop itself or from an island 2 miles (3.5 kilometers) north.

So the spring looks like a gathering place, where people arrived carrying tools made elsewhere. The knoll looks like a quarry beside a river.

On land, nearby sites that became islands when the sea rose show mostly local stone, which fits a world where the long walks had ended.

The artifacts lie loose on the seafloor, with no buried layers or charcoal to date. Their true age could be anywhere from 9,000 years back to the 50,000-year record of the wider region.

Their edges are slightly rounded by sand, but current meters show the tides move sand, not rocks this size.

The samples are small, 12 tools at the knoll and 17 at the two spring sites. An unmapped outcrop of the island rock could also sit closer on the seabed than the geology on land suggests, though the team considers the known map reliable.

Vincent Adams, director of the Murujuga Aboriginal Corporation, said the finds change the map of what needs protecting.

“What this research shows is that our cultural landscape extends far beyond the shoreline and into Sea Country, where important parts of our story remain preserved beneath the sea,” he said.

Everything found so far lies inside the 100-foot (30-meter) depth contour. The drowned coast runs out to about 425 feet (130 meters), where the oldest material should be.

Reaching it will take bigger boats and faster ways to tell a tool from a rock. The team wrote that both are on the agenda.

The full study was published in the journal Quaternary Science Reviews.
