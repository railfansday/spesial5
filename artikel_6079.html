Dubai's airline return hinges on a regulator's deadline. Riyadh's depends on weeks without strikes, and until then its hotels lose the regional traffic that fills them midweek.

Foreign airlines are trickling back to Dubai. They aren’t going back to Riyadh.

Dubai’s return has a deadline: Jan. 31, when the European Union Aviation Safety Agency’s (EASA) conflict-zone bulletins for the Middle East expire. Riyadh’s doesn’t. It depends on Iran-backed Houthi strikes stopping, and no foreign long-haul airline plans to fly there before January.

The hotel numbers show the gap. Riyadh’s occupancy fell to 34.3% in October, from 69% a year earlier, according to CoStar’s STR. Dubai’s fell to 68.4%, from 84.9%. Dubai is down but Riyadh has been cut in half.

Cancellations and postponements are rising across the Saudi hotels that Perfetti Hospitality advises, according to its CEO Turab Saleem. Hotels that depend on international corporate and group business are under the worst occupancy pressure, he said.

Foreign airlines are returning to Dubai in stages while Riyadh stays largely cut off, and the two recoveries run on different clocks. Dubai's constraint is regulatory: EASA's conflict-zone bulletins for the Middle East expire Jan. 31, and carriers including British Airways, Air France, Lufthansa's Munich service, and Eurowings from Berlin have already resumed, though Dubai International was served by 57 airlines in mid-November against 108 a year earlier. Riyadh's constraint is Houthi strikes that closed King Khalid International in October, when 86% of scheduled flights were canceled, and that have continued since, including hits on the Aramco tower on Nov. 8 and the Public Investment Fund tower on Nov. 19 and 24. No foreign long-haul carrier plans to fly to Riyadh before January, and Delta and Singapore Airlines have pushed launches to March. The hotel data shows the split: Riyadh occupancy fell to 34.3% in October from 69% a year earlier, while Dubai fell to 68.4% from 84.9%, according to CoStar's STR. Linus Bauer of BAA & Partners said airlines can manage elevated risk but struggle with sustained unpredictability, and that four to six weeks of uninterrupted stability could bring some carriers back in phases, with conservative long-haul operators needing two to three months.
