United Airlines Pilot Suspended On TSA Orders Over Support For Terror Group, Report Says

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

The Transportation Security Administration (TSA) has reportedly directed United Airlines to suspend a pilot last week over alleged support for a terrorist organization. According to a report by Jewish News Syndicate (JNS), citing a federal government source, the pilot's crew security credential was also revoked. Neither the pilot's identity nor the terrorist organization they allegedly supported has been disclosed.

The reported intervention follows several controversies surrounding United pilots and comes amid heightened international scrutiny of aviation personnel following an attempted terrorist attack aboard a flydubai flight in September. CNN reports that US security agencies have subsequently issued a joint advisory to the aviation industry warning of renewed calls for attacks against commercial aviation.

The federal intervention reportedly occurred between November 16 and 22, and included the withdrawal of the pilot's Known Crewmember (KCM) credential, which facilitates expedited security screening for eligible airline employees. The existing program is already undergoing significant changes, with the Association of Flight Attendants confirming that its replacement, the Crewmember Access Point (CMAP) program, will be administered directly by the TSA by the end of 2026.

The suspension follows earlier reporting by journalist Mike Wagenheim on November 17, citing two sources who alleged that United was aware of up to six additional crew members who had expressed support for terrorist organizations. According to View From The Wing, the federal source subsequently alleged that resistance from the pilots' union contributed to the TSA's decision to intervene.

JNS approached both United and the TSA for comment on multiple occasions, but was not able to obtain a response from either organization. However, Michael Wilson, spokesperson for United's Master Executive Council of the Air Line Pilots Association (ALPA), explained that the union assesses allegations against its professional ethics code and United's workplace guidelines, stating that:

“[The union] has a robust professional standards committee and legal team that evaluates any accusation or pilot action.”

The latest case follows renewed scrutiny of United Captain Ibrahim Mossallam, a Newark-based pilot qualified to operate the Boeing 757 and 767. United initially suspended Mossallam with pay in November 2023 after he published social media comments praising the October 7 Hamas-led attacks on Israel. According to Airline Ratings, he subsequently returned to flying duties, although Aerotime reported last month that United had confirmed that he had again been removed from service pending a review.

A separate controversy emerged in October when the Jerusalem Post reported that Israeli authorities had barred United pilot Sina Amjadian from operating flights into the country following allegations of support for Hezbollah and Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC). However, United responded that that Amjadian, a Boeing 737 pilot, was not qualified on United's long-haul aircraft, and had been on leave since 2024.

While United has confirmed the employment status of both previously identified pilots, several other allegations remain unverified. The carrier has also explicitly denied reports that Mossallam returned to flying immediately after his latest suspension. Nevertheless, the reported intervention by federal authorities raises important questions about where an airline's responsibility for assessing potential security threats ends, and the government's begins.

It has also emerged that some of the pilot's colleagues gave up first class seats to monitor him on a flight he was operating.

The potential consequences of insider threats were demonstrated on September 30, when a flydubai flight bound for Tel Aviv became the subject of an attempted terrorist attack involving its first officer. The aircraft landed safely in Saudi Arabia, and the UAE's attorney general subsequently characterized the incident as an attempted suicide attack.

In the US, the TSA's authority extends considerably beyond airport security checkpoints. Under Section 46111 of Title 49 of the US Code, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) must take action when notified by the TSA of a qualifying security threat. The TSA can also request that the action take immediate effect and, separately, issue mandatory security directives to airlines.

Although there is no evidence that the United pilots were planning similar attacks, the broader security threat remains real. On November 19, the FBI, Department of Homeland Security and TSA jointly warned US airlines and airports about extremist calls for further attacks following the flydubai incident. While authorities were reportedly unaware of any specific credible plot, the advisory underscores the importance of preventative security measures, rigorous personnel vetting, and federal intervention powers.
