Toxic Lufthansa Plans 1,000 Job Cuts But Cannot Explain Which Jobs Will Go

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Lufthansa plans to eliminate 1,000 full-time positions at its Frankfurt and Munich hubs as management warns that its turnaround is falling short. The financial problems are real, but I am skeptical that another vaguely defined job-cut announcement will help an airline that desperately needs to make peace with its employees in order to ultimately succeed.

According to an internal letter to employees, Lufthansa management plans to cut approximately 1,000 full-time positions across its two main hubs.

The letter, signed by Lufthansa Airlines CEO Jens Ritter and his fellow board members, describes an airline struggling to become competitive. Management reported an adjusted operating loss of €180 million for the first nine months of 2026, an average of €20 million per month. It cited high fuel costs, the Middle East conflict, and nine strike days among the pressures on the business. Management also acknowledged shortcomings in costs, efficiency, and productivity.

(That last point is particularly important)

The pilot union Vereinigung Cockpit responded sharply, accusing management of using an ominous but imprecise announcement to pressure employees as labor negotiations remain unresolved. The union wants answers about which jobs are affected and whether these are additional reductions. It also argues that management must take responsibility for its own operational and strategic decisions.

Ground-staff union Verdi has raised similar concerns. Proposed savings include consolidating technical and administrative work currently performed separately within the organization. There may be a sensible case for that, but telling employees that 1,000 positions will disappear without explaining where or how is an excellent way to spread anxiety far beyond the people ultimately affected…

I keep thinking about my Lufthansa A340-600 flight from Frankfurt to Washington earlier this year.

It was a superb flight, despite a business class seat that was well behind the competition. The improved food and bedding helped, but one of the main reasons I enjoyed it so much was the crew.

The flight attendants were friendly and engaging: the flight attendant working my side was so pleasant. I also sensed pride in delivering the improved onboard service, even though the new service flow created more work.

Those employees gave me a reason to look forward to flying Lufthansa again.

That anecdote certainly does not establish how many employees Lufthansa needs or whether its current labor agreements are sustainable (and this announcement does not suggest any crew positions are being cut). But my experience illustrates something management should remember as it tries to rebuild the business: good employees can make passengers want to come back, even when the seat itself is nothing special. And “happy employees make happy customers” include ground staff, lounge staff, and customer service folks too.

Lufthansa has published its latest proposal to the pilot union, offering the prospect of up to 1,000 cockpit hires by 2033 and up to 150 additional captain promotions annually. In exchange, it seeks changes to compensation and productivity. The proposal also includes one-time payments for 2027 and 2028, with salary increases beginning in 2029.

Those prospective hires are a separate issue from the proposed hub job cuts. The different figures concern different roles and timeframes.

Management should not have to accept every union demand and employees also have an interest in a financially viable airline: reaching an agreement will require concessions. But Lufthansa must find a way to make peace with its workforce. The latest announcement has already inflamed matters, and I struggle to see how leaving so much uncertainty helps either side reach an agreement.

If Lufthansa can eliminate unnecessary duplication while protecting the operation and treating affected employees fairly, it should be clear about it.  I want Lufthansa to succeed. My A340 flight reminded me how good it can be. I would hate to see the enthusiasm I encountered onboard become another casualty of the never-ending conflict between management and labor.

Lufthansa plans to cut 1,000 full-time positions in Frankfurt and Munich, but has yet to publicly explain several essential details, including whether those reductions are part of an existing program.

Cost control is necessary, especially as market uncertainty and high oil prices continue. But so is a workforce willing to help deliver the airline that Lufthansa wants passengers to pay a premium to fly. My recent experience demonstrated how much its employees contribute, and I hope management can find a path to profitability that gives those employees a reason to remain invested in its success. Lufthansa is in desperate need of strong leadership…I’ll just leave it at that.

Lufthansa always has been a mediocre European airline except in F where they were well above the mark. In exchange for being mediocre their fares from outside Germany to third countries were always something of a bargain and provided you didn’t mind a last generation product then they were OK.

Those bargain fares have largely gone and now are equal to many way better alternatives especially Air France who are now Europe’s number one in all regards.

Lufthansa is hurting but it’s not going to cut it’s way back to profitability they need more to invest. The one thing that they have always stood out for is their exceptional crews, it’s so rare you don’t get one and losing any of them will just sink LH further – they should take a lesson from BA on that who now offer gutter class service in all cabins having ousted the long standing crew to save money.

Moving corporate HQ from Köln and integrating it with the existing operations base at FRA is a must, as it’s clear that the executive is on a completely different planet to the operations staff. If the board really wants to create meaningful change with long-term benefits, start at the top rather than from the bottom.

If “management warns that its turnaround is falling short” then it sure would appear that senior management should be the first ones fired. That’s their primary job function, no?

Start with the C Suites, fire those at the top first.
