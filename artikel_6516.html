The Vera Rubin Has Found Two More of the Milky Way's Dim Satellites

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Massive galaxies like the Milky Way have a retinue of smaller, dim satellites on their outskirts. They're hard to detect, and while astronomers have found about 65 of the Milky Way's dwarf satellite galaxies, and about 40 globular clusters in its halo, they suspect there are many dwarf satellites and globular clusters yet to be found. Now the Vera Rubin Observatory has found two more.

The Milky Way's satellites are either small dim galaxies or globular clusters, and both are part of the assembly history of the Milky Way through mergers. These two detections by the Rubin, found in its Early Data Preview 2 (EDP2) released in July 2026, are almost certainly the beginning of many more discoveries of Milky Way satellites by the powerful observatory. One is a most likely a dim dwarf galaxy named Aquarius IV, and one is a globular cluster named Rubin-GC1.

The discoveries are presented in two new papers.

One is titled "Discovery of the Distant, Ultra-faint Milky Way Satellite Aquarius IV with the Vera C. Rubin Observatory Early Data Preview 2." It's published in Research Notes of the American Astronomical Society, and the lead author is William Cerny, an astronomy student in Yaleâs Graduate School of Arts and Sciences.

It was confirmed in images from the Dark Energy Camera. The authors say it's a low-luminosity, compact stellar system in the outer Galactic halo.

It's stellar population is ancient and low-metallicity. "These properties closely resemble those of the smallest and faintest confirmed ultra-faint dwarf galaxies (UFDs), though a globular cluster classification is not ruled out," the researchers write.

Ultra-faint dwarf galaxies can have as little as a few hundred stars, which makes them smaller than some globular clusters (GCs). But there's one critical difference between UFDs and GCs: UFDs have dark matter while GCs don't (except for a few possible outliers).

Only a small number of the stars in Aquarius IV have been detected so far, so whether or not it is a UFD for sure will depend on deeper imaging and spectroscopy.

The second paper is "Rubin Observatory Reveals a Dust-Shrouded Halo Globular Cluster in Ophiuchus," and it will be published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters. It's currently available at arxiv.org and the lead author is Aashay Pai. Pai is a graduate student in the Department of Physics at the University of Chicago.

"We present the discovery of the Milky Way satellite Rubin-GC1 (Rubin J1628â1735) using data from the NSF-DOE Vera C. Rubin Observatoryâs Early Data Preview 2," the authors write. The stars in Rubin-GC1 have an intermediate age of about 9.2 billion years, and are moderately rich in metals, meaning it's most likely a very faint GC. "Together, these properties suggest Rubin-GC1 is most likely a star cluster in the faint tail of the luminosity function of globular clusters surrounding the Milky Way," the authors write.

Based on comparisons to the Sagittarius dwarf spheroidal galaxy, Rubin-GC1 is most likely a cluster accreted to the Milky Way, associated with the Sagittarius leading arm. The Sagittarius leading arm is a massive tidal stream of stars from the Sagittarius Dwarf Spheroidal Galaxy, torn away as that galaxy merges with the Milky Way.

The Vera Rubin Observatory is mostly known as a transient-detecting observatory, and as the maker of a ten-year long movie of the sky in its Legacy Survey of Space and Time. But it's more than that. As it images the sky repeatedly, dim objects will become clearer. "The sensitivity of Rubin LSST will steadily increase, and systems similar to Aquarius IV are ultimately expected to be detectable with >85% efficiency using the same search algorithm applied here," the authors of the first paper write.

Not only that, but its observations will work with observations from other observatories, like the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, to detect even more objects. "The identification of Rubin-GC1 heralds an exciting era of discovery as Rubin, Euclid, and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope pierce the shroud of Galactic dust to reveal faint satellites that were hitherto hidden by our Galaxy," the authors of the second paper write.

Ultra-faint dwarf galaxies and globular clusters are puzzle pieces in the history of the Milky Way's growth through mergers. The mergers are ongoing, as interactions between the Magellanic Clouds and the Milky Way show.

By finding more of the Milky Way satellites, a picture of all of these mergers, and the role dark matter plays, will become clearer. By mapping out these objects and the streams they're sometimes a part of, astronomers can detect where dark matter also concentrates in clumps. These dark matter sub-halos will show how our galaxy grew larger by consuming smaller galaxies with their own dark matter halos.
