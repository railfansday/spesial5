Scientists Found the Secret to Real-Life Invisible Tech—and It’s Amazingly Simple

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Northwestern engineers used AI to sift 20,000 designs for a drone built around a flaw in human sight.

Invisibility is the sci-fi dream. Whether a Klingon Bird-of-Prey or James Bond’s Aston Martin, it’s undeniably cool for an object to blink out of existence—or at least, as it appears to our naked eye. However, while impressive stealth technologies can render aircraft invisible to electromagnetic “eyesight” such as radar (and some have even tried to channel Q’s engineering genius), tricking the human eye has proved to be much more difficult.

That’s why engineers at Northwestern University tried a different approach when attempting to design an “invisible” drone. Instead of using a complicated system of cameras, camouflage, or other light-bending materials, they went with another, more simple tactic: Just spin the thing really fast. The result was the Phantom Twist, a palm-sized drone capable of spinning 25 times per second. At that speed, the human eye can't keep up, and the drone shows up as little more than a faint blur. Details of this new device, which weighs little more than an ounce, were presented at the Robotics: Science and Systems 2026 conference in Sydney, Australia earlier this summer.

“Most efforts to hide drones focus on making them look like their surroundings,” Northwestern’s Michael Rubenstein, who led the work, said in a press statement. “Instead, we asked whether we could design the drone itself around the way humans perceive motion. This idea of low visibility through persistent motion is something few people have explored.”

One of those “few” was Michael Dammar. Back in 2006, the inventor was working for Minneapolis-based VeraTech, which patented a y-shaped, boomerang-like drone that could spin fast enough to become invisible to a casual observer. At the time, the company saw possibilities for the drone in the realm of military surveillance, and a few years later, engineers at MIT developed a different kind of monocopter, though their version was very much visible.

To build the Phantom Twist, the team used AI-driven modeling to generate up to 20,000 drone designs that could fly. Optimization algorithms then rearranged the drone’s main parts (a motor, propeller, circuit board, counterweight, and batteries) to find the best layout for stable flight and, yes, invisibility. The result is an extremely small machine that looks just like a small blur against the background as it rapidly speeds around its center of gravity. But the drone isn’t actually invisible. Its disappearing act comes from a limitation in how our eyes process motion, not from anything that hides the drone itself.

“The human eye takes time to accumulate signals, roughly analogous to the exposure time of a camera,” Northwestern’s Emma Alexander, who was also involved in the project, said in a press statement. “When an object spins quickly, we perceive it as blurring out and losing distinct features. Because this new drone is almost entirely transparent, its few opaque components are visually averaged with the background for an overall appearance of a slight haze.”

The researchers hope the tiny drone will be a boon for inspecting infrastructure without visual distraction of passing drivers, for example, and also a less-invasive method for tracking wildlife. Although for the drone to truly help biologists keep tabs on elusive species, engineers will need to perfect one of the drone’s core weaknesses—despite its small size, the drone is mighty noisy.

Rubenstein hopes that future iterations of the drone will feature more transparent support structures and quieter propulsion so that the Phantom Twist truly lives up to its ghostly name.
