Most estate planning advice assumes you will work until 60-65, accumulate wealth gradually, and have employer-provided benefits until Medicare kicks in. The FIRE path breaks every one of those assumptions. You may stop working at 35, 40, or 50 — meaning your estate plan must cover a potentially 40 to 50-year retirement, not the 20 to 25-year retirement conventional planning addresses.

The SECURE Act fundamentally changed how inherited retirement accounts work, and this change matters enormously for FIRE households that have built large tax-advantaged portfolios.

Before the SECURE Act, non-spouse beneficiaries could "stretch" inherited IRA distributions over their own lifetime, allowing decades of tax-deferred growth. Now, most non-spouse beneficiaries must withdraw the entire inherited IRA balance within 10 years of the original owner's death. For a beneficiary in their peak earning years, this compressed timeline can push them into the 32% or 37% tax bracket on every distribution.

The FIRE advantage: Early retirees have years — sometimes decades — of lower taxable income between leaving work and taking Social Security or required minimum distributions. This creates a golden window for Roth conversions that no conventional retiree has.

A married couple who retires at 40 with $1.5 million in a traditional 401(k) and a $500,000 Roth IRA could systematically convert $80,000 to $100,000 per year from the traditional to the Roth, staying within the 22% bracket. Over 15 years, they convert $1.2 million to $1.5 million at a known, relatively low tax rate. Their heirs then inherit a Roth IRA that is completely tax-free — even under the SECURE Act's 10-year distribution rule.

Without conversions, those heirs would owe ordinary income tax on every dollar withdrawn from the inherited traditional IRA, potentially at 32% or higher if they are working professionals during their peak earning years. The tax savings for heirs can easily exceed $200,000 to $400,000 on a $1.5 million inherited traditional IRA.

Key Roth inheritance rule: Inherited Roth IRAs are still subject to the 10-year distribution rule, but the distributions are tax-free. The 10-year clock starts the year after the original owner's death.

For FIRE households pursuing charitable giving, a donor-advised fund (DAF) allows you to front-load charitable deductions in high-income years (or Roth conversion years), reduce your taxable estate, and distribute grants to charities over time. Do not wait until traditional retirement age to complete your estate plan. The moment you leave employer benefits, you need a fully executed will or trust, healthcare directives, powers of attorney, updated beneficiary designations, and umbrella insurance. Many FIRE retirees leave their jobs with $1 million or more in assets and no estate plan in place. Every day without these documents is a day your family is exposed to probate, court-appointed guardianship, and preventable tax consequences.

For most early retirees, transferring your primary residence to a revocable living trust makes sense. It avoids probate on the home, enables seamless management if you become incapacitated, and does not affect your mortgage, property tax, or homestead exemption in most states. Consult your estate attorney about your state's specific rules.

Umbrella insurance provides liability coverage above the limits of your auto and homeowners policies. A $1 million umbrella policy typically costs $200 to $300 per year and protects against lawsuits, car accident judgments, and liability claims that exceed your underlying policy limits. For FIRE households with significant assets, it is one of the most cost-effective forms of protection available.

Review your estate plan every three years at minimum and immediately after any major life event: marriage, divorce, birth of a child, death of a beneficiary, significant change in net worth, or a move to a new state. Early retirees have a longer planning horizon, making regular updates even more important than for conventional retirees.

Estate planning for early retirees is not a modified version of conventional retirement planning — it is a fundamentally different discipline. You face a longer incapacity window, a bigger healthcare gap, more exposure to tax law changes, and decades of asset protection needs. The FIRE community excels at building wealth through savings rate optimization and investment discipline. Apply that same rigor to your estate plan: execute foundational documents before leaving your job, optimize beneficiary designations for the SECURE Act, pursue Roth conversions during low-income years, and protect your assets with umbrella insurance and trust structures. The wealth you built deserves a plan that survives as long as your retirement.
