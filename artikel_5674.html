Opinion: Medical school should start after students take the Step 1 exam

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Test your knowledge with our new weekday mini crossword

Fabi and Caruso Brown are bioethicists at SUNY Upstate Medical University.

This article is the first in a new First Opinion series on redesigning medical school.

There are more than 150 medical schools across the United States, and although each has a unique approach to medical education, all must grapple with certain challenges. Attendance at in-person lectures and learning sessions hovers around 50% across the country. Students increasingly rely on expensive commercial resources to prepare for their board exams, much to the dismay of their faculty. Meanwhile, students are taking on increasing amounts of debt to finance their four years of medical school.

It’s time for a radical reimagining of medical education. We propose separating medical education into two parts.

Part one would encompass the foundational, preclinical sciences that are tested on the U.S. Medical Licensing Exam Step 1, which students typically take during or just after after the second year of medical school. Part two would include everything else that is critical to becoming a medical doctor: clinical skills and clinical reasoning, professional identity formation, deeper dives into ethics and population health (beyond the bare basics tested on Step 1), and learning in clinical spaces.

Instead of learning the crucial content of part one in medical school, students could take individual pathways to master the content. Rather than requiring students to enroll in an M.D. program to learn the foundational sciences, part one could take many different forms to suit a variety of learners and institutions. This could include a DIY approach, in which a student pieces together available prep materials (Anki, UWorld, etc.) on their own timeline and takes Step 1 when they are ready, at an estimated cost of less than $2,000.

Alternatively, students could take packaged Step 1 courses, online and/or asynchronously, which would perhaps be equivalent to a certificate course, for an estimated $5,000-$10,000.

A third option could be to enroll in a one-year online master’s degree in biomedical sciences, for an estimated $10,000-$25,000.

Yet another option might be for undergraduate schools to offer official “pre-medical” bachelor’s programs that prepare students for Step 1.

Finally, schools could offer a one-year in-person master’s degree, ideally with guaranteed acceptance to the institution’s M.D. degree program following passage of Step 1. At more than $30,000, this would likely be the most expensive option.

At the end of whichever part one preparation pathway a student chooses, they would sit for the Step 1 exam and apply for medical school after passing. It is worth noting that the USMLE itself responds to the question “How do I prepare for the test? Is there a review book for USMLE?” without mentioning medical education, instead emphasizing review of the content online, practice examinations, and self-assessments.

Part two of medical education, which would begin after passage of Step 1, could also take different forms. This training could include two years of classroom instruction on how to be a doctor, potentially tied to pre-acceptance into certain residency slots in order to encourage students to enter specific high-needs specialties. Alternatively, part two could take three years and include time for research or a capstone project alongside education in doctoring.

The arguments for reimagining medical education to move foundational sciences from the M.D. curriculum to a pre-admission period are many. Perhaps most importantly, the cost of medical education has increased over 80% since 2001, adjusting for inflation. At the same time, the federal government is now capping federal direct unsubsidized loans at $50,000 annually and $200,000 total, putting four years of medical school out of reach for many students. For medical students who struggle and have to repeat a year in the preclinical curriculum, this financial burden is even more significant. Adding in the cost of external Step 1 study resources, which students often pay for out of pocket on top of their tuition, makes medical education in our current model even more prohibitively expensive. Moving part one of medical education out of the traditional medical school setting could save these students from untenable amounts of debt.

The debt concern also interacts with the not insignificant number of students who do not currently pass Step 1 on their first attempt (9% of students in 2024 and 7% of students in 2025 — around 2,000 students annually). Failing Step 1 even once reduces a student’s chance of matching, and may mean they cannot go into the specialty that was their major motivator for entering medical school in the first place.

The students may also incur more debt if they have to take a leave to study and retake the exam, and given that pass rates drop with each retake, some of these students may never pass, leaving them with very few career paths open to help recoup the debt they’ve incurred. Even “compassionate off-ramp” programs — still a rarity — often leave students with significant educational debt and without a clear pathway to a high-income career. By making Step 1 passage a prerequisite for entering medical school, institutions can circumvent this possibility.

Having a variety of part one pathways could additionally diversify the applicant pool. A flexible timeframe for studying for and passing Step 1 might allow people to work and/or support and raise a family before entering the more rigidly structured part of medical school.

Similarly, a lower financial bar and degree of investment in preclinical education might make it more accessible.

Of course, this would require equitable access to part one preparatory materials, curricula, and biomedical sciences programs. That could happen through a free clearinghouse of publicly accessible materials and scholarships to access privately available ones. If these necessary components of part one preparation are financially out of reach for some prospective medical students, the plan might actually reduce the diversity of admitted medical students.

Our proposed restructuring of medical education might also help to decrease the stigma in residency applications associated with multiple attempts at Step 1 — but again, if stigma is not reduced by this approach, then this might further advantage good test-takers, who often come from affluent backgrounds, over other students.

Another argument in favor of this new model of medical education is the positive effect it could have on the quality of education, by enabling schools to allocate teaching resources more effectively. We strongly believe that medical school should be about learning how to be a doctor, not how to pass a test, but currently many students prefer packaged commercial test prep materials to the courses their schools are offering. This is a misuse of resources that reflects a misalignment of values, particularly when many students report that they do not value in-person class time and watch lectures on 2x speed.

M.D. programs have made efforts to address this disconnect by incorporating early clinical exposure and education in areas not emphasized on Step 1, such as ethics and structural competency. But these efforts also miss the mark when students remain preoccupied with passing the exam, rather than absorbing the lessons about how to be a compassionate, well-rounded physician.

We experience these challenges firsthand in the ethics and health systems sciences courses we teach, as medical students in our classrooms visibly choose not to engage with our course materials, opting instead to review flashcards from external Step 1 study sites. Restructuring medical education to begin after Step 1 might allow schools the flexibility to reallocate resources required to implement time-variable, competency-based clinical training, wherein students advance through training based on the acquisition of core skills, rather than completion of a certain number of weeks on a given rotation.

Such approaches are effective but resource-intensive, requiring a shift in resources from preclinical to clinical training. For example, foundational sciences faculty might be re-deployed as coaches to help support students who are struggling to apply foundational knowledge to clinical reasoning.

And indeed, the type of competency-based overhaul of medical education would inevitably require time, resources, and very careful planning to assure that it doesn’t create other undesirable eventualities. Medical schools might reasonably be concerned that this move could negatively affect students and faculty if implemented without proper consideration of the risks.

It’s certainly possible that our proposal might lead the content of Step 1 to shift into undergraduate college courses, driving students to focus narrowly on a “pre-med” major that becomes increasingly intensive and competitive, an outcome we consider undesirable. However, this could be mitigated by additional restrictions on the timing of Step 1: Say, for instance, a requirement that students enroll in a clinical undergraduate medical education program within one year of passing. State medical licensure requirements (i.e., that all Step components be passed within a seven-year old period) might also help to avoid this outcome.

We haven’t worked all the kinks out of this. This proposal would create other logistical puzzles in need of solutions: do osteopathic medical schools follow suit? How would this affect the timing of application and admissions cycles? What would be the role of the MCAT going forward? What would happen if more students pass Step 1 than can be admitted into medical schools? How would M.D./Ph.D. and other Medical Scientist Training Programs (MSTPs) be reorganized?

We acknowledge the scale and breadth of these potential complications, and we invite our colleagues in medical schools across the country to join us to imagine creative ways to improve and renew our approach to educating our nation’s future physicians.

Rachel Fabi, Ph.D., and Amy Caruso Brown, M.D., M.Sc., are bioethicists at SUNY Upstate Medical University. Fabi also writes the daily STAT mini crossword. Views presented here are their own and do not represent the views of their institution.
