New laser mapping tool helps scientists track baby trees and monitor forest regrowth on a massive scale

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Culling deer let 2.65 million young trees spread across the Cairngorms, and a laser scan from a plane mapped Scotland's forest recovery.

The Cairngorms, in Scotland’s Highlands, are famous for bare, heather-covered hills. Those hills used to be forest. Woodland once covered half to four-fifths of the country, but only about 4 percent of the native forest is left.

Across one stretch of the Cairngorms, though, the forests are coming back on their own, and a plane carrying a laser scanner has now counted them.

The scan found 2.65 million young trees under 16 feet (5 meters) tall across 220 square miles (569 square kilometers).

About 13,000 acres (5,325 hectares) of open ground now carries enough saplings to count as young woodland. Very little of it was planted. The land managers shot deer instead.

Researchers at the University of Cambridge led the mapping, with partners at RSPB Scotland and Cairngorms Connect, the partnership that manages the land. Sudina Thapa and Aland Chan were the first authors.

The scan is a single snapshot of trees, commissioned in July 2023, and the smallest seedlings, the ones still hidden in the heather, are not in the count.

In an interview with Earth.com, Chan said the scale was what surprised him. “You notice regenerating trees on the ground, but that feels very different from seeing millions of trees plotted on the map,” he said.

Four land managers formed Cairngorms Connect a decade ago, with a 200-year plan to let native forest climb back up to its natural tree line inside the Cairngorms National Park.

Their main tool is culling deer, which eat seedlings before the seedlings can get established. Ecologists have estimated that deer numbers have to fall to roughly 3 to 5 per square kilometer, about 8 to 13 per square mile, before young trees can regenerate.

Across the partnership, densities now average below 4 per square kilometer. That is the threshold the whole plan rests on, and the hills have crossed it.

Young woodland here means more than 100 trees per hectare, about 40 per acre. Of the 6.17 million trees the scan picked out, 43 percent were under 16 feet tall.

The authors wrote that this colonization was largely natural, with small areas of planting where no seed trees were nearby.

Mapping trees from the air with laser scanning is not new. The LiDAR scanner fires pulses at the ground, times the return of each one, and builds a three-dimensional picture of everything below.

This flight, about 2,000 feet (600 meters) up, recorded 22 pulses for every square meter of ground.

The hard part was the small trees. Heather in these hills grows to about 3.6 feet (1.1 meters), and bracken grows taller, so a young pine can be shorter than the shrubs around it.

“We’re not the first study to use LiDAR to map trees, but it’s the first time we’ve used it to map these small, regenerating trees at this scale,” Chan told Earth.com.

The team’s method found trees once they outgrew the heather. Checked against Cairngorms saplings measured on the ground, it caught 83 percent of tree clusters between 4.6 and 5.2 feet (1.4 to 1.6 meters) tall. Above 6 feet (1.8 meters) it caught more than 95 percent.

Trees still under about 3.3 feet (1 meter) were almost all missed.

With every young tree on a map, the team could ask where the forest is spreading and where it isn’t. Distance from existing forest mattered most. The farther a patch of ground was from a seed source, the fewer saplings it held.

Bogs consistently held fewer young trees than dry heathland. Elevation made little difference low down but was more important above about 1,300 feet (400 meters), where the ground approaches the natural tree line.

Where deer had hit hardest in the past, fewer saplings stood now. Parcels rated low or medium for browsing damage in surveys run between 2006 and 2013 carried more young trees than parcels rated high.

One oddity stood out. The parcels rated very high carried more saplings than those rated high. The authors suggested heavy browsing may have opened up the vegetation and exposed bare ground for seeds.

Chan said ecologists have long known that trees regenerate less readily far from seed sources or on boggy ground. Putting a number on how much less, he said, is what makes it possible to model what happens next.

Asked by Earth.com what walkers in the Cairngorms should notice, Chan said Scots pine tends to come back first, with birch and rowan following. A self-seeded forest grows differently from a plantation.

“Instead of dense rows of trees of roughly the same size shading out the entire understory, visitors will see trees of different shapes and sizes scattered across the landscape,” he said.

That mix of shapes and sizes creates more kinds of small habitat for animals, he added.

Capercaillie, a large grouse, and red squirrels are among the species now confined to the remaining fragments of old pine forest. More woodland is more room for them.

Pip Gullett of Cairngorms Connect, a co-author, said the method is also good at tracking the effects of fires, which are growing more severe with climate change.

Field crews from the partnership and RSPB Scotland had already surveyed parts of the landscape on foot. The laser count of land with more than 100 trees per hectare came out in the same range as those ground estimates once a correction was applied.

Field crews who measured saplings a year later found them slightly taller than the laser had. A batch of 136 Scots pines averaging 7 feet (2.1 meters) tall had grown about 7 inches (17.6 centimeters) in that year. Seedlings still below the heather line remain uncounted.

Rocks and clumps of bracken can be mistaken for trees, and the authors filtered them out using color images taken on the same flight. The method has only been tested at this one site, with this one scanner and flight height.

The Scottish Government is now commissioning a laser scan of the whole country. The authors wrote that their approach could be scaled up to build a national record of woodland expansion.

Chan said the next thing he wants to map is the shrub layer itself. Dozens of species depend on the heather and blaeberry, he said, and that layer sets much of the fire risk.

Until that map exists, the count stops at the trees tall enough to clear the heather.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Applied Ecology.
