Decades of clues helped scientists discover a massive freshwater lake miles beneath the ice in Antarctica

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Lake Vostok, a vast freshwater lake hidden beneath 2.5 miles of Antarctic ice, was mapped using radar, seismic data and satellite measurements.

In June 1996, a paper in Nature described a large, deep freshwater lake beneath the ice of central East Antarctica. The lake, now called Lake Vostok, lies under about 2.5 miles (4 kilometers) of ice.

The authors found that its area was about 50 percent larger than earlier estimates, with dimensions comparable to those of Lake Ontario.

The paper drew on clues that had been building for decades. They ranged from seismic soundings in the 1960s to airborne radar flights and, in 1993, satellite measurements.

The lake takes its name from Russia’s Vostok Station, which sits above its southern tip, according to Michael Studinger, then at Columbia University’s Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory.

The former Soviet Union set up the station in 1957. It lies about 800 miles (1,300 kilometers) from the geographic South Pole, on roughly 2.3 miles (3,700 meters) of ice, according to NASA’s Earth Observatory.

About 30 people work at the station during the summer research season.

In 1983, the station’s air temperature fell to -129°F (-89°C).

Far below, the lake water sits under more than 360 times the atmospheric pressure at sea level.

Where the ice sheet crosses the lake, it goes afloat, Studinger writes. In some areas, lake water freezes onto the base of the ice.

Russian scientists suggested from seismic soundings in the 1960s that water might lie beneath the ice. Later, a joint U.S., British and Danish airborne radar project found flat reflections that pointed to water.

A 1974 to 1975 airborne radio-echo survey outlined a lake of about 3,900 square miles (10,000 square kilometers), the 1996 paper reports.

Robin Bell, a geophysicist at the Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory, described those flights in a NASA Earthdata feature.

“Scientists flew around Antarctica in C-130 airplanes that sent electromagnetic waves through the ice sheet,” Bell said.

The flat layers in the radar records were the first signs of subglacial lakes, or water trapped beneath the ice. At the time, researchers took them for small, isolated ponds.

“Quite frankly they didn’t seem to be important,” Bell recalled.

In the 1990s, the European Remote Sensing satellite, ERS-1, produced the first comprehensive map of Antarctica’s ice surface using radar altimetry, the same feature says.

Scientists studying that map noticed an unusually flat area near the center of the ice sheet, about the size of Lake Ontario.

“The ice surface will look rugged. But if water is underneath, the ice surface will look very flat,” Bell explained.

In 1993, satellite altimetry independently confirmed the extent of the lake first outlined by radar, the 1996 authors wrote.

The paper’s five authors came from Russian and British institutions. They included Andrey Kapitsa of Moscow State University and Martin Siegert, then at the University of Wales.

Another author was glaciologist Gordon de Quetteville Robin of the University of Cambridge’s Scott Polar Research Institute.

The team combined new altimetry and radio-echo data with existing seismic data. They found that the lake was deep, with an average depth of 410 feet (125 meters) or more, and that its water was fresh.

The team also estimated that water stays in the lake for tens of thousands of years. They put the mean age of the water at about one million years.

That age counts from when the water was deposited as surface ice on the ancient Antarctic ice sheet.

The regional flow of the ice could be explained as steady flow along and over the lake.

Robin had suggested in the 1950s that geothermal heat from the bedrock could warm the base of the ice sheet. He could not prove it until ice-penetrating radar arrived in the 1970s.

The base of the ice stays warm because of geothermal heating from Earth’s interior, Siegert wrote in a 2005 Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences paper. About 50 milliwatts of heat per square meter is enough to melt the base of an ice sheet 2.5 miles (4 kilometers) thick.

The same review describes the lake as over 155 miles (250 kilometers) long, with depths reaching about 0.6 miles (1 kilometer) in places. It is one of over 100 subglacial lakes identified beneath Antarctica.

In 2012, a Russian team drilled all the way down to the lake to look for microbes, NASA’s Earth Observatory reported. Scientists estimate the lake has been sealed under ice for up to 25 million years.

Studinger also calls Lake Vostok the closest terrestrial analog to Europa, the ice-covered moon of Jupiter.

The study was published in the journal Nature.
