The Best Adventure Gifts for Masochists Who Love Type 2 Fun

While the Type 2 athlete on your shopping list might enjoy simple pleasures, adventurers like them are hard to keep up with and even harder to shop for—after all, their gear closet is already overflowing. So we looked for gifts that either solve a real problem or create an excuse for a new adventure.

From an $8 sit pad to an inflatable 13-foot sea kayak, these are presents for people who are happiest when they’re uncomfortable.

I’ve used enough backpacking towels that feel like oversized eyeglass cloths to appreciate one that’s an actual towel. Nomadix’s 54-by-30-inch Ultralight weighs about 8 ounces and packs down to roughly 5 by 2 inches, but its recycled-polyester MicroSuede fabric offers a genuinely pleasant hand against bare skin and absorbs enough water to get you truly dry after a dunk.

This is both the least expensive gift in this guide and potentially the one that the hardo athlete on your list will use the most. Gossamer Gear’s Little Sit Pad weighs just 1.7 ounces and folds down tiny, but it opens into a roughly 13-by-10-inch patch of foam between the user’s butt and a wet log, snowy rock, dirty parking lot, or whatever else they want to sit or kneel on. That packability means even a gram-counting athlete is likely to bring it with them for a little respite.

Klättermusen’s Algir is a minimalist crossbody bag for people who want organization without a lot of baggage. Its 52-gram (1.8-ounce) weight makes it an easy choice when trying to pack light, and the exterior mesh pocket helps keep need-to-reach gear visible and accessible. When not in use, toggles on the side make it easy to clip onto any bag with gear loops.

By now, I’ve probably used this bag 200 times because of its robust yet simple features. The Gateway uses TPU-coated 600-denier nylon, a roll-top closure, and an IP66-rated main compartment to keep wet things contained and dry things protected. External attachment points make it easy to lash onto a paddleboard or boat, and the removable shoulder strap comes in handy off the water. Mine has hauled lunches, wet swim gear, first-aid items, kids’ stuff, and all manner of random gear on rivers and at wet beach hangs.

Platypus’ Big Zip EVO Reservoir is the ideal gift for a picky athlete who doesn’t have time to fuss with hydration. On the trail, its thick 5/16-inch drink tube and efficient self-sealing bite valve make it easy to drink on the move. Back at home, and perhaps just as importantly, the Big Zip EVO is easy to reach in and scrub between adventures thanks to its cavernous top opening and little bit of structure from its semi-rigid center panel.

The Rift sits perfectly in a sweet spot between “technical fleece” and “sweatshirt I accidentally wore hiking.” This half-zip uses lightweight 150-gsm recycled-polyester fleece with a stretch-ripstop chest pocket and weighs just 249 grams (8.8 ounces). Its zipper lets you vent quickly when hammering uphill, while the simple cut doesn’t scream “ATHLETE” when you wear it to the bar after a ski day.

Patagonia’s original R1 fleece hoody became a classic because it was warm enough to wear for cold-weather workouts and breathable enough to absolutely hammer without holding moisture. The R1 Ultralight pushes harder in the latter direction. The 7.76-ounce hoody uses lofty, breathable fleece beneath a smoother, abrasion-resistant face and gets a deep zipper for dumping heat fast on climbs. It was built not just to breathe but also to move with freeing raglan sleeves and underarm gussets.

In an age of overbuilt helmets, Sweet Protection’s Primer keeps things beautifully simple while still delivering solid protection. MIPS Evolve is designed to help manage rotational forces in certain impacts, and the 360-degree Occigrip system lets you dial fit around your entire head. Good ventilation helps on sustained climbs, and a breakaway visor is meant to release during a crash. At roughly 389 grams (13.7 ounces), it feels protective without weighing down your noggin.

Kid meltdowns are entertaining to watch as a bystander at the ski resort but unbearable as a parent. Nothing derails a family ski day quicker than an uncomfortable six-year-old in the parking lot. Fischer designed the One around small, developing feet rather than simply shrinking an adult shell, with kid-specific proportions, soft flex, and an easy-entry design that makes popping in cold little feet considerably less dramatic. These aren’t just a gift for the kiddo using them—comfortable feet mean the entire family has a better chance of taking turns past lunch.

Rumpl’s Gametime Puffy is two gifts in one: a cushy four-inch stadium cushion that unfolds into a cozy 50-by-70-inch insulated blanket. The 30-denier ripstop exterior is tough enough to drape over rocky ground at camp, and a burly Hypalon handle and waterproof zipper make it easy to throw in a truck or camping kit without babying it.

Poles are incredibly useful on giant climbs and equally annoying when you no longer want to use them. Black Diamond’s Distance Carbon Z FKT solves the second problem with ultralight carbon shafts and folding Z-Pole construction that collapses down to about 35 centimeters (14 inches) at the shortest size. A pair starts around 294 grams (10.4 ounces), which is light enough that carrying them across flats doesn’t feel like punishment for needing them uphill.

While it’s a physically huge gift, Old Town’s Discovery 158 is the sort of present that will change how an entire family spends summers. The 15-foot-8-inch hull is made from tough three-layer polyethylene and has more than 1,000 pounds of usable carrying capacity, enough for two paddlers, camping gear, a dog, and at least three wildly heavy cast-iron pots and pans. At 87 pounds, it’s decidedly not ultralight, but it’s stable, durable, and built for decades of lake time.

Cooking well while camping is one of life’s great pleasures, but that joy is significantly diminished if your kitchen is a hectic jumble of plastic bins spread across a picnic table. Cinch’s Kitchen Cruiser corrals the mess into one 43-pound aluminum box that unfolds into roughly 50 inches of cooking workspace with dual burners, counters, storage, and an integrated rechargeable water pump. It’s the ideal gift for the Type A camper with the entire operation folding back into a tidy, rectangular case that slips in the back of a truck when mealtime is over. “Yes, it’s heavy, and the legs are a pain to deploy. But I’ve decided this thing could weigh 100 pounds and I’d still find a way to cart it to every campsite,” says Outside associate gear director Jenny Wiegand after taking it camping this summer.

An 88-millimeter waist gives the Arcade enough platform for soft and chopped-up snow, while tip-and-tail rocker and a relatively tight turn radius keep it lively on groomers. A poplar core backed by a full Titanal layer adds stability when speed picks up. In other words, this ski will be fun in a few inches of fresh snow but will still hold an edge at speed on sheet ice. Heavily rockered fat skis deliver a religious experience in pow, but they’re miserable in the conditions most people ski most of the time. Trust us, get the skier on your list this crowd-pleasing all-mountain ski instead.

The NeoLoft is a comfort level-up for backpackers willing to carry a little extra weight for a solid night of sleep. It’s on the luxury side of the backpacking weight spectrum at 850 grams (30 ounces), but those extra grams pay soft-touch dividends with a toasty 4.7 R-value and a stretch knit exterior that feels like a supple shirt on bare skin.

For the right paddler, the Aleutian opens destinations that normally require renting a boat or driving thousands of miles with a kayak strapped to a roof rack. This inflatable touring kayak packs down small enough to check on a plane, then provides the length, shape, and rigidity needed for lakes, protected coastlines, and multi-day flatwater trips once inflated. A 13-foot sea kayak that you can check on a plane sounds like one of those outdoor-industry claims that require an asterisk. Alpacka nailed the construction and usability here, and it needs no asterisk.
