My Favorite Spot in Denali National Park Is a Literal Pile of Trash

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Old cans abound at one historic dump in Denali National Park. (Photo: Ryleigh Norgrove)

One of my favorite spots in Denali National Park’s vast wilderness isn’t some grand vista or rocky overlook. It’s much trashier than that.

Wander enough of the park’s frontcountry woods and you’ll come across all sorts of things: pioneer graves, little slate caves, mossy goshawk perches, a fantastic blueberry patch. Keep walking, and you might find a drainage as wide as a semi, full of garbage.

There are beer cans and glass bottles, coffee cans and broken gravy boats. It looks like a house disgorged its contents on a hillside and the tundra is slowly gobbling them up

The first time I encountered it, I wondered why no good Samaritan had come along with a bag or several by now, to haul this away to some other, less federally protected pit. We’re supposed to pack it out, after all.

After asking around at the local dive, I learned something else: This trash was considered historic. It felt like an oxymoron to me: How could a bunch of nasty empty beer cans tell us anything about the past other than a bunch of old-timers here liked to get drunk together, or that they were serving alcohol during prohibition?

One answer is that people don’t tend to write down what they’ve had for lunch. Or what kind of socks they wore through, or what they dropped and shattered. Discarded objects don’t mean much once they’ve hit the bin. And that’s exactly why impromptu landfills like this are one of the most significant sources of data archaeologists have.

“A lot of the stories of these individuals in the past never got written down,” says Dr. Justin Cramb, an assistant professor of archaeology at the University of Alaska Fairbanks, told me. “So the material traces are what we have.”

Archaeologists actually have a word for this historic trash: middens. They’re nasty and they’re everywhere. Depending on their age, they could contain shells, animal bones, or the last of someone’s canned dinner. They’re treasure troves of information, teaching us how people lived. Enough of them, alongside the other things people discarded, might tell you something about making a home in a place like Denali where getting groceries was considerably more complicated than driving to the store.

It’s something Cramb knows well. Throughout his work, he looks for details of daily lives: what resources people had, how they built their camps, and where they might have gathered building materials. Garbage is a part of that evidence.

“At the time, the world was kind of considered to be too vast to worry about those kind of things,” Cramb said. “And often in cases, somebody’s living at a place like Denali, they’re not going to haul their trash out, right? There was no mechanism for that to happen.”

Still, surely not every empty beer can deserves to spend eternity in a national park. But what’s the birthday on which it graduates from trash to treasured artifact?

The answer I got from the National Park Service on this was less tidy than I expected: “Age alone does not determine whether an object warrants preservation.”

It comes down to preserving cultural resources as much as natural ones. There are a few laws involved: Under the National Historic Preservation Act, historic areas generally need to be at least 50 years old and meet certain criteria for historical significance and integrity. The Archaeological Resources Protection Act also covers archaeological resources more than 100 years old that have cultural significance. Neither amounts to a blanket instruction to save every old can, but they do offer about 50 years of leeway on either end to determine if something is worth saving.

Cramb put it this way: “The house I grew up in is over 50 years old, right? But it’s not a historic property.”

Apparently, getting old and falling apart isn’t enough. The issue lies in what an object can teach us. Was it associated with a significant person or event? Could it fill in a piece of history we don’t know much about? And is enough of that evidence still there to make sense of it?

Around nearby Riley Creek, there’s quite a bit of history to make sense of. Before this was a place to order a latte or catch a bus, it was a place people lived. On Thanksgiving Day, 1921, Maurice Morino opened his Mount McKinley Park Hotel to feed and entertain miners, trappers and travelers. Just down the road, there were cabins, a trading post, and a silver fox ranch. Down below the old railroad bridge, you could visit “the Hole” for gambling, bootlegging, and other activities you won’t find on the ranger-program schedule.

Later on, in 1932, the park boundary absorbed that community. Today, cabin remains, debris, and trash dumps are among the traces of McKinley Station. Morino’s grave is still here, too. I haven’t been able to trace who exactly left behind my little trash pile, but it’s tucked into a landscape with considerably more human history than the trees let on.

Of course, I’d left an imprint getting there, too. I’d followed one of those little paths through the woods and added my footsteps to the record. Even my wandering had inherited a shape.

There’s something difficult in that. I want to know these woods well enough to return to the blueberries when they’re ripe, to recognize the perch before I look up for the bird. That kind of knowing takes effort. But each one presses a little more of a path into the ground.

“I always say that the goal is really to leave as little trace as possible,” says Erin Collier, Director of Education for Leave No Trace. “That doesn’t sound as good, you know, on the T-shirt and on the logo, but that’s really what the goal is.”

“If we were truly leaving no trace,” she says, “we wouldn’t be out there.”

We leave evidence of ourselves just by visiting. Roads, campgrounds, the little paths we wear into the woods. The trick is in paying attention to what we’re adding, and what we might accidentally take away.

Sometimes that means resisting the urge to clean up. The Park Service advises leaving potentially historic objects exactly where they are and asking staff if you’re unsure. Where something lies, among the other things people left behind, is part of its story.

But that doesn’t give the rest of us permission to start contributing.

“Today we have the infrastructure to deal with things like taking our trash out of the park,” Cramb told me. “We can do better. So we should.”

I know these woods, and knowing them means accepting that other people have known them, too. Their lives are still here, in cabin foundations and coffee cans, slowly disappearing into the hillside. My own footprints will leave something behind. I’d like to be thoughtful about what that is.
