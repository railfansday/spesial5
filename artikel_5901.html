New aviation funding request hits Senate turbulence

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Senate Republicans appeared skeptical Monday about the need for as much as $50 billion to upgrade the nation’s aging air traffic control system, after a nationwide ground stop last Friday prompted the massive, increased asking price from top administration officials.

Speaking with reporters, Senate Majority Leader John Thune, R-S.D., said he believed the department still had about $8 billion outstanding from an air traffic control modernization package included in last year’s reconciliation law.

“I got to obviously see the explanation behind it, or sort of laying out the numbers why it's necessary,” Thune said of the $50 billion, which jumped from $30 billion a day earlier.

Sen. Thom Tillis, R-N.C., told reporters he was unimpressed by Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy’s request and that he wanted to see a breakdown of how the department would use the funds.

“They got to give us an update,” Tillis said. “I mean, the $50 billion request — I thought that was $30 billion. So people have got to explain to me, really, even the basis for the $30 billion, let alone the additional $20 billion that Duffy mentioned the other day.”

Sen. John Hoeven, R-N.D., similarly said legislators would need “more detail” on the request but said “obviously there needs to be more” funding for the modernization efforts.

Hoeven serves on the Senate Transportation-HUD Appropriations Subcommittee, which rolled out a fiscal 2027 spending bill Monday that would increase the FAA’s annual allocation to facilities and equipment by $500 million, a nearly 13 percent boost.

Duffy is slotted to attend the Senate GOP conference lunch on Tuesday, where he is likely to be peppered with such requests for specifics. Meanwhile, FAA staff are expected to brief House Transportation and Infrastructure Committee aides on Tuesday, after that meeting got pushed on Monday.

The Senate’s go-slow approach is in contrast to Speaker Mike Johnson, R-La., who said over the weekend that he would add the $50 billion request to a year-end legislative package.

The White House’s call for additional funds ballooned last week in the aftermath of the nationwide ground stop that created confusion and concern at airports around the country. The Federal Aviation Administration said Sunday that the unusual move was made “out of an abundance of caution” after legacy equipment became flooded with data during routine upgrades.

The FAA’s initial investigation tied the data flooding to work being done at a radar site in Texas and a radio communications site in Arkansas by contractor L3Harris Technologies.

Speaking on CNBC’s Squawk Box, Duffy said the server thought to have caused the flooding was taken to the L3Harris lab in Florida where FAA personnel are working with the FBI to analyze the router that likely caused the system instability.

Still, as administration officials attempt to understand the system errors that led to the ground stop, Duffy has used the moment as justification to lobby for additional funds.

“In this old telecom network, I do have money to switch over from the old copper wires to fiber and we’re 70 percent completed with that mission, but we need a brand new architecture for our telecom and aviation,” Duffy said on CNBC Monday. “This can happen quickly to build a new state of the art telecom network, it’s just that it has to be funded.”

A day before the incident, Duffy and Office of Management and Budget Director Russ Vought wrote to congressional appropriators, formally asking for $30.1 billion in added funds for the modernization effort, in line with a bill introduced by Sen. Eric Schmitt, R-Mo., in late September. That ask inexplicably jumped up to $50 billion by Friday night in the aftermath of the ground stop.

On Monday, Thune noted the prior request for approximately $30 billion, which he said included “some other stuff” as well.

Schmitt’s bill and the White House request would provide $10.1 billion for air traffic control upgrades, including $7.2 billion for a “common automation platform and a common controller workstation,” and $2 billion for cloud computing and telecommunications infrastructure.

Another $10 billion would go toward airport improvement projects, including $2 billion to “construct, replace, and improve terminal air traffic control facilities, including activities using digital tower technologies.”

The request includes $10 billion for a terminal modernization or reconstruction pilot program for certain midsized airports.

Thune said Monday that he tallied about $12 billion in the new request specifically for air traffic control related issues, likely a reference to the $10.1 billion plus the $2 billion for terminal facilities.

“I think there's more we need to figure out, and obviously I think we all agree that we need to be modernizing and updating, particularly in an area like that where safety is the number one priority, but also we want to do that in a smart way and make sure that we're using taxpayer dollars wisely and well,” Thune said. “We’ll hear him out.”

Meanwhile, Democrats kept up the drumbeat of criticism, both for the airspace management that led to the ground stop and the rollout of the new funding request.

“So typical of this incompetent administration — they have money, they don’t use it and then they come back and say ‘give us more money,'” Senate Minority Leader Charles E. Schumer, D-N.Y., said on the floor.

Savannah Behrmann and Jacob Fulton contributed to this report.
