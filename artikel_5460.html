Proposed DHS rule would end deference to prior H-1B approvals. Here are 4 effects

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A proposed DHS rule would end USCIS deference to past approvals. Four effects workers and employers should plan for on H-1B renewals.

For a foreign professional on an H-1B visa, a renewal has usually meant building on a decision the government already made. The immigration officer who reviews an extension has started from the worker's last approval and leaned toward it, rather than judging the job and the employer from a blank page. The Department of Homeland Security has proposed taking that starting point away. Nothing has changed yet for anyone filing today, and the existing deference rules govern every petition until a final rule is issued. The public can comment on the proposal until Nov. 13, according to IANS, and the proposal is not yet law. Workers and their employers can use the time before a final rule to prepare for renewals that would have to stand on their own record.

Most of the change would land on workers from one country. DHS estimates the rule would reach about 208,000 petitions each year, and in fiscal 2024 Indian-born professionals received about 79% of the approvals for workers continuing in H-1B jobs, according to IANS. Indian workers and the companies that employ them would therefore face most of any added scrutiny of extensions. For those workers, a renewal that once rested on the last approval would become a new test of whether the job and the employer still qualify.

Mantra Law Office, an immigration law firm, laid out what could happen to H-1B renewals once U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services no longer gives weight to its own past decisions, from more paperwork for companies to less certainty for workers. These effects form a sequence. Each one follows from the one before it, beginning with an officer who is free to judge a renewal from scratch. The cost of that sequence won't be spread evenly. Many renewals may go through much as they do now, while the cases an officer singles out for a closer look can stall. The gap between the typical case and the unlucky one matters for each of the effects that follow.

The proposal changes where an officer starts a renewal, not what an H-1B worker has to prove. Today an officer reviewing an extension begins by leaning toward the earlier approval. Under the proposed rule, officers would lose the duty to start from that approval and could reassess a worker's eligibility independently, according to Mantra Law Office. The worker's history would still count. "This proposal would not prohibit USCIS officers from considering a prior approval where relevant," DHS said, as reported by IANS. An earlier approval would become one piece of evidence that an officer can weigh or set aside. A clean record would help a worker, but it would no longer guarantee the result.

Today an officer needs a reason before taking a second look at a case that was already approved. Under the existing standard, an officer can depart from the earlier decision only on finding that it rested on a material error, that the facts have materially changed, or that new information has come to light. If none of those is present, officers treat the questions the earlier approval answered as settled. The proposal would remove the need for any such finding. An officer could reopen questions that a previous officer already answered, even when nothing about the worker or the job has changed.

Without deference as the starting point, the outcome of a renewal would depend more on the officer who reviews it. Two officers looking at the same file would have more room to reach different decisions. How closely they examine a case would depend more on who opens it. A worker should treat the next renewal as a case to be made again rather than as a formality, even with the same employer and in the same job.

Every question an officer reopens is one the worker's file would have to answer again.

Officers who review an H-1B renewal without leaning on the last approval are likely to ask workers and employers for more evidence. An officer who starts fresh has to settle questions that the earlier approval once answered, such as whether the job still qualifies for the H-1B category and whether the employer still meets the requirements. The Department of Homeland Security anticipates that dropping the deference requirement could lead to more requests for evidence, according to Mantra Law Office. Such a rise would follow from the fresh reviews, because an officer who must judge every element again may ask for whatever the earlier file once settled. For a worker, a request for evidence means the petition isn't decided on what was filed, and the case stays open until the officer receives more.

Nobody knows how large any increase might be, because the DHS expectation has no number attached and no result has been measured yet. Officers could send requests in a handful of renewals or in a large share of them. That unknown is the real burden for workers, who can't tell in advance whether their renewal will be approved as filed or held for questions. The practical response is to stop treating the last approval as proof and to file as though a request is coming, with the case for eligibility already documented.

Each request has to be answered with documents, and those answers add up to a heavier filing burden.

Under the proposal, an employer filing an H-1B extension would have to make the case for the job again rather than rely on the fact that an officer approved it before. Employers may need fuller proof that the job and the employment relationship still fit the visa category, according to Mantra Law Office. An extension would then turn on what the company can show about the job today, not on a decision made years earlier. Closer reviews and more evidence requests leave employers with one sensible response, which is to answer an officer's likely questions in the original filing instead of waiting to be asked.

Most of the added proof concerns how the employer has set up the job, not the worker's degrees or experience. The company may have to show what the role is, what the worker does in it and how the company stands as the worker's employer, and the company keeps the records on each of those points. A short job description that won approval the first time may need to be rewritten in fuller detail so that it can justify a renewal without help from the earlier decision. For the worker, the outcome of a renewal therefore rests heavily on how carefully the employer describes and documents the role.

The earlier approval still belongs in the file as supporting evidence. Officers would not be barred from considering prior approvals when they are relevant, so employers have good reason to include them. An old approval can back up a renewal, but it cannot replace current proof that the job still qualifies.

The safest renewal is one an employer prepares to pass even if the officer has never seen the first approval.

With closer reviews, more requests for evidence and heavier paperwork, a worker filing to renew an H-1B can no longer count on how long the case will take or how it will end. Mantra Law Office writes that added scrutiny and possible requests for evidence raise burdens and make plans for continued employment less predictable. The uncertainty concerns the timeline and the outcome, not the worker's right to keep working once status ends. NIH says in guidance from 2021 that an officer does not have to approve an on-time extension before the worker can stay on the job. A wait with no decision date is harder to plan around than a known deadline, because the worker and the employer cannot settle their plans until an answer arrives.

That protection has a firm limit. In the same 2021 guidance, NIH says a worker may keep working up to 240 days past the end of status. When an officer slows a decision with added scrutiny and requests for evidence, each extra week of review uses part of that period. A fixed limit and a review of unknown length fit poorly together, because the slower the decision, the less of that cushion the worker has left.

Filing early is the one part of the process a worker controls. The 240-day protection applies only if USCIS receives the petition, with fees, before status expires, according to NIH guidance from 2021. With closer scrutiny likely, a worker who files late would give up the one protection that makes a long review bearable. A worker who files well ahead lets more of the review happen before status runs out, which leaves more room before day 240 if an officer pulls the case aside for questions.

If the proposal becomes final, a renewal will no longer rest on the last approval, and filing early will become the worker's main safeguard.
