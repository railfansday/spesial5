It's a Bot World. We're Just Living in It

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Cybersecurity In-Depth: Feature articles on security strategy, latest trends, and people to know.

Bot detection has become a losing game. Security teams need to limit privileged access for humans and their machine counterparts equally.

The Internet is flooded with bots. People use them to organize their emails, shop, chat, and handle some of their busywork.

At the other end of the spectrum sit attackers who want to increase their speed just as much as, if not more than, legitimate users. The faster they can conduct distributed denial-of-service attacks or brute-force password attacks, the quicker they can breach an organization and the more victims they can claim.

The line between legitimate and illegitimate activity grows finer by the minute, as bots have become pros at mirroring human behavior — even its flaws. For example, during the infamous incident involving OpenAI and Hugging Face, bots reportedly found ways to communicate with one another by accessing the outside Internet — something they weren't programmed to do.

Behavioral baselines that security teams once relied on are becoming moot, since bots can impersonate humans. As the volume of bots accelerates, the real question isn't human versus bot but what that activity is telling teams and how to police it.

Security tools will miss something, so teams should choose systems that allow them to quickly edit, adjust, and continually adapt to a changing environment, explains Jeremy London, director of engineering, AI, and threat analytics at Keeper Security.

Artificial intelligence (AI) agents, which can perform automated actions similar to bots without human oversight, are only adding to environmental complexities. While some advancements can be exciting, they also introduce more gaps and zero-day vulnerabilities to the attack surface that "we just didn't understand," London says.

"I think the continual effort, as a security organization, is to show up with the right tooling, understand what you're trying to solve for, and then be able to react quickly because your control plane probably missed something — that's a guarantee," London tells Dark Reading.

Organizations underwent significant transformations over the past decade, propelled by AI. Adoption moved at a scale that "no one ever predicted," London says. That has forced teams to cut corners on important safeguards.

Code reviews and security practices need a serious makeover, especially regarding boundaries and compliance. Bots and agents have new levels of autonomy and access, leading to more unpredictability and changing what security risks even look like.

However, such a makeover has proved to be a difficult undertaking because AI doesn't come with a security playbook, London explains. A decade ago, bots were rigid and scripts performed a single action. Now "teams vibe code stuff and throw things at the wall and see what works, and the scale that [bots] can operate at is totally different," he says.

While organizations struggle, attackers are having a field day with these advancements. Given all of the recent disclosures and reports, it's no surprise that attacks are rising. What is surprising is how quickly threat actors can adapt and respond to countermeasures, says Jérôme Segura, VP of threat research at DataDome, a bot management and agent trust platform.

"Anytime we put challenges in front of them, we see it slows them down and costs them more because to run the same operation, they have to use more resources, which becomes expensive," he explains.

The reason the attackers aren't giving up despite the obstacles is that their attacks are working, Segura tells Dark Reading.

"Because the payout is so great, they're doubling, tripling their investments," he says.

The persistence is challenging and frustrating for defenders. But it's just the stark reality that threat actors are highly motivated, and "we're battling against that," Segura adds.

When threat actors use bots against online retailers, they replicate the exact flow of a human but without all the telltale signs, such as hesitating over a purchasing decision, fumbling to enter card or shipping details, or pausing to solve a CAPTCHA challenge. They don't need to click through every page like a human would or wait for the site to load all the images either. As a result, bots complete transactions in a split second, Segura says. The speed can create security risks and unfair competition for legitimate users.

More concerningly, bots complete these tasks in ways that appear legitimate because they're aware that companies are trying to block this malicious activity, he says. And the volume is only increasing. Segura conducted research analyzing traffic hitting retailers' product pages and found that 91% were actually bad bots.

Taking legal action is difficult when it's nearly impossible to differentiate legitimate and illegitimate activity, which puts even more pressure on businesses. Scraping product pages or ticket scalping remains "kind of a gray area in terms of the legal aspect," Segura explains, adding that the law isn't quite clear yet on what is permitted.

In some cases, the culprits are organized groups just looking to make money and oftentimes use loopholes to circumvent the law. For example, they end up using existing infrastructure to collect scarce products and resell them, Segura says.

Threat actors organize so that they have specialties, just as defenders do, he has observed. Some specialize in reverse engineering specific parts of a website's code to understand how it works. Others focus on providing the necessary infrastructure, including all the IP addresses, to carry out those kinds of attacks, he says.

"You can source IPs from real users that are extremely convincing because they're also shared by humans," Segura explains. "So all these specializations within these groups, working well together, and making sure everybody's getting paid, is really interesting."

Behavioral analytics are struggling right now, London says, and is a "pain point" rippling across the industry.

Some bots can move the mouse pointer and send keystrokes to mimic typing, so verification techniques like measuring mouse movements or typing speeds need to evolve. Models can be a bit slow, just as humans can be, he notes.

"Although they are robotic in nature, some of those behavioral or human factors that teams have been using for indication points are really close," London says.

Since it's so difficult to determine whether activity is from a human or a bot, organizations should manage them both the same way when it comes to privileged identity: the less access, the better.

Given all of the risks and potential blast radii, "security teams, not developers, should probably decide whether AI is allowed in an organization," London recommends.

"From a security perspective, it makes it somewhat simple because AI agents can do bad things [and] humans can do bad things," he says. "So if we just look at it through that perspective, I don't care if it's a human or an agent."
