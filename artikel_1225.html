A death sentence for ‘sloppy’ cancers

Lorenzo Valcanover is in the Division of Oncogenomics, Oncode Institute, the Netherlands Cancer Institute, Amsterdam 1066 CX, the Netherlands.

Reuven Agami is in the Division of Oncogenomics, Oncode Institute, the Netherlands Cancer Institute, Amsterdam 1066 CX, the Netherlands, and the Department of Genetics, Erasmus University Rotterdam, the Netherlands.

Identifying cellular properties that distinguish a cancer from healthy tissue is a crucial step for developing efficient treatments. One such characteristic is the ‘sloppy’ way in which some cancer cells implement translation — the process by which proteins are made from messenger RNA by protein complexes called ribosomes. This process can go awry. Writing in Nature, Wang et al.1 reveal a cancer-specific mechanism by which translation loses its normal ‘track’ of how it reads an mRNA sequence to encode the expected amino acids, resulting in a phenomenon called frameshifting. This generates proteins that have a distinct sequence of amino acids that are not usually made, and the authors show that this abnormality might be exploited for therapeutic purposes.
