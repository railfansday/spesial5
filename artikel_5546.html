The 8 habits the American Heart Association says to protect your heart

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The American Heart Association's Life's Essential 8 covers diet, activity, tobacco, sleep, weight, cholesterol, blood sugar, and blood pressure.

A typical day holds many small choices about what to eat, how far to walk, and when to turn off the lights. Few of those choices register as medical decisions, yet together they shape the condition of the heart and blood vessels over years. Stronger cardiovascular health cuts the odds of heart disease and stroke, along with other serious illnesses. The payoff extends beyond one organ. Everyday routines amount to a long-running health plan, whether or not anyone writes it down. A clear checklist turns that plan into something a person can follow, revisit, and adjust.

Cardiovascular needs shift across a lifetime, and women meet several turning points along the way. Pregnancy, motherhood, and menopause can each raise a woman's chances of heart disease and stroke. A woman's heart health can also reach the next generation. A mother's cardiovascular well-being shapes her baby's for years after birth. Good heart health begins before a child arrives. The checklist reflects those life stages with separate versions for women, for pregnancy, and for kids. People who want a sense of where they stand can turn to My Life Check, a self-assessment tool that produces a heart health score. The tool offers a baseline, and the checklist supplies the habits to improve on.

American Heart Association guidance presents Life's Essential 8 as the organization's core measures for building and sustaining cardiovascular health. The eight measures fall into two broad areas, Health Behaviors and Health Factors, though the group does not say which item belongs where. The association's web page reports no study, survey, sample, or rating formula, and it does not explain how My Life Check calculates its results. The page works as a consumer guide, and the association says it reviewed and approved all health information on its site, drawing on scientific research and its own guidelines. Every measure carries a short description, and each links to a downloadable fact sheet offered in other languages as well. The numbering below follows the association's list order and implies no hierarchy of importance. This article covers all eight measures in that sequence.

Eat Better opens the American Heart Association's list of eight measures with a call for an overall healthy eating pattern built on whole foods, plenty of fruits and vegetables, lean protein, nuts, and seeds. The guidance also steers cooking toward non-tropical oils, naming olive and canola as examples. A downloadable fact sheet and a "Learn how to" link round out the entry, which sets no numeric target.

The entry reads more like a shopping list than a formula. It names categories of food and leaves portions, calories, and serving counts unstated. Whole foods lead the description, and fruits and vegetables draw the only hint of quantity, with the page asking for lots of them. Protein comes with one qualifier, lean, which narrows the choice without ruling out any source. Nuts and seeds fill out the foods. The oil guidance sorts by type, setting two named options against a tropical category the page never spells out.

Framing the measure as a pattern points it at diet across many meals. One salad or one heavy dinner counts for little against what a person eats week after week, and the sustained habit is what the checklist asks for.

Healthy Eating heads the site's list of related resource topics, ahead of Fitness, Quit Nicotine, Sleep, Losing Weight, Cholesterol, Diabetes, and Blood Pressure. Those topics track the numbered measures one for one. A reader who starts with food can follow the same order through the rest of the association's material.

Four other entries supply a benchmark a reader can test against, covering weekly activity minutes, nightly sleep hours, body mass index, and a blood pressure reading. Eat Better offers no comparable yardstick on the checklist. Progress on diet comes down to how closely meals match the foods the entry names, with the fact sheet and the Healthy Eating resources available for specifics.

To be more active, the American Heart Association asks adults to log 2½ hours of moderate physical activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity each week. Both routes satisfy the same requirement, and the gap between them reveals how the guidance weighs effort. Two and a half hours equals 150 minutes, so the vigorous option demands half the time on the clock. In effect, the AHA counts one minute of hard exertion as worth two at a moderate pace.

A weekly target leaves adults room to arrange their own schedules. Spread across seven days, the moderate goal works out to about 21 minutes a day, and the vigorous goal comes to about 11. A busy Tuesday does not sink the plan. The AHA tallies the total across the full week, so someone with a crowded workweek can shift more of the minutes to days with open time.

Children answer to a stricter standard, with the AHA calling for 60 minutes of activity every day. Across a week, those daily sessions add up to 420 minutes, 2.8 times the adult moderate benchmark. The adult moderate goal, averaged out, fills about a third of a child's daily hour. A daily mandate removes the calendar slack adults enjoy. A child cannot bank extra time on Saturday to cover a quiet Monday. Play earns credit toward the hour alongside structured activities, which broadens the menu and puts a child's free time to work for the heart.

Partial progress toward these numbers has value of its own. Any amount of movement outperforms none, and adults coming off an inactive stretch should build up their activity in stages. A beginner can start with a few moderate minutes and add more each week. Every increment counts toward the weekly total, so the climb itself delivers part of the payoff.

Traditional cigarette smoking drives most preventable deaths, the checklist warns. Newer nicotine products have joined the field. E-cigarettes and vaping arrived later, and researchers have yet to finish measuring what long-term use does to the body. The open question around vaping leaves the cigarette as the product with the clearest and most lethal record.

About 40% of U.S. children ages 3 to 11 breathe secondhand smoke, which spreads the danger to people who never lit anything. The figure stretches from preschoolers to kids finishing elementary school. None of them chose to inhale those fumes. Exposure on that scale makes tobacco a household issue as much as a personal one. A parent or caregiver who quits removes one source of that exposure from a child's daily surroundings.

Quitting rewards the heart on a short timeline. The steepest drop in coronary heart disease risk comes in the first one to two years after a person stops, with a gentler slide in later years, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Giving up cigarettes reduces markers of inflammation and of the blood's tendency to clot, and it lifts HDL cholesterol in quick order. Stroke odds shrink in parallel, approaching the level seen in people who never smoked. Former smokers face a smaller chance of an abdominal aortic aneurysm, and the gap widens the longer a person stays smoke-free. Researchers can chart how cigarette damage recedes across years without tobacco. E-cigarettes have not yet built up a comparable body of evidence.

Age and smoking history leave the benefit intact. Long-term and heavy smokers gain from stopping, and the payoff holds at any stage of life. People living with coronary heart disease can lower their chances of another heart attack and of dying from heart disease once they quit. Quitting doubles as protection for hearts that coronary disease has damaged.

Most adults need seven to nine hours of sleep every night, a window the American Heart Association writes into its eight-part checklist for heart health. Checking the target takes no lab work. A bedtime and a wake-up time supply the whole calculation.

Children require more rest, and the amount shrinks as they grow. Kids age 5 and younger need 10 to 16 hours, and the guidance folds naps into the total for this group alone. Children ages 6 to 12 should get nine to 12 hours, and teens ages 13 to 18 need eight to 10 hours. At its upper end, the youngest group's range fills two-thirds of a 24-hour day. The oldest group's floor sits an hour above the adult minimum. A 16-year-old who sleeps seven hours, an amount that satisfies an adult, falls below the teen range.

The spread between minimum and maximum also tightens with age. The span for the youngest children covers six hours, the span for school-age kids covers three, and teens and adults each get a two-hour band. Ten hours marks both the floor for the youngest group and the ceiling for teens. School-age children can need as many as three hours more than the adult maximum.

Adequate sleep helps the body heal, sharpens brain function, and lowers the risk of chronic disease, according to the association. Research ties insufficient sleep to higher odds of obesity, diabetes, and high blood pressure. Each of those conditions raises the likelihood of heart disease. Short nights, repeated over time, can feed the problem the checklist exists to prevent.

Obesity, diabetes, and high blood pressure correspond to three other entries on the same list, which gives weight, blood sugar, and blood pressure their own lines. Researchers said scientists have yet to pin down how poor sleep leads to heart disease. They added that better sleep could benefit the heart. A full night of rest may serve several measures on the checklist at once.

A body mass index under 25 marks the optimal weight on the checklist, turning a fuzzy goal into a number anyone can follow. BMI expresses a person's weight relative to height as a single figure, and the American Heart Association treats it as a helpful yardstick. Height enters the calculation, so two people who weigh the same can land on opposite sides of 25. The index has a floor. A reading below 18.5 counts as underweight. The two thresholds frame an optimal band 6.5 points wide, opening at 18.5 and closing short of 25.

Trouble can come from either direction. The underweight line keeps the goal from turning into a race toward the smallest possible number. Reaching a healthy weight and holding it there brings many benefits, according to the association. A single good reading falls short of the measure, which asks people to stay inside those limits over time.

Excess body fat travels with an unhealthy lipid profile. Obesity, the term for that excess, tracks with higher levels of "bad" cholesterol and triglycerides and lower levels of "good" cholesterol. The same condition can help bring on high blood pressure, diabetes, and heart disease. Cholesterol and blood pressure each hold a separate slot among the eight, which places weight beside two other items that obesity touches. A person whose weight climbs may see the effects surface on more than one line of the checklist.

An online calculator or a health care professional can supply a personal BMI, the association notes. Either route rests on two inputs, height and weight, which makes the result easy to recheck as habits change. Repeated checks can show whether a person is drifting toward the 25 line before crossing it. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends that people with obesity work with their medical team on a plan to lower their weight.

High levels of non-HDL cholesterol, the type the American Heart Association calls "bad," can lead to heart disease. The warning steers attention toward one slice of the blood's cholesterol and away from the overall total.

The non-HDL count comes from taking total cholesterol and subtracting the HDL portion. The remainder holds LDL along with other types such as VLDL. LDL carries its "bad" reputation as the leading cause of the cholesterol buildup that clogs arteries. HDL earns a "good" label by helping clear cholesterol out of those vessels. Stripping out the helpful fraction leaves a figure built around the main culprit behind blocked arteries. A total reading, by contrast, folds protective and harmful cholesterol into one number. A high total can include a generous share of HDL.

A health care professional may treat non-HDL cholesterol as a better number to follow than total cholesterol, according to the association. The group gives two reasons. Patients need not fast before the test, and the calculation yields a dependable result for everyone. The usual preparation for a lipid panel, the blood test that measures cholesterol, allows nothing but water ahead of the draw. The no-fasting option removes one hurdle between a person and a cholesterol screening. A test that skips the fast and works for any patient also suits repeat checks, letting someone see whether changes in diet, activity, or smoking show up in the number.

Cutting high cholesterol can start with a diet light on saturated fat, regular exercise, and an end to smoking. Doctors can add prescription drugs such as statins. All three habits mirror other measures on the same list: Eat Better, Be More Active, and Quit Tobacco. Progress on any of those fronts can help pull non-HDL levels down as well. A person working through the list in order meets the cholesterol item after three habits that feed into it.

Blood sugar that stays high for a long stretch can damage four parts of the body: the heart, the kidneys, the eyes, and the nerves. The kidneys, eyes, and nerves sit outside the cardiovascular system, so the stakes of this measure reach past heart disease.

Glucose starts at the dinner table. The body breaks most of what a person eats down into this blood-borne sugar and then burns it for energy.

The body depends on this sugar as fuel, which locates the danger in levels that remain elevated over time.

The supplied notes for Manage Blood Sugar cover how the body turns food into glucose for energy and how sustained high blood sugar can harm the heart, kidneys, eyes, and nerves, and they do not supply enough further substance to reach the minimum. No approved supplementary details cover this measure. The slide states only what that evidence supports.

The American Heart Association treats blood pressure readings below 120/80 mm Hg as optimal, and it offers no other threshold for this measure. Keeping pressure inside healthy ranges, the AHA notes, can lengthen the years a person stays well. The group steers readers to a separate summary of 10 key facts from its high blood pressure guideline. Each reading measures a physical force. Blood pressure tracks how hard blood pushes against the walls of the arteries with each heartbeat. A reading qualifies as high once that force stays elevated over time.

Hypertension, the medical term for high blood pressure, tends to produce no signs or symptoms anyone would notice. People can feel fine while their pressure runs high. A measurement provides the sole way to learn whether pressure has climbed. Readings rise and fall over the course of a day. A health care provider may take several of them across more than one visit before diagnosing high blood pressure. Clinicians may suggest home checks with a validated monitor to help confirm the result. One high number at a single appointment may not settle the matter. The AHA's target means little without a reading to hold against it.

Everyday habits and medication that a patient takes as prescribed can bring high blood pressure under control. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention suggests going over several lifestyle steps with a health care team. Its recommendations cover regular physical activity, avoiding tobacco, a healthy diet that limits sodium and alcohol, a healthy weight, and stress management. Four of those steps line up with separate measures on the AHA's list: activity, tobacco, diet, and weight. Bringing pressure down can cut the risk of heart disease and stroke, which share the label cardiovascular disease. One blood pressure reading can capture progress across four other measures on the checklist.
