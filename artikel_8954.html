After a Historic Electoral Rout, Trump Again Asks the Supreme Court to Greenlight His Ballroom

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

One day after the historic rout by the Democrats nationwide on Election Day a week ago, Trump took to the construction site of the sprawling ballroom he is constructing on the grave of the East Wing of the White House, which he abruptly razed in October 2025. “Trump invited reporters into a cavernous construction site and talked about the building project for nearly 20 minutes,” reported PBS, “without mentioning sweeping Republican defeats around the country the night before.”

The next day, Solicitor General D. John Sauer filed a petition asking the Supreme Court to greenlight the 90,000-square-foot construction project, including the 22,000-square-foot ballroom-cum-military complex. He asked the justices to rule on whether a district court, at the behest of the National Trust for Historic Preservation, erred in August in enjoining the ongoing “renovation” of the East Wing. The $400 million project has been the subject of heated debate and lawsuits since its inception. Sauer asks that the court reverse a lower court’s ruling outright or hear oral arguments, explains SCOTUSblog. The petition starts off by claiming the injunction was granted “at the behest of a single passerby claiming she will dislike how the new building looks.” That passerby was Alison K. Hoagland, a member of the board of the National Trust for Historic Preservation. The question of whether she has legal standing to sue is a central issue in the ongoing argument.

Sauer claims she has no standing, because the court has ruled that “mere offense, disagreement, or distaste does not qualify as a concrete and particularized injury.” He further calls the ballroom a “vital national-security effort taking place on the White House grounds with the President’s personal involvement,” arguing that it is necessary because the president faces “an onslaught of assassination attempts.” (There have been, counting generously, three.)

A consultation with the Secret Service and the National Parks Service, says the petition, determined that demolition and rebuilding would be the most cost-effective course of action and would be best “to ensure that the East Wing remains minimally visible from public spaces.”

The old structure couldn’t support the ballroom’s necessary security features, says the White House, including blast-proof windows, fortified walls, and missile-resistant columns. Nor could the underground military installation be built under the existing structure.

In April 2026, US District Court judge Richard J. Leon wrote a searing 35-page opinion, employing some 18 exclamation marks, rejecting Trump’s plan for the ballroom, saying that “the President of the United States is the steward of the White House for future generations of First Familes. He is not, however, the owner!”

The US Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia froze Leon’s order to allow the court time to consider the government’s appeal, outlines SCOTUSblog. A three-judge panel upheld Leon’s ruling in August but pushed its effective date back two weeks to allow the federal government to go to the Supreme Court. The majority wrote that only Congress has the power “to regulate the construction and demolition of White House structures.” A dissenting judge said Hoagland had no standing.

The Trump administration called Leon’s ruling “extraordinary and unlawful,” and Sauer asked the justices to pause the ruling, saying construction was “vitally required by national security” and that Hoagland’s “dislike” for the new construction doesn’t constitute a legal injury.

In August, a federal court ruled that Trump lacked the authority to build without Congressional approval, but the Supreme Court greenlighted the construction project in a stay issued by Chief Justice John Roberts; the Court ruled 5–4 for the White House on August 31. Roberts dissented and, along with Ketanji Brown Jackson, Elena Kagan, and Sonia Sotomayor, ruled that the construction “is likely unlawful” and that the majority decision was “no victory for the separation of powers” and that Hoagland did have standing.

In any event, there’s no turning back now, says Sauer, writing that the project is 70% complete, “and, in turn (sic), there remains no sound way now to redesign or unwind the massive superstructure that is currently standing at the White House.”
