Ron Howard Wasn’t Sure He Was the Right Director for ‘Avedon’; This Is What Changed His Mind

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

The documentary about famed fashion and portrait photographer Richard Avedon premiered on Netflix on Nov. 11.

Ron Howard knows his way around a camera: in front of and behind the lens; scripted film or documentary feature. But when his production company, Imagine Entertainment (co-chaired by Brian Grazer), was considering a biodoc on fashion photographer Richard Avedon, Howard wasn’t sure if he should be at the helm.

“I really went into it thinking, ‘This is a great subject, it’d be great for Imagine,’” Howard said of the Avedon Foundation opening up the artist’s archives to him during a screening at SCAD Film Festival on Oct. 28. “As I began sort of seeing the breadth of that work, I was immediately impressed, but I still felt like, I’m not sure I’m really the right filmmaker for this, but this is fascinating and I bet we could explore what went on behind the scenes making many of these great photos that we remember.” Related Stories Beyond the Book From Book Club to Netflix: How Katherine Center's 'The Bodyguard' Became 'Guarding Stars' Movies Lee Chang-dong on the Mysteries and Meaning of 'Possible Love'

“As I began to do some research, soon I really did connect with it as this very human story that I wanted to tell,” Howard continued. “It was less anecdotal about how photos were done and what glamorous or intriguing figures sat for him, and much, much more about an artist’s journey, and I related to it. In some ways, we’re very different people — he’s an extrovert, I’m not; he was the life of the party, I avoid them — but, man, did he have drive and ambition.”

The resulting film, Avedon, now streaming on Netflix, blends commentary from collaborators — former magazine editor Tina Brown, model Lauren Hutton and dancer and choreographer Twyla Tharp — with Richard’s works, both commercial and historic, like his portraits of figures of the civil rights movement. The project heavily explores Avedon’s legacy as an artist with strong creative and political convictions, as well as his struggle balancing those passions with his obligations as a husband and father.

“Ultimately, we had 500 photographs in the documentary, but our amazing producing partner, Dallas Brennan Rexer, combed through 16,000 sittings of Avedon,” producer Sara Bernstein, president of Imagine Documentaries, explained during the Q&A. “Initially, we thought there might be a select number of hero images that would take us through Avedon’s creative story. But then there’s this backdrop of, essentially, the back half of the 20th century, so you could match these images with these different moments in history or these different personalities who represent different buckets of history. And then you were able to see a deeper, personal story when John Avedon sat down for us.”

Richard’s son, John, whose son, Michael, is also a fashion photographer, was a “reluctant interview,” despite being supportive of the project, Howard noted as he shared how he got John on board as a talking head.

“Frankly, I said to him, ‘There’s a gap here because we have your father’s voice. We have people who worked with him, but we don’t have that intimate connection, that deeper understanding that’s away from the studio.’ And I think as a writer himself, he recognized that there was a role for him to fill,” said Howard, later adding, “now [the film] became a generational thing about fathers and sons in addition to everything else.”

In one of the final scenes of the film, Avedon, who died in 2001, makes a startlingly reticent prediction about the outlook on his art form, stating, “I don’t think there will be photography in the future, only machines.”

Asked what he thinks Avedon would capture at this moment in time, where the battle between art and AI seems to be reaching its peak, Howard said, “He’d probably still be shooting on the white paper and probably still using that three-by-five camera because, for him, it did become a kind of communication and an exploration, capturing those moments. I think he would still be entirely relevant because he became the story behind the story as an artist. And we began to recognize his work; whether we knew it was Avedon, we could feel the hand of a creator.”

Added Howard of how Richard might approach AI, “My guess is also with his sensibility and aesthetic, he’d be using it as a tool to advance his aesthetic. I don’t think he’d be necessarily running away from it, but he would want to stay in control.”
