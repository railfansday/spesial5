The Celtics taught the Wizards’ defense a lesson

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

During the Washington Wizards' much-needed three-game win streak, they made a few key adjustments. They yanked underperformers out of the rotation and limited the Deandre Ayton solo-big minutes, in which the Wizards have gotten caved in all season. They played Trae Young off the ball more and gave Kyshawn George and AJ Dybantsa more creation responsibility.

More than anything, though, they cleaned up some of the low-hanging fruit. They grabbed more rebounds, matched up better in transition, and eased some of the most damaging defensive mistakes. They knocked down some more open threes. The Celtics, in Tuesday night's primetime (first half) onslaught, showed the Wizards how far they still have to go.

Boston is a team built to punish glaring weaknesses. They relentlessly attack bad defenders, and of course were merciless in dragging Young into every action and forcing the Wizards to make difficult rotations. The Celtics scored 80 first half points and poured in 15 threes — the sort of Mazulla-ball explosion that they've been waiting to unleash.

Too often, the Wizards were slow to react to the Celtics' core actions. Boston, when they're locked in, is adept at running early-clock plays that prey on inattentive defenses, like this flare screen:

In semi-transition, the Wizards are cross-matched, a common hazard of the double-big alignment. The Celtics are on the same page: Neemias Queta steps into a screen for a trailing Jayson Tatum on an unsuspecting Dybantsa.

Dybantsa realizes slightly too late that he needs to switch the screen and take Queta. By then, it's too late. The floor is open, and Queta has cleverly slipped to the basket.

(Note, too, that Sarr and Davis are the weak side defenders here. In a few situations on Tuesday night, the Celtics generated open threes by capitalizing on that particular alignment. Sarr and Davis are slower at completing the "X-out" rotation, with the low man swarming the rim and the other playing two shooters at once.)

Again, the Celtics get right into their action. Derrick White is set up for a Spain, or stack, pick-and-roll — where he sets a back-screen on a dropping big man. White is still so valuable to the Celtics, despite his ice-cold shooting, in part because of plays like this; he is clever about screening his own man and disguising his intentions.

On this one, White's screen slams Ayton and springs Paul George into the paint. The problem there starts with Dybantsa, who is hit hard by Luka Garza's initial screen and forced into a switch. To his credit, Tre Johnson sees what's happening and recovers toward George. But Bilal Coulibaly senses disaster and sprints over to help, leaving Payton Pritchard alone.

It's these emergency defense instincts that the Wizards struggle with. Coulibaly is right that he may be needed at the rim, but he commits so fully that he has no chance to rotate back to Pritchard. The most in-sync defenses are on a string when the offense gains an advantage — perhaps, in this situation, with the guy in Dybantsa's spot peeling off to the corner.

The Wizards have recently been better at erring on the side of effort defensively, and that's the first step toward building something real. Dybantsa has room to grow on the little things, like screen navigation, but he flies around and pressures relentlessly. Coulibaly knows he's on the court for his defense and plays like it.

When opposing teams line up transition layups and grab endless offensive rebounds, that's when the pressure on coach Brian Keefe starts heating up. But lately, that pressure is getting a bit lighter, and the second half against the Celtics was much better, with Boston cooling off and the Wizards limiting some of the easiest baskets.

They'll have a few days to rest and take stock before a rematch with the Orlando Magic on Saturday.
