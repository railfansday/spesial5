3 Investments to Give a Child This Holiday Season and Leave Alone Until 2040

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Which holdings can stay untouched for 14 years without anybody watching them?

By Daniel Sparks – Nov 18, 2026 at 7:38PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Vanguard S&P 500 ETF ›

A few shares of stock can be a better holiday gift than most toys, partly because a child won't think about them for a long time. A gift given this season and left alone until 2040 has around 14 years to compound.

The past 14 years show what that can look like. A $1,000 gift of the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO -0.28%) at the end of November 2012 would be worth almost $6,700 as I write, with dividends reinvested. The same $1,000 in Berkshire Hathaway (BRKA -1.71%)(BRKB -2.07%) Class B shares would've reached around $6,700, too. In Amazon (AMZN +3.82%), it'd be worth about $20,500.

No one should count on a repeat, especially from Amazon. But these are still the three investments I'd choose for a child now, with the index fund as the anchor. (The shares belong to the child, but gifts like this normally sit in a custodial account an adult manages until the child comes of age.)

The Vanguard S&P 500 ETF holds 506 stocks, pretty much the full S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.23%) index of big U.S. companies. Its expense ratio is 0.03%, or $0.30 each year for every $1,000 invested.

For a gift no one plans to watch, the fund's best feature might be that it updates itself. Companies that shrink tend to fall out of the index and are replaced by growing ones, and the fund handles those trades itself, so its owner doesn't have to.

Of course, that also means the fund tilts heavily toward what's largest today. Information technology stocks were around 37% of it at the end of October, so a long slump in tech could hold it back.

Berkshire Hathaway owns the BNSF railroad, a big utility business, and a lot of manufacturing, service, and retail companies. Its insurers also had almost $179 billion of float (premiums collected before claims are paid) as of Sept. 30.

Showing how steady the non-insurance side has been, Berkshire's railroad, energy, and manufacturing, service, and retailing businesses together earned $7.4 billion in the third quarter, 13% more than a year earlier. Their nine-month earnings rose 13%, too. Insurance underwriting earnings, though, dropped 43% in the quarter, and investment income dipped as well, so total operating earnings declined around 6%.

Berkshire hasn't declared a cash dividend since 1967. Instead, it reinvests in its businesses and buys back shares -- around $14.4 billion worth through September. And it still had more than $350 billion in cash and short-term Treasury bills, over a quarter of its market value.

Of course, Warren Buffett won't make the big calls over the next 14 years. Greg Abel is CEO, and Buffett became chairman emeritus in September. How Abel spends that cash might matter more than anything else.

Amazon offers the most growth of the three. Its third-quarter operating income surged 61% year over year to $28.1 billion, and its operating margin of 13.9% was its highest ever. Its cloud unit, Amazon Web Services (AWS), made up $17.8 billion of that, as sales growth accelerated to 39%.

"There has never been a trillion dollar revenue business, and we may have two of them in the next 5-10 years," CEO Andy Jassy said in Amazon's third-quarter earnings release.

Getting there is expensive. Operating cash flow for the last 12 months climbed 29% to $168.3 billion. But trailing-12-month free cash flow dropped from positive $14.8 billion a year ago to negative $7.6 billion as of June, then to negative $42.4 billion as of September, as artificial intelligence (AI) spending piled up.

In short, Amazon is taking in more cash than ever and spending even more. If that spending earns a weak return, the stock might struggle for a long time.

At about 24 times its 2027 earnings estimate, Amazon stock isn't a bargain. But I think it's a fair price for two big growth engines.

I'd make the index fund the largest part -- maybe half the gift, with the rest split between Berkshire and Amazon. The fund spreads the money among hundreds of businesses, so a setback at either company wouldn't ruin the whole gift.

All three will probably drop together in the next bear market. But a gift put aside for a child is less likely to be sold in a panic, which can matter a lot over 14 years. I'd give all three this season.
