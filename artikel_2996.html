What The ‘Vulnpocalypse’ Means For Cybersecurity In The AI Age

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Julio Fort is a co-founder at Blaze Information Security. He has over 20 years of cybersecurity experience advising global organizations.

​October is Cybersecurity Awareness Month, and in 2026, the wake-up call came from the software businesses depend on.

Critical vulnerability disclosures and reports of AI-assisted intrusions raised a difficult question: How much time do companies really have to find and fix their weaknesses?

The wave of disclosures and incidents, which were part of the ongoing concern described as the “vulnpocalypse,” deserves attention beyond the immediate scramble to patch. AI is changing how software is examined and how attacks are carried out. Security programs built around monthly maintenance windows, manual reviews and annual assessments need to account for that change.

For leaders, the consequences are practical. They affect which systems can stay online, how engineering teams spend their time and who has the authority to act when security and business priorities conflict.

In October, PHP published fixes for two critical vulnerabilities known as php2shell, which could allow attackers to execute code on vulnerable servers without authentication. European authorities advised organizations to prioritize internet-facing systems and to consider isolating exposed applications if they could not fix them immediately.

Based on my experience advising organizations on cybersecurity, the first problem for companies was knowing where the affected software was running. PHP can sit inside third-party products, so an application inventory alone may not answer that question. Teams also needed to understand which services depended on those systems and what taking them offline would mean for customers and operations.

How these vulnerabilities were discovered matters, too. The researchers behind the discovery acknowledged using AI (subscription required) to examine the source code of PHP, identify an exploitable pattern and investigate related flaws, according to the New York Times. Their knowledge of the software and its vulnerability history helped guide the work, but AI massively sped up the process.

This presents a reason to reconsider confidence in familiar software. A component that has been used for years can still contain weaknesses that a fresh investigation uncovers. AI gives researchers more capacity to carry out that investigation. Companies need a clearer basis for confidence than the fact that a system has been running without a known incident.

​October also provided evidence of AI being used in actual intrusion campaigns.

CrowdStrike identified Vapor Panda, an allegedly state-sponsored hacking group, that targeted more than 200 organizations across at least 14 countries over 11 days beginning October 17. The researchers confirmed post-exploitation activity at nine organizations. The attackers used the ARTEX AI framework to pursue several intrusions at once and try alternative approaches when one failed, with a human operator providing direction at important points.

The significance for defenders is how much work an attacker can carry out between human decisions.

From what I see, many companies still handle vulnerabilities through a series of manual steps. Someone reviews a finding, identifies the owner, opens a ticket and waits for engineering time. A fix then waits for testing and a suitable deployment window.

Those steps serve a purpose, but the delay between them can leave an important system exposed. AI-assisted attacks put more pressure on companies to reduce that delay and restrict what an attacker can reach in the meantime.

An annual penetration test still has value. The problem is treating it as sufficient evidence until the next assessment.

Applications change as teams introduce new features, connect services and adjust permissions. A test completed earlier in the year cannot establish whether those changes created new ways into the business.

Security and engineering leaders should, therefore, agree on which changes require additional testing. A significant change to authentication or a new internet-facing service should prompt a discussion about what needs to be checked before and after release.

The same applies to fixes. Closing a ticket should mean that someone has confirmed the affected systems were updated and that the relevant weakness was addressed.

Finally, while AI can help defenders examine code, investigate findings and expand testing, leaders should judge those capabilities by the work they complete and the evidence they produce. Decisions that could interrupt production still need clear accountability.

Finding vulnerabilities faster will accomplish little if the organization cannot resolve the important ones. Security may identify the issue, but another team owns the application and a business leader controls its priorities. A CISO cannot create engineering capacity or approve downtime simply by marking a finding urgent.

Companies investing in faster discovery should also fund the work that follows. That includes upgrades, compatibility testing, changes to system design and checks that fixes worked. If the same issues keep getting postponed, leadership needs to understand why and decide what must change.

October’s PHP guidance also exposed the cost of relying on systems that cannot be patched promptly. Keeping a vulnerable service online may require removing public access or isolating it from the rest of the environment. That can interrupt customer delivery, which makes the decision a business responsibility.

This gives familiar controls, such as network segmentation, renewed urgency. A legacy application awaiting an upgrade should be restricted to the connections and credentials it needs to operate. If those restrictions cannot sufficiently reduce the risk, leadership must fund replacement or accept the operational cost of taking it offline. Repeatedly postponing that investment leaves the company facing the same decision at the next critical disclosure.​

October’s “vulnpocalypse” should prompt a direct conversation about readiness. How quickly can the company identify affected systems, fix them and confirm that the problem is resolved? If that takes longer than expected, what protects the business while it waits?

The answers require decisions about engineering priorities, system design and authority. Those decisions will determine how well the organization responds as AI increases the pressure on software security and attackers move even faster at machine speed.​
