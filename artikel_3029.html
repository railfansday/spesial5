With Democrats to Control Congress, Is It Better to Buy Bitcoin or an Index Fund?

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Investors tend to have a number of misconceptions about what happens when Congress changes political hands.

By Alex Carchidi – Nov 18, 2026 at 7:45AM EST

Control of Congress just changed poltical hands in a big way, and, much as with all major changes, it's no surprise that it's making some investors a bit uneasy. Many holders of Bitcoin (BTC -2.27%) openly worry that the new Democratic majority will have a grudge against crypto due to the Trump administration's embrace of it. At the same time, index fund owners, even for the funds tracking the entire market like the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO -0.31%), are concerned about high valuations, persistent inflation, and artificial intelligence (AI), and those worries are amplified by widespread (and overstated) impressions that Democratic congresses tend to be bad for asset prices.

So, in light of the congressional switchover and the many uncertainties in the market right now, does it make more sense to buy Bitcoin, or an index fund?

First, let's establish some basic facts. A party gaining control of both houses of Congress is not something that's strongly associated with the market tanking or flying afterward.

From 1955 to 2023, the S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.38%) delivered a total return of 8.6% annually in years when a Republican president was balanced by a Democratic Congress (and one of those years, during the 2008 financial crisis, skews those results to the downside significantly). Patience and consistency were still the most important traits that investors needed to have to build their portfolios during those periods.

For Bitcoin, the last time Democrats held control of both congressional chambers, through all of 2021 and 2022, it fell 48%. But don't read too much into that; during the same time, the crypto industry was being rocked by the spectacular collapse of the FTX exchange, and Bitcoin was in one of its bear markets after a strong upward trend in the prior years.

Still, Senator Elizabeth Warren, a loud critic of the cryptocurrency industry, is expected to chair the Senate Banking Committee when the new Congress is seated in 2027. She's also one of the senators that detractors traditionally single out as being noxious to stock and crypto prices. A closer inspection reveals that Warren's recent crypto scrutiny has focused on allegedly corrupt practices of some crypto businesses, like those associated with the Trump family and the Trump administration. Similarly, Warren has been in politics for a long time, and the stock market has still on average increased annually during her tenure.

So, it's my assessment that many of the politics-related concerns about Bitcoin and the U.S. stock market are heavily overstated despite being modestly grounded in reality.

Politics aside, there are other reasons that investors propose for being a bit squeamish about buying either Bitcoin or an index fund.

The lofty valuation of the market is a big and real concern. One valuation metric, the Shiller cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio, divides the S&P 500's price by its average inflation-adjusted earnings during the prior decade, and it was near 40 on Nov. 17, whereas its long-run mean is 17.5. High valuations tend to predict lower future returns until either earnings catch up or share prices go down; Vanguard's forecast as of Sept. 30 is for  U.S. stocks to return 4.8% to 6.8% per year during the next decade, suggesting a lower-than-average annual return.

Bitcoin is having somewhat of the opposite problem. With its price at $63,360 as of Nov. 17, it's almost 50% below its peak set in October 2025. Its price now is just a bit higher than it was five years ago. That can make it hard to stomach making a purchase.

If you must pick between buying Bitcoin and shares of an index fund, pick the index fund.

An index fund tracking the S&P 500 holds shares of roughly 500 large companies, so its value rests on corporate profits even if valuations shrink. Furthermore, a committee at index provider S&P Dow Jones Indices periodically swaps out companies that no longer meet its criteria, and companies whose market value is growing carry more weight in the index. In contrast, Bitcoin has no cash flow, so its price depends on new buyers willing to pay the market price, and it's obviously harder to attract them when its price isn't climbing steadily.

Of course, most people needn't choose only one of these assets. Anyone buying Bitcoin should already own plenty of index fund shares, and Bitcoin is also something that should be a limited part of most diversified portfolios already.
