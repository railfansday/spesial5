Should you go with a headphone DAC/amp combo or a stack?

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Both will do a fine job, and it depends on what you need from your gear.

You don't always need a DAC and amp to enjoy music through headphones. If your computer or laptop's headphone jack already delivers clean audio at a comfortable volume, then you're probably fine. Some MacBooks from 2021 and later, for example, support both low- and high-impedance headphones and can deliver up to 3 volts RMS through their headphone jacks when you use headphones rated between 150 and 1,000 ohms. If your headphone jack isn't quite as capable, or if you're not satisfied with the sound, though, then it's time to spend on some hardware.

A DAC and a headphone amp can come together in a single unit (usually known as a DAC/amp) or as a stack of separate units. And while the latter seems like the more serious setup, it doesn't necessarily mean you'll enjoy a better listening experience. The main job of a DAC is to turn digital audio signals into analog signals, while the amplifier takes those analog signals and makes them loud enough to power your headphones. Both a DAC/amp combo and a stack do the same thing, and which you go for largely depends on your headphones, how much flexibility you need and, of course, your budget.

For most listeners, a DAC/amp is the way to go, especially if you're trying to improve your computer's audio without taking up too much space. Not only is a single unit more convenient, but it also means fewer cables dangling around and one less power adapter to worry about. You can even use a portable USB-C DAC/amp dongle designed for smartphones, but desktop versions usually offer better connectivity and power.

DAC/amps can also offer better value than separates, too. Schiit's Magni 6 sells for $169 with its optional internal USB DAC. Conversely, you can buy the Magni 6 sans DAC for $99 and pair it with the Modi 5 DAC for $149 (or $164 with a power adapter). That brings the total to over $250, and you don't even get an interconnect cable. The Modi 5 has optical and coaxial inputs, sure, but that doesn't automatically equate to better sound.

DAC/amp combos don't necessarily have to come with major compromises, either. The $549 JDS Labs Element IV delivers up to 3.2 watts per channel into 32 ohms, and comes with features like automatic gain switching, EQ and even preamp outputs for powered speakers. That's more than enough for many demanding headphones, eliminating the need for a dedicated amplifier. The downside, of course, is that you're stuck with the combo and can't upgrade piecemeal.

While DAC/amp combos are great, a stack can still make sense for audiophiles who use a wide variety of headphones or who like to tinker with their hi-fi setup. Going with separates gives you the freedom to upgrade the amplifier down the line without giving up a perfectly capable DAC, or vice versa.

Certain headphones may also benefit from the power a dedicated amp can provide. Some high-impedance headphones just ask for a lot of power, while planar magnetic models, for example, can combine low impedance and low sensitivity. Let's take the HiFiMAN Susvara Unveiled, for example, which has a 45-ohm impedance and only 86 dB sensitivity. The Absolute Sound advises at least 6 watts of amplification, so a standalone amp may be the better choice here. However, too much power can be a bad thing, too: a very powerful amp may result in audible hiss with very sensitive headphones. So start by checking your headphones' power requirements and the amplifier's noise performance instead of just assuming that you need a beefy amp.

You may also get extra analog inputs, more gain settings or extra headphone outputs (such as balanced connectors) when you go for a dedicated amplifier. This extra flexibility is not unique to separates, but you're more likely to get these bonuses with a dedicated amp. There is, however, a price to pay for these perks, as higher-end headphone amps like Topping's $499 A70 Pro can cost more than many DAC/amps on their own. You'll also have to deal with the potential hassle of accommodating a pair of interconnects and an extra power cable. It's unavoidable if you need what a stack offers, but not everyone will.
