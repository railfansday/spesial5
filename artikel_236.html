Today, I’m talking with Jon Favreau, a political commentator and cofounder of Crooked Media. You probably know Jon best from a little show called Pod Save America, which Jon started with a group of fellow Obama staffers in the wake of Donald Trump’s election in 2016. It’s now one of the most popular podcasts in the country.

Since then, Pod Save America and the company created around it, Crooked, have become one of the major success stories of independent liberal media. So one of my central questions for Jon was why he thought Democrats swept the midterms earlier this month and, more importantly, why it happened now — especially after a wave of tech billionaires made big swings to the right while buying huge pillars of the information ecosystem.

That’s Elon Musk buying Twitter and turning it into X, Mark Zuckerberg sucking up to Trump while tearing down Meta’s content moderation infrastructure, the Ellison family buying stakes in TikTok, turning over the leadership of CBS News and now potentially CNN while integrating Warner into Skydance… the list goes on. How did they all miss the huge swing in the mood of the public — or, more apocalyptically, fail in their attempts to control the media and thus influence the public?

Crooked itself is also just a fascinating business, as all creator businesses are. When we think of what you might call news creators, we tend to look to the right — after all, conservative media have been building an alternative, direct-to-consumer pipeline since the dawn of talk radio.

But Jon and his fellow Crooked cofounders created a reliable template for liberal creators, and it’s working. Crooked hired its first CEO a few years ago, now offers more than a dozen original shows, and has a staff of around 100 people working on everything from live events to political advocacy. Jon tells me the subscription business has crossed 65,000 members.

So of course I wanted to know how Jon feels about topics we cover a lot here on Decoder: online distribution, the platform power of YouTube, and how you balance running a media business that has to make money with a newsroom that, ultimately, has a political mission and a strong point of view.

Of course, AI. Jon has turned his own podcast Offline into something close to an AI policy show this year. He has a lot of thoughts on how Congress should tackle the issue.

Okay: Jon Favreau, the co-host of Pod Save America and cofounder of Crooked Media. Here we go.

This interview has been lightly edited for length and clarity.

Jon Favreau, you’re the co-host of Pod Save America, you’re the host of Offline. You’re also the co-founder of Crooked Media. Welcome to Decoder.

I’m excited to have you here. I’ve been on Offline with you. It was a really fun conversation.

I have a million ideas about what to talk about with you. I want to talk about Crooked as a creator company in this particular moment in media. I feel like we have to start with the midterms though. The blue tsunami is here. It feels like a repudiation of the billionaires owning all the social platforms, the billionaires owning all the broadcast networks.

What do you make of that? I’ve just been turning this over in my head that the media was purchased in a huge and meaningful way, and Americans said, “No, we still see through it.”

I think it’s a repudiation of a government that came into power with the chief promise of bringing inflation down and making life more affordable, and it didn’t do that. If you’ve looked at the last couple of elections, people voted a party into power and they said, “Everything’s too expensive and I want stuff to be cheaper and I don’t want to be struggling. Can you do something about it?”

And then either they try to do something about it or they fail to do something about it or like Trump, you don’t even try at all. You actually make life harder, and then people say, “The propaganda that’s coming out of Fox or any of these right-wing platforms doesn’t matter. I fill up my car and gas is expensive and I don’t know if I’m ever going to be able to afford a house because interest rates are up, et cetera, et cetera.”

You can say whatever you want to say, but people’s lived experiences end up being more salient than the messages that they get from politics.

It’s just, “Gas prices are up, just throw the bums out.” But I would say the last 10 years of politics are, “Can I tweak policy into reality?” We’ve seen it happen several times. “Can I wage memetic warfare on the American public until these Republican talking points are the framework in which all further conversation must happen?”

We’ve seen it happen on the left too. I think Defund the Police is a great example of a thing that just spiraled out of control and maybe turned an election. But in particular, we’re going to wake up and the Fox News machine is going to define the terms of the debate, and then we’re going to wake up and whatever’s happening on Twitter is going to define the terms of the debate. That has been how it’s worked for an awfully long time, and “the gas prices are too high” and “this war is stupid” maybe just defeated it.

It works to a point, but there are certain conditions that messaging and media just can’t overcome, especially for the party in power or forever, whoever’s running the White House.

The same was true with Joe Biden. Joe Biden could go out there and say, “We passed this bill. We gave money out. We gave the checks out and things are getting better and we’re just recovering from Covid. You haven’t felt it yet, but it’s getting better and look at the economic statistics.” And people just said, “No, I don’t feel that yet. So I’m going to maybe give Donald Trump another try, even though I think he’s a shitty guy and I don’t like him very much.” But maybe they remember the economy of the first term being good.

We dealt with this when Obama ran and became president in 2009. They thought, “Financial crisis. Can’t stand Bush anymore. I’m going to try Obama.” And then Obama gets into office and for the first two years, the financial crisis is over, but the recession is still there. People are still out of work.

So they said, “I know you passed healthcare and I know you passed the recovery act, but I still can’t find a job. The economy just still doesn’t feel great to me. So it doesn’t matter what you say you did or how great your messaging is, I don’t feel it. I’m going to vote in a Republican Congress.” It’s been like that for as long as I’ve been in politics.

Is there some reason the incumbent party does not realize that saying something that sounds like “the fundamentals of the economy are strong” kills you? Because you’re describing a pattern that repeats and it’s that phrase almost exactly.

Yes. That’s why we understood it once 2012 rolled around because people were still not satisfied with the economy when Obama was running for reelection and we did everything we could to make that election a choice. We acknowledged that people were still struggling and people were still in pain. We totally avoided the long list of statistics about how wonderful the economy was getting and how everyone had recovered and all that kind of stuff. We just said, “Look, things are still rough out there, but it’s a choice. This is the kind of vision Mitt Romney has for the economy and this is the vision we have for the economy. You get to pick one.”

There’s a way, if you are the incumbent party in power, to still keep people on your side, but you’ve got to at least acknowledge and be honest about how people are feeling. You can’t tell people to feel a way that they aren’t feeling. You can’t tell people that a whole bunch of issues are important that aren’t the issues that matter most to them in their lives or at least to most people, which is, “Do I feel economically secure and do I actually feel safe?” That’s why people care about crime and they care about safety in their communities and then they also care about whether they can afford gas and groceries.

The one issue that really struck me in that framework is surveillance. We’ve done a number of episodes about Flock cameras and AI-enabled surveillance and the idea that you can just link all the databases together and Palantir can send a drone to your house to murder you. That connects directly to safety.

The CEO of Ring has been on the show and his argument is, “If I put up enough Ring cameras, I will zero out crime in certain neighborhoods.” I’ve asked him about the mechanism of how that will exactly work and the answer is that the thieves will be deterred by surveillance. It’s the same for the CEO of Flock. It’s the same argument everywhere. “If we just watch you enough, we’ll stop crime.” The American public hates this idea. There’s some limit to how much crime-stopping you want compared to how much surveillance must occur.

I watched the CEO of Flock in particular try to go hard MAGA in this election cycle and use the phrase, “The radical leftists want a crime world,” or whatever it is he was saying, and it just didn’t work. That to me felt like a glimmer of a realignment where the libertarians in America were saying, “No, we still don’t want a state with this level of power,” and your classic ACLU Democrats were saying, “Yep, that’s pretty bad,” and then throw all the bumps out, like, “Just get out of here and we’ll start over.”

What do you make of that? Because there are other glimmers of realignment, but that is the one that really stuck out to me.

The same people who wanted a more secure border suddenly looked around and said, “I also don’t want the government with masked, armed federal agents coming into my community and kidnapping people and taking them off the street and having that fear as I go about my day that I may be taken by the state just because of what I look like or how I talk or who I’m associated with. Or if I’m trying to protest the government, which is my constitutional right, then they might come after me then too.”

It’s too early to tell if it’s a realignment, but I do think there’s this resistance to state power, or state overreach, that we haven’t seen the extent of in another administration in our lifetime, that is as bad as it’s been with Donald Trump in his second term.

I think we’re going to see it. There are incoming members of Congress talking about FCC oversight today. But that one felt like another glimmer of, “Oh, this one party has gone too far. They own all the channels of speech. We are watching Elon Musk turn the moderation knobs however he wants it and the chairman of the FCC is attacking comedians. We don’t have to overthink this. We can just call this bad.”

The third one is AI. It’s data centers where you see both sides coming together to say no. Something like 41 of 43 data center moratoriums passed. And that’s just classic American NIMBYism: “I just don’t want this loud building here.” What do you make of that one?

It’s classic American NIMBYism, but it’s also this larger feeling that we’re losing control of the forces that shape our lives. Because when you talk to people who oppose the data centers, so much of it is about, “There was an NDA. It was all a secret. We couldn’t express our opposition to this before the deal was already done.”

Part of it is connected to AI. Part of it is just, “My electricity bills are going up. It’s fucking noisy.” Any of the people who live around data centers, it’s unbelievably noisy. It’s bright in the middle of the night. In some cases, you see communities where the community has gotten together and said, “I don’t want a data center,” and the local politicians say they don’t want it and they get overruled and somehow the data center ends up there anyway. At least there’s been a turn there and that’s not happening as much anymore.

But I do think there’s this feeling that, “We don’t want this in our community and yet more powerful companies and politicians are shoving it down our throats anyway, and we’re not going to take that because we should have control over what happens in our lives and what happens in our community.”

We did a big story on Kevin O’Leary’s project in Utah. We reported it out and we were there. And we assumed he would talk to us because he talks to everybody. We got to the end and we sent our “no surprises” email and he sent his lawyers back at us and I thought, “Thank you for this incredible gift of threatening us.”

Threatening us with legal action for talking to people in Utah about his data center. But that’s the level of aggression that most of these big projects come with.

Meta really loves talking about its project in Louisiana, but it doesn’t feel like the people in Louisiana felt like that was a choice they made. That was something Meta imposed on them and now the teachers got a raise and they need to be quiet.

They don’t feel the need to explain the projects or the data centers to the community. Even the people who are very pro-data centers say, “There are a lot of conspiracy theories about data centers and all this opposition is based in propaganda. Actually you can build them so that they run on clean energy and that they pay for the power.”

All of this might be true, but the companies — the hyperscalers, Meta and all these places that are trying to build these data centers — didn’t bother to try to explain the benefits. They didn’t bother to try to tell people in advance that this was happening or negotiate with communities. They’re doing it all now because of the pushback.

But at the time, it was more, “You say that this is going to go great for us, but you also said social media was going to go great for us.” If you just look at the larger economy with AI, people said globalization was going to go great for people. People are tired of hearing from people in elite circles, whether it’s in business or government, who say, “This new technological advance in development is going to be great for everyone. Trust us and just let us do what we want and you’ll benefit at some point.” People just say, “No, I don’t trust you anymore.”

Yeah, it’s funny. My hottest take continues to be that if consumer AI products were any good, these companies would have an easier time. Google’s AI overviews are not good enough to convince anyone to build a data center. Deepfake slop on Instagram is not going to convince anyone to build a data center.

The best pitch they have is, “One day we’ll cure cancer,” but you might still have to navigate the healthcare system for that to happen to you. That seems pretty opaque. And then it’s like Salesforce will get better and an ultra-weaponized B2B SaaS environment is not going to do it.

If you just put the Netflix logo on the side of the data center, people might calm down. They like Netflix, but there’s no consumer product that is making the sale. It’s just these guys and they don’t seem capable of doing it.

No. And they continue to sit down for interviews and say, “Yeah, and it could kill everyone and it could do all that, but also there’s plenty of good that’s coming from it and we’ve got it covered. We’ve got it covered.”

So to wrap this all up — and then I want to talk about Crooked and I want to talk about where we go from here — the thing that I was thinking about as I watched the election returns come in was at the beginning of Trump 2.0, there was a Steve Bannon quote that just ricocheted through my mind in our coverage. It was “politics is downstream of culture.“ This is just the [Breitbart] thesis.

I tend to believe Steve Bannon on this actually, that the cultural atmosphere of the country has some big effect on our politics. And then I watched the Trump administration engineer the sale or control of TikTok to Oracle and Larry Ellison. I watched CBS News get taken over by the Ellisons and Bari Weiss. I watched Elon Musk turn the knobs on X even harder. I watched the FCC work its way towards the removal of the restriction on broadcast ownership, so even fewer big companies could own more broadcast stations in the country.

I thought, “Oh, the mechanisms of culture are bending their will to the Trump administration and this is going to lead to some permanent political outcome.” We covered it that way. Then this election happened, and that appears to have been totally wrong. I just got that wrong and I’m still trying to figure that out.

Politics is downstream from culture, but material conditions rule out. As you are trying to form an opposition to the incumbent party — whatever it may be, and now it’s the Trump administration — culture is important to bringing people onto your side and building a movement and building an opposition that’s powerful enough to defeat the current incumbents. That’s going to be important.

But in terms of whether you are the incumbent and whether you are the party in power and material conditions aren’t great, ultimately it doesn’t matter if you control the culture, or at least it doesn’t help you as much as people think it might because you can’t outrun material conditions.

I wonder if that’s going to lead to a Woke 3.0. A former Verge reporter, Kylie Robison, tweeted at Jason Calacanis, “Put the pronouns in bio lil bro,” which is just one of the funniest reposts on election night that I saw.

I’m wondering, do you think that there is a cultural pendulum swing coming, or is it straightforwardly “improve our material conditions”?

Now, this could end up being naive, but I think that the left has grown over the decade and learned from the excesses of Woke 1.0.

[Laughs] Woke 2.0. My staff said this too. They were talking about Woke 3.0 and I said, “Guys, what happened to Woke 2.0?”

Woke 2.0 is crypto. We don’t talk about that.

I was asking them, “Where did Woke 2.0 go?” But, no, I do think that there’s tons of factional fighting in the Democratic Party as there always is.

But there does seem to be broad agreement on making sure that the party focuses on people’s material conditions and affordability. You even see that acknowledgement on the right as well. JD Vance said that, Ted Cruz said that. So it’s on the mind of all the people who are going to run for president in 2028.

The left is very cognizant, especially progressives, of what the culture wars and woke and all that kind of stuff cost them, fairly or unfairly. They don’t want to make that mistake again. Because I think everyone on the left, from the far left to the center left, understands the stakes in a way that we might not have when Trump first took office in 2016.

I want to come back to that as we talk about the future of the media ecosystem, but this is a good place to talk about Crooked itself. You started Crooked in the Trump administration. It’s a product of the first Trump administration. It has grown to become a force. I have here in my notes that some people call you a kingmaker. It’s an honor to be in your presence.

If you could make me one, that would be great. I think I’d be an excellent king of America. Tell me about Crooked. How is this company structured? How does it work?

We started in 2016 as a podcast. When we were in the White House, Tommy Vietor, Jon Lovett and I had talked about how there’s not enough progressive media out there. Part of it is because people in progressive media, just like people in politics, take themselves too seriously. There’s not a lot of joking around because everything is “end of the world” kind of stuff.

We have Vote Save America, which is the advocacy arm of the company. It just raised a couple million dollars for this last cycle and asked people to run for office themselves who listened to the show. We had 2,000 people raise their hands to run for office this last cycle. A bunch of them won. Part of the theory when we started is we don’t want to just tell people what’s broken about the world, but give them the tools and information they need to fix it. There was advocacy built into the founding of the company.

But somewhere around 2022, we finally decided that we need a CEO for this company. We need a real grownup. So we hired Lucinda Treat to be our CEO in 2022, and it has let the three of us focus on editorial and making content. Lucinda runs the company, and that has been a real blessing. Because running a company of 100 people is not something I’m good at, I would say.

Yeah, I run a newsroom of like 50 and I’m still figuring it out every day. So 100 people, 16 podcasts — how is that basically organized? What’s the general structure there?

Lucinda is the CEO, and under her we have a head of news and politics and a head of culture and entertainment. Just as every company has, we have finance and marketing, and people and culture. Vote Save America is headquartered in DC, and we have a few news folks in DC. We have some newsletters as well, because we’re on Substack now.

We have two reporters who are in DC and covering stuff there. Now we’re expanding. We just launched Still Counting with Nate Silver, Clare Malone and Galen Druke, which is a reboot of FiveThirtyEight, and they’re in New York. So we now have a presence in New York as well.

We’re in a real moment for creator-led media. You’re describing a group of three creators who expanded and have more people making content. There’s a business operation around you. You have a newsroom. You’re creators with a newsroom. That is fundamentally what I’m hearing. Maybe I’m getting that wrong, but it sounds like you’re creators with the newsroom.

Somewhere in there is, “We have to find new people to pay for a subscription, and we need new people to listen to the podcast.” All of that discovery happens on someone else’s platform. That is the real dynamic for every creator, right? We need to invest in YouTube. At any moment, YouTube could turn the knobs, just as Elon turned the knobs on X. How do you feel about that? How do you manage that pressure?

It’s just the reality, and so we have to go with it. It’s funny, because when we look at the RSS feed, which is where regular old audio podcasts are—

I do think YouTube has been a wonderful growth opportunity because it’s much harder to grow the actual audio RSS feed. But to your point, the fact that we’re all dependent on a YouTube algorithm is not great. The only solution for that is to just be on as many platforms as possible, and to be everywhere all at once.

Do you find that the revenue opportunities on all those platforms equal out? Did some rise and some fall, or do you have a more lucrative one?

The podcast ad market is still the bread and butter, just because it’s more reliable and sustainable. YouTube monetization is catching up now. But most of our revenue is still from the ads and the podcast stuff.

One of the most accidentally rude things I find myself saying about the creator industry is that everyone has to run a little ad agency. That big creators are better described as little ad agencies than whatever it is that they thought they were doing.

I say it’s accidentally rude because I don’t begrudge anyone running a business. It’s very difficult to run a media company. Please go and succeed. But when you’re running a little ad agency, sometimes it just comes through that you’re running a little ad agency. There’s a whole suite of tech creators and AI creators where I think it’s coming through more clearly than ever before. There are some that are very good, and like everything, there’s a wide range.

You explicitly have an advocacy arm. Does that make it easier to do the advertising? Does that make it easier to have that point of view? I find it very challenging.

There are just a lot of advertisers that we won’t work with. There are a lot of advertisers who won’t work with us because of our political affiliation.

Black Rifle Coffee isn’t coming to your doorstep?

The more explicitly partisan and political the podcast, the fewer advertisers you have, at least the bigger blue chip ones that want to get involved in politics. But then for us, there are a lot of companies and a lot of products where we think, “Does that conflict with our values?” And if it does…

We have these conversations all the time. We don’t just take whoever to be our advertisers. That makes it a little bit trickier. But there’s also plenty of advertising we do for causes and campaigns and stuff like that. I guess we make it up a little bit there. But it can be more challenging.

One of the ideas that I’ve just been turning around in my head as I’ve been talking to more and more creators lately is that the value of the creator advertising is actually in what you say no to. I know big creators who could say yes to everything and they say, “No, I don’t want to do it. My audience would be upset if I did this.”

Whereas, if you work at a media company, on the other side of the firewall there’s a group of people whose job it is to say yes. And you’re saying, “Well, I guess we’re doing that today. I had nothing to do with it.”

Our young audience does not actually understand that wall exists. I find myself explaining this hundred-year-old journalism tradition all the time. Because in the creator world it just doesn’t exist, and you are making those choices all the time. How does that express itself? You said you have these conversations. What does that actually look like?

There are certain categories of ads that we have to think about, like banks. Do we want to be advertising for big banks? Do we want to be advertising for any company or product or service which is explicitly against our political values? We dealt with this a lot earlier too, which is then you have to look at the company and say, “Is it a perfect company? Has it had complaints?”

At some point you can’t be looking at every single complaint about the company as a reason to not advertise for that company. Drawing the lines is not a perfect process. It’s necessarily ad hoc, which is why even though the three of us are mainly focused on editorial and content, we will still take a look at the advertisers coming on, or at least it’ll come to us if they may be controversial. And then we have to have a conversation about that.

One of my friends, who is a tech writer, has the point of view, “I will accept money from everyone except the seven most successful companies in the history of the world,” which is just not an economical way of thinking about it, but that’s the line. “All right, I’m just not going to take big tech money because I need to talk about them all the time.”

And it’s tough because also big tech money and crypto, and the betting markets, are the big advertisers these days.

Not for Pod Save America, no. We have deals with hosts who are independent contractors, and so if a host came to us and said they wanted to do one, we could talk about it. But not for Pod Save America. I wouldn’t do it for Offline either. I just think the betting on politics... First of all, I’m in the camp that doesn’t think prediction markets for politics are that useful. They mostly reflect conventional wisdom, and oftentimes conventional wisdom is right. It often isn’t.

The idea that the prediction markets, at least around politics, have this very special insight into what’s going to happen that polls can’t offer you has not been proven out to me yet.

There’s a part of the whole prediction market dynamic where eventually they will have to admit that the markets are only predictive if they have illegal inside trading in them.

Like that’s the only way they can actually be predictive.

The reason I’m asking this is I think we’re going to enter an era where creator businesses are more ascendant than ever. We saw the takeover of the platforms not be effective. We saw the takeover of the broadcast media not necessarily be effective. Fox News doesn’t seem all that effective.

It’s going to be this — it’s going to be creators on platforms talking directly to audiences about material conditions and how that not just remains sustainable, but grows to meet the ambition you might have, other creators might have, and also the demand for audiences.

Somewhere in there you run out of saying, “We’re just an ad agency.” You have to diversify and you have to do subscriptions. You have to find other lines of business. You might have to consolidate. How are you thinking about that growth? Or is that just Lucinda’s job?

That is mostly Lucinda’s job. But we want to take big bets on shows. It is interesting, because we know that our audience comes to us for politics and political analysis. So when we launch new shows, it doesn’t mean that every show we launch has to be all about politics, but it still has to appeal to our audience or audiences that would say, “Okay, it makes sense to come to Crooked to listen to this.”

Jon Lovett has Lovett or Leave It, which is his comedy show. It works really well because Lovett has political experience and all the jokes are about politics. That makes sense as a Crooked product. It’s the same thing with Nate Silver’s pod, which was a natural acquisition for us because our audience loves getting into the weeds on numbers.

Alex Wagner came to us and she has a pod, Runaway Country, also a natural fit. Then the question for us as we expand is, from a content perspective, “How can we grow the type of content that we offer audiences while not going so far afield from what our core business is that people would not come to us first versus another competitor or somewhere else that does entertainment or culture or tech better than we do?” That’s usually the thought process we go through as we’re trying to figure out how to grow.

I feel like I should have asked you the other Decoder question because we’re in the weeds of it. But the other Decoder question I ask everybody is: How do you make decisions? What’s your framework for that?

It’s funny, we have this whole other lens here, which is how to influence the political debate. At least for me, that’s the most important thing. We’ve now built a successful business that yes, I want to see grow. It also chugs along nicely on its own right now.

When there is a debate between whether we want to do something that’s going to make us more money or whether we want to do something that we think is going to move along the debate in politics, we choose the latter. At least I choose the latter. A lot of the guys want to as well.

We want to be influential in the political debate. It’s partly because of what you just said about the media environment, where there are so many different sources of information now that I think from a political perspective, it’s really difficult to get a message out to the electorate that everyone hears the same.

We’re not in the world where everyone’s going to watch the nightly news. When I was on the Obama campaign in ‘08, we knew that whatever events he did, we would judge the success of them through the prism of the three nightly news networks. That worked out pretty well for us as a campaign, even though we were on Facebook and trying to organize people on Facebook and do all that stuff back then.

The way you judged the success of an event in a day was, “How did it play on the evening news?” That world is long gone. From the perspective of how we keep democracy together so that everyone is getting good, reliable information and that everyone is debating the same information, that’s just a really, really hard task. I see our job as trying to make that a little easier for people, at least on our side, on the left side of the political spectrum.

We want to be a place where people on the far left, people on the center left, Never Trumpers and people on the right can at least feel comfortable coming on, debating, talking to us because we’re trying to reconnect the civic discussion that has been just completely balkanized over the last 10 years.

My favorite example of what you’re describing is Zohran Mamdani. I don’t think he’s ever answered a question without saying he loves New York City in the same way that Dr. Pimple Popper is always like, “I’m a dermatologist.” Literally just saying the first fact, reading his LinkedIn, because he can’t assume that you have any idea what’s going on. So “I love New York City” is just the first fact and then whatever answer. It comes up every single time.

That is partially an algorithmic problem. It’s partially just a media fragmentation problem. But you’re describing something bigger than that. You’re describing a shared value system. We might have different political ideas or different policy prescriptions, but we will agree that the Strait of Hormuz is closed.

We’re going to start there. That requires you to fight the algorithm. It requires you to fight the filter bubble. How do you take that approach? Are you just saying you love New York City over and over again or is there something else?

You try to appear in a bunch of different information streams. You try to be on a whole bunch of different platforms. You try to go on a bunch of different shows. You try to have people on with all kinds of different perspectives from different parts of the political spectrum, just so that you’re cross-pollinating more with different audiences. That’s really important. We think about that as one strategy.

But beyond that, it’s really difficult. People bemoan the demise of monoculture, and I think it’s not just a problem for entertainment and culture, or whatever it may be. It’s the biggest problem for politics. If there are no events, shows, or moments in time where everyone in the country is focused on the same thing, consuming the same information, and consuming the same entertainment, that’s a problem for a society that needs to govern itself.
