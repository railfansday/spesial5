Four French teenagers climbed down a hole and found cave paintings that are 17,000 years old

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

Discovered by four teenagers in 1940, France's Lascaux Cave holds hundreds of prehistoric Ice Age paintings.

On September 12, 1940, four teenagers slid down a narrow shaft on a hill above Montignac, in southwestern France. At the bottom, they found the painted cave of Lascaux.

The cave contains hundreds of prehistoric animal paintings and engravings. Radiocarbon results on charcoal from the cave first came out at about 15,500 to 17,200 years, though the art is now thought to be older.

Lascaux closed to visitors in April 1963, after crowds of tourists began to damage its fragile painted walls.

The opening was a foxes’ den halfway up the hill. A local legend said it led to an underground passage to the Lascaux manor.

Marcel Ravidat, a young apprentice mechanic, made the first attempt to explore it, as the French Ministry of Culture recounts in its history of the discovery. He had to put it off for lack of the right tools.

Four days after his first attempt, Ravidat returned with the necessary tools. The group enlarged the opening, allowing him to slip down a small vertical shaft.

He landed on a cone of rubble and slid to the bottom, and his three friends followed. By the light of a hastily made lamp, they walked along a gallery about 100 feet (30 meters) long.

As the passage narrowed, they spotted the first paintings, in what is now called the Axial Gallery.

The next day, the boys brought a rope, and Ravidat lowered himself down a shaft about 26 feet (8 meters) deep. There he found a scene of a man facing a bison.

All four discoverers were teenagers. Ravidat, from Montignac, was 17, and Jacques Marsal, also from Montignac, was 15.

Simon Coencas, 15, had taken refuge in the town. Georges Agniel, 16, was from Nogent-sur-Seine and was on vacation at his grandmother’s house.

The boys told their teacher, Léon Laval, who went into the cave on September 18. Prehistorian Henri Breuil, who had taken refuge in the region, visited soon after to authenticate the figures.

Photographer Fernand Windels began documenting the cave the following month, under Breuil’s direction.

Ravidat and Marsal guarded the entrance during the winter of 1940 to 1941. Both later became guides at the cave.

The four friends were not reunited until November 1986, 46 years after their discovery.

All of the accessible parts of Lascaux add up to no more than about 770 feet (235 meters). The cave is traditionally divided into seven sectors, including the Hall of the Bulls, the Nave and the Shaft.

Horses are by far the most common animals on the walls, followed by equal numbers of stags and aurochs, then ibexes and bison.

The few carnivores, such as bears and felines, appear in the farthest corners of the cave. The only human-like figure is in the Shaft scene.

Lascaux also holds many Ice Age engravings, which the prehistorian Father André Glory studied from 1952 to 1963. He identified 286 figures in the Passageway, most of which he recorded, and 87 in the Chamber of the Felines.

Deciphering and recording the roughly 600 figures of the Apse required prolonged visits.

In 1951, charcoal from the Shaft gave a radiocarbon result of 15,516 ± 900 years, equated with about 19,000 years before present.

Glory’s charcoal later returned 17,190 ± 140 and 16,000 ± 500 years, or between 21,000 and 18,000 years before present, according to the ministry’s review of the dates.

In 1998 and 2002, dates on reindeer antler from earlier excavations pushed the estimate back to between 23,500 and 22,000 years before present.

A newer radiocarbon program on reindeer bone places the occupation of the cave slightly later, between 21,500 and 21,000 years ago.

The results remain puzzling: some artifacts have Magdalenian characteristics, while the painting style looks Solutrean. Both are cultures of the late Ice Age.

Work to open the cave to the public was completed in 1948. Before its closure, Lascaux received up to 1,800 visitors a day, according to the regional cultural affairs office.

The ever-increasing numbers of tourists were raising carbon dioxide levels inside. Ravidat and Marsal pointed out the appearance of green algae in 1958 and 1959.

Max Sarradet, the cave’s curator, alerted Culture Minister André Malraux to the danger from 1960. On Sarradet’s advice, Malraux closed the cave on April 20, 1963, and Marsal became its guardian.

Lascaux II, a replica of the Hall of the Bulls and the Axial Gallery, opened in 1983 and has since drawn ten million visitors. Another replica, Lascaux IV, has welcomed visitors since 2016.

As of 2020, research and monitoring inside the original cave were limited to fewer than 200 hours a year. The Apse remained the most sensitive area.
