Between a modem, desktop computer, and network-attached storage (NAS), modern computing setups require a reliable power source to function effectively. If you work from home or own a small business, you know that nothing halts your workday and puts you behind like an unexpected power outage. An uninterruptible power supply (UPS) keeps your essential devices temporarily online, which provides a window for you to hit send on that last-minute email.

While a standard power strip or surge protector offers sufficient, albeit basic, protection, a UPS ensures devices are properly shut down and are protected from data loss. Beyond just keeping your devices running, the best UPS battery backups also shield sensitive electronics from potentially damaging power surges and fluctuations throughout the day. Automatic Voltage Regulation, or AVR, stabilizes inconsistent incoming voltage. It converts fluctuating voltage from the power source into a steady, consistent output for connected devices. During an outage, a UPS inverter converts the direct current (DC) stored in its battery into the alternating current (AC) that devices expect. Power from the wall doesn’t just switch on and off. Its voltage rises and falls in a smooth, rounded curve called a sine wave, swinging from positive to negative and back 60 times a second.

Pure sine wave models output a smooth wave that closely matches utility power, so your devices run on battery the same way they run on the grid. Some models output a simulated sine wave instead, a stepped signal that switches between positive and negative voltage with brief rests at zero. That output works for basic electronics, but it could cause problems for more sensitive electronics, like audio equipment and desktop PCs.

Desktop PCs are the biggest concern. Most use power supplies with active power factor correction (PFC), a circuit that improves efficiency and helps the unit meet energy standards. On a simulated sine wave, some of these power supplies can shut down unexpectedly or run under extra strain. Most devices without active PFC, including modems and routers, can handle a simulated wave without trouble during a typical short outage.

For a gaming PC, workstation, or home theater system, a pure sine wave UPS is the safer bet. The CyberPower CP1500PFCLCD lists sine wave output on battery and is designed for active PFC power supplies. The APC BR1500MS2 lists sine wave output on battery with a transfer time of as little as 8 milliseconds.

Setting up a UPS is straightforward: Simply plug the UPS into a wall outlet and connect your devices to the UPS. This plug-and-play simplicity means a UPS device is great for anyone, no matter their level of technical know-how. But be aware of the following safety guidelines before powering on a UPS in your home office.

Companies advise against connecting devices like copiers, laser printers, or space heaters to a UPS, as these can overload the device. Instead, use a separate surge protector.

Additionally, never plug a UPS into a surge protector or vice versa. It’s also important to avoid plugging a UPS into an extension cord because this can potentially overload the cord and create a fire hazard. It’s recommended to always plug your UPS directly into a grounded wall outlet. Doing so will ensure your UPS operates safely while providing the best possible protection for your devices.

Finding the right UPS battery backup largely comes down to the total wattage you need to keep your computer up and running while you save and shut down. Speaking broadly, we'd tell you that most home office setups with a desktop PC, monitor, modem, and router should look for a UPS around 600 to 1500 volt-amperes (VA).

To get a more accurate idea of what you specifically need, add up the wattage of all your connected devices, then look for a backup that can handle 120 to 130 percent of that load. That will give you enough time to shut down safely with some wiggle room in case you need to finish a task (or have something urgent come up before you can finish up).

Every system is different, but we'd expect a UPS in the 1000 to 1500VA range to keep your desktop and monitor running for about 15 minutes, which should be long enough to save and safely shut down. A smaller model can probably keep laptop-based setup for a similar amount of time, or power a modem and router for 30 to 45 minutes.

These are all broad estimates, though, and will vary based on what specific gear you're using. We recommend calculating your own power needs to figure out exactly how much time you'll have to shut down in the event of an emergency.
