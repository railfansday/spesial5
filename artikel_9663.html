That was Sara Jane Osborn's answer when someone asked who might know what she saw over Anchorage.

She had been taking pictures of a rainbow in June when she spotted something unusual above a distant tree line. It looked like a dark jellyfish, so she started recording. The video lasted about three minutes and eventually went viral in November.

The footage, however, left some important questions unanswered. Nobody could tell how big the object was or exactly how far away it was.

A similar-looking object filmed over Iraq received a much closer examination. The Pentagon's unidentified-object office reviewed 17 minutes of footage, along with the recording details and viewing angles. Its conclusion? With moderate confidence, the object was a cluster of balloons.

The difference between these two cases will allow us to examine how often we make claims in our writing without giving readers enough information to verify them.

The Anchorage object is what happens when there's not enough information to support a conclusion. Writers often run into a similar problem when they half-remember something they read years ago and present it as a fact.

Empty claims often come disguised as convincing statements. Here are some common examples:

"Studies show." Which studies? Who conducted them? How many people were involved? A sentence like "Studies show that waking at 5 a.m. makes people more productive" sounds solid until you ask those three questions.

"Experts agree." Which experts? Are they researchers, practitioners, or people with a large following? If you can't identify them, the statement doesn't carry much weight.

Suspiciously neat statistics. "90 percent of people struggle with this" sounds like a proper statistic. But where did it come from? Who was surveyed? How many people took part? A number doesn't become evidence simply because it has a percentage sign.

The word "best." Best according to whom? Best for what purpose? And compared with which alternatives? Without some context, it's an empty label.

Like the Anchorage video where we couldn’t establish the object’s size, distance, or exact position, writing articles that include claims without backed-up data makes it difficult to investigate.

If a sentence can't answer at least the first three, it is probably a jellyfish.

A reader who spots an unsupported statement may start questioning the entire article. Even the claims you've carefully researched can lose credibility because of one sentence you couldn't verify.

The problem goes beyond individual writers, too. When there's no reliable information to establish what something is, people can attach almost any explanation to it. That's what happened with the Anchorage footage. Claims without evidence leave room for speculation, and speculation can spread quickly.

So, how do you catch these problems before publishing?

Start by reading your draft and highlighting every sentence that states a fact, gives a number, or explains why something happened. Then run each one through the four questions. For every one that fails, choose one of four moves.

Add the evidence. Give the number, the source, or the date.

Shrink the claim. Say only what you saw. "In my case" or "among the 12 people I asked" is honest and strong.

Label it as opinion. "I think" costs nothing and protects your credibility.

Cut it. If the article reads fine without the sentence, then take it out. Not every thought needs to make it to the final draft.

Before you hit publish, go back to the boldest claim in your article and ask yourself how much? Compared with what? Says who? When? If you have the answers, include them. If you don't, make the claim smaller, present it as an opinion, or take it out altogether. Whatever you do, don't leave your readers staring at another mysterious jellyfish.
