Falling Short In Life

We all fall short in life, and maybe that’s a good thing. Keeps us close to the Terra Firma.

Forgiveness of self and others, and the drive to press on in tough, wrenching times are coins of life. Pulitzer Prize-winning poet and journalist the late Carl Sandburg said years ago, “Time is the coin of life. It’s the only coin you have, and only you can determine how it will be spent.”

And that’s not pocket change. It’s a road map to success, though one that takes great persistence. I know firsthand from my life challenges. At 76 now, I’ve learned it’s not so much about the fall, but when and how you get back up—a humbling directive, though one that has helped me persevere through critical memory loss and advancing cancer, as others would know in their own trials.

I learned more about this recently in symbolic ways from my beautiful 40-year-old daughter Colleen, a writer and a teacher. November 16 was the 38th birthday of my late son Conor, who died several years ago from grand mal seizures and other issues. Conor traveled with me cross-country as a “guard dog,” as he called it, for speeches related to my book, On Pluto: Inside the Mind of Alzheimer’s. We were close, and in heavenly ways, closer now.

In a Facebook post on Conor’s birthday, shared also with son Brendan, Colleen—struggling through the emotion of this loss—wrote words that deeply touched my wife Mary Catherine and my soul—words that may touch you in losses in your lives.

Colleen wrote: “Happy Heavenly Birthday to my little brother, Conor, and my guardian angel. Your birthday is always especially hard for me because it makes me think of all the ‘could-haves’ and ‘should-be’s.’ But I feel you in the little things, the unexpected signs, and the moments that seem too coincidental to be anything else. I talk to you, I look for you, and I like to think you’re never far away.“Losing someone changes the way you look at life and the people around you. It reminds you that everyone is carrying something we may never see. That life is incredibly fragile, that tomorrow is never promised, and that we don’t always get another opportunity to say what we wish we had said. “I’ve learned how important it is to give people grace. To forgive, to let go of what weighs us down, to be kinder than we think we need to be, and to remember that none of us get through life without making mistakes. We don’t have to agree with everyone’s choices to show them compassion. We don’t have to understand someone’s struggles to remind them they’re not alone.“Make the phone call. Send the text. Forgive when your heart is ready. Ask a second time if someone says they’re okay, but something tells you otherwise. Tell people you love them while you have the chance.“I hope you’re proud of me, Conor. I hope you see how hard I’m trying. And I hope you know that no matter how much time passes, you will always be a part of me. Keep sending those signs. I’ll keep looking for them.“Love you, always, Conor. Until we meet again, Colleen.”

As I reflect on Colleen’s comments and Conor’s birthday, I am reminded again that it is crucial to rise from the falls of life. And I’m trying.
