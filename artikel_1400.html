Algal bloom demise, often caused by viruses, is a major driver of carbon cycling in the surface ocean1,2. However, although blooms collapse synchronously, fewer than 30% of algal cells are actively infected by viruses3, indicating the involvement of additional mortality agents. Here we quantified tripartite interactions within a synthetic community composed of the abundant coccolithophore Gephyrocapsa huxleyi, bacteria and viruses isolated during a bloom. We found that bacteria significantly enhanced virus-induced algal mortality in G. huxleyi as well as in three other globally widespread microalgae. We quantified this interaction using two new metrics: the synergy index, which measures viral–bacterial interaction strength, and the benefit index, which assesses changes in bacterial fitness. We identified Alteromonas bacteria as drivers of viral–bacterial co-infection, with consistently high synergy index and benefit index. We found that during algal blooms, Alteromonas displayed high chemoattraction towards infected cell extracts, accumulated in aggregates derived from bloom demise, and increased in abundance during oceanic bloom collapse. Viral–bacterial synergy represents a previously unrecognized mortality agent that controls bloom termination, modulating the balance between the carbon-recycling viral shunt and the carbon-exporting viral shuttle pathways, which influence global carbon cycling4,5,6.

About 50 gigatons of carbon are fixed annually by marine phototrophs, feeding the entire microbial food web7,8,9. A substantial portion of this carbon fixation occurs during large-scale algal blooms1,10. Therefore, microbial processes that control cell fate in these blooms can have profound effects on marine carbon cycling11. Algal blooms are hotspots for diverse interactions and metabolic exchange with grazers, bacteria, fungi and viruses12,13. Marine viruses, the most abundant biological entities in the ocean, are major mortality agents, driving nutrient cycling globally and significantly impacting algal bloom demise2,14,15.

Viruses regulate the fate of carbon and other nutrients16 through two distinct ecosystem processes: the viral shunt4,5, which fuels the microbial food web by catalysing nutrient recycling in the surface ocean; and the viral shuttle5,6, which facilitates carbon export through enhanced aggregation of lysed cells and sinking of particulate organic matter in the oligotrophic ocean17 and during algal blooms18. Viral lysis of algal populations creates a distinct metabolic footprint19,20,21, which can promote the growth of adapted heterotrophs22, thereby altering microbial biodiversity23,24. Yet, how virus-induced shifts in microbial community composition influence algal cell fate—and thus global carbon cycling—remains poorly understood.

We selected G. huxleyi (previously known as Emiliania huxleyi) to study algae–virus–bacteria interactions owing to its ecological significance in marine ecosystems. This cosmopolitan unicellular eukaryotic alga forms extensive blooms, covering thousands of square kilometers25. It has a crucial role in global carbon and sulfur biogeochemical cycles due to its carbon fixation, its calcite exoskeleton production, and the release of sulfur volatiles such as dimethyl sulfide25,26,27. These blooms are often terminated following infection by a giant double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) virus, the Emiliania huxleyi virus (EhV)28. Despite the observed synchronized collapse of entire blooms29, quantification of active viral infection using single-cell approaches revealed a discrepancy: fewer than 30% of algal cells were infected by the virus during the bloom’s demise3,30. This suggests that additional mortality agents may act together with viral infection. Inspired by findings from the biomedical field demonstrating that viral–bacterial co-infections synergistically increase mortality31,32, we investigated the interplay between bacteria and viruses during algal bloom demise. Using a bacterial consortium isolated from natural algal blooms (Extended Data Fig. 1), we showed that bacteria can exacerbate virus-induced algal mortality, revealing a previously unrecognized tripartite interaction with potentially important implications for global biogeochemical cycles.

We sought to determine the role of bacteria as additional mortality agents during virus-induced algal bloom demise. We hypothesized that if bacteria that are naturally associated with an alga contribute to its mortality, then an antibiotic treatment would reduce the overall death rate during viral infection. To test this idea, we cultured a xenic strain of G. huxleyi isolated from a bloom induced during a mesocosm experiment near Bergen, Norway24. In the absence of viral infection, the xenic cultures subjected to the antibiotic treatment showed up to 1.2-fold higher algal growth (linear mixed-effects model, P < 10−4; Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 2a), indicating that under normal growth conditions, bacteria naturally associated with G. huxleyi maintain a competitive relationship. Infection with EhV induced algal population decline from day 1 (Fig. 1a). However, when antibiotics were administered to reduce the abundance of the algal microbiome (Extended Data Fig. 2a), we observed reduced algal mortality, up to 19-fold, compared with cultures that were not treated with antibiotics (linear mixed-effects model, P < 0.05 from day 2; Fig. 1a). This difference suggests that the bacterial community may contribute significantly to algal mortality during viral infection, indicating a potential synergistic pathogenic interaction between viruses and bacteria. Analysis of bacterial abundance revealed that bacteria grew to 1.5-fold higher abundance in the virus-infected cultures compared with the uninfected condition (linear mixed-effects model, P < 10−4 from day 4; Fig. 1b). The differences in viral production between antibiotic-treated and untreated algal cultures were significant during the days of viral release, but the abundances leveled off resulting in similar total abundances (linear mixed-effects model, P > 0.05 on day 4 and day 6; Fig. 1c).

To determine the generality of our findings, we assessed whether such synergistic pathogenic interactions occur for other marine microalgae whose bloom demise is controlled by viral infections, namely Phaeocystis globosa, Micromonas commoda and Ostreococcus tauri33,34,35. The mortality of all three algae was significantly attenuated when treating virus-infected, xenic algal cultures with antibiotics (linear mixed-effects model, P < 10−4; Fig. 1d–f). These results demonstrate that viral–bacterial synergy leading to algal mortality is a widespread phenomenon among key marine algal species. Notably, antibiotic treatment did not reduce the mortality of O. tauri upon infection with a specialist virus (linear mixed-effects model, P > 0.05; Extended Data Fig. 2e), possibly owing to the rapid recovery of a resistant population. This exemplifies that the extent of viral–bacterial synergy may depend on various factors such as host susceptibility, viral infectivity and microbiome composition. We propose that bacteria and viruses kill synergistically, whereby viral infection creates favourable conditions for bacterial pathogenicity, accelerating algal mortality and fuelling bacterial growth. Thus, bacteria naturally associated with G. huxleyi and other cosmopolitan microalgae can expedite their bloom demise during viral infection.

We sought to disentangle the interactions between algae, viruses and bacteria during algal bloom demise. To this end, we developed a tripartite laboratory model system using algal, viral and bacterial strains that were isolated from an algal bloom (Extended Data Fig. 1). We isolated the bloom-associated microbiome (BAM) from the demise phase and determined its effect on G. huxleyi during viral infection.

We next reconstituted the tripartite system by exposing axenic G. huxleyi cultures to EhV with and without the BAM community, and observed an enhancement of algal mortality in the presence of bacteria (Fig. 2a). Although viral infection alone significantly reduced algal abundance compared with uninfected cultures, the addition of the BAM accelerated this decline, resulting in approximately fourfold lower algal cell densities after 7 days (linear mixed-effects model, P < 10−4; Fig. 2a). This recapitulated our observations in xenic G. huxleyi cultures (Fig. 1a), confirming that bacterial presence synergistically enhances virus-induced algal mortality.

Bacterial population dynamics revealed significant proliferation in the tripartite system compared with the bacteria-only treatment (Fig. 2b). Similar to xenic G. huxleyi cultures (Fig. 1b), bacterial abundances in the virus-infected cultures were more than threefold higher than in the uninfected cultures 7 days post-infection (linear mixed-effects model, P < 10−4 from day 2), suggesting that bacterial communities exploit metabolites that are released during viral infection.

By contrast, viral production remained similar between axenic and BAM-supplemented cultures (linear mixed-effects model, P > 0.05; Fig. 2c). Additionally, we quantified the effect of BAM supplementation on the fraction of virus-infected algal cells (Extended Data Fig. 3), enumerated at the single-cell level using single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH)3, based on the expression of the viral major capsid protein (mcp) gene. During the lytic phase, up to 70% of algal cells were virus-infected independent of the presence or absence of BAM (linear mixed-effects model, P > 0.05; Extended Data Fig. 3d), proportions consistent with previous work with infected G. huxleyi cultures3. These findings suggest that the bacterial consortium augments algal death without affecting viral infection.

To identify whether specific bacteria are driving the synergy of this tripartite interaction, we characterized changes in microbial community composition during viral infection using 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing (Fig. 2d). The bacterial community changed reproducibly in all replicates during viral infection. Whereas the uninfected cultures maintained a stable community dominated by Marinomonas, a pronounced shift occurred in the virus-infected cultures by day 4, coinciding with algal decline. Notably, the relative abundances of Alteromonas and Olleya increased in the infected cultures, from approximately 2.1 ± 0.3% and 0.01 ± 0.01%, respectively, at the beginning of the experiment (day 1), to 20.2 ± 2.7% and 32.9 ± 4.4% by day 7, thereby dominating the bacterial community (Fig. 2d). Concurrently, Pseudoalteromonas and Yoonia-Loktanella increased in their relative abundances, whereas Marinomonas declined. These findings suggest that viral infection creates a distinct metabolic niche that selectively promotes specific bacterial taxa that are capable of exploiting virus-induced changes in algal metabolism or cell lysis products, as previously shown24,36.

To disentangle the microbial dynamics governing algal bloom demise, we developed two quantitative metrics that assess the contribution of individual bacterial taxa to virus-induced algal mortality, and the effect of viral infection of algal cells on bacterial fitness. The synergy index (SI) quantifies the decrease in the algal population during viral infection in the presence of bacteria compared to viral infection alone. The benefit index (BI) measures the growth advantage that bacteria gain during viral infection compared to their growth with uninfected algae.

To calculate the SI, we use the area under the algal growth curve during viral infection alone (AUCvirus) and during viral–bacterial co-infection (AUCtripartite). We selected area under the curve (AUC) as our metric because it provides a biologically meaningful measure of cumulative biomass change that captures complete temporal dynamics rather than single time points and offers robustness through multiple daily measurements. We calculated the difference between AUCvirus and AUCtripartite and normalized this value using AUCvirus, which is represented mathematically as SI (%) = (AUCvirus − AUCtripartite)/AUCvirus × 100% (Fig. 3a and Methods). Normalizing the differences in the AUC creates a dimensionless metric, enabling direct comparison across bacterial strains and experimental conditions regardless of baseline differences. A positive SI indicates that the bacterial presence reduces algal populations beyond the reduction caused by viruses alone, with higher values reflecting stronger effects (for example, SI = 10.0% means a 10% increase in tripartite-induced algal decline compared to mortality induced by viruses alone). The BAM had an SI of 22.2 ± 2.3%, which confirms the substantial effect of this bacterial community on the virus-induced demise of G. huxleyi (Fig. 3b).

The BI quantifies the growth advantage bacteria derive from viral infection, calculated as BI (%) = (AUCtripartite − AUCbacteria)/AUCbacteria × 100%, where AUCbacteria represents the area under the bacterial growth curve with uninfected algae (Fig. 3e). The BAM community exhibited a high BI (259.8 ± 17.0%), indicating that viral infection of G. huxleyi creates a metabolically accessible niche that enhances bacterial proliferation almost fourfold (Fig. 3f).

To identify key contributors to this synergistic interaction, we examined the SI and BI for individual bacteria isolated from the BAM (Supplementary Data 1). Alteromonas stellipolaris MB63 exhibited a high SI (20.2 ± 2.7%), indicating that this species enhanced virus-induced algal population decline by about 20% (Fig. 3c). By contrast, Sulfitobacter pontiacus MB3 had a low, non-significant SI (7.8 ± 2.7%), suggesting that this species is not affecting algal death during viral infection (Fig. 3d). Correspondingly, A. stellipolaris MB63 exhibited a high BI (112.4 ± 1.0%), reflecting more than twofold growth enhancement during viral infection (Fig. 3g), whereas S. pontiacus MB3 showed a slight growth deficit during virus infection (BI = −17.2 ± 3.0%; Fig. 3h).

To expand the taxonomic range of our analysis, we tested a total of 12 bacteria isolated either directly from the BAM community or from the G. huxleyi bloom (Supplementary Data 1), and observed high variability in both metrics (SI range from 5.4% to 23.2% and BI range from −17.2% to 451.0%; Extended Data Figs. 4 and 5). The three Alteromonas strains tested (MB63, MB67 and MB68) consistently exhibited a large effect on algal population decline (SI range from 18.6% to 21.7%; Fig. 3i), while simultaneously deriving growth benefits from viral infection (BI range from 18.5% to 112.4%; Fig. 3i).

By mapping all bacterial isolates onto a coordinate system defined by their SI and BI values (the SI–BI phase space), we observed distinct phenotypes (Fig. 3j) and propose a framework comprising four ecological strategies: ‘opportunistic pathogens’ (high SI, high BI), exemplified by Alteromonas species, which both accelerate algal mortality and efficiently exploit the resulting metabolic niche; ‘consumers’ (low SI, high BI), such as Olleya sp. MB6, which derive substantial growth benefits from viral infection without contributing significantly to algal mortality; ‘killers’ (high SI, low BI), including Sulfitobacter sp. MB51, which enhance algal mortality but gain no growth advantage; and ‘agnostics’ (low SI, low BI), such as S. pontiacus MB3, which neither increase algal mortality nor derive substantial growth benefits from viral infection.

To determine whether synergistic bacteria directly affect the mortality of algal cells, we quantified the fraction of dying algal cells during tripartite interaction (Fig. 4a,b and Extended Data Fig. 3). Dying algal cells were identified as cells with compromised membranes using the DNA stain SYTOX Green (Fig. 4a), and were further differentiated by their chlorophyll autofluorescence as metabolically active (high chlorophyll autofluorescence) or metabolically impaired (low chlorophyll autofluorescence) cells (Fig. 4b). During viral infection, there was an accumulation of dying (SYTOX+) cells reaching up to 76.9 ± 2.4% of the algal population (on day 4; Fig. 4a). The addition of bacteria significantly reduced the percentage of dying algal cells (linear mixed-effects model, P < 10−4) reaching 21.0 ± 1.5% (on day 3; Fig. 4a), suggesting that bacteria selectively remove dying algae during viral infection. Bacteria selectively cleared low-chlorophyll cells versus high-chlorophyll cells. In the presence of bacteria, the percentage of SYTOX+ cells among all low-chlorophyll cells decreased from 54.0 ± 4.1% to 30.3 ± 3.0% on day 2 (linear mixed-effects model, P < 10−4; Fig. 4b), whereas the percentage of SYTOX+ cells among high-chlorophyll cells remained similar (linear mixed-effects model, P > 0.05; Fig. 4b). These findings show that bacteria preferentially remove metabolically impaired dying cells during virus-induced algal demise.

We sought to determine the metabolic basis for viral–bacterial synergy in algal killing. We focused on lipid metabolism since EhV infection profoundly remodels the lipidome of G. huxleyi, inducing triacylglycerol (TAG) production and accumulation of intracellular lipid bodies37. We thus sequenced and annotated the genomes of all bacterial isolates and correlated the SI of each one with the completeness (per cent of genes encoded in the KEGG pathway) of its fatty acid degradation (FAD) pathway. We found a significant positive correlation between SI and the FAD pathway completeness (Spearman’s ρ = 0.669, P = 0.017; Extended Data Fig. 6a and Supplementary Data 2). Detailed examination revealed that within the BAM consortium, the species with the highest SI values, all belonging to the genus Alteromonas, also encode the complete enzymatic machinery for TAG hydrolysis and subsequent fatty acid transmembranal transport and β-oxidation, including TAG lipase, fadL, fadD, fadE, fadB and fadA and their homologues (Extended Data Fig. 6b). By contrast, species with low SI values (for example, S. pontiacus MB3) showed several missing enzymes in the FAD pathway. Among bloom-associated bacterial isolates, only Alteromonas and Marinobacter strains encode these metabolic genes (Supplementary Data 2). Although this may be a phylogenetic trait that is not causally related to SI or BI, these genera were recently shown to selectively degrade algal lipids38. Most importantly, Alteromonas encodes a TAG lipase, an enzyme that releases free fatty acids from TAGs for cellular metabolism. This feature may be particularly advantageous during viral infection of G. huxleyi as EhV infection induces the release of extracellular vesicles that are enriched in TAGs39.

Having identified Alteromonas as having key roles in the viral–bacterial synergy during algal demise, we investigated the ecological importance of this genus during natural bloom succession. To this end, we tracked algal, viral and bacterial dynamics in a natural environment, measured daily during G. huxleyi bloom succession in a mesocosm experiment24. Following six days at low abundance (less than 5 × 105 cells per ml; pre-bloom phase), G. huxleyi showed a steady increase in cell numbers (days 7–14; bloom phase) and, upon viral infection (days 15–23; infection phase), its population collapsed (Fig. 5a).

We used 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing to track bacterial abundance in two size fractions to distinguish between planktonic (0.2–2 μm) and particle-attached (20–200 μm) populations (Fig. 5b and Extended Data Fig. 7). We observed significantly higher relative abundance of particle-attached Alteromonadaceae in all bloom phases (Kruskal–Wallis test, P < 0.05; Extended Data Fig. 8a). During the onset of viral infection and subsequent bloom demise (days 15–23), Alteromonadaceae became on average sixfold enriched in the particle-attached fraction (Welch’s t-test; P = 0.088; Extended Data Fig. 8b) compared with the uninfected bloom phase, suggesting that Alteromonadaceae specifically colonize algal aggregates generated during infection. Viral infection of G. huxleyi blooms enhances the production of transparent exopolymer particles up to 4.5-fold (ref. 24), which catalyse aggregate formation18,40 and are likely to provide a unique ecological niche for colonizing bacteria.

The dynamics of other bacterial families were markedly different from Alteromonadaceae during the G. huxleyi bloom (Fig. 5b). Rhodobacteraceae were more abundant in the planktonic fraction than in the particle-attached fraction during the pre-bloom and bloom phases (Kruskal–Wallis test; P < 0.05; Extended Data Fig. 8c), with an increase in the particle-attached fraction throughout the phytoplankton bloom succession (Extended Data Fig. 8d). Pseudoalteromonadaceae exhibited an increase in relative abundance in both planktonic and particle-attached fractions during the pre-bloom phase followed by a decrease during the bloom phase (Fig. 5b), suggesting that these bacteria inhabit a different ecological niche.

To further investigate whether Alteromonadaceae actively seek out virus-infected cells during the bloom and demise of G. huxleyi (Fig. 5c), we deployed the in situ chemotaxis assay (ISCA)41,42 at multiple times during bloom succession. The ISCA is a microfluidic device containing an array of micro-wells that are filled with different chemicals. Here we loaded the ISCA wells with cell extracts from virus-infected or uninfected laboratory G. huxleyi cultures, as well as filtered seawater from the mesocosm acting as a reference. We then deployed the ISCAs in the mesocosm water, allowing the chemical extracts to diffuse out of the wells, which generate concentration gradients that can attract chemotactic bloom-associated bacteria into the wells. We then characterized the composition of the chemotactic bacterial communities in the ISCAs using 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing (Fig. 5d).

Alteromonadaceae were enriched in ISCA wells containing extracts from both infected and control G. huxleyi during the peak of viral infection in the demise phase of the bloom (days 18 and 20), accounting for nearly half (45.3 ± 6.3%) of all chemotactic bacteria in the infected wells on day 18 (Fig. 5d). Notably, Alteromonadaceae, including Alteromonas (Extended Data Fig. 9), were significantly enriched in the ISCA wells containing virus-infected G. huxleyi cell extract compared with a seawater reference (day 18; Welch’s ANOVA, P = 0.008; Fig. 5d), suggesting they are attracted by the algal metabolites. On average, Alteromonadaceae were 19-fold enriched in infected ISCA wells (13.1 ± 19.6%) compared to their planktonic abundance across the bloom succession (0.7 ± 0.7%). This pattern contrasts with other bacterial families, including those whose abundance in the bloom was higher. For example, Rhodobacteraceae accounted for 18.3 ± 4.8% of planktonic bacteria, which is 26 times higher than the proportion of Alteromonadaceae during the overall bloom succession (Fig. 5b) but had a 20 times lower relative abundance in the infected ISCA wells (0.9 ± 1.0%; Fig. 5d). Pseudoalteromonadaceae were also enriched in ISCA wells containing G. huxleyi extracts but primarily during the pre-bloom and bloom phases (days 3, 9 and 12), accounting for up to 44.9 ± 31.4%, rather than during the viral infection phase at which Pseudoalteromonadaceae accounted for only 5.2 ± 6.6% in control and 3.6 ± 1.3% in infected ISCA wells (days 18 and 20; Fig. 5d), which is consistent with their higher relative abundance during these early bloom phases (Fig. 5b).

We next isolated seven Alteromonas strains directly from the ISCA wells (Supplementary Data 1) to quantify their selective chemoattraction to infected algae under controlled laboratory conditions (Extended Data Fig. 10). Five out of the seven Alteromonas isolates showed a significantly stronger chemoattraction towards virus-infected cell extracts versus extracts from uninfected cells (Welch’s t-test, P < 0.05; Fig. 5e and Extended Data Fig. 10). One of the two strains that were isolated from ISCA wells containing uninfected G. huxleyi extracts, namely Marinomonas sp. 3g5, showed a preference for the uninfected extract.

To determine whether Alteromonas are also enriched during natural bloom demise, we analysed data collected from two cruises in the North Atlantic that examined the ecological and biogeochemical effects of viral infections on coccolithophore blooms. These blooms were sampled during the NA-VICE cruise in 2012, with large spatial coverage18, and the Tara Breizh cruise in 2019, with high temporal resolution43 (Extended Data Fig. 11). We found a significant enrichment of Alteromonas relative abundance in the NA-VICE cruise blooms compared with its abundance across global Tara Oceans data44 (two-sided Mann–Whitney U test, P < 10−4). We next examined the data from the Tara Breizh cruise (Fig. 5f), which tracked natural bloom dynamics over five consecutive days. These data only allowed us to resolve taxa to the family level, and we used it to establish whether Alteromonadaceae increases in abundance in virus-infected blooms. Using data on calcified G. huxleyi cells and intracellular EhV abundance, as well as remote-sensing data, we established that the sampling occurred during early bloom demise21. During these 5 days, we found a more than 100-fold increase in relative abundance of Alteromonadaceae (Fig. 5g). By contrast, Rhodobacteraceae, although higher in relative abundance, did not increase during bloom demise (Fig. 5g). Pseudoalteromonadaceae had a higher relative abundance compared with Tara Oceans data, but increased only slightly in abundance during bloom demise (Fig. 5g). Together, these results show that Alteromonadaceae and Alteromonas inhabit the ecological niche of virus-infected, demising coccolithophore blooms.

The viral shunt is a key ecological process that recycles photosynthetic biomass generated in the sunlit surface ocean, providing nutrients and metabolites that fuel the marine microbiome. Our findings demonstrate that this process can selectively reshape the bloom microbiome and thereby expedite bloom demise. This viral–bacterial synergy helps to explain why entire G. huxleyi blooms collapse synchronously despite fewer than 30% of the cells being actively infected by EhV3,30. Viral infection may therefore be a trigger for additional pathogens expediting bloom demise.

Here we introduced a new experimental framework for studying complex microbial interactions. By generating synthetic communities composed of algae, bacteria and viruses isolated from algal blooms, we experimentally quantified their tripartite interaction dynamics. To this end, we developed two novel quantitative indices: the SI, measuring killing interaction strength, and the BI, assessing bacterial fitness gain. We measured more than 20% increase in virus-induced algal mortality in the presence of the BAM compared with viral infection alone, and an increase of more than 250% in bacterial benefit (Fig. 3).

This quantitative approach enabled us to disentangle these complex natural microbial communities in the laboratory and identify key players in algal bloom demise. We identified Alteromonas as major drivers of this globally important process. This cosmopolitan genus exhibits remarkable lifestyle plasticity45, including pathogenicity46,47 and chemoattraction towards photosynthetic microorganisms48. Using our quantitative framework, we showed that these bacteria become opportunistic pathogens during viral infection of G. huxleyi, exhibiting high killing interaction strength (SI; Fig. 3) and fitness gain (BI; Fig. 3). In addition, Alteromonas were chemoattracted towards infected cell extracts, accumulated in particles derived from bloom demise, and increased in abundance during coccolithophore bloom demise in the open ocean (Fig. 5). Thus, Alteromonas, despite its low abundance in healthy algal-associated populations, is a keystone bacterial genus adapted to virus-induced algal bloom demise.

Viral infection of G. huxleyi rewires its metabolism49 characterized by specific halogenated metabolites20 and lipids50,51 and enrichment—for example, in TAGs37 that may be consumed by Alteromonas (Extended Data Fig. 6b). We propose that the ephemeral nature of the release of such unique and nutritious metabolites during virus-induced bloom demise selectively recruits bacterial taxa adapted to consume these metabolites (as was shown in other algal–bacterial interactions52). Viral infection also stimulates the production of transparent exopolymer particles, which increases cell stickiness and facilitates bacterial adherence18,24,40. We showed that Alteromonas is significantly enriched in particle-associated microbial communities during algal bloom demise (Fig. 5b), highlighting its specialization in the viral shunt niche.

We complemented the laboratory model for viral–bacterial synergy with an ISCA, which enabled us to quantify bacterial behaviour directly in the environment at different phases of bloom succession. We tailored this experiment specifically to detect, isolate and characterize bacteria that were attracted to virus-infected G. huxleyi cell extracts. During bloom demise, Alteromonas was attracted towards the chemicals released by infected G. huxleyi cells (Fig. 5d), which probably led to the concomitant increase in particle association (Fig. 5b). Indeed, Alteromonas were previously shown to exploit adhesive algal surfaces for colonization53.

Our results demonstrate viral–bacterial synergy at the community level, raising questions regarding the specific cellular mechanisms that drive both the synergistic killing (SI) and the associated bacterial fitness benefits (BI). We propose four non-mutually exclusive scenarios that may mediate this phenomenon. First, viral infection may trigger the release of infochemicals that induce bacterial pathogenicity towards both infected and uninfected algal cells, thereby increasing mortality beyond the virus-infected population. Second, bacterial metabolites or signalling molecules produced during viral infection may interfere with algal cell division, reducing population growth rates even in the absence of direct cell lysis. Third, bacteria that proliferate by exploiting viral lysate may competitively exclude algal cells for essential nutrients, creating resource limitations that reduce algal growth. Fourth, bacteria may accelerate the death of virus-infected algae by efficiently metabolizing essential cellular components such as membrane lipids and sugars, thereby compromising cellular integrity. In support of this fourth scenario, we showed that bacteria selectively remove dying algal cells thereby accelerating their demise (Fig. 4). Future mechanistic studies will be essential to distinguish between these scenarios and identify the precise molecular drivers of this ecologically significant tripartite interaction.

To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of how co-infection by two agents—viruses and bacteria—can act in synergy in algal bloom demise, akin to co-infections that are major mortality agents in human diseases31,32. Although bacterial benefits resulting from viral infection of marine bloom-forming algae have been reported previously36,54,55, there is a lack of observations of viral infection-induced bacterial pathogenicity. Bacterial pathogenicity has been shown to depend on contextual parameters such as temperature56 and the growth phase of the algal host57,58. Specifically, aged and senescent algal cells can induce bacterial pathogens46,59 via the release of specific algal metabolites and reactive oxygen species60,61, thereby accelerating death in the algal population.

Here we introduce viral infection as a major environmental factor that may be the trigger of bacterial pathogenicity driving bloom demise. We show that these algal–viral–bacterial interactions occur across four cosmopolitan algal species (Fig. 1). For G. huxleyi, we provided insights into cellular mechanisms (Fig. 4), revealed chemotaxis as an important bacterial trait and found evidence in an oceanic bloom (Fig. 5). For the three additional algal species, further studies are required to assess the effect of algal–viral–bacterial interactions on their bloom termination, and to resolve the mechanism underlying these viral–bacterial co-infections. We thus propose that these tripartite interactions have profound biogeochemical implications. Viral–bacterial synergy (SI) expedites bloom demise, which is likely to reduce the amount of carbon that is fixed during algal blooms. Bacterial growth due to algal bloom demise (BI) can affect the balance between the viral shunt (increasing the recycling of carbon in the ocean surface, reflected in a high BI) and the viral shuttle (exporting carbon to ocean depths via sinking particles). Deciphering the nature of alga–virus–bacteria interactions is critical given the role of large-scale oceanic blooms in carbon and sulfur cycling as well as climate regulation.

Chloroplast reads accounting for 7 ± 8% (minimum 1% and maximum 36%) in the particle-attached fraction were removed. Kruskal–Wallis tests were performed to compare planktonic and particle-attached bacterial fractions for each family and bloom phase (pre-bloom, days 1–6; bloom, days 7–14; and viral infection phase, days 15–23). Pairwise Welch’s t-tests with the Holm–Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons were performed comparing the log2-transformed fold change of particle-attached planktonic bacterial fractions between the bloom phases. For missing samples (day 2 of the planktonic and day 1 of the particle-attached size fraction), the relative abundances were interpolated. Absence of Pseudoalteromonadaceae in some planktonic samples prevented log2 fold change computation.

To prepare cell extracts as chemoattractants, G. huxleyi CCMP 2090 was grown in 19 l modified K/2 medium in 2 carboys with stirring and aeration. One exponentially growing culture (1.2 × 106 cells per ml) was infected with EhV 201 at an MOP of 1:1 while the other culture served as an uninfected control. The viruses were propagated on CCMP 2090, washed and concentrated on 100 kDa Amicon-ultra filters (EMD Millipore), and stored at 4 °C until use within two months. Cells were counted daily using a Multisizer 4 Coulter Counter (Beckman Coulter). After 48 h, the uninfected (4.4 × 106 cells per ml) and infected (0.9 × 106 cells per ml) cultures were filtered onto pre-combusted GF/C filters (47 mm; Whatman) using gentle vacuum (600 mbar under-pressure). Filters were immediately transferred to 500 ml pre-cooled (−20 °C) methanol (HPLC grade, J.T. Baker) and extracted overnight at 4 °C while shaking. Extracts were sonicated for 30 min and filtered through pre-combusted GF/C filters before being evaporated to 20 ml using a Rotovap (30 °C, 70–100 rpm). Extracts were centrifuged at 20,800g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were evaporated to complete dryness using a SpeedVac (28 °C, 1,980 rpm). Dried extracts were re-dissolved in methanol to reduce the salt load, aliquoted into eight centrifuge tubes, dried using a SpeedVac, and stored at −80 °C until further use. The dry weight was 57.5 ± 0.3 mg and 38.3 ± 0.5 mg for the uninfected and infected extracts, respectively.

In situ chemotaxis assays (ISCAs) containing the G. huxleyi cell extracts were deployed five times throughout the mesocosm bloom succession, namely at days 3, 9, 12, 18 and 20. For each deployment, 12.5 ml of bloom water were collected per mesocosm bag (1–4; 50 ml in total), mixed and subjected to a quadruple-filtration process. Specifically, the mixed bloom water was first filtered through a 0.2-μm Millex FG (Millipore) to remove large particles, twice through a 0.2-μm Sterivex filter (Millipore), and finally through a 0.02-μm Anatop filter (Whatman). This quadruple filtration aimed to remove all microorganisms from the seawater. We confirmed the efficiency of the filtration procedure using flow cytometry and DNA sequencing78. The filtered bloom water was used as a control in the ISCA and was also used to resuspend the dried G. huxleyi extracts at a final concentration of 1 mg ml−1. The resuspended extracts were filtered through a 0.2-μm Millex FG (Millipore) to remove undissolved particles.

The working concentration of G. huxleyi extracts (1 mg ml−1 of dry weight) is representative of environmental hotspots. Indeed, chemical concentrations decay exponentially with distance away from the ISCA opening (port)41. This means that concentrations sensed by bacteria in the surrounding bloom water will be substantially lower than those in the ISCA wells and within the same order of magnitude as the concentrations arising from the lysis of a phytoplankton cell79.

Treatments (filtered bloom water and the two G. huxleyi cell extracts) were randomly allocated to an ISCA row (consisting of 5 wells with 110 μl each). All wells in a row contained the same treatment, and each treatment was replicated on three different ISCA devices, which were deployed in parallel to act as biological replicates (n = 3). Each ISCA was placed into a flow-damping acrylic enclosure, following a pre-established protocol80. Each enclosure was filled with water coming from the first four mesocosm bags (bags 1–4), previously mixed at equal volume. The flow-damping enclosures were filled slowly with water from the inlet in the lower surface and were sealed with a plug once full. The enclosures were then incubated in situ at 1 m depth for 2 h (ref. 78). Following deployments, enclosures were retrieved and slowly drained. The volume of each ISCA well was retrieved using a sterile 1 ml syringe and 27G needle.

ISCA samples for DNA extraction were snap-frozen immediately. DNA extraction was performed using a microvolume extraction approach (physical lysis method81). Libraries for 16S rRNA gene sequencing were generated for all samples using the Nextera XT DNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina) following a previously described protocol for low-input DNA libraries82. Primers targeting the V4–V5 regions of the 16S rRNA gene were used, namely 515F and 926R (CCGYCAATTYMTTTRAGTTT65). Samples were then sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform with a paired-end 2× 300 bp output. Libraries were pooled on an indexed sequencing run and yielded ~300 bp per sample. The composition of the chemotactic bacterial communities was analysed as described above using the DADA2 pipeline, and ASVs were annotated with the SILVA_nr99 reference database. For day 18, a Welch’s ANOVA followed by two-sided pairwise t-tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction were performed to compare the fraction of Alteromonadaceae across chemoattractants using the R ‘stats’ package (v4.5.1; Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene test were performed to assure normality and homogeneity of variances).

To isolate chemotactic bacteria throughout the succession of the induced G. huxleyi bloom, we prepared agar plates made with 0.22-μm-filtered environmental bloom water and amended with 1 mg ml−1 of the corresponding G. huxleyi cell extracts. The agar plates were inoculated with the chemotactic bacterial community from the ISCA wells containing uninfected and virus-infected G. huxleyi extracts and incubated at fjord water temperature (10 °C) until colonies appeared. Individual colonies were then grown in 10% MB in autoclaved seawater overnight at room temperature and at 10 °C. Each isolate went through two additional rounds of purification via successive growth on plates and in liquid cultures. Isolates were identified by 16S rRNA gene Sanger sequencing78. Selected strains (Supplementary Data 1) were further used for genome sequencing and characterization of their chemotaxis towards different G. huxleyi cell extracts.

For genome sequencing, overnight cultures were inoculated in MB medium at 0.1 OD600. Exponentially growing cultures at 1.0 OD600 were centrifuged, and cell pellets of 15–30 mg were re-dissolved in 0.5 ml Zymo DNA/RNA Shield. Genomic DNA extraction and Nanopore sequencing were performed by Plasmidsaurus following their standard bacterial genome sequencing service. Assembled genome sequences were classified at the species level using the GTDB-Tk software toolkit.

For laboratory-based chemotaxis assays, the bacteria were plated from cryostocks on MA. Multiple colonies from each strain were inoculated in 1% MB in artificial seawater (33 g l−1 Instant Ocean, Aquarium Systems) and incubated at 180 rpm and 27 °C for 4 h to promote bacterial motility. An aliquot was fixed with glutaraldehyde (1% final concentration; Sigma Aldrich), stained for 15 min with SYBR Green I (ThermoFisher), and bacterial abundance enumerated using a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) as described above. Chemotaxis was characterized using ISCA devices that were each loaded with four different 0.2-μm-filtered treatments and incubated at room temperature for 1 h in artificial seawater inoculated with 106 bacteria per ml. As chemoattractants, we used: (1) extract from uninfected G. huxleyi CCMP 2090 cultures (1 mg ml−1 in artificial seawater); (2) extract from virus-infected G. huxleyi CCMP 2090 cultures (1 mg ml−1 in artificial seawater); (3) 10% MB (acting as a positive control), and artificial seawater (acting as a negative control to account for cells entering wells by random motility). Chemoattractants were inoculated in ISCA wells using a sterile 1 ml syringe (Codan Medical ApS) and needle (27 G, BD Microlance). In each ISCA, each row of five wells served as technical replicates for an individual treatment. Each chemoattractant was deployed simultaneously in four ISCA replicates. After incubation, contents of the ISCA wells were recovered using a sterile syringe and needle, technical replicates were pooled80, and 200 µl per sample were fixed, stained and enumerated using flow cytometry. Chemotactic indices (Ic) were determined as ratios of the cell concentrations in treatment wells relative to the average artificial seawater control. For statistical analysis, Ic values were log1p-transformed (ln(1 + x)) and the Levene test of homogeneity of variances and Shapiro–Wilk test of normality were performed using R. All bacteria showed significant differences between the artificial seawater control and the treatments, including the MB control (Dunnett’s test with P < 0.001). Differences between the two cell extracts were assessed using a two-sided Welch’s t-test with the Holm–Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. For fold change comparison, the transformed Ic values were further log2-transformed, the normality per strain and treatment validated using a Shapiro–Wilk test, and treatment differences per strain assessed using a two-sided Welch’s t-test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons. The log2-transformed fold changes (LFC) between the infected and control extracts were computed as LFC = mean(log(Inf)) − mean(log(Ctl)) with s.e.m. = √ (VarInf/nInf + VarCtl/nCtl).

Coccolithophore blooms were sampled in the North Atlantic during the NA-VICE cruise in 2012 with large spatial coverage18 and the Tara Breizh cruise in 2019 with high temporal resolution43.

During the NA-VICE cruise, coccolithophore blooms across a 2,000-mile transect between the Azores and Iceland were identified via satellite remote sensing and sampled at 30 stations onboard the RV Knorr between 13 June and 16 July 2012, as previously described18. Samples for DNA extraction and 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing were collected between the surface and 150 m depth with Niskin bottles, and filtered through a 200-μm prefilter onto 0.8-µm polycarbonate filters. Genomic DNA was extracted and amplified in a dual PCR strategy using primers targeting the V1–V3 region of the 16S rRNA gene83 linked to adaptors, namely CS1_27F_YM (ACACTGACGACATGGTTCTACA-AGAGTTTGATYMTGGCTCAG) and CS2_534R_Y (TACGGTAGCAGAGACTTGGTCT-TYACCGCGGCTGCTGG), and subsequently linked to unique sample barcodes for multiplexing. For PCR amplification, the AccuPrime SuperMix II (Life Technologies) was used using the following cycling conditions: denaturation for 5 min at 95 °C, 28 cycles of 30 s at 95 °C, 30 s at 56 °C and 5 min at 68 °C, and final 7 min elongation at 68 °C. The PCR amplicon quality was validated by gel electrophoresis. In a second PCR step, sample barcodes were amplified with the following cycling conditions: 5 min at 95 °C, 8 cycles of 30 s at 95 °C, 30 s at 60 °C and 30 s at 68 °C, and final 7 min elongation at 68 °C. Samples were pooled and purified using AMPure XP beads, and concentrations were estimated using Qubit. Sequencing was performed using a paired-end 2× 300 bp Illumina MiSeq technology at the DNA Services Facility, University of Illinois at Chicago.

During the Tara Breizh cruise, a G. huxleyi bloom patch in the Celtic Sea was identified by satellite remote sensing and sampled onboard the SV Tara between 27 May and 2 June 2019, as previously described21,43. Samples for DNA extraction and sequencing were collected at the maximum turbidity depth (bloom depth) by pumping seawater through a 20-μm prefilter and sequential filtration onto 3-μm and 0.2-μm PC filters. Following DNA extraction, amplicon sequencing of the V4–V5 region of the 16S rRNA gene was performed twice using Illumina MiSeq paired-end 2× 250 bp sequencing technology. Fastq files were obtained from the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA: PRJEB50692). Intracellular virus abundances were quantified from DNA extracts of 3-µm filters using droplet digital PCR and specific primers for the mcp gene of EhV. Samples for flow cytometry were collected at the bloom depth with a Niskin bottle and immediately analysed using a BD Accuri C6 Plus Personal Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences). Cell abundances of calcified G. huxleyi were determined by gating high chlorophyll (excitation: 488 nm; emission: 663–737 nm) and high SSC (488 nm) particles.

All paired-end 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequences were processed using QIIME 284. ASVs were inferred using the DADA2 paired-end denoising workflow, with trimming and truncation parameters adjusted according to amplicon type. For the NA-VICE samples (V1–V3 amplicons), 20 bp were trimmed from the 5′ ends of both forward and reverse reads, leaving 280 bp (forward) and 230 bp (reverse). Only samples that retained at least 10,000 non-chimeric reads after denoising were considered for downstream analysis. Taxonomic assignment was performed on representative sequences from DADA2, using the QIIME 2 classify-sklearn classifier with the SILVA v138 reference database. ASV tables were aggregated to the genus level, and relative abundances were calculated from the full dataset. The Tara Breizh data (V4–V5 amplicons) were analysed using QIIME 2, as previously described43 with the following modifications. After removal of empty reads, the demultiplexed read pairs were repaired using SeqKit285 (v2.3.1). Primer and orientation handling was performed with CutAdapt (v4.2) in two branches per sample, where each branch represents a primer-orientation-specific read set corresponding to one of the two detected amplicon orientations: one in the original R1/R2 orientation and one in the swapped R2/R1 orientation. Reads lacking the primer sequences, that were <75 bp, and had insertions or deletions were removed. For each sequencing run and orientation branch, denoising was done using QIIME 2 DADA2 with pseudo-pooling. Reads were truncated to 215 bp (forward) and 190 bp (reverse) for the first branch, and to 190 bp (forward) and 215 bp (reverse) for the second branch. Before merging, sequences of the second branch were reverse-complemented. ASV identifiers were normalized by sequence content and feature tables merged by summing identical ASV sequences across the sequencing runs and branches. Chimeras were removed using the de novo UCHIME algorithm of vsearch86 (v2.22.1) and non-chimeric ASV counts of the sequencing duplicates were summed. Counts were summed at the ASV level before conversion to relative abundance. Taxonomy was assigned in QIIME 2 using the SILVA v138 99% Naive Bayes classifier. ASV tables were aggregated to the genus level and relative abundance computed. For Fig. 5, a pseudocount of 1 × 10−5 was assigned to samples with relative abundance of exactly ‘0’. A map of the sample locations was created using the cartopy (v0.25.0) package in python (v3.10) with the Natural Earth map data.

To compare relative bacterial abundances from the North Atlantic coccolithophore blooms with diverse oceanic environments, taxonomic profiles from Tara Oceans44 were obtained (https://www.ocean-microbiome.org/). Per sample, relative abundance at the genus and family levels was calculated by dividing read counts for each taxon by the total bacterial and archaeal reads in the corresponding sample. Only samples representing the planktonic bacterial fraction (0.2 to 3 µm and 0.2 to 1.6 µm filter size fraction) were selected. NA-VICE samples were filtered by collection method to keep only cast samples and omit trap samples, while for Tara Breizh all samples labelled as outside the bloom were excluded. Pairwise comparisons of relative abundance distributions were performed at matched taxonomic level and depth range. For the Tara Oceans and NA-VICE comparison, we compared the Alteromonas genus from surface to 150 m depth. Due to limited sequencing depth, which affected our ability to reliably detect Alteromonas, the Tara Oceans and Tara Breizh comparison was performed for the Alteromonadaceae family from surface to 25 m depth. Differences between groups were evaluated using a two-sided Mann–Whitney U test. For Extended Data Fig. 11, a pseudocount of 1 × 10−5 was assigned to samples with relative abundance of exactly ‘0’. A map of the sample locations was created using the cartopy (v0.25.0) package in Python (v3.10) with the Natural Earth map data.

Single-cell analysis of viral gene expression during G. huxleyi bloom demise revealed that at most 30% of the population was infected with the virus3,30. The residual cell mortality supporting a synchronized algal bloom demise might be linked to induced pathogenicity within the algal bloom microbiome. To study alga-virus-bacteria interactions, we isolated G. huxleyi, its virus, and the bloom-associated microbiome (BAM) from the G. huxleyi bloom and demise phases. We then followed the dynamics of algal cultures following individual and co-infections with the virus and the BAM. Individual BAM-members were isolated, characterized regarding their enhancement of the algal population demise, and their metabolic niche was evaluated via functional pathway analysis.
