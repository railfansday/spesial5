European Commission scrambles to contain AI-assisted cyberattack

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Staff HR and medical reimbursement systems taken offline as officials investigate whether personal data was compromised

The European Commission is picking up the pieces after hackers wielding an AI-powered security testing tool targeted its internal IT systems, potentially exposing staff members' personal data.

The attack, first reported by Politico, has caused more than 10 days of disruption at the EU's executive arm. Among the applications temporarily taken offline were Sysper, which staff use to access payslips, submit timesheets, and request vacation time, and MyPMO, which handles medical reimbursements.

Both were brought back online earlier this week, though Sysper fell over again on Wednesday. The Commission's translation services were also affected.

"The European Commission has taken the necessary steps in response to a detected cyber campaign," Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier told Politico. "Individual systems were targeted and, in line with our standard practice, we have informed those concerned."

According to Politico's sources, the Commission's cybersecurity defenders detected hackers using ARTEX, a publicly available AI-powered penetration testing framework. The incident prompted an emergency meeting of the Commission's Corporate Management Board earlier this month, and tech commissioner Henna Virkkunen briefed her fellow commissioners on Wednesday.

The Commission has yet to respond to The Register's questions about the attack, including whether the crooks got their hands on employee data or whether the applications were simply taken offline as a precaution.

The attack might not come as much of a surprise to the Commission's own security bods. CERT-EU, which looks after the cybersecurity of EU institutions, warned on November 5 that it had spotted what it believed to be ARTEX-assisted attacks against organizations under its watch.

The developer behind ARTEX pulled the project's GitHub repository in October after reports that hackers were putting the software to use. But by then, the code was out in the wild, and anyone wanting to have a crack at an organization's IT estate could get hold of one of several forks.

The FBI also sounded the alarm last week [PDF], revealing that researchers had found more than 1,600 ARTEX consoles exposed to the internet. In some of the attacks detailed by the Feds, hackers went from identifying a target to obtaining privileged access in less than three hours.

“What used to take a skilled operator days or weeks of chaining is now happening in hours because frameworks like ARTEX let a single actor stand up fleets of agents that plan, probe, exploit, and move laterally with almost no further human input after the initial goal is set,” Snehal Antani, CEO of security firm Horizon3.ai, told The Register.

Antani said the proliferation of these tools could also make life harder for investigators trying to establish who's behind an attack, as government-backed hackers and garden-variety cybercriminals increasingly draw from the same toolbox.

Commission staff at least appear to have been paid on time. According to Politico, some couldn't book time off or submit their timesheets, but salaries landed as usual. Whether the hackers managed to snoop on their payslips is something the Commission has yet to establish. ®
