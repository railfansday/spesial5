“I’m afraid this is going to continue”: Justice Alito, the executive, and the emergency docket

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

Justice Samuel Alito sat for an interview with the Associated Press in early October about his new book, “So Ordered.” In the book and the interview, he made three claims about the court’s emergency docket. First, applications on it now rival the number of cases the court decides after full briefing and oral argument. Second, this growth can be traced to presidents who govern by executive order, which produces lower-court rulings that move quickly to the Supreme Court. Third, he does not expect the trend to reverse. “Unless something changes there, and I don’t foresee it right now,” he said, “I’m afraid this is going to continue.” Alito added, such “[a]n application is something that I think we have to rule on.”

Readers of this column know I prefer data to anecdotes, even when the anecdotes come from a Supreme Court justice. The 2025-26 term, which ended on Oct. 4, provides the receipts. And the data support part of what Alito said but complicate the rest.

In the 2024-25 term, the Trump administration filed 27 substantive applications that reached the full court. In the 2025-26 term, it filed 12. Every other applicant held steady. States filed nine applications in the 2024-25 term and 8 last term, and private parties filed 19 and 20. The entire decline came from the executive branch.

For most of the past quarter century, the federal government rarely asked the court for such relief. It filed 6 applications across the 2000-01 through 2015-16 terms, never more than one in a single term. That changed during the first Trump administration. Federal applications rose to eight in the 2018-19 term and nine in the 2019-20 term, then held at five or six a term under the Biden administration. In the 2024-25 term, the number jumped to a staggering 27.

Against that record, the 2025-26 term’s 12 substantive applications from the executive branch are still the second-highest total in 26 years. The volume Alito described did fall. But it fell from an extraordinary peak to a level the court never saw before 2024.

What “rival” means depends on what you count

The court resolved 123 emergency applications last term and decided 56 merits cases after full briefing and oral argument. By that measure, applications more than rival the merits cases.

But I would argue not every application belongs in that count. Forty-three of the 123 were refiled applications. Under the court’s Rule 22.4, an applicant denied by one justice may renew the request with another. The court denied all 43 refiled applications, and none drew a noted dissent. In fact, the court has not granted any of the 453 refiled applications it has resolved since 2000. Without refiles, the court resolved 80 applications last term.

Another 44 were capital cases. The court has to rule on these, and they arrive on an execution timetable the court does not control. The court denied 42 of them. They take time, but they rarely divide the court.

That leaves the 36 substantive applications, the ones that produce written opinions, noted dissents, and national consequences. Measured that way, the court decided 36 substantive applications and 56 merits cases.

Alito’s explanation for the growth is specific. Presidents act by executive order, lower courts block those orders, and the government brings the fight to the Supreme Court.

Last term at least, that process carried less weight than Alito’s explanation suggests. Of the administration’s 12 applications, four challenged lower-court rulings against an executive order or a policy that implemented one: Trump v. Orr, Trump v. California, United States Postal Service v. California, and Department of Homeland Security v. League of Women Voters. The other eight arose from other presidential directives, agency decisions, and personnel actions, including the National Guard deployment in Illinois, the attempted removal of the head of the U.S. Copyright Office, and the termination of temporary protected status for Haitian and Syrian nationals in the U.S.

As I explained above and elsewhere, the emergency docket has three different types of cases: capital cases, refiled applications, and substantive applications. Executive orders appear in only substantive applications. The four executive order applications make up 11% of the 36 substantive applications and 3% of all 123 applications the court resolved. None of the other 27 substantive applications involved an executive order. Redistricting alone accounted for 11 of them, more than the administration’s nine.

The administration filed in two bursts, each with a different focus. Its seven applications in the fall of 2025 and early 2026 ranged from passports and SNAP funding to the Copyright Office. Four had ties to immigration: the National Guard deployment in Illinois, a dispute on immigration judges’ speech, and the temporary protected status terminations for Syria and Haiti. Then the administration filed nothing for more than four months. It returned in late July with five more applications, three of them about elections, including two over the president’s executive order on mail-in voting. The busiest stretch of the administration’s term thus came in the months before the midterm elections.

The administration won five of the nine applications the court decided last term, or 56%. The term before it won 19 of 21. That 56% is the lowest win rate for any administration in the data. By comparison, the Trump administration won 77% of its applications in its first term, and the Biden administration won 63%.

Pooled together, the two terms look ordinary. Adam Feldman’s recent analysis for SCOTUSblog found that the second Trump administration won 82% of the applications it brought through mid-October, close to its first-term rate. My data produce nearly the same pooled figure: 24 of 30, or 80%. This hides how things shifted: The administration won nearly every application in the 2024-25 term and barely more than half in the 2025-26 term.

Specifically, the administration lost Trump v. Illinois, Blanche v. Perlmutter, Margolin v. National Association of Immigration Judges, and the Postal Service case. No justice noted a dissent in Margolin (though the administration later won the underlying dispute in May). Two of the four losses had ties to immigration: Illinois, over the National Guard deployment, and Margolin, over a policy limiting what immigration judges may say in public. But neither turned on immigration law itself. The administration won the one application that did, Department of Homeland Security v. D.V.D., which allowed it to deport noncitizens to third-party countries. It went 2-1 in elections and 2-1 in executive power.

The 2024-25 term the court decided the administration’s applications in an average of 21 days, compared with 36 days for all other substantive applications. Last term the pattern reversed itself. The court took an average of 26 days on the administration’s applications and 15 days on everyone else’s. That 26-day average is the slowest for any administration in the data except the Bush administration, which filed only five applications.

The new term has followed the same pattern. Since Oct. 4, the court has decided three more administration applications and granted one, allowing border wall construction to resume in Texas. It denied the other two. One was the administration’s request, on the day before the midterm elections, to set aside about 20,000 California mail ballots. The court denied it in a one-line order, without asking California to respond and without a noted dissent. Counting from July, the administration has won two of its four election applications.

Alito predicted that the volume of emergency applications will continue to grow. A single term certainly cannot settle that question. And the administration keeps filing: It brought a cluster of election applications in the months before the midterms, filed again on the eve of the election, and has brought three applications to decision in the new term.

The 2025-26 term does show, however, how much the volume depends on a single litigant, in particular the executive. Indeed, the entire decline in substantive applications came from the executive branch. The administration asked the court for emergency relief less often, won less often, and waited longer for answers.

So Alito gets a mixed scorecard. He is right that the volume of such applications remains historically high: The executive branch’s 12 substantive applications are the second-highest total in 26 years, and the court resolved more applications than merits cases. The data cut against him on the source, though, at least last term: Only four of the administration’s 12 applications stemmed from executive orders. Finally, as to whether “this is going to continue,” one term does not make an era. The answer depends less on executive orders and more on the U.S. solicitor general’s office.

Dr. Taraleigh Davis is an Assistant Professor of Public Law and American Politics at Bradley University.
