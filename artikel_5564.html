He Thought It Was a Muscle Strain. Then, Doctors Raced to Save His Leg.

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

— Skiing injury led to acute compartment syndrome, requiring urgent surgery

A 50-year-old recreational skier noticed pain in his calf after a collision with another skier, although he was able to ski down the mountain afterward. But an hour later, he presented to the emergency medical center at the base of the resort, complaining of pain and swelling in his left calf.

Ultrasound showed loss of typical muscle architecture, and a large hematoma within the medial gastrocnemius muscle, but there was no bone break on plain radiography. Later, the patient's pain worsened and numbness developed, leading doctors to be concerned about the possibility of acute compartment syndrome.

"Given this concern, as well as limitation of resources in the current clinical setting, immediate transportation to a level 1 trauma center 90 minutes away and orthopedic consultation on arrival was arranged," Morteza Khodaee, MD, of the University of Colorado School of Medicine in Aurora, and colleagues wrote in BMJ Case Reports.

There, he worsened quickly and was rushed into the operating room.

Compartment syndrome is most commonly caused by direct trauma, often in conjunction with fracture of the tibia, as with high-speed motor vehicle accidents or blunt force crush injuries like those seen in earthquake victims, the authors wrote. But in rare cases, it can also arise from non-contact mechanisms such as muscle strain.

"The patient's severe pain, along with physical findings of increased calf diameter and ultrasound-confirmed hematoma, was a key factor prompting transfer to a higher-level care facility for urgent fasciotomy," they noted.

Compartment syndrome is "a potentially life-threatening condition" typically arising after trauma or tissue ischemia, the authors noted, and can affect any myofascial compartment in the body. It's considered a surgical emergency because elevated intracompartmental pressure can compromise local blood flow and lead to decreased tissue perfusion.

"The earliest and most characteristic symptom of acute compartment syndrome is severe pain that is often described as out of proportion with the apparent injury," they wrote. It is also described as burning, deep, and made worse by passive stretching of the muscles involved. Over time, other symptoms appear, including paresthesias, sensory disturbances, and localized motor weakness. "Notably, pain may diminish or even be absent in the later stages of the syndrome, particularly in the presence of nerve ischemia," they wrote.

Confirming the diagnosis often requires measuring intracompartmental pressure using direct invasive measures like needle manometry, they noted. But while this can be helpful in more equivocal cases, it is not always necessary before proceeding to surgery, especially in the absence of a pulse in the affected extremity.

"Acute compartment syndrome is an orthopedic emergency that requires prompt surgical intervention," the authors wrote, most commonly a two-incision fasciotomy using anterolateral and posteromedial incisions to decompress all four compartments.

In this case, the patient was evaluated at the trauma center by the orthopedic team, and his condition was worsening quickly so physicians decided to go forward with surgery.

Findings during the fasciotomy included severe injury with a grade 4a tear of the medial gastrocnemius muscle, and hematoma greater than 500 mL in volume. The musculature of the superficial posterior compartment appeared viable, and while torn and contused, the gastrocnemius muscle was not necrotic, so significant debridement was not necessary, they wrote.

A vacuum dressing was applied to his incisions, and he was sent to the recovery room, the authors noted. There he showed recovery of sensation and motor function in the ankles and toes, but he continued to bleed significantly. He was sent for angiography that showed active bleeding and a pseudoaneurysm in the proximal calf from a branch of the medial sural artery, which was controlled successfully with embolization. Follow-up surgery confirmed no further necrosis, and the fasciotomy incisions were closed.

At both 8 weeks and 3 months of follow-up, his scars were well healed, his distal sensory and motor function were intact, and he was walking without a limp, the authors noted.
