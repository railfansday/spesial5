Mitochondria power our cells, but the ones inside parasites tell a stranger story

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Mitochondria in five parasites carry dozens of protein families that people lack, which makes them candidate targets for new antiparasitic drugs.

Biology class calls mitochondria the powerhouses of the cell, but that lesson was built mostly on information from human and yeast cells, not from the cells of parasites. Across the wider tree of life, human mitochondria may, in some ways, be the exception.

A new catalog of the proteins inside mitochondria covers five single-celled parasites and the cells of one plant. It turned up dozens of protein families that the parasites share but people lack entirely.

Because these parasites tend to depend on their mitochondria to survive, those missing-in-humans proteins are now candidates for new antiparasitic drugs.

The researchers belong to the MitoCarta Tree of Life consortium, led by Vamsi Mootha of the Broad Institute. He is also a professor of systems biology at Harvard Medical School. Michael Chen, a Harvard M.D.-Ph.D. student, led the comparison.

What the study produced is a list of candidates, not any tested treatments.

About 2 billion years ago, a host cell swallowed a bacterium but didn’t break down its molecules. That bacterium’s descendants became mitochondria.

Over time most of its genes moved into the host’s nucleus, leaving only a small stretch of mitochondrial DNA behind. Today almost every protein inside a mitochondrion is made outside it and imported.

Researchers call that full set of proteins the mitoproteome. Versions already existed for humans and yeast.

The consortium added the plant Arabidopsis and five parasites, among them Giardia, which causes diarrhea, and Acanthamoeba, which can infect the eyes of contact lens wearers. Together, those five parasites cause an estimated 1 billion infections a year.

Across all eight organisms, the team counted 8,619 mitochondrial proteins. The sizes varied wildly. Giardia‘s mitochondria held 59 proteins, while the parasite Trypanosoma held 1,666, nearly half again as many as found in mitochondria of a human cell.

So there’s no single standard version of a mitochondrion. The team sorted the proteins into 3,199 families. Of those, 43 percent carried no feature linking them to any well-studied protein. That suggests mitochondria still hold plenty of unstudied biology.

Giardia‘s mitochondria have shrunk so far that they no longer make energy. The only job anyone knew they did was building iron-sulfur clusters, tiny bundles of iron and sulfur atoms that many proteins need in order to work.

In an interview with Earth.com, Mootha said the team chose Giardia because its mitochondria were already known to be among the simplest.

“Indeed, its mitoproteome proved to be very small – but excitingly, one-half of its proteins are of unknown function.”

Some relatives go further. The team’s computer predictions found only 23 likely mitochondrial proteins in Monocercomonoides exilis, a distant single-celled relative reported to have lost the organelle entirely.

Another, Skoliomonas litria, was also thought to have lost its mitochondria, but the team found signature mitochondrial parts in it, which raises the possibility that a tiny remnant survives.

Mitochondria are already a target for some antiparasitic drugs. Atovaquone, used against malaria parasites and Babesia, targets a mitochondrial protein that humans also carry. And drug resistance to these established medicines is emerging.

So Chen’s team looked for proteins that at least two kinds of parasites keep in their mitochondria and humans don’t make at all. They found 33. Two of them, enzymes called NDH2 and class I fumarate hydratase, have already been studied by other groups as drug targets.

Three of the 33, class I fumarate hydratase among them, do the same jobs as human enzymes while sharing no resemblance to them. In principle, a drug aimed at the parasite version could leave the human version alone.

The team also checked a malaria parasite. Seventeen of the candidates have a counterpart in Plasmodium knowlesi, and a recent gene-knockout screen found 13 of those 17 were essential to its survival.

Biologists call single-celled organisms like these protists, and a drug that worked against several of them at once would be a pan-parasite drug.

“As mitochondria tend to be essential for pathogenic protists, these proteins may represent promising ‘pan-parasite’ drug targets for neglected tropical diseases,” Mootha told Earth.com.

The team then trained an AI program on the new protein lists and used it to predict the mitochondria of roughly two hundred species.

With those predictions, the team worked backward to the last common ancestor of all complex cells, which lived roughly 1.5 to 2 billion years ago.

The team estimated that the ancestor had about 1,438 mitochondrial proteins, with a wide margin of error.

By their math, about 70 percent of human mitochondrial proteins trace back to this ancestor, compared with 14 to 16 percent in earlier attempts. By that estimate, most parts of our own mitochondria are very old.

Most of the ancestor’s protein families show up today mainly in microbes that use oxygen. But about 15 percent are linked to microbes that live without it, including enzymes that make hydrogen gas.

Acanthamoeba still has both abilities. Without oxygen, mitochondria purified from the amoeba produce hydrogen gas. The team concluded that the ancestor’s mitochondria may have been able to work with oxygen or without it.

Human mitochondria lack dozens of proteins that the ancestor had and many other species kept. Mootha said the textbook picture comes largely from mammal and yeast cells.

“In some instances, our mitochondria appear to be the odd ones,” he added.

Scientists have long debated whether mitochondria arrived early or late in the making of complex cells. So the team estimated when each ancient protein arrived, using the length of branches on protein family trees.

Proteins inherited from the host cell, a relative of today’s Asgard archaea, came in early. Mitochondrial proteins came in later on average, after the nucleus, the internal skeleton and other cell parts were already in place.

That supports what researchers call the mito-late view, which earlier studies had also backed. Peroxisomes, another kind of compartment inside the cell, appear to have arrived around the same time.

The ancient proteins matter for medicine, too. Of 266 genes tied to mitochondrial diseases in people, the team traced 240 back to that ancestor.

The authors list several limits. Sequenced genomes still favor well-studied groups of organisms, and some protein families that look unique to one group may have distant relatives that current tools can’t detect.

Reconstructing cells from well over a billion years ago is also hard. The authors said the limits of current methods may affect how accurate the ancestral reconstruction is.

For drugs, a missing-in-humans protein is only the first filter. Mootha said the next steps are to learn more about the pathways these proteins work in and whether the parasites need them at every stage of life. Researchers also have to judge whether each protein can be blocked by a drug at all.

“Then once candidate targets are identified, there is a long path for drug discovery and development and testing,” he said.

The team’s data are public, so other labs can take up that work. The next question is whether blocking any one of the 33 candidates can stop a parasite without harming the person it infects.

The full study was published in the journal Cell.
