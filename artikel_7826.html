Dry potting mix doesn't always mean a thirsty plant. A chopstick inserted near the roots tells you everything – and you may already have one in a kitchen drawer.

Figuring out when to water houseplants seems like an easy call, right up until you're standing over a pot with the watering can. The top of the mix looks pale and dusty. You stick a finger in; it's dry, so you water. Then, a week or two later, the lower leaves go yellow, and it turns out the soil underneath was damp the whole time.

Too much water is behind a lot of the trouble people run into when watering houseplants, and fall makes it easier to get it wrong. Days are shorter now, and the light is weaker, so growth slows down, and a pot can stay wet far longer than it did back in July. Roots sitting in soggy mix can't get the air they need – and that's how rot gets started.

Your finger only reaches the first inch or so. In a deeper pot, that top layer can dry out days before the root zone does. One fix is a moisture meter, like the Yamron 4-in-1 from Amazon. They're not expensive and offer an accurate assessment of soil moisture. But another way of checking is to use a wooden chopstick – it costs nothing, if there's one left over from takeout in a drawer somewhere, and it reaches right down to where the roots are.

Bare wood soaks up moisture. Push an untreated chopstick or bamboo skewer down into damp potting mix, and the wood darkens wherever it touches wet soil, and crumbs of mix stick to it when you pull it out. Dry mix behaves differently. Dry particles just fall away, and the stick comes back pale and mostly clean.

Have you ever done the toothpick test on a cake? It's the same idea, just backward. With a cake you want the toothpick clean, but in a pot, a clean chopstick means the mix has dried out at that depth.

One important point: you must use plain wood for this. Lacquered or painted chopsticks shed water, so they won't darken much. A plastic one only shows you whatever soil happens to cling to it, and not much else, honestly.

Grab a clean, dry stick first. Any leftover dampness from the last check will throw off the reading. Standard chopsticks are long enough for small and medium pots, but big floor plants sit in deeper containers, and that's where 12-inch bamboo skewers come in handy. Walmart sells a pack of 100 for a few dollars.

Push the stick in partway between the main stem and the rim, straight down into the root zone. Did it hit resistance on the way? Ease off and try another spot, because forcing it through a thick mass of roots can damage them. Then leave it there, alone, for about 30 seconds to a minute so the wood has time to pick up any moisture.

Slide it out slowly and look along the whole length. If there's a dark band near the bottom with mix stuck to it, the lower part of the pot is still wet – even if the top inch came out clean.

If you still can't make sense of the stick, use a soil moisture meter or a leave-in option, like the Sustee Aquameter, which sits in the pot full-time and fades from blue to white as the mix dries. Either way, the reading only tells you what's in the pot. What you do next depends on the plant.

A damp chopstick generally means there's still water down there, and a clean, dry one suggests the mix has dried out at the depth you tested. That isn't an automatic cue to grab the watering can, though. Peace lilies need their mix kept moist but never soggy, so water once the stick starts coming out dry through the upper part of the pot. Wait much longer, and the whole plant usually droops hard.

Pothos can go drier than that, a fair bit drier. Let the stick come out dry through a good share of the pot before you water. Snake plants and succulents go further still, and they're safest when the stick comes back clean all the way to the bottom. In winter, a snake plant may only need water every month or two. When one of them has been sitting in soggy mix for weeks, repot it into a fast-draining mix, like Miracle-Gro cactus, palm and citrus mix from Home Depot.

Chunky orchid bark is the big one. Those chunks are too coarse to stick to wood, and the bark soaks up water inside each piece rather than holding it on the surface. You can pull a clean, dry-looking chopstick out of a pot that's still plenty moist. Gritty, very coarse mixes muddle things the same way. A badly rootbound plant is tricky too, just in a different way. There's hardly any mix left between the roots, the stick won't go in easily, and water poured on top can run straight down the sides and out the drainage holes.

Use the chopstick as one clue, then. It can't measure anything precisely, so back it up with a few other checks. Pick the pot up right after a good soak and again a few days later, and you'll learn how light it feels once it's dry. Check the leaves as well. Yellowing lower leaves on a heavy pot usually point to too much water. And make sure every pot drains freely. Clear nursery pots with drainage holes, like these from Walmart, let you see roots and dark, wet mix through the side.

A calendar can't see inside a pot. If you water on the same day every week, it can go fine all summer and then start leaving roots wet for days once fall arrives and the light drops off.

Chopsticks are about as cheap as plant tools get, and one shows what's happening under the surface while watering needs shift with the seasons. Keep a dry one next to the watering can. It'll be right there the next time the soil has you guessing.
