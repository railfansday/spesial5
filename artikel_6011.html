Marriott’s Worst Hotel? Rated 1.6 Stars On Its Own Site—A Guest Found Radisson Signs, Stained Sheets And A Filthy Sofa

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Marriott’s Buena Park Grand Hotel & Suites promises “award-winning hospitality.” Guests rate it 1.6 out of 5 on Marriott’s own website. A reader checked in and found stained bedding and a sofa he wouldn’t sit on. I’ve written about the 1.7-star Woodlands Plaza Toledo Hotel as a contender for the worst Marriott in North America, and about the Carambola Beach Resort, Marriott’s “regret resort.” Apparently there’s more competition in the race to the bottom. You didn’t need to investigate eeach property to find out whether it met basic standards. You knew what the brand stood for. Now I find Marriott overpromises and enforces too little, afraid of alienating owners by making them spend money. Net rooms growth has become the priority, even when the rooms undermine the reason customers choose Marriott in the first place. Here, the property isn’t even flying one of Marriott’s flags yet and presumably there will be some investment. But they aren’t making the owner do that first, before delivering their guests. They’re trading a reputation built over decades for current fee revenue. Owners pay Marriott to deliver customers. If travelers stop trusting the company’s hotels, Marriott has fewer customers to send those owners. Then the owners have little reason to keep paying Marriott. Marriott wanted $144.37 a night for the Grand Hotel & Suites in Buena Park, California, an unbranded property that guests had rated 1.6 out of 5 on Marriott’s own website.

I booked it for less through Delta and found stains on the fitted sheet, red stains beneath a pillowcase and hair on a sofa I wouldn’t sit on.

When you book a Marriott, you expect a Marriott. The whole point of having a hotel company’s name behind a reservation is that somebody has exercised a little judgment on your behalf. What judgment did Marriott International exercise here?

The Grand is supposed to, at some point, become a SpringHill Suites by Marriott. When I arrived, I saw no Marriott signs. Some of the old signs and placards still said Radisson.

I paid $125.22 through Delta Air Lines’ hotel booking portal. With the branding up in the air, I couldn’t tell what Bonvoy benefits or points I’d receive for paying $144.37 to book directly with Marriott. Priceline would have saved me $13.25 over Delta, but I’d have missed out on the nearly 600 SkyMiles I earned.

I arrived just after 2 p.m. The front desk clerk said my room was ready, but I’d have to pay $35 to check in before 3 p.m. I passed and came back a little more than an hour later.

To be fair, I’d booked through a third party. Still, I gave the clerk my Bonvoy number at check-in. She never acknowledged my Lifetime Titanium Elite status or mentioned any benefits. I didn’t even get a bottle of water.

The thing is, I liked the building. Its Spanish mission revival architecture was distinctive. You could also see where a restaurant and lounge had once been located. Whoever built the Grand clearly spent money on it. I suspect it was a great hotel in the early 1980s. More than four decades of neglect had, however, turned it into a dump.

I booked a park-view suite with one king bed and one sofa bed. The view from my second-floor junior suite was of a parking lot and, beyond it, the main thoroughfare.

At first glance, the room looked clean. Apart from the flooring and the one TV, I’d guess it had been 20 years since anything had been updated. Even the laminate flooring was peeling and torn. The toiletries in the bathroom were a mix of brands. The tub and shower had extensive patches of what looked like black mold.

Then I looked at the sofa. There was hair and debris on it. One reason to book a suite is to have somewhere to sit besides the bed. That left the bed, where I found what appeared to be fluid stains on the bedding, including the fitted sheet. Under one pillowcase, the pillow protector had red stains of its own.

The Grand fell well short of what I’d expect from a Quality Inn or a Ramada. I’d put it at what I expect from the nearby Motel 6. At Marriott’s rate of $144.37, staying here is highway robbery.

I don’t believe for a minute this property would have made it into the chain in this condition when the Marriott family ran the company. I can imagine Bill Marriott saying, “Call us when the renovations are finished.”

Arne Sorenson, the first CEO from outside the Marriott family, and his successor, Tony Capuano, ruined the brand the Marriotts built.

SpringHill Suites is supposed to be one of Marriott’s better limited-service brands. It will be a long time before the Grand comes anywhere close to that standard.
