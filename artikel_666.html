Common medications may secretly rewire your gut, with effects lasting for years

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Medications you stopped taking years ago may still be influencing the bacteria living in your gut. A large study from the University of Tartu Institute of Genomics has found that many commonly prescribed drugs leave lasting changes in the gut microbiome, even long after people stop using them.

The gut microbiome is the vast community of bacteria and other microorganisms that inhabit the digestive tract. These microbes play important roles in digestion, immune function, and overall health. While antibiotics are well known for disrupting gut bacteria, the new findings suggest that many other medications can also have effects that persist for years.

To investigate these connections, researchers examined stool samples and prescription histories from more than 2,500 participants in the Estonian Biobank's Estonian Microbiome cohort. They discovered that most of the medications studied were associated with changes in gut bacteria. Even more surprisingly, a substantial number left detectable microbial "fingerprints" years after patients had stopped taking them.

Common Medications Leave Lasting Changes in Gut Bacteria

The findings extend far beyond antibiotics. Antidepressants, beta-blockers, proton pump inhibitors, and benzodiazepines were all linked to differences in the gut microbiome.

These medications are widely used to treat everyday health conditions. Beta-blockers are commonly prescribed for high blood pressure and certain heart conditions, while proton pump inhibitors reduce stomach acid and are frequently used to treat acid reflux. Benzodiazepines are often prescribed for anxiety, and antidepressants help manage depression and other mental health conditions.

"Most microbiome studies only consider current medications, but our results show that past drug use can be just as important as it is a surprisingly strong factor in explaining individual microbiome differences," said Dr. Oliver Aasmets, lead author.

This discovery suggests that looking only at the medications someone currently takes may overlook an important part of their gut health history. Previous prescriptions could help explain why the composition of gut bacteria differs so much from one person to another.

Anxiety Medications Show Effects Comparable to Antibiotics

One of the most striking findings involved benzodiazepines, a class of medications commonly used to treat anxiety.

Researchers found that these drugs were associated with changes in the gut microbiome comparable to those seen with broad-spectrum antibiotics. These antibiotics target a wide variety of bacteria and are known to substantially alter the microbial communities living in the digestive system.

The researchers also discovered that medications within the same drug class did not necessarily produce the same effects. For example, diazepam and alprazolam are both benzodiazepines used to treat similar conditions, yet they appeared to differ in how strongly they disrupted gut bacteria.

This suggests that the effects of medication on the microbiome may depend not only on the type of drug being taken, but also on the specific medication.

Gut Bacteria Can Change When Medications Are Started or Stopped

To investigate whether medications were actually contributing to these microbial changes, researchers collected additional stool samples from a smaller group of participants.

By examining how the microbiome changed when people started or stopped certain medications, the team identified predictable shifts in gut bacteria. These findings provided additional evidence that the drugs themselves were contributing to the changes, rather than simply being associated with differences already present in patients.

Although the number of participants in this follow-up analysis was relatively small, the researchers were able to verify lasting effects associated with several drug categories. These included proton pump inhibitors, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, and certain antibiotics, including combinations of penicillins and macrolides.

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, often called SSRIs, are a widely prescribed type of antidepressant. Macrolides are a class of antibiotics used to treat various bacterial infections.

Why Your Medication History May Matter More Than Previously Thought

The findings point to an important consideration for researchers studying the relationship between gut bacteria and disease. Differences in the microbiome may reflect not only a person's current health and medication use, but also treatments received years earlier.

If previous medications are not taken into account, scientists could mistakenly attribute certain microbial differences to a disease or another health factor when past drug exposure may have contributed.

The results also highlight how much remains to be understood about the long-term relationship between medications and the gut microbiome. Although the study identified persistent microbial changes, these findings do not establish that such changes are necessarily harmful or that they lead to specific health problems.

"This is a comprehensive systematic evaluation of long-term medication effects on the microbiome using real-world medical health records," said Professor Elin Org, corresponding author. "We hope this encourages researchers and clinicians to factor in medication history when interpreting microbiome data."
