A Survivor of Alleged Sex Abuse at Miss Hall’s School in the Berkshires Speaks Out.

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

At 6:04 on the morning of March 29, 2024, my phone lit up with a long text from Matt Rutledge. The head of school had called him the night before, he wrote, while he was watching television with his wife. He was on paid administrative leave, his school email was frozen, and he assumed this was about him and me. He called what he had done to me a relationship. He didn’t regret his feelings for me. Could I call him when his wife was out of the house?

I was thirty-seven years old. I was a few weeks out from my partnership interview at a New York law firm. I had a husband and a three-year-old son. I spend my working life telling clients which sentences will look bad in discovery. I read that text twice, and my first thought was, How can I help him?

He had spent years teaching me to use his word for it. It was never a relationship. He groomed me, he exploited me, and he raped me.

Vanity Fair has written about that morning before. Evgenia Peretz opened “Girls, Interrupted” with that text in January 2025, and she went back to Miss Hall’s last fall after the investigation came out. In March, Dan Adler covered the indictment. This time I’m telling it myself.

Later that morning an email arrived from an attorney in Boston. Subject line: “Reaching out about Miss Hall’s School.” I didn’t open it right away. I didn’t need to. Some part of me had been composing that email in my head since I was a teenager. The attorney represented a woman named Melissa Fares, a Miss Hall’s alumna who had been there a few years after me. I had never heard her name.

She had heard mine. Years earlier, older girls at the school had told her about me. She held on to my name, and when she went to a lawyer she remembered it. By then I had married and changed it, and they found me anyway.

I called Matt and asked. “Who is Melissa?” There was a pause long enough to hear him decide something, then a sigh, and then he told me he had had sex with Melissa while she was a student.

Here is what I did next, and I’d ask you to withhold judgment. I told him I would call the lawyer, find out what she had, and report back.

That’s what twenty-two years of being groomed looks like at a desk in Midtown. You don’t make a decision. The decision was made for you a long time ago, in a dorm room, and you’re simply carrying it out.

I did call the lawyer. And when I heard my own voice say “I’m not ready to talk to you, I want to talk to Melissa,” I think that was the first sentence in two decades that I said entirely for myself.

Melissa called that afternoon. I don’t remember how she started. I remember what I said before she got far.

“I’ve been waiting for your call for twenty years.”

I meant it literally. Some part of me had always known I wasn’t the only one. I just couldn’t go looking. I could only wait to be found.

She told me her story, and I heard my own memories come out of a stranger’s mouth. He called her “Little One.” He called me “Little One.” He took her to the same hotel in Northampton. He told her he would kill himself if she ever told anyone, which is a thing he had also told me, more than once, in nearly the same words. He had made himself the center of her emotional world before he ever touched her. He had a script.

For twenty years he had me believing that what happened was singular, and that it was my fault. By the end of that call it was a pattern, and I was a data point. I was not special. It is a strange thing to grieve, not being special to the person who hurt you. I grieved it anyway.

After more than three decades teaching at that school, he resigned the same day.

I arrived at Miss Hall’s, an all-girls boarding school in Pittsfield, Massachusetts, in the fall of 2001. I was fourteen, homesick, and like every other girl my age, positive I was more grown up than I was.

Rutledge had been teaching history there for a decade. He was a big man with a booming voice, married with two daughters. He lived with his family in a school-owned house across the street from the main entrance. He used to cut the lunch line announcing himself as “Mr. Wonderful.” Girls talked about him like he was a minor deity. They also mentioned, in the breezy way teenagers mention terrible things, that he had a type.

Some evenings, he was one of the adults who did bed checks in the dorms. The others always stood in the doorway. He came in and sat on the edge of my bed.

By the next fall he was my advisor and my cross-country coach. There was no door at that school he couldn’t open. On the long drives to meets, he told me to sit next to him in the school van. He called me his copilot. The rest of the team sat behind us. He would put his hand on my upper thigh, or pull me close until our legs were touching. I watched the Berkshire hills go by and tried not to look down.

That was the first line he crossed. I was fifteen. After that he crossed them one at a time until there were none left. He kept abusing me for years after I graduated.

From the outside, those years looked like a success story.

I went to college and then law school. I became a civil defense attorney. I got married. I had a son. I was good at my job.

I could find the unanswered email in ten thousand pages of discovery. The report nobody followed up on. I couldn’t open my own file.

When I tell this story, people ask me some version of the same question. How did nobody notice?

In the late 1990s, years before I ever set foot on Miss Hall’s campus, a student stood up at a school meeting and said Mr. Rutledge was having sex with another student. The school suspended her for the rest of the year, barred her from graduation and made her write him an apology letter. He kept teaching.

On my graduation day, Rutledge kissed me and told me that he loved me. A classmate told a staff member, who reported it to the administration. In a memo, Rutledge admitted telling me he loved me but said “[it was] not a romantic expression.” He denied the kiss. They took his word for it. No one called me or my parents.

A few years later, after a family reported that he had made advances toward their daughter, the head of school at that time, Jeannie Norris, put a warning to Rutledge in writing. “This isn’t the 70s or the 80s,” she wrote. “Society has no stomach for any kind of inappropriate behavior between adults and girls or teachers and students.” She was worried about the school’s reputation, and about his. In the letter she said the incident had “the potential for seriously damaging the School and your [Rutledge’s] reputation.” He was not fired.

Over the years that followed Rutledge was made chair of the history department and collected faculty honors. More than one person gave the school my name as a girl he had singled out. Nobody ever called me. In June 2023, I heard another alumna tried to raise concerns about him with the head of school at that time. He was still teaching nine months later, when Melissa reported him.

In August 2025 Miss Hall’s published the results of an outside investigation it had commissioned from Aleta Law. The investigators substantiated grooming and abuse by Rutledge involving at least five students. They found school leadership had been warned about him over the years and hadn’t acted. Staff members told investigators they had suspected him and kept quiet, some because they feared retaliation, others because they assumed no one in charge would listen.

It went beyond Rutledge. The investigation found that seven other Miss Hall’s employees had engaged in sexual misconduct with students, dating back to the 1940s. Eight adults across eighty plus years. That’s a culture of abuse.

A Berkshire County grand jury has since indicted Norris on a charge of reckless endangerment of a child. She has pleaded not guilty. Another former Miss Hall’s teacher has been indicted for assault with intent to rape and indecent assault and two counts battery of a student. He has also pleaded not guilty.

I read all sixty pages of the Aleta Law report in one sitting. Nothing in it surprised me. It still knocked the air out of me to see my adolescence reduced to numbered findings.

Melissa arrived at Miss Hall’s in 2007, two years after I left.

People sometimes call us friends. That’s true, but it’s a small word for it. I call her my sister survivor.

I wouldn’t be writing this if Melissa hadn’t gone first. She went to the school alone. In April 2024, she told her story publicly. A few weeks later, I told mine. Then other women came forward. They said Rutledge had abused them too.

In October 2024, the Berkshire district attorney said he didn’t believe he could charge Rutledge. In Massachusetts, the age of consent is sixteen. Under the law, a teacher could claim that a sixteen- or seventeen-year-old student had consented.

Melissa and I set out to change the law. We testified at the State House and knocked on every door that would open. Enough Abuse, a Massachusetts nonprofit, had been fighting for this for years. Two national advocacy groups, World Without Exploitation and I Have The Right To, stood with us. Representatives Leigh Davis and John Lawn pushed for it in the House, and Senator Joan Lovely added it to the state budget. In July, Governor Healey signed the Trusted Adults Act.

In Massachusetts, it is now a felony for a teacher or coach to have sex with a student under eighteen.

Melissa and I spoke at the signing ceremony. I stood right next to the governor and thought about the girl in the front seat of the school van, with her teacher’s hand on her leg.

While we were working on the law, Massachusetts State Police and a new team of prosecutors in the Berkshire district attorney’s office had reopened the investigation into Rutledge. Melissa and I testified before a grand jury. In March, he was indicted on three counts of rape.

In April, he was arraigned. It was the first time Melissa and I faced him together. In the courtroom, I handed out orange heart stickers. Orange is Melissa’s favorite color. When we saw Rutledge, we both went numb. We had learned to do that as girls, when going somewhere else in our heads was the only way through. He pleaded not guilty.

I looked back at the benches. They were full of orange. Teachers from Miss Hall’s, people from the community, alumnae across generations. I had been alone with this since I was fifteen. That day, a whole room sat behind us. Afterward, Melissa told reporters that for the first time, she held the power in a room with him. I felt it too.

This month, nine years after #MeToo took off, a jury in London convicted Russell Brand of one rape and three sexual assaults. A judge sentenced him to ten and a half years in prison. His crimes went back to 2004. Brand’s attorneys argued the women had waited too long to be believed. I waited twenty years. Delay is often the most ordinary fact in these cases.

In the midterms, Ohio elected Amy Acton governor. A few weeks before the vote, her opponent’s campaign ran an ad in which her estranged mother called her a liar. Acton’s stepfather had been charged with raping her when she was a child. Republicans and Democrats both condemned the ad. She won anyway.

I watched the returns on election night and thought about the girls at Miss Hall’s who told the truth and were called liars. Survivors get called liars all the time. We almost never get to watch one win.

Tarana Burke knew survivors weren’t being believed long before most people were listening. She started saying “me too” in 2006 to reach Black girls who had survived sexual violence, more than ten years before it became a hashtag. Every survivor who spoke after her made it a little easier for the next one.

In November 2024, a Cornell student told campus police and the Title IX office that she had been gang-raped at a fraternity house weeks earlier. No one has been charged. Cornell’s president set up a task force on sexual assault, but he later admitted he never checked how her case ended.

When I read that, I was back at Miss Hall’s on graduation day, waiting for a phone call that nobody made.

The Epstein survivors know that silence too. For decades, powerful people looked away. Last year survivors pushed Congress to pass a law requiring the Justice Department to release its Epstein files. Millions of pages came out. There have been no arrests in the United States.

I’m still hopeful. Survivors everywhere keep coming forward, and some of the people in charge are finally acting. My body knew what Rutledge had done to me long before I let myself say it aloud. I didn’t have the words. Survivors today do, and they have each other. Melissa found me.

The criminal case against Rutledge isn’t over. His lawyer has asked a judge to dismiss all charges. We don’t know yet whether there will be a trial. Whatever the judge decides, I’ve already reached my own verdict. I’m healing and Rutledge doesn’t get a say in that.

For most of my life, I carried shame that was never mine. He handed it to me. Every adult who looked away handed me a little more.

Predators don’t work alone. Someone always sees something. In the movie Spotlight, a lawyer says that if it takes a village to raise a child, it takes a village to abuse one. My village saw. The people in charge did nothing.

I’ve given the shame back. It belongs to him, and to the people who could have stopped him.

If you are a survivor and you are still carrying shame, you can put it down.

Borrow my courage if you need it. Melissa lent me hers.

Neither Matthew Rutledge nor Jean Norris responded to Vanity Fair for the original story, and neither of them has made direct statements to the press. Norris, through her attorney, claimed not to know that Rutledge abused students.
