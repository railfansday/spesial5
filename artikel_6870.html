Is It Rude to Ask Guests to Take Their Shoes Off at Your House?

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Your party, your rules? Etiquette experts say holiday hosting calls for a little more nuance.

You’ve asked guests to dress for dinner. Is it fair to ask them to take part of the outfit off at the door? It’s a small holiday-hosting dilemma with reasonable arguments on either side: The host would rather not have wet boots on the good rug; the guest would rather not spend a cocktail party in stocking feet.

So, is the request rude? Not inherently, according to the etiquette and entertaining experts we asked. But the occasion, the advance notice, and the room you leave for exceptions all matter. Here’s how to handle it before the doorbell rings.

For some households, taking shoes off is simply what you do when you come in, whether you’re stopping by for coffee or staying for Thanksgiving dinner. There’s a practical argument for keeping the rule during the holidays, too. Stacey Gustafson, Bona’s flooring and cleaning expert, points out that grit tracked in on outdoor shoes can scratch hardwood and wear down its finish over time.

But not every shoes-off request is a bid to preserve perfect floors. Removing outdoor footwear is an established cultural custom in Japan, for example, where the genkan, or entrance area, separates outside from in. What one guest considers an unexpected inconvenience may be an entirely ordinary expression of respect in another person’s home.

For David Jubé, founder of food and entertaining platform The Gourmet Host, the request itself is less of a problem than the way it’s delivered. “Asking guests to take their shoes off is not rude. Making them guess is,” he says. “The rudeness, when it happens, comes from springing the request at the door with no warning, so a guest finds out in front of everyone that they wore mismatched socks or that their boots take five minutes to unlace.”

“If you’ve invited guests to dress up for an elegant holiday dinner, asking them to remove their shoes upon arrival can undermine the very experience you’ve invited them to enjoy,” says Erika Gessin, founder and creative director of entertaining brand The Fête Edit. “Shoes are often an intentional part of an outfit.”

If shoes are coming off regardless, guests should know before they plan what to wear. For hosts willing to relax their usual rule, etiquette expert Nick Leighton suggests letting the occasion take precedence: “For larger or more formal gatherings, it’s ideal to just let everyone keep their shoes on and treat the cleaning you’ll have to do afterwards as part of the cost of entertaining.”

Comfort matters as much as appearance. Amber Dawn Schultheis, creator of hosting and recipe site The Host Notes, points out that some guests need the support of their shoes, especially if they’ll be standing for an extended period. Her preferred approach leaves the decision with them: “Shoes are optional. Do what makes you feel good.”

If you’re keeping the rule, make it easy to follow. Jubé suggests writing, “We’re a shoes-off house, so bring cozy socks or slippers if you like” on the invitation. At the door, give guests somewhere to sit while they unlace their boots, a rack or tray to put them on, and a basket of clean slippers or grippy socks. If someone needs to keep their shoes on for health, mobility, or safety reasons, let them do so without asking for an explanation.

And if you’re the guest? Take your cue from the shoes already by the door, not from what you do at home. If it isn’t clear, Gessin recommends asking quietly: “Would you prefer I take my shoes off?”

Jubé recommends a sturdy mat outside, a washable one inside, and a boot tray for wet footwear. Gessin suggests washable runners in high-traffic areas—the hall between the front door and the dining room is a good place to start. Those small adjustments let you give guests a choice without spending the party keeping tabs on the floor.

Once everyone is inside, resist the urge to monitor every footprint. “Guests remember how a gathering felt, not whether the floor stayed spotless, and a host fretting over every footprint makes everyone tense,” Jubé says.

“Floors can be cleaned tomorrow,” Gessin says. “How you made your guests feel is what they’ll remember.”
