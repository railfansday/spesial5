Quantum computing’s first real-world payoff may come in energy, not drug discovery

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

For years, the case for quantum computing has rested on ambitious promises, from discovering new drugs by simulating how molecules behave to optimizing supply chains and airline schedules. Many of the biggest aims are still likely to be years, if not decades, away.

But quantum software, which uses algorithms designed to solve complex problems faster than conventional computers, is already being tested on a more immediate challenge: the energy transition, from keeping power grids stable to finding better materials for solar cells and hydrogen production.

Phasecraft, a quantum software company founded in Bristol, England, in 2019, is one of the firms putting the technology to work in energy. It raised $34 million in Series B funding in 2025, and in September this year announced plans to set up U.S. headquarters in Arlington, Virginia.

There are enormous opportunities to solve problems that are both important for energy security and commercially valuable, Ashley Montanaro, co-founder and CEO of Phasecraft, told CNBC.

Phasecraft isn't alone in applying quantum software to the energy industry.

Quantinuum, which was formed by the merger of Cambridge Quantum and Honeywell Quantum Solutions and listed on Nasdaq in June, is using the technology to model materials for hydrogen fuel cells for transportation. It is also working with partners on materials for carbon capture.

Another U.K. player, ORCA Computing, a spinout from the University of Oxford, has partnered with BP to better understand how a molecule's physical, chemical and biological properties are governed. That could help explain how biofuels burn and how to make them burn more efficiently

In a separate project, ORCA worked with Japanese software company JIJ on a challenge power companies face: deciding which generators to switch on or off, and when, to meet demand at the lowest cost.

That project also involved BP and the National Quantum Computing Centre (NQCC), the U.K.'s national laboratory for quantum computing, which is part of the government-backed U.K. Research and Innovation. The involvement of the NQCC, like that of ARPA-E, suggests governments are paying close attention to quantum software's potential role in energy, as are companies such as BP.

For Phasecraft, a priority is grid stability and optimization. It has partnered with the National Energy System Operator (NESO), the independent system planner that balances energy supply and demand in England, Scotland and Wales, on grid optimization tasks, including managing power exchanges and isolating network sections during emergencies. The company says its software can complete these tasks up to 1,000 times faster than conventional computing.

Phasecraft believes its grid software could be particularly sought after in Asia, where electrification is rapidly expanding, increasing the need for grid resilience to balance high and growing demand with the intermittent supply offered by renewables.

The company is also working with U.K. solar cell maker Oxford PV on the development of new materials for photovoltaic cells capable of operating more efficiently than existing ones.

Separately, it has teamed up with London-listed specialty materials developer Johnson Matthey, Harvard University and Boston-based quantum hardware specialist QuEra, in work overseen by the U.S. government's Advanced Research Projects Agency–Energy (ARPA-E), to discover novel catalysts that would reduce reliance on scarce metals like iridium — which is currently critical in the production of hydrogen.

Asked why Phasecraft is focusing on energy rather than sectors that might seem more lucrative, Montanaro said the returns were there too.

"When you get this right, the commercial returns are real: more efficient energy networks mean lower operating costs and more value from existing infrastructure," he said.
