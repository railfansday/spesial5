The rocks beneath forests may hold a hidden key to carbon storage

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

New research reveals how the weathering of limestone may help forest soils accumulate and preserve carbon from microbial remains.

Dead leaves are the obvious source of the carbon in soil. About half of soil organic carbon, though, comes from the remains of dead microbes.

What soil bacteria and fungi leave behind when they die outlasts the plant matter it was built from.

A team in China has now tied that microbial carbon to the rock underneath. Forest soils over limestone and dolomite, rocks that dissolve in rainwater, hold more of it than soils over other rock.

At the sites where the rock was dissolving fastest, there was more of it still.

Peilei Hu led the work at the Institute of Subtropical Agriculture, part of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, with Wei Zhang and Kelin Wang as senior authors.

Co-authors came from CREAF in Barcelona and Aarhus University in Denmark.

The team worked at three scales. There was a reanalysis of forest soils worldwide, a survey of 60 plots in southwest China, and a run of jars on a laboratory bench.

Plants pull carbon out of the air, and some of it reaches the soil as litter and root material.

Microbes eat it, grow, divide and die, and what is left of their bodies is called necromass.

That necromass can last a long time, especially once it sticks to mineral surfaces where other microbes can’t easily reach it.

So two steps decide how much piles up. First, plant carbon has to be turned into microbial bodies.

Then those microbial remains have to resist decomposition.

Soil chemistry governs both steps, and the rock below sets much of the chemistry.

Carbonate rocks underlie more than 15% of the continental land surface and dissolve at least ten times faster than many silicate-bearing rocks.

As they go, they release calcium and raise soil pH.

Studies of carbonate weathering have mostly asked how much carbon dioxide the dissolving rock itself consumes. That left the organic side of the ledger open.

The team started with an existing global database and kept 466 forest topsoil records.

Each one measured total soil organic carbon and amino sugars, the fragments of microbial cell walls that stand in for necromass.

A karst aquifer map sorted the records into 69 over carbonate rock and 397 over other kinds.

Per unit of soil, karst sites held 43.4% more amino sugars than the rest, and 57.6% more organic carbon overall.

As a share of the carbon present, though, microbial remains were no higher over carbonate rock than anywhere else. Globally, both pools simply ran bigger together.

The field survey in southwest China came out differently.

The work ran across six regions: Nanchuan, Suiyang, Dushan, Huanjiang, Wuming and Longzhou. In each one, the team paired a forest on carbonate rock with a forest on clastic rock and sampled five plots of each.

The karst forests carry less wood and fewer tree species than their neighbors, on soil that is shallow and patchy. Total soil carbon came out about the same between the pairs.

But the proportion of carbon derived from microbial remains was 19.3% higher in karst soils, and 57.8% more of their amino sugars were bound to mineral surfaces.

To put a number on the weathering itself, the team buried limestone tablets in the karst plots and left them there for a year.

One lay on the soil surface, one sat 4 inches (10 centimeters) down, and one was wedged at the boundary between soil and bedrock. Weighing what each tablet had lost gave a rate.

Those rates were the strongest predictor of microbial carbon in the karst soils, ahead of the microbes’ own numbers and diversity.

Of the combined weight the model gave its predictors, weathering rate accounted for 46.5%, and the abundance of genes for breaking down tough plant compounds another 31.9%.

Lignin, the stiff compound that makes wood woody, was scarcer in karst soils and more chemically chewed up. Over non-karst rock, the same analysis turned up climate and plant community instead of weathering.

Field patterns cannot settle which way the arrow points. Microbes help dissolve rock as surely as dissolving rock changes microbes, and the authors say as much.

The laboratory part was built to answer that. The team stirred 4 grams of either limestone powder or quartz sand into 40 grams of karst soil, 54 jars in all.

Each jar was then fed plain water, glucose or glycine, an amino acid.

The glucose and the glycine were built with carbon-13, a heavier form of carbon that can be traced wherever it goes. The jars then sat at 77°F (25°C) for 64 days.

Limestone raised the pH and the calcium available in the soil, as expected. It also shifted which enzymes the microbes made.

They put less into splitting simple sugars and more into attacking tougher material, though that second shift was only marginal.

The labeled carbon is the result that matters.

With limestone in the jar, more of it ended up in microbial remains rather than breathed out as carbon dioxide. The gain was 22% for glucose and 71% for glycine.

For the second step, keeping the necromass, the team separated the calcium in the field soils into four forms and asked which tracked the mineral-bound amino sugars.

Two did: a loosely held, readily exchangeable form, and a form tied up with carbonate minerals themselves.

Calcium can bridge organic matter to mineral surfaces, and carbonate surfaces can take up and bury the remains directly.

Neither bond is permanent on its own, the authors note, but together they put microbial carbon somewhere microbes have trouble getting at it.

That connects the two halves. The same dissolving rock that helps turn plant carbon into microbial bodies also supplies the calcium that keeps those bodies in place.

The global half of the study is forest topsoil only, down to 8 inches (20 centimeters), and 69 karst records against 397 others.

Whether the pattern holds in grassland, farmland or deeper soil is untested. The regional work did not measure leaf litter directly, so plant inputs enter it only through proxies.

And the jar experiment cannot say which part of weathering did the work. Limestone changes pH, calcium and probably nitrogen and phosphorus all at once.

Telling those apart, the authors wrote, needs “longer-term factorial experiments.”

Crushed rock spread on farmland is already being tested as a way to pull carbon dioxide from the air, and limestone is one of the rocks on the table.

This study does not measure how much carbon that would store, or for how long. What it offers is a mechanism to test.

The rock may add carbon to the soil through the microbes living in it, and not only through the chemistry of its own dissolving.

The full study was published in the journal National Science Review.
