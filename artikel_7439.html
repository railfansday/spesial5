Companies may need to restrict AI use to manage costs

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI and Meta have all spent enormous amounts of money to build out their artificial intelligence projects, but have yet to see returns.

The pressure is rising for artificial intelligence projects to show some level of return, with industry leaders OpenAI and Anthropic both posting massive losses in their earnings results, and xAI reducing access capacity due to non-viable cost-to-revenue calculations.

First, on OpenAI and Anthropic, two of the biggest AI players, which are both looking to launch their respective IPOs sometime soon.

As reported by Reuters, OpenAI is facing another year of heavy financial losses, with the company recently informing its investors that its projected 2026 revenue could be significantly lower than it had previously forecast.

As per Reuters: “OpenAI has told investors its annualized revenue for September was almost $50 billion, a drop ​from what it signaled earlier, a person familiar with the ‌matter told Reuters on Thursday. The company previously indicated to investors at a separate event that its revenue run rate for last month was approaching $70 billion.”

According to AI Funding Tracker, OpenAI has already weathered more than $20 billion in operating losses in the first half of 2026, with revenue of around $12 billion for the same period. The company had hoped to win more investor confidence ahead of a planned IPO with a better Q3 performance.

But a lower-than-expected revenue result could mean that OpenAI is on track to post a major financial loss in 2026.

Anthropic, meanwhile, recently shared that while its revenue is increasing, its costs are also set to skyrocket as the company spends big to keep up with the evolving AI development race.

As reported by CNBC, Anthropic reported a significant Q3 2026 revenue increase, rising almost 1,127% year over year to $17 billion. At the same time, the company also announced plans to spend more than $895 billion on compute and infrastructure over the next few years.

CNBC added that while Anthropic’s revenue is up 47% quarter over quarter, its compute and infrastructure costs increased by $14.4 billion for the nine months ended Sept. 30, up 329% compared to the same period last year.

Meanwhile, xAI owner Elon Musk told Grok Bot users on Nov. 1 that the company had implemented new restrictions on access because “the cost of serving Grok Bot was unsustainably high relative to revenue.”

Taken alongside these other notes, this is pretty much a summary of the entire AI industry, as companies pour more and more money into AI projects without a clear revenue pathway to justify that investment.

Meta is facing the same challenges. The company has seen strong early adoption of its Muse agents, which are its big hope for driving consumer AI adoption. However, Meta has also committed hundreds of billions of dollars to AI development and support infrastructure, which sets the bar very high for a cash flow-positive result.

Indeed, based on the company’s existing financial commitments to AI projects, it’s got a long, long way to go.

In 2025, Meta generated $200.97 billion, with $4.8 billion of that coming from non-advertising intake, which includes AI subscriptions.

In order to break even on its current AI spending commitments, Meta would need to generate around $100 billion per year from AI subscriptions and partnerships over the next decade.

In other words, Meta needs to build a whole new business offering that generates half as much income as one of the most profitable businesses in the world if it hopes to even come close to turning its AI investment into a profit anytime before 2036.

The operational cost-to-benefit ratio is almost too high to fathom. Moreover, with AI projects continuing to face opposition and negative perception increasing among consumers, many companies may be forced to reconsider their approaches and re-examine the openness of their AI models.

Restricting usage would reduce costs, as Musk said, but it could also slow adoption.

Then again, maybe taking generative AI tools out of the hands of the public, and reducing the amount of AI slop being published to social apps, would lead to a better outcome overall.

Doing that could also make it cost-prohibitive for scammers to generate AI replicas of other people’s content, which is an issue that Instagram Chief Adam Mosseri recently highlighted as a major concern.

Essentially, the cost crunch could soon see AI tools reducing their usage limits, which could lead to less AI use overall.

The question then is whether Meta will end up winning by eating the losses that other companies can’t in order to maximize the take-up of its Muse agents.
