Tracking water in real time could help scientists catch harmful algal blooms before they spread

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

DNA read from Florida lagoon water turned up 11 kinds of harmful algae, most at trace levels and often several at one site.

A red tide looks like something that arrives. One week the water is clear, and the next it’s stained and fish are washing up dead. In a lagoon on Florida’s east coast, the algae behind it were there the whole time.

Researchers who read the DNA in the lagoon’s water found 11 kinds of harmful algae, each a species known to cause blooms. Most were at levels so low that a microscope would likely have missed them, and they tended to turn up in the same places.

Finding them together is the surprise. Several harmful species sharing one stretch of water suggests they answer to the same triggers or help each other grow.

Justin Gibbons, a computational biologist at the USF College of Public Health, led the work with Rays H.Y. Jiang, an associate professor of genomics there.

Research colleagues came from Florida Atlantic University’s Harbor Branch Oceanographic Institute.

The water was collected in 2018 and 2019, and the method has not yet been tested as a way to warn of a bloom ahead of time.

The Indian River Lagoon runs 156 miles (251 kilometers) along the coast, from a temperate climate in the north to a subtropical one in the south. The team sampled 20 sites along it in the wet season of 2018 and again in the dry season of 2019.

At each site, three 17-ounce (500 milliliter) bottles were filled below the surface and chilled. The water was filtered within six hours, and the team pulled DNA off the filters. The river DNA test lists every species that shed cells upstream.

The sequencer was a MinION, a palm-sized, portable device that reads long stretches of DNA. Microbial monitoring often reads a bacterial gene called 16S.

Algae aren’t bacteria, so the team read a gene called 18S, which cells with a nucleus carry, and they read it nearly end to end.

In an interview with Earth.com, Jiang said that choice covers a blind spot in bloom monitoring. “Full-length 18S helps reveal the culprits.”

Across four sequencing runs, the team identified more than 200 named species. Between a third and 45 percent of the reads matched nothing in the reference library, so the true count may be higher.

Each species was then checked against the harmful algae list kept by UNESCO’s oceanographic commission. Eleven matched, counting the brown tide species that the team added to the list, and two stood out.

Brown tide alga Aureoumbra lagunensis appeared in 13 of the 40 samples. In the northern lagoon and the Banana River, it reached more than half of all the DNA reads at some sites, with its highest levels in the dry season.

Biologists counting cells in the Banana River had recorded an unusually long brown tide there across 2018 and 2019.

Northern stretches are where the lagoon’s trouble has concentrated. They take nitrogen from household septic tanks and hold their water longer than the south does, and they suffered a large-scale seagrass die-off in the 2010s.

Of every stretch sampled, the Banana River had the lowest diversity and the highest share of harmful algae species.

Pyrodinium bahamense, which makes the nerve poison saxitoxin, showed up at four northern sites in the wet season and at none in the dry season. It reached at most 4 percent of the reads.

Toxin sampling from the same places and months had found saxitoxin too.

Nine other harmful species each stayed below 1 percent. One of them, Gonyaulax bohaiensis, had not been recorded in North America before, and it turned up at seven sites.

“The result that stood out to me the most was the frequent co-occurrence of the harmful algal bloom species,” Gibbons said.

“This suggests that there’s either shared ecological drivers for them or that they somehow interact and promote the growth of each other.”

Most stretches of the lagoon held more than one harmful species. Asked what surprised her about species showing up together, Jiang told Earth.com that she kept her answer to the forecasting problem.

“A bloom is a community response, not just one species,” she said. “Forecasting needs to capture that complexity.”

In the same interview, Greg O’Corry-Crowe, a research professor at Harbor Branch, said the physical and chemical triggers have had most of the attention so far.

“A major challenge is understanding what the triggers are that lead to periodic explosive growth, where the benign becomes the harmful,” he said.

A read count is a share, not a cell count. The MinION misreads more letters than the standard lab sequencers do, so the team wrote that the rarest detections should be treated with caution.

The primers that copy the gene may also favor some species over others.

Two samples came from water so cloudy with a Pyrodinium bloom that less of it could be filtered, and the rarest species there may have been missed.

One toxic diatom group that is common in the lagoon, Pseudo-nitzschia, appeared only when the team searched at the genus level.

The samples are also seven to eight years old. Whether a reading taken at the water’s edge can flag a bloom before anyone sees one is the claim the study sets up and does not test.

Monitoring programs already count cells under a microscope and measure toxins directly, and Jiang said the sequencer belongs beside them rather than in their place.

“The technology exists, and monitoring exists,” she said. “The next step is integrating rapid sequencing with established standards like cell counts and toxin measurements.”

The open question is whether species that share a site also share a trigger. The team proposed tracking which harmful algae rise together, as a first step toward a bloom forecast.

A warning that comes before a mass die-off rather than after one is the payoff that would justify the effort. For now, though, the record is two seasons of water from one lagoon, read once.

The full study was published in the journal PLOS Water.
