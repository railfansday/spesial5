Vanguard Short-Term Treasury ETF vs. iShares Corporate Bond ETF

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

VGSH prioritizes government-backed stability with lower volatility, while IGSB diversifies across 4,700+ corporate issues for higher income potential.

By Sarah Sidlow – Nov 19, 2026 at 6:15AM EST

The Vanguard Short-Term Treasury ETF (VGSH -0.06%) provides government-backed security stability, whereas the iShares 1-5 Year Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF (IGSB -0.01%) offers higher income through diversified investment-grade corporate debt.

Investors often choose between government and corporate debt based on their need for safety versus potential income. While both funds target the shorter end of the maturity curve, they offer distinct risk profiles. This analysis compares a fund backed by the U.S. government against one that captures yields from corporate borrowers.

Beta measures price volatility relative to the S&P 500; beta is calculated from monthly returns over the available fund history (up to five years). The 1-yr return represents total return over the trailing 12 months. Dividend yield is the trailing-12-month distribution yield.

The Vanguard fund is slightly more affordable with its 0.03% expense ratio. However, the iShares fund provides a notably higher payout, reflecting the additional yield typically required by investors to hold corporate debt over government securities.

The Vanguard Short-Term Treasury ETF holds 91 securities, focusing on debt issued by the U.S. Treasury with maturities between one and three years. It was launched in 2009. Vanguard Short-Term Treasury ETF has paid $2.19 per share over the trailing 12 months, which on its recent ~$57.6 share price works out to a 3.8% yield.

The iShares 1-5 Year Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF holds 4,807 securities, emphasizing high-quality corporate debt with remaining maturities ranging from one to five years. The fund is highly diversified -- no single position exceeds 0.29% of the portfolio. It was launched in 2007. iShares 1-5 Year Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF has paid $2.40 per share over the trailing 12 months, which on its recent ~$51.5 share price works out to a 4.7% yield.

Income investors will likely be drawn to IGSB's 4.7% dividend yield, which is more than 4 times the S&P 500's current yield. But as is often the case, that higher payout comes with slightly higher risk. IGSB holds corporate debt, and companies theoretically face a greater risk of defaulting on their loans than the U.S. government. That said, IGSB's portfolio of nearly 5,000 holdings and its focus on high-quality debt mitigates a good deal of that risk.

VGSH is likely the better play for investors looking for maximum capital preservation. Moreover, its massive size will appeal to conservative investors who are looking for maximum liquidity. Plus, the interest from U.S. Treasuries is usually exempt from state and local taxes, which could appeal to high earners.

The shorter maturities for short-term bond funds mean investors are shielded from interest rate fluctuations, and these vehicles typically generate more income than cash or money market funds. For most investors looking to juice their passive income, IGSB is a solid choice. But if you're looking for extra security or have specific tax considerations, the short-term Treasuries in VGSH could be right for you.
