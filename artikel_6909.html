Kyle MacLachlan and Michelle Zauner Celebrate the Power of the Written Word at the National Book Awards

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Every product on this page was chosen by a Harper's BAZAAR editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

The evening’s nominees included authors in fiction, nonfiction, and poetry, among others

The 77th National Book Awards opened with a song. Japanese Breakfast’s Michelle Zauner, also the author of the best-selling Crying at H Mart, walked onto the stage to serenade the crowd with her loving ballad “Be Sweet.” She then welcomed the evening’s host, Kyle MacLachlan, who did a theatrical reading of the children’s tale Paddle-to-the-Sea by Holling C. Holling. The actor, who just recently published a memoir, Fictional Selves, shared tales of his first library card, his now-ironic childhood obsession with Frank Herbert’s Dune (he’d go on to star in David Lynch’s ’80s adaptation), and the beauty of reading stories to his children—the last being of the utmost importance.

Two Lifetime Achievement Awards were handed out first. Perri Klass, the medical director of Reach Out and Read, received the Literarian Award for Outstanding Service to the American Literary Community. “[Children] need to grow up with their bodies and their brains cherished and protected,” she said to the crowd after taking the stage with a stethoscope and a copy of Goodnight Moon. “They need to grow up properly nourished. Their minds and their bodies.” Next, Salman Rushdie, the 79-year-old Indian-British writer who has devoted his life to fighting censorship after several of his books were banned in multiple countries, received the National Book Foundation’s Medal for Distinguished Contribution to American Letters.

“This boy is born in Bombay at the end of the British Empire—79 years ago—born just before the freedom that came at midnight, and that same boy improbably ends up halfway around the world on a stage in New York City with this medal dangling from his neck,” Rushdie said in his acceptance speech. “If that's not magic realism, I don't know what is. I have given my life to the art of words, to the creation of books and to their defense, to the exercise of artistic freedom, and to an insistence of the importance of that freedom.”

David Levithan and Gabriel Duckels’s The Fight of Our Lives: AIDS in America, a nonfiction book tracing the history of the AIDS epidemic and the first grassroots movements, was the recipient of the Young People’s Literature Award. “Our aim with this book has been to write a history of HIV and AIDS that didn’t oversimplify or generalize, that centers HIV and AIDS as not just part of the past but part of the present, an epidemic whose legacy young people all deserve to understand,” Duckels said in his acceptance speech. “We will not let the noise of others drown out the music of who we are,” Levithan added, highlighting the need to protest trans and nonbinary youth.

The award for best nonfiction went to Huna: A Memoir of Revolution, Prison, and Becoming by the Egyptian author Abdelrahman ElGendy, which his editor, Helen Thomaides, accepted after ElGendy was denied entry into the country. The memoir details his six years in a political prison after being arrested during the 2013 military coup in Egypt. “I used to fantasize about writing a memoir,” Thomaides read from ElGendy’s note. “I would slip under a bright-red duvet. My mother had bribed the guards to let through and imagine that everything happening to me would one day end up in a book.”

The night concluded with the fiction award, given to Hannah Lillith Assadi for Paradiso 17, which follows a Palestinian man’s lifelong search for home. “I’ve watched cancer ravage my father's body, and genocide ravaged his people and their children,” Assadi said in her acceptance speech. “Perhaps this is the Palestinian story on this earth. So beautiful, so painful. And isn’t that story all of ours? Maybe that is why we long for that land, because it reflects the beauty and pain in us.”

In an evening dominated by the heavy and relentless state of the world, MacLachlan brought levity, sprinkling in jokes about dating Laura Dern (“If you ask Claude what it’s like to date Laura Dern, it’s almost exactly my words”), criticizing censorship and AI (“We need to stick together, especially in what feels like a never-ending fight against censorship in our libraries, in our schools. Every time you think the battle is won, it pops up again. It’s a war out there, and every one of you here tonight is on the front line.”) and noted how new tech problems place intense pressure on the publishing industry, adding, “No wonder you’re drinking so heavily!” to uproarous laughter.

But the spirit of the evening was best captured by The Invisible Mountain author Caro De Robertis, who chaired the fiction category, as she introduced the award. “From this vantage point, U.S. literature is alive and radiant and thriving,” she said. “Do not let anybody tell you different. Books are essential, and they aren’t going anywhere.”
