AI’s Last-Mile Problem: Why Working AI Tools Don’t Always Deliver Business Value

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

At one point in an AI project, everyone gets excited. The team has tested the idea, the results are promising and the conversation turns to what it could mean beyond the group that built it. But expanding a controlled test across the business raises new questions about workflows, ownership and what needs to change.

Technology rarely slows progress. The harder questions emerge after the proof of concept: Who owns the process? How does the solution fit into existing workflows? How will the organization maintain it as the technology changes?

These questions are part of what is being called the last mile of AI. The organization has proven that something can work. The challenge is making it work consistently enough, and broadly enough, to become part of how the business operates.

Consider a customer service team that tests AI to summarize interactions and identify follow-up actions. During the pilot, a few employees use the tool successfully, and the team sees that it can reduce the time spent documenting calls.

That result matters, but scaling the idea requires different decisions. How does the summary enter the customer record? Who reviews it? What happens when the output is incomplete or incorrect? Is the workflow still efficient when the entire team uses it? How will the organization measure whether the time savings continue after the initial enthusiasm fades?

Moving from a promising experiment to something the business can rely on means figuring out how it fits into how people actually work, who needs to be involved and what has to change for it to work beyond the pilot.​

One reason AI initiatives struggle to move beyond experimentation is that organizations often add AI to existing processes without rethinking them.

A finance team might use AI to summarize a monthly report but still require the same manual steps, reviews and handoffs as before. A sales team might use AI to draft proposals but still route every proposal through a workflow designed for a fully manual process.

In those situations, the technology may be working exactly as intended while the business captures only a fraction of its potential value.

Moving through the last mile often requires asking a more fundamental question: If this capability is now available, should the process itself work differently? That does not mean rebuilding an entire function around AI. It means identifying where AI changes the work sequence, removes unnecessary steps or lets people spend more time on judgment-based decisions.

The most valuable implementation creates a better way of working by connecting AI to the surrounding process.

Ownership becomes especially important when an AI initiative moves from experimentation into daily operations.

During a pilot, responsibility often sits naturally with the team that created it. Once the initiative becomes part of a broader workflow, that ownership can become less clear. Technology may own the platform. A department may own the process. Employees may be responsible for reviewing outputs. Leadership may be responsible for the business result.

Without clear accountability, small issues can quickly become organizational problems.

A good implementation defines ownership before the solution scales. Someone should be accountable for the business outcome, someone should understand how the workflow is maintained and employees should know where human judgment remains necessary. Clear responsibilities help teams address problems while keeping the initiative focused on its intended outcome.

This is also where measurement becomes more useful than enthusiasm. If an AI initiative is expected to reduce processing time, improve response rates, reduce errors or increase capacity, someone should be responsible for knowing whether that result continues after implementation. AI doesn’t create lasting value just because employees use it. The organization has to own what the use is supposed to accomplish and have a way to determine whether it is actually delivering that value.

An AI solution can perform well in isolation and still struggle inside the business.

A tool that produces a useful output in a standalone environment may create little value if employees have to move information manually between systems, duplicate work or maintain another process to use it.

That is why the last mile often has more to do with integration than invention. The question is no longer just whether AI can produce a useful result. It is whether that result can become part of the systems and workflows employees already rely on.

For example, an AI system that identifies important information from customer conversations becomes far more useful when that information can flow into the systems where sales, service or operations teams already work. The goal is to make the improved workflow part of how work already gets done, with technology supporting the process rather than creating another layer employees have to manage.

The closer AI is to the actual operating process, the more likely its value is to persist.

Organizations often talk about scaling AI as though it means giving more employees access to a successful tool. In practice, scaling is a much broader operational decision.

Before expanding an AI initiative, leaders should understand whether the process is repeatable, ownership is clear, the necessary systems are connected and the organization can keep measuring the intended outcome. They also need to account for change. AI capabilities will evolve, models will improve, vendors will change their products and a workflow that works today may need adjustment six months from now.

That is why successful AI implementation requires more than proving that a technology works. It requires building an operating model that can adapt as the technology changes, while keeping the original business objective at the center of those decisions.​
