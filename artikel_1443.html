Spitting row mars feisty Millwall-West Ham clash

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

A spitting row marred the 100th Millwall-West Ham United derby and first for 14 years.

Taty Castellanos was facing trial by video after being accused of spitting on Filip Marschall during Saturday’s febrile 2-2 draw at the Den.

Millwall goalkeeper Marschall made the allegation to referee Paul Tierney, who was expected to include it in an official report that would then be assessed by the Football Association.

Spitting on an opponent is punishable with a six-match ban.

Telegraph Sport has been told West Ham striker Castellanos denies he spat on Marschall and believes he will be vindicated by video footage of the incident.

Lying little prick. Taty didn't spit ❌ pic.twitter.com/5vYxOtKqof

Millwall manager Alex Neil said after the game that fourth official Lewis Smith had confirmed a spitting accusation had been made.

Asked if he had spoken to Marschall about the claim, Neil replied: “I haven’t asked him about it. But it’s like anything else; I’m sure there’ll be footage of it, or not of it, if it didn’t happen.

“I’d be extremely surprised if Fil’s made an accusation and nothing’s happened. But that’s not really for me to deal with; I’m sure the authorities will deal with that.”

West Ham manager Nuno Espírito Santo said: “I’m aware of the allegation. I didn’t see the image; I didn’t speak with Taty. I cannot go much further on that.”

He added: “I’m going to try to look at the images.”

There were also ugly scenes at full time when players had to be separated at the end of a derby that had been at the centre of an unprecedented police operation.

There were six arrests as of 4pm on Saturday for offences including tailgating, assault on a police officer and entering or attempting to enter a stadium while intoxicated.

Eleven men were also arrested at around 7.15pm on Friday on suspicion of conspiracy to commit criminal damage.

The group, believed to be Millwall fans, were detained in the vicinity of a known West Ham supporters’ pub in Stratford.

Saturday’s fixture was subject to a five-mile dispersal zone, the largest ever for a football match in London, with officers granted special powers to stop and search people and vehicles without reasonable suspicion and to remove anyone causing disorder.

Riot police were out in force outside the ground, with dog units and mounted officers among 400 personnel deployed – roughly quadruple the usual number for a game there.

Those and other measures – including the use of live facial recognition, a strict segregation policy and a 12.30pm kick-off to minimise supporters’ pre-match drinking time – prevented a repeat of the kind of violence which has marred this fixture down the years.

West Ham fans also received a police escort to a game that saw both sets of supporters hurl foul-mouthed insults at each other throughout.

Plans to keep rival spectators apart after the match were reportedly tested by scuffles that broke out as people left the ground.

If the police were ready for a fight before the game, West Ham’s players were not as Millwall tore into them from the first whistle and dominated a first half in which they scored twice through Caleb Taylor and Camiel Neghli and hit the crossbar through a stunning 35-yard strike by Mark Sykes.

This rivalry was forged in different circumstances from most derby confrontations. There is none of the religious divide of Celtic and Rangers, or the politics of Barcelona against Real Madrid, or the spat over title-winning superiority of Manchester United and Liverpool. This is a tale of proximity, an internecine feud.

“There’s no politics, no nationality, nothing like that,” says Nick Hart, who hosts the weekly podcast Achtung! Millwall. “We’re very similar people, from the same backgrounds, same communities. But it’s in the Millwall supporter’s DNA for West Ham to be the ultimate rivals. Nobody else comes into the equation. You’re brought up on this stuff. It’s your history.”

Though possibly more recent history than might be suggested by the two clubs’ early foundations. The hostility that West Ham will face at the Den is no more about dockland rivalry, north-south London disputes or strike-breaking than the animosity when Manchester United visit Anfield is caused by the building of the Manchester Ship Canal.

“It really is about the hooligan era,” says a podcaster who uses the pseudonym Former West Ham Employee on the West Ham Way podcast. “The two clubs had notorious fanbases. And they grated. It came about through vying to be the No 1, battling to see who had the hardest mob. Whenever we’ve played – or even been in close proximity – things have happened.”

There have only been 18 meetings between these fierce rivals since the Second World War, but absence has certainly not made the heart grow fonder. Here are some of the feud’s most notable flashpoints.

At the 1972 testimonial in honour of Harry Cripps – the renowned Millwall hard man – police horses were sent on to the pitch after West Ham supporters entered the home section at The Den. Fighting spilt over into the streets surrounding the ground.

An even bleaker turn came in 1976, when Millwall fan Ian Pratt died in a skirmish with West Ham fans at New Cross station that led to his fall under a train.

Leaflets were later handed out in the Den’s Cold Blow Lane end with a grainy picture of Pratt and a declaration in capital letters: “A West Ham fan must die next week to avenge him.”

Millwall plundered four goals on Mothering Sunday in 2004 as Tim Cahill’s double helped them to a 4-1 victory over West Ham at the Den in an incident-filled derby.

The hosts missed a penalty and had another saved by Stephen Bywater, who was later sent off. Violence followed in the stands and the match was named “The Mothers’ Day Massacre”.

Mother's Day Massacre ⏮️A 4-1 victory over The Hammers back in 2004. pic.twitter.com/TLi7xkcVJG

A hundred West Ham and Millwall supporters clashed during a screening of England’s 2006 World Cup match against Paraguay in Canada Square, which led to 16 injuries and one hospitalisation.

The clubs’ League Cup meeting in 2009 at Upton Park was listed as Category Five by police, the most serious risk of violence, and resulted in a Millwall supporter being stabbed in Priory Road, near the ground.

The Metropolitan Police said that “planned” scuffles, which involved more than 100 fans, appeared to have been caused by fans without tickets to the game.

Nuno Espirito Santo has only been through the door at West Ham for a matter of months, but he insisted he is fully aware of the significance of today’s fixture.

When asked about his perception of the rivalry, Nuno said: “Since the fixture list came out, this date has been [important], for both of the clubs. It’s special, special game. We look forward to go and compete against a good team.

“It’s a different game from all of the other games, it’s a special game, the atmosphere will be different… Steve [Potts, first-team coach and former West Ham player] has been involved in many of these games, he’s constantly speaking about how special it is for West Ham fans, so we have a lot of information and we are fully aware of the importance of the game.

On potential unrest between fans and the significant police presence, Nuno added: “What we want most is that everything goes well, both fans supporting their teams and no conflict. We have people inside the club who are taking care of that. We have to focus on our tasks.”

Riot police are very much out in force outside The Den today for the 100th competitive derby between Millwall and West Ham United and first for 14 years. Dog units and mounted officers can been seen patrolling the perimeter of the ground, with 400 personnel deployed to stop violence erupting between rival fans – roughly quadruple the usual number for a fixture here. There has been no sign of any trouble with less than an hour to go before kick-off.

The game has been scheduled for 12.30pm to minimise supporters’ pre-match drinking time, although plenty of fans have been nursing pints before entering the ground. Other measures in place include the use of live facial recognition cameras, while a dispersal order has been imposed covering a large area around the stadium. Police were also planning to escort West Ham fans from South Bermondsey station, which will also be closed to home supporters for up to an hour after full-time. The latter measure has been deemed safer than holding the 1,800 away fans back following the game.

The Millwall-West Ham derby is one of the most notorious in English football and was most recently marred by serious violence when the sides met at Upton Park for a League Cup tie back in 2009. One man was stabbed and the match itself was interrupted on three occasions by fans invading the pitch.

A Section 35 order authorises a Police Constable and a Police Community Support Officer in uniform to “exclude a person from an area for a period of up to 48 hours with an Inspector’s authority”.

Good morning and welcome to our live coverage of the highly anticipated Championship match between Millwall and West Ham United as the two clubs meet officially for the 100th time. This fixture has not been staged since 2012, and the intervening 14 years have done little to temper the ill-feeling between their passionate supporters, who will be anxious to claim bragging rights over their Dockers Derby rivals. The Metropolitan Police have placed a Section 35 dispersal order in the area surrounding The Den and will deploy 400 officers on the streets of east London.

Acrimony between the two clubs has existed since the late 19th century when Millwall Athletic, originally Rovers, battled it out against Thames Ironworks, later renamed West Ham at the Athletic and Memorial Grounds. Sixty of the two clubs’ 99 meetings came between 1899 and 1915, and while they played just seven times between 1946 and 1987, the irregularity of the game only added to its mystique, which has often spilt over into the ugly.

Both head coaches spoke at length about the significance of today’s match, with Alex Neil, in the Millwall dugout, describing it as an encounter that means “absolutely everything” but imploring his players to keep “cool heads”. His counterpart Nuno Espírito Santo revealed that he had been well advised about the game’s importance by Steve Potts, his first-team coach and former West Ham player.

Since the club’s last meeting in 2012, West Ham have ascended into the Premier League, moved into the London Stadium and won the Uefa Conference League, only falling back into England’s second tier last summer where Millwall have remained for all but two seasons. The Lions brushed maddeningly close to promotion in their last campaign as they pushed for an automatic spot until the very last weekend of the regular season before falling to Hull City in the play-off semi-finals.

Much of the pre-match talk will centre on the clubs’ long-standing rivalry, but beneath the bile and belligerence is an intriguing football match between two sides. Millwall are missing 11 first-team players and face the daunting task of stopping a West Ham side who have found their feet in the Championship, scoring 16 goals in their last four league matches.
