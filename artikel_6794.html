Before &amp; After: Dark, Underutilized Storage Spaces Make Way for a Cozy Library, Breakfast Nook, and Family Room

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

An intentional gut renovation turned dead space into room for a growing family.

With certain homes, it can take time (and a designer’s eye) to realize the full potential. For some, that means living within a less-than-perfect layout for a while to really understand the pain points. When a couple with twin boys bought this Greek Revival farmhouse in Connecticut’s tony Fairfield County in 2022, they knew changes were in store down the line.

“It didn’t feel like them yet,” says the project’s interior designer, Vivian Shao Chen. “The house was in great condition, but the layout didn’t work for how they lived.” The 1940s home had a considerable amount of dead space, including entire rooms and shadowy corners that felt disconnected from the rest of the home.

On top of that, planning a rework around the two youngsters was an additional challenge. “The question was always how a space can grow with them: what deserves the investment and what should stay flexible,” Chen adds.

Once Chen began working on the project in 2024, she refocused the homeowners' attention away from the idea of a home addition and sparse, “Scandinavian beach house” aesthetic toward a warmer, more traditional sensibility in line with the house’s original period style.

“I always start with trying to understand existing site conditions as much as possible,” Chen says. “What is the architectural language of the house? Where is it inconsistent, what can we edit? Often, very small but thoughtful changes can make all the difference.”

Ultimately, a gut renovation was necessary to address the home’s layout limitations, and new millwork was added throughout to provide character and a strong visual throughline. Now, each space has its own unique identity and every corner is packed with purpose—with plenty of wiggle room to allow for the young family’s necessary changes over time.

“This house feels so warm, cozy, and grounding,” Chen says. “Our hope is that we've designed for their current season of life and set a good foundation for the future without overdesigning, leaving room for the house to change as life does.”

A formerly nondescript pass-through area, the home’s entry now gives visitors a proper greeting with its lush Sandberg wallpaper bridging indoors and out. “It speaks to the beautiful Connecticut setting we are in and also says, ‘Welcome, come in and relax, it’s not too formal here,’” notes Chen.

Additionally, choosing darker tones in a foyer or hallway is a great way to emphasize an airier mood elsewhere. “These transition spaces are meant to feel cozy, enveloping and a little moody, so when you step into the open expanse of the living room, with all its light and views, the house seems to exhale,” the designer adds.

One space that was spared a major redesign was the living room, as its existing architecture already suited the household’s needs. “The living room has always been a breath of fresh air in the house, being the first big expanse you walk into, with wonderful views out onto the property and beautiful daylight,” Chen attests.

A touch-up was still in order, though. Chen refreshed the wall color and chose updated furnishings upholstered in velvet and linen to complement the family’s antique Chinese chest, which became the natural focal point in the room. “The contrasting elements balance out the lightness of the room in a way that feels quiet and grounding,” she adds.

One of the biggest triumphs of a home renovation is when an awkward spare room becomes the new go-to gathering spot—and that’s exactly what happened with this former storage space-turned-library.

Chen removed an en suite powder room, which helped anchor the layout around a seating area instead of feeling transitory. She then wrapped the room in quarter-sawn white oak millwork, which layered in all the stately personality that the former space was missing.

The millwork, while providing shelving and storage opportunities, also included a media center, a built-in desk, and a daybed. “The daybed cushions are intentionally sized like twin mattresses, so with a fitted sheet they become an extra bed for guests or sleepovers,” Chen says.

The space is now appropriately flexible, able to be cordoned off from the main living room with pocket doors or inviting enough for games or movies, with classic library sconces that make it especially cozy at night.

“The kitchen was a gut renovation, but in reviewing the layout, I felt like the bones were mostly right,” Chen muses. “It just needed a lot of editing and new finishes.” Without altering the footprint, Chen maximized the flow of the space by removing the ceiling beams, relocating the refrigerator, scaling up the windows overlooking the yard, and adding interior windows to open up views to the adjacent mudroom and breakfast room.

Then there was the kitchen island: a solid white oak piece on custom turned legs, topped with a seamless Calacatta Monet slab that Chen calls “sublime.” Overhead, an artful single pendant by Roll & Hill adds a far more impactful ambiance than a trio ever could. “It’s about finding restraint and negative space where it can make an impact,” Chen notes.

One notable omission in the cook space is the presence of upper cabinets—Chen chose hutch cabinets instead that “hide a microwave, a pantry, and a coffee station.” Apart from the millwork, finishes, and fixtures, the kitchen also includes all-new appliances, aside from the statement-making blue La Cornue range that was already in the home.

Even if breakfasting outdoors is only realistic for a few months in the Northeast, this nook right off the kitchen now provides the illusion of alfresco enjoyment. Originally, the room felt too out of step with the rest of the home, and even its most desirable feature—the view out of the picture window—was hard to appreciate in such a segmented space.

Chen reimagined the room as an interior porch, replacing the entry wall with a muntin division to lure guests from the kitchen to this sunny spot. The nook’s newly added bay windows provide more light and dimension, and its nickel-gap paneling has a soft, rustic touch. Chen also tucked a built-in banquette into the bay for the most obvious purpose: “You're now sitting in the view, not just looking at it.”

Through the breakfast room (and another set of muntin dividers) is the cozy, cocooning dining room. Rather than flooding this windowless room with light, Chen employed a clever strategy to maximize the ambiance. “The custom plaster finish on the walls and ceiling has a subtle sheen that catches the light, so what was once the room's drawback is now its greatest charm,” she notes.

Basements without a proper design outlook are destined to become clutter overflow areas. That’s exactly what was happening on the home’s lower level, so a gut renovation was in order.

To make the space more livable, Chen added millwork to conceal laundry and utility access, plush furnishings, and a new door and windows that open up to a walk-out terrace. Though the low ceiling had to stay, new nickel-gap paneling makes the space feel cozy rather than cramped. “The room reads as a true family room,” she says.

The former mudroom was a rough-and-tumble catch-all passage, but considering that this area functions as the family’s main entrance, it deserved a gut renovation that they could take pride in—starting with the floor. Chen swapped out the no-nonsense tile for a striking limestone checkerboard. “The light squares are actually the same limestone as the kitchen floor, only done in a different pattern,” she says.

New lockers and coat cabinets along the perimeter conceal all the out-the-door essentials, while a floating console, clad in drab olive, has the look of an antique find but was custom-made for the space. It functions as the necessary landing zone that every bustling family home needs; Chen intentionally kept the bottom open for sliding packages and storage baskets (and perhaps the odd shoe) out of the way.

This office bathroom was previously awash in neutrals but still managed to look dark and drab, so Chen revitalized it with a much-needed dose of drama. Her go-to design reference? Old New York members’ clubs.

“Instead of wrapping the room in dark wood, we kept the stained wood to the custom vanity and let color and pattern do the rest,” she notes. Outfitted with the white penny-tile floor, striped zellige in the shower, and racing-green color drenching, the space now nods to tradition while keeping it playful.

“The primary bedroom already had so much going for it: beautiful windows, generous proportions, and absolutely peaceful,” Chen attests. “Our goal was to keep and elevate those qualities.”

Chen amplified the room’s natural daylight with a fresh coat of Farrow & Ball's Slipper Satin, then softened the space with more natural textures, including a slipcovered linen bed, woven linen for the drapery, and a textured wool rug. Even though the bedroom has one of the most restrained palettes in the whole home, it celebrates textural variation.

To maintain the same serene but texturally rich feel as the adjoining primary bedroom, the bathroom needed a layout and material rework. To make the most of a modest footprint, Chen opted for slim Shaker linen cabinets and a floating vanity. “Seeing the floor continue beneath it tricks the eye, making the room feel larger than its actual standing space,” she says.

The room’s warm marble floors and soft off-white color also provide an ideal backdrop for the room’s showstopper moment: a Calacatta Viola slab shower and backsplash, with deep, wine-toned veining.

For now, the twin boys share this bedroom, so the existing architecture was left alone. Instead, the focus was on enriching the palette with taupe, cream, blue, and copper tones and choosing furnishings that can flex over time. “The art is by the boys themselves, which was so special to incorporate into the room,” says Chen.

Vivian Shao Chen is an interior designer specializing in whole-home designs, including interior architecture. She has over 15 years of architectural training and professional experience, and focuses on residential interiors and lifestyle design, including home goods and clothing.
