NTSB: Missed Inspections Let Storm-Damaged Southwest 737 MAX Fly 44 Times Before Dutch Roll Accident

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

This article was updated on Sunday, November 8, 2026, to include a statement from Southwest Airlines and details of its safety changes. It was originally published on Saturday, November 7, 2026.

A Southwest Airlines Boeing 737 MAX flew 44 times with undetected storm damage to its tail after missing a post-storm inspection, then later passing a flawed maintenance test. On May 25, 2024, the aircraft experienced a Dutch roll event — classified as an accident due to the substantial damage to the plane — prompting an investigation by the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB).

In its final report, the NTSB found that severe winds damaged the plane while it was parked in New Orleans nine days earlier. The damage affected the standby rudder power control unit's (PCU) mounting structure and its connection to the vertical stabilizer, resulting in improper rudder operation. However, a series of maintenance oversights allowed the damage to go undetected for nine days and 44 flights.

The aircraft involved was a Boeing 737 MAX 8 (registration N8825Q), which had been in service for less than 18 months when the following events occurred. On May 16, 2024, it was parked overnight at Louis Armstrong New Orleans International Airport (MSY) when strong winds damaged the vertical stabilizer structure around the standby rudder PCU. However, the aircraft showed no obvious external damage, and no inspection was conducted.

N8825Q then left New Orleans the following morning and completed 44 flights over the next nine days. Then, on May 25, while operating Southwest flight 746 from Phoenix Sky Harbor International Airport (PHX) to Oakland International Airport (OAK), the crew experienced "a small amount of Dutch roll" at 34,000 feet, which lasted a few seconds and occurred again at 32,000 feet.

A Dutch roll is defined by the NTSB as "a coupled oscillation in an airplane’s yaw and roll axes." The phenomenon causes an aircraft's nose and wings to oscillate in opposing movements, potentially making it difficult to control. The 737 MAX continued and landed safely in Oakland, but inspections found damage to the standby PCU's mounting bracket and surrounding vertical stabilizer structure.

Investigators also found that a scheduled maintenance test in Houston two days earlier had been incorrectly signed off, despite evidence that the rudder was not moving correctly. The NTSB also found that the airline did not notify it of the event until June 7, 2024, almost two weeks after flight 746 landed in Oakland. By that time, the aircraft’s cockpit voice recorder had been overwritten, leaving investigators without the recording. The NTSB stated the probable cause as:

"Damage to the standby rudder power control unit connection to the vertical fin due to an extreme high wind event while the airplane was parked, which remained undetected and resulted in anomalous operation of the standby rudder power control unit."

Boeing's maintenance manual for the 737 included inspections for aircraft exposed to extreme winds, but it did not specify a wind speed threshold. Southwest also had no procedure for alerting maintenance control when parked aircraft experienced extreme winds. As a result, no inspection was carried out immediately after the storm.

Following the storm damage, the aircraft completed 38 flights before it underwent a scheduled overnight maintenance check in Houston on May 23, just two days before the Dutch roll event. This included a test requiring a mechanic to observe the aircraft's tail and visually confirm that the rudder moved correctly in both directions.

However, hangar footage of that test showed the rudder moving to the right but not to the left, with nobody positioned at the rear to observe it. Despite this, Southwest's maintenance records showed the test as passed, and the aircraft returned to service. The following day, the crew of the aircraft's fourth flight after the Houston check disconnected the yaw damper after it appeared to overcorrect during turbulence.

The yaw damper was deferred under the minimum equipment list (MEL), and the jet flew one more sector to Phoenix, where the system passed a functional test and was returned to service. Flight 746 was the aircraft's sixth flight since the Houston check and its 44th since the storm in New Orleans.

The term may sound delicious, but these safety-related occurrences are anything but tasty.

Following the accident, Boeing revised its 737 maintenance manual in May 2025, requiring an extreme high-wind inspection when ground winds reach 65 knots (75 mph). This is close to but still below the 73-knot (84 mph) gusts recorded at New Orleans on the night N8825Q was damaged.

Meanwhile, Airbus sets a higher threshold for most of its aircraft, requiring an inspection when ground winds exceed 80 knots (92 mph). Southwest subsequently conducted checks across its entire MAX fleet. Between June 17 and 20, 2024, the airline inspected all 231 737 MAX aircraft in its fleet at the time, finding no similar damage.

Responding to a request for comment from Simple Flying, Southwest said it had taken steps to improve how it identifies potential damage after high-wind events. This includes monitoring weather reports for winds above 65 knots, which trigger an inspection protocol. Spokesperson Lynn Lunsford said:

"Southwest took steps to bolster its safety process for identifying possible damage following high wind events."

Another key change concerns the maintenance test that was incorrectly signed off in Houston. The operational test of the standby hydraulic system, performed as part of Southwest’s A Core Check, was previously listed as a single-line requirement. It now has its own work instruction card, with step-by-step instructions and separate sign-offs required from every mechanic assigned to the task.

Had the fault been caught during this scheduled test, the damage could have been discovered two days and six flights before the Dutch roll. The aircraft was later flown to Boeing's Everett facility for repairs and returned to service on July 4, 2024, less than six weeks after it was removed from service.
