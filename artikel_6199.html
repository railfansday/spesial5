The hotel industry’s distribution war has an overlooked third party: the owner. New questions about AI, loyalty and customer acquisition could make that imbalance even more consequential.

Maybe we've been looking at the hotel distribution war all wrong, all along.

For years, we at Skift have chronicled the battle between hotel brands and online travel agencies. Hilton and Marriott want guests booking direct; Booking.com and Expedia want those reservations to come through their platforms. Every new technology, most recently AI agents, is assessed by which side it will help.

The fight is real. But the usual framing leaves out a party with a very different balance sheet: the hotel owner.

The owner pays the brand for access to its commercial system and may also pay an OTA when that platform delivers the guest. The brand and the OTA negotiate over the customer, but it is often the property that must absorb the cost of both relationships and still turn a profit.

Skift argues the brand-versus-OTA distribution fight leaves out the party that absorbs the cost of both relationships: the hotel owner. Hilton's third-quarter results, reported October 27, showed $815 million in franchise and licensing revenue across a system of 8,683 franchised and licensed properties, 892 managed hotels, and 45 hotels in its ownership segment. Hilton's annual filing states franchise royalties are calculated as a percentage of gross room revenue, with separate program fees for marketing, reservations, technology, and Hilton Honors. In Skift's illustrative example, a $200 room sold through an OTA at a hypothetical 15% commission leaves the owner with a $30 acquisition charge while the royalty is still calculated on the full $200. Hilton's fee economics track revenue flowing through its network; the owner's economics depend on what is left after acquisition costs. MCR Hotels CEO Tyler Morse, who owns and operates 150 hotels including the unflagged TWA Hotel, has argued for years that owners should move away from OTAs and has described hotel ownership as a business of buying reservations. On Hilton's latest earnings call, CEO Chris Nassetta said AI should eventually bring more direct business at lower cost while OTAs remain important for a segment of the business, and cited the RISE system-fee cuts launched in June 2025 — 30 basis points off loyalty costs system-wide and another 50 basis points for hotels meeting a quality threshold, both reductions to system fees rather than royalty rates. Skift's analysis: if AI agents route bookings into brand systems, the owner's test is whether the new channel improves margin after every fee, and that data doesn't exist yet.
