Editor’s Letter: The Benz Patent Motorwagen Has Big Perambulator Vibes

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

I love new cars, but lately I’m fixated on cars of the past.

From the November/December 2026 issue of Car and Driver.

These days, I’m particularly interested in cars of the past. By that I mean the distant past. Such is the case for the 1886 Benz Patent Motorwagen I drove during Monterey Car Week. Now when someone asks, “What’s the oldest car you’ve ever driven?” I have an unassailable answer. That is, unless you’ve driven Cugnot’s steam car. You did? No way. Was it cool?

The Patent Motorwagen gets credit for being first because Carl Benz designed it to be a car from the get-go. He didn’t simply take a horse-drawn carriage, add an engine and drivetrain, subtract the horse and reins, carry the oats.

I love primordial stuff because things we take for granted today had not yet been figured out. There were lots of guesses—some right, others not. Driving a vehicle that old is disorienting, challenging, and sometimes dangerous. Maybe something will explode. Maybe you’ll fall out. Boring hadn’t been engineered into the experience yet.

The Patent Motorwagen gives big perambulator vibes, and baby, I am here for it. It has only three wheels, to keep it simple and light. Carriages turned their entire axle, since uprights and kingpins at the wheels were still years away. Steering the first car involves grabbing what appears to be the wood handle of an awl—the automotive steering wheel hadn’t been invented yet either. Also not in existence in 1886: carburetor jets, engine radiators, radios, tetanus shots.

Another hitherto unimagined concept: hiding the crankshaft and flywheel behind casings. The crank, connecting rod, cam gear, and drive belt all sit out in the open. Some of those parts have to be greased up before your drive. For others, oil is metered out onto the cylinder and the intake valve by drip lubricators before it runs over less critical parts and leaves behind a greasy trail on the road.

To start the Benz, switch on the ignition that sends energy to the roughly eight-inch-long spark plug, then grab the horizontal flywheel. Give that a Wheel of Fortune–worthy spin and wait. The air-hexane fuel mixture is adjustable, so you can richen it to help the car start (it originally ran on naphtha, which was available at apothecaries in 1886, right next to the laudanum). There’s no throttle, so the 1.0-liter doesn’t stray far from its 400-rpm operating speed. A 4.8-quart copper vessel perched over the engine holds the water that cools the cylinder. After about an hour of operation, the water boils over, and you stop and take a calf’s-head-stew break to let things cool.

To accelerate, push the long lever on the left side of the leather-trimmed park bench to engage the engine-driven leather strap to the axle. Feel the 0.88 horsepower. To stop, pull that lever back to actuate a shoe that drags on the axle. Not to sound like an 1886 infomercial, but driving the Patent Motorwagen is so easy, a toddler could do it. It’ll hit a claimed 13 mph, and it was the most amazing 13 mph I’ve ever experienced. There’s no way I’m going to be this fun when I’m 140 years old.

Senior features editor Elana Scherr shares my fondness for the supercentenarian set. She owns a 1915 Dodge, making her the perfect person to organize a U.S. equivalent of Britain’s London-to-Brighton race for early cars. I rode along and got to exceed a 45-mph speed limit in a 116-year-old Pullman, ancient proof that speed limits are set too low. Not to worry, I was running Waze. Scherr’s account begins on page 64.

If you aren’t in the mood to go antiquing, there’s a 795-hp Mustang on page 34, a nearly 1000-hp Audi super car on page 58, and the new Corvette Grand Sport on page 72. Encompassing both new and old, this is a great issue.
