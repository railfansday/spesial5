The Detroit Lions’ road to a Super Bowl will be paved with adversity

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

The Detroit Lions' Super Bowl aspirations took another painful hit, but coach Dan Campbell isn't interested in letting adversity change how this team pursues a championship.

Following the injuries to Jack Campbell and Aidan Hutchinson, Campbell was asked about the frustration of not having his full arsenal of players available. His response was about as Dan Campbell as it gets.

"If you're not careful, you lay in bed at night and you start thinking about all the what ifs, and at some point, you just become a miserable piece of shit. And I'm not going to do it. We're not doing it. I refuse to look at it that way."

Losing players like Jack and Aidan stings. Of course it does.

"What has happened with Jack Campbell and with Hutch now will not be in vain. We can't allow it to. They got us to this point. And so what are we going to do about it, man? Are we just going to let it go to waste, or are we going to do something and pick up the slack?"

It's a motivating response, but there's something deeply familiar about this message.

Almost six years ago, Campbell stood before the Detroit media for his introductory press conference and laid out the DNA of what he wanted this football team to become.

"This team is going to take on the identity of this city. And this city's been down, and it found a way to get up. It's found a way to overcome adversity."

Then, the kneecaps. Instantly legendary. But the underlying message was laced with wisdom beyond the sound bites: Detroit is going to fight.

And as this team continues to navigate the ups and downs of the Dan Campbell era, I've started to appreciate something about the journey that I don't think I fully understood when it began.

If the Detroit Lions eventually win a Super Bowl, I don't think it will come through a perfect season or a series of fortunate circumstances. It will come through adversity. It will come through setbacks, difficult decisions, unexpected challenges, and moments of doubt.

And deep down, I believe that's the version of this story worth having. That's the version tailor-made for this city.

For most of my life, believing in the Lions required a certain degree of delusion. I gaslit myself into believing some embarrassingly bad football decisions were winning decisions (I was all-in on the 2019 signing of free agent tight end Jesse James).

Then coach Campbell arrived in Allen Park.

The first year and a half was rough. I could spare you from revisiting the details. But I won't. I want you to remember what it felt like. 3-13-1 in 2021, including Justin Tucker's record-breaking 66-yard field goal as time expired at Ford Field, an excruciating 16-16 tie against the Pittsburgh Steelers, followed by a 1-6 start in 2022. The Lions had spent nearly two full seasons getting knocked down, but they kept getting back up.

By the end of 2022, the Lions were 9-8 and had won eight of their final 10 games, including the greatest game the Lions have ever played at Lambeau Field, which sent Aaron Rodgers packing.

Dan Miller's impassioned speech spoke to the whole city, signifying a great shift in mindset.

"Let's get comfortable with being uncomfortable because you know what uncomfortable is? It means you're good. It means you have expectations upon you–and now this team does."

For the first time in generations, Lions fans had a legitimate reason to believe they were watching something special. Detroit exorcised demons by winning its first division title since 1993, beat Matthew Stafford and the Los Angeles Rams in the playoffs, and won a second playoff game at Ford Field, beating the Buccaneers to reach the NFC Championship Game.

It was extraordinarily satisfying to watch Jared Goff beat Sean McVay and Stafford. There was something prideful about watching Ford Field become one of the most intimidating home-field environments in the NFL.

The city was unhinged. OnePride was a factor. The team was on the verge of kicking the damn Super Bowl door down.

And, for a moment, it was all right there for the taking.

Detroit led the San Francisco 49ers 24-7 at halftime of the NFC Championship Game. Just 30 minutes away from the biggest game in franchise history.

There it was. The light at the end of the tunnel. Except it wasn't a light. It was a freight train. The Lions climbed all the way to the doorstep of the Super Bowl, only to get knocked down again.

In hindsight, maybe my mistake was ever thinking that reaching that level of success—that light at the end of the tunnel—meant the hard part was finally over. After the game, Campbell spoke to a reality that, looking back on, I didn't fully believe or couldn't truly appreciate.

"I told those guys, this may have been our only shot. Do I think that? No. Do I believe that? No. However, I know how hard it is to get here. I'm well aware. It's going to be twice as hard to get back to this point next year than it was last year. That's the reality."

After unfamiliar success, I was convinced the 2024 season would be the year.

Sure enough, the Lions got back up off the mat after the loss in San Francisco, went 15-2, and secured the NFC's No. 1 seed. The offense was dominant, and the team had established itself as one of the NFL's best.

The gut punches, however, wouldn't stop. The injuries kept coming. Hutchinson, Alim McNeill, Carlton Davis III, Derrick Barnes, and Malcolm Rodriguez all suffered season-ending injuries. Ultimately, the Washington Commanders ended Detroit's season in the divisional round.

But I also think something else happened during that period.

For a fan base that had spent decades hoping to see a competitive Lions team, the Super Bowl had finally become the expectation. I know it happened to me.

I believed in what Dan Campbell and Brad Holmes had built so much that I started expecting the Lions to win every single week. That was not necessarily a bad thing. This team had earned that confidence.

Back to Dan Miller's proclamation to get comfortable being uncomfortable: the uncomfortable feeling can't fade away as the seasons go by. When Campbell spoke to the reality about the difficulty of repeating success in this league, I should have fully believed him.

See, when you stop feeling that uncomfortable feeling, when the expectation is not complemented with appreciation for the moment and adversity, the edge becomes lost. The fire built up over years of starving for winning dies down.

Ben Johnson left to become the head coach of the Chicago Bears. While many have called it a sign of betrayal, I think Ben's deliberate choice to take the Bears job signified more. It suggests, perhaps subconsciously, his true belief: that he is better. That he would build a team better than Campbell and the Lions ever could without him.

And in just one season, he started to make his case. The Lions struggled to recalibrate, injuries continued to take their toll, and the season ended with a 9-8 record and no playoff appearance. Meanwhile, Chicago won the NFC North.

For a team that had spent the previous two seasons establishing itself as the division's best, watching Ben Johnson instantly bring success to Chicago was humbling. Campbell's immediate response at his end-of-season press conference, however, was notable. He said he wanted to get back to what the Lions were doing in 2022.

"I want to get back to some of the things we were doing a couple of years ago. And what I mean by that is just getting back to a little bit of what we did at the ground level, just a little bit of the way we train, the way we go about things."

See, Dan speaks from his gut. He lets his experience and instincts be his compass. I think that quote is representative. His gut told him the team had lost something it once embodied. He recognized that some of the foundational elements that made them who they are as an organization needed revisiting.

Success changed things. Expectations changed. Mindsets shifted. The challenge became maintaining the habits that got them there while continuing to evolve.

From Dan's perspective, the team needed that reminder.

Here we are in 2026, and the Lions are continuing to get knocked down again, and again, and again.

The NFC North has become the best in football. Detroit, Minnesota, and Chicago are all 7-2, while Green Bay is 6-4. There is no room for error, and the race is on.

Jack Campbell's and Aidan Hutchinson's injuries have been difficult to watch. They are difficult to accept. The initial reaction has been to wonder what this team's ceiling is without two of its defensive captains.

But Campbell's viral response brought everything back to where this story began.

"If you're not careful, you lay in bed in night, and you start thinking about all the what-ifs. And at some point, you just become a miserable piece of shit. And I'm not going to do it. We're not doing it. I refuse to look at it that way."

It is incredibly valuable to have a leader who understands that a championship pursuit isn't linear. Dan understands that circumstances will change, often in ways nobody can anticipate.

I believe in him. I believe in the "Grit" philosophy he's been preaching since he arrived in Detroit.

That doesn't mean I agree with every decision, every draft pick, or every fourth-down call. It means I believe that a Dan Campbell team will always get back up. The team will have the hunger to compete. The appreciation of the moment. The willingness to embrace the fight.

The Lions have an opportunity to make a statement as they host the Tampa Bay Buccaneers in Week 11 and then the Bears just four days later on Thanksgiving.

The Chicago Bears are coming to Ford Field, and the national conversation has increasingly shifted toward the Bears and Vikings.

Let them have the attention. Let them have the expectations. For decades, I craved that attention for the Lions. But it's meaningless. In fact, I think I feel more comfortable on this side. And I think Dan Campbell likes it this way, too.

From the beginning, Dan Campbell told the fan base what kind of team he wanted to build. A team that would get knocked down, get back up, and keep fighting. He never promised that the road would be easy. He never guaranteed wins. He promised a team that would take on the identity of its city.

Almost six years later, that message is as true as ever.

If the Lions eventually win a Super Bowl, it won't erase the heartbreak in San Francisco, the demoralizing injuries of 2024, 2025, and now 2026. Those experiences will be part of the story. I'm not rooting for the injuries. I'm not rooting for the setbacks. I'm not suggesting the Lions need to suffer to win. I'm saying that adversity is inevitable, and how this team responds is what makes the journey worth following.

Embrace the fight. Appreciate the opportunity. Keep the edge.

And when the Lions finally get there, I have a feeling we'll appreciate every gut punch and every scar for the triumph each represents.
