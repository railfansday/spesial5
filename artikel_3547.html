Of Mics and Men: After MAGA Meltdown, the Manosphere Winds Down

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Two years ago, on the night of the 2024 presidential election, Ultimate Fighting Championship CEO Dana White took to the podium at Donald Trump’s Palm Beach victory party and rattled off a list of thank-yous to some rowdy podcasters and YouTube pranksters: the NELK Boys, Adin Ross, Theo Von, Bussin’ With the Boys, and “last but not least, the mighty and powerful Joe Rogan.” Just months before, these names were seldom mentioned in the mainstream press, but now they were recognized on a national stage as having powered Trump’s return to office by hosting him on their platforms and revealing his more personable side. The victory that night seemed to go well beyond the electoral conquest; one of the defining cultural legacies of the 2024 election cycle is the imprecise but vivid concept of the manosphere.

By 2026, the novelty of Trump and Von joking about the potency of cocaine, as they did in one of the signature moments of the 2024 campaign, has worn off. The president’s approval rankings tanked despite his enduring presence as a pop culture figure. Rogan endorsed Trump in the final days of that 2024 race, but he recently described Trump’s “really crazy” 1,300-word Truth Social tirade against Bill Maher as having the ring of a “girl who got dumped.” He has roundly criticized Trump’s handling of the Epstein Files, a signature issue for the podcast class, and described the Iran war as a “betrayal.” If the end of MAGA is in sight amid Tuesday’s blue tsunami, so is the notion that Republicans had captured the coveted, low-propensity demographic of young men: The New York Times estimated that Democrats won 18-to-29-year-old men by 24 points on Tuesday, a 38-point swing from Trump’s 2024 victory among them. Democrats are content creators too now, and the most notable political appearance on The Joe Rogan Experience over the past couple of years was James Talarico’s. “You need to run for president,” Rogan told Texas’s new senator-elect.

And so when C-SPAN made the somewhat mind-boggling announcement that the staid public-affairs network would simulcast election night coverage hosted by Patrick Bet-David, a Florida-based podcaster known for his interviews with Andrew Tate and Nick Fuentes, it was less of a capitulation to the so-called manosphere than an emblem of the modern media free-for-all. Of the online personalities loosely associated with Rogan’s milieu, Bet-David’s backstory is the most colorful. A refugee from Iran, he joined the Army upon arriving in the United States; he has said he worked for a time as a bodyguard to a high-level Los Angeles cocaine dealer. Bet-David now operates a media company called Valuetainment, formed after he made his riches with an insurance company that has been widely described as a multi-level marketing operation. After interviewing the leading looksmaxxing personality, Clavicular, last week, he was now responsible for providing C-SPAN’s conservative perspective. A spokesperson for the network described the programming to me as an effort to achieve “a marriage of traditional media and new media.”

“In the 2024 election, we invited Pete Buttigieg, God knows how many times, never showed up,” Bet-David told me in the lead-up to the evening. “Pete Buttigieg was here last week. We did a two-hour podcast together right before the midterms. And so the market is realizing that’s the direction you got to go.”

As he entertained a characteristically ragtag group of political-for-the-night personalities, Bet-David sat at his decision desk in a pinstripe suit with cartoonishly wide peak lapels. There were the young conservative provocateurs CJ Pearson and Nick Shirley, and the Groyper-adjacent, recent Florida gubernatorial candidate James Fishback, who described the night as a “really tough election for my party.”

Bet-David joked to Michael Franzese that he knows about gas prices—before refashioning himself as a political commentator, the former Colombo crime family capo ran a multi-million-dollar gasoline tax bootlegging scheme in the 1980s. In voicing his opposition to the war in Iran, Franzese said, “We had a saying on the street, the best way to defeat your enemies is to make them your friends.” He bemoaned how Trump “makes everything all about him.”

Other guests included Ann Coulter, Piers Morgan, Roger Stone, Bill O’Reilly, and Cenk Uygur, whose Young Turks show was simulcasting on another C-SPAN channel. For some reason, Jackass’s Steve-O appeared toward the end of the night. He pointed out to Bet-David that Marco Rubio is “stronger for you guys” than JD Vance.

Two years ago, I watched a similar scene unfold in an airplane hangar in Fort Lauderdale. It was, as Bet-David and I agreed on Tuesday, a wild night: He was hosting an election night party at Valuetainment’s headquarters at the peak of the manosphere chatter and at the onset of what seemed to so many to be the era of MAGA cultural domination. Men in tightly tapered polyester pants hopped out of Ferraris and Teslas to watch a crowd of UFC fighters, rappers, former mobsters, and Candace Owens wax poetic about how the age of Jennifer Lopez and Beyoncé, who had appeared alongside Kamala Harris in previous weeks, was over. In this new era of masculinity, there was no time anymore for liberal Hollywood political pieties: Mark Zuckerberg had just started wearing a gold chain and describing Trump’s fist pump after the attempted assassination as “one of the most badass things I’ve ever seen in my life.”

And the vibe shift, as it could only be called, was shocking for a period. There was a moment, for instance, in the heady aftermath of the 2024 election when Von attended an Oscars week poker night hosted by Timothée Chalamet at the Chateau Marmont. (The actor had recently appeared on his show.) Just weeks later, Von was at a UFC fight where White warmly embraced the Tate brothers. The entire category of celebrity appeared to be flattening in real time.

But to those on the inside of the podcaster-comedian boom, another swing of the pendulum already seemed to be coming. Andrew Schulz, a frequent Rogan guest, conducted one of Trump’s trademark podcast interviews in the summer of 2024. “After the Trump thing, I wasn’t like, ‘We got it now,’” he told me the following year. “After the Trump thing, I was like, ‘We just interviewed the president of the United States, what the fuck?’” He thought that the deafening manosphere discourse might indicate the presence of a cultural bubble. “I just think that we are what people are listening to now,” he said, “but that could change tomorrow.”

Alex Bruesewitz, a 29-year-old Trump adviser, was largely credited with orchestrating the candidate’s podcast tour in 2024. A social media operative who had built connections with big-name celebrities, influencers, and podcasters in the lead-up to the presidential election—he had brokered Trump’s alliance with Nicki Minaj and recently flew on Air Force One with Mike Tyson—Bruesewitz told me on Wednesday morning that he had soured on the Republican party.

“I consider myself more of a Trump voter than a Republican voter,” he said. “If the party moves in the direction of people like Ted Cruz, for example, in 2028, there's going to be a lot of people like myself…who will not participate in the Republican elections moving forward.”

In the midterms cycle, Bruesewitz said, the podcasting world “wasn’t there for us to engage.” The likes of Von and Schulz have echoed Rogan’s criticisms of the Iran war and how the Trump administration managed the release of the Epstein files.

“I don’t think that any of them were ever team Trump,” he said. “We simply just had conversations with them in 2024. The post-election reaction, saying that they’re part of this right-wing manosphere, I think that actually had an effect on how they covered us during our first two years. I think that they didn’t want to be labeled right-wing, and so they evened out a bit.”

Another reason, Bruesewitz supposed, for the end of Republican podcasting dominance and the blue tsunami: “Republican candidates were really lame and they weren’t interesting people…there was a little bit of an expectation that these candidates could go communicate with low-propensity voters with podcasting again, but I pushed back on their teams. I was like, Look, there’s zero percent chance that any of these podcasters are going to want to have on your boring candidates.”

By the end of the night on Tuesday, Bet-David and Steve-O were making plans to hang out before the stuntman left town. All of the guests on the C-SPAN–Bet-David collaboration seemed likely to keep talking about politics online, but it would be difficult to assign any of them a specific party agenda. In the airplane hangar two years prior, Bet-David had ended the night with what felt like a seismic exhortation, telling a raucous crowd, “For the rest of our lives we will share this night together.” On Tuesday, he was more practical as he signed off for the evening.
