You Look Like a Clown! The Thoughtless Things Relatives Say

“You are looking a little chunky!” My father made this offhand comment when I was a sophomore in college. His words stung. Like many, I had gained the “freshman fifteen” and was desperately hoping no one would notice. But he did.

That memory is so vivid that I can see exactly where I was standing, in my dorm room next to the dresser, when he spoke those words. Coming from a man who valued fitness, I knew exactly what he meant. Yes, I was devastated by his comment about my body, but underneath, I was scared that he would be embarrassed by me or, even worse, that he would love me less. Would he still be proud of me if I gained more weight? I told no one what he said.

To make matters worse, my boyfriend broke up with me shortly after that. I was convinced it was because I had gained weight. I started wearing baggy clothes to hide my body and doubted anyone would ever want to be in a relationship with me again. I went home for the holidays, and worried about what relatives might say and declined invitations to get together with high school friends. I felt embarrassed and ashamed of my body.

I have worked as a psychologist for over 30 years helping people with body image issues and have not yet met a person who feels good about every part of their body no matter their level of fitness, subjective physical attractiveness, or body weight. I have met people who have come to a place of acceptance of how they look and appreciate their body for what it can do, but when it comes to fully embracing their physical attributes, not one has given themselves a perfect score.

Bodies change over time. Bodies in their twenties are not the same as bodies in their thirties, forties, or beyond. Five key factors can potentially ignite body image issues for individuals or couples.

I call them the five arrows because they can pierce a relationship at any time and powerfully affect body image. They are aging, health conditions, medications, pregnancy, and words. The fifth arrow, words, critical comments from others or negative thoughts about oneself, is perhaps the most lethal because it can pierce not only the body, but the mind and the soul.

Not everyone will experience critical comments from others, but most of us can remember something said about our body that did not feel good. Critical comments include bullying at school or work, teasing, comments by friends, discrimination such as weightism, or repeated criticism by parents, siblings, or other relatives.

Negative comments about self can cause self-consciousness when around others, self-hate, avoidance of social situations, eating disorders, distorted body image, avoidance of sexual intimacy, depression, anxiety, and at worst, suicidal ideation.

When we meet someone, and they are attracted to us, and they want to touch us and make love to us, a host of insecurities can be triggered, generating waves of shame or embarrassment that wash over us. What if they touch the legs and think they are fat? What about the stomach? What if they think the lips are too small? When we enter a relationship, we take our bodies with us; and with the body comes all the history, feelings, and insecurities about the physical self since childhood.

I had a friend who repeatedly criticized parts of my body when I was a child. One day, out of the blue, she told me I had a big nose. I told no one about her comment. I had never thought about my nose before that, and from that day onward, I felt self-conscious. I wish I had had a safe place to share her words. The “arrow” of words could have been deflected by perspective and reassurance I could not give myself. Fornaini and colleagues (2021) found that positive comments about body image reduced fear of abandonment, increased satisfaction, and served as a protective factor for couples. I have a relationship like that with my husband.

We can find relationships that shield us from the emotional consequences of arrows that can negatively affect body image. The shield should provide love, perspective, empathy, and reassurance—not problem-solving. This can be a love relationship or friendship. Choose wisely.

It was the holidays. The family gathered to celebrate and eat a traditional turkey dinner. I had just dyed my hair what I thought was a gorgeous red. My aunt approached me and proclaimed, “You look like a clown.” She was gaping at my hair. Her words hurt; they hurt a lot. This time I did tell someone, my husband. His love and empathy reassured me and gave me perspective. For this I am grateful.

International Journal of Environmental Research Public Health, 18, 931-939. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph18179319
