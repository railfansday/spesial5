The Digital Euro: Solving Problems You Didn’t Have, With Rules You Don’t Write

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Do you actually need a digital euro because payments weren’t convenient enough already?

I pay for everything with my phone. Instantly. Even when I’m abroad. To be fair, I’m still glad we actually get to live in the 21st century.

And no, it’s not because they want to ban cash. Cash is already quietly fading out of daily use in Europe. It’s certainly not because someone at a central bank suddenly developed a burning passion for transparency and democratisation.

So let’s look at what’s actually being built, who gets to set the rules, and most importantly, why it’s being built at all.

(It’s just the rehearsal where nobody gets to leave)

According to ECB Executive Board member Piero Cipollone’s speech on 5 November 2026, they plan a 12-month pilot in the second half of 2027.

It will involve 36 payment service providers, including Deutsche Bank, UniCredit, Revolut, Stripe, and Adyen. Testing will cover ordinary situations: purchases, transfers, and refunds.

According to the ECB, the target for a possible first issuance is 2029, but only if the necessary legislation is adopted by the end of 2026. Only then would the Governing Council decide whether to issue it at all.

Preparing a system and approving its use are two separate decisions. News headlines tend to treat them as the same thing.

It might come as a shock, but the balance you see in your banking app isn’t actually your money. It’s a promise from your local bank that they’ll repay it one day. A digital euro would move that promise even further away, all the way behind the walls of the ECB headquarters in Frankfurt.

Apart from that minor detail, your brand-new digital euro would be almost identical to what you already use.

It would still be worth one euro. It would still appear on your screen as if you owned it. And it would still not be immune to money printing, inflation, or devaluation.

Under the proposed design it wouldn’t even pay the princely 0.05% interest some accounts currently offer. And there would be a limit on how much you could hold. Roughly €3,000 was one of the options analysed, not a final rule.

Now, to be fair, there is one legitimate point in favor of the digital euro.

For card payments, Europe still depends heavily on non-European companies, in practice, mainly Visa and Mastercard. A shared European payments infrastructure makes sense as a political and economic objective. The real test is whether regular people actually get enough out of the digital euro to bother switching.

Good luck convincing 450 million people to just switch up their daily habits. Especially when the new system arrives with holding limits, no interest, and a few extra questions about why you’re using it.

That does not mean the entire wallet would operate anonymously like cash. Anti-money-laundering checks would still apply when depositing, transferring or withdrawing funds.

In other words, the ECB says it will not monitor everything you buy. Your local bank will.

The ECB says the digital euro would not be programmable money. The issuer wouldn’t dictate where, when or on what you could spend it. That’s different from a conditional payment you choose to arrange yourself.

So there’s no need for panic about your money expiring or a sudden ban on steak purchases.

There is a good reason to check whether protection against such restrictions remains firmly written into the final rules. A press release is no substitute for a legal safeguard.

The same applies to cash. The ECB says the digital euro would complement it, not replace it. What matters is whether people can actually keep using cash, and whether pulling it out of your wallet is still treated as normal rather than vaguely suspicious.

The digital euro and Bitcoin serve different purposes.

A digital euro would remain a euro, governed by monetary and public institutions.

As I learned the hard way after a single copy-paste mistake sent a test transfer straight into oblivion: with Bitcoin, I don’t need a central issuer to hold or send my money. The catch? If I hold my own keys, protecting them is entirely on me.

Bitcoin isn’t magically anonymous, either. Its public ledger and wild price swings don’t vanish just because we’re fed up with central banks.

I judge the digital euro on whether it actually fixes payments without chipping away at our rights. I judge Bitcoin on its own merits and risks. They’re playing entirely different games.

The digital euro isn’t about easier coffee purchases.

Once the rules are written and the system is live, complaining that “this wasn’t what we signed up for” will be largely irrelevant.

The real question isn’t whether the digital euro happens.

It’s whether we wake up in time to realize our right to use our own money is being quietly rewritten. Politely, step by step, and backed by world-class PR.
