Avedon Director Ron Howard On Richard Avedon’s “Creative Courage,” and His Favorite Images From 'In the American West'

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

In his new documentary Avedon, director Ron Howard traces the vivid legacy of the influential photographer Richard Avedon, which premieres November 11 on Netflix. “The subjects he worked with on the commercial side of his career were dazzling and beautiful, and he was so entertaining and expressive in the way that he would shoot them. But I really had no idea how he applied his status, his influence, his access to tell this other kind of story about human beings that was honest, insightful, and more journalistic,” Howard tells Vanity Fair.

The glamorous heights of Avedon’s career were what first caught Howard’s attention. “I became aware of him really in the ’80s. Then it was even his TV commercials, like Brooke Shields [in Calvin Klein ads],” Howard said. Later, he realized a few of his favorite portraits were taken by Avedon. One of these was a portrait of a young Kareem Abdul-Jabbar at a playground in New York holding a basketball “and kind of casually standing there looking straight into the camera. It’s not against a white background…” notes Howard. “I know Kareem a little bit. It so captured the foundational strength of character and intelligence that Kareem carried with him along with his basketball talent.”

Initially, Howard, who spent three years making the film, thought it was going to be more focused on the fashion and celebrity worlds that Avedon inhabited. However, his fascination with photographs like “sad Marilyn” (Avedon’s 1957 portrait of Marilyn Monroe) grew into a respect for the full breadth of Avedon’s career, and his “creative courage as an artist.”

In the film, footage of Avedon and new interviews with Lauren Hutton, Tina Brown, Adam Gopnik, Hilton Als, Joel Meyerowitz, Larry Gagosian, and Avedon’s son John, among others, paint a picture of a driven, curious, and sometimes mercurial artist. In the 1950s, Avedon’s career took off when he began shooting for Harper’s Bazaar, Vogue, and Vanity Fair. He injected a sense of play and prewar European glamour into his images by drawing inspiration from the films of Fred Astaire and Ginger Rogers. Eventually, he ventured into documentary assignments and more political subjects, including Nothing Personal (1964), a photobook with text by James Baldwin that captured the tensions of the Civil Rights era alongside a broader look at American life, a three panel portrait of anti-war protesters the Chicago Seven, and members of America’s political and financial elite at the time of the country’s bicentennial (“The Family”) for Rolling Stone. At the height of his career, he seemingly shifted quite easily from photographing celebrities to documenting anti-war protests and the counterculture of the 1960s, even when his work was met with mixed reactions.

“We tried to reflect the risks he took and the transitions he made with his art and his career,” Howard shares. Had Howard made the documentary in Avedon’s lifetime, he would have liked to have asked him directly about the turning points in his career. “In all the interviews, he just kind of made it seem like it was a natural step. But to me, it's not a natural step to stop getting thousands and thousands of dollars an hour to shoot commercials and shoot for the world's most read magazines, and go to Vietnam where no one was really. He wasn’t on assignment initially… he just committed.”

In 1979, the Amon Carter Museum of American Art in Fort Worth, Texas, commissioned Avedon to capture a portrait of the West. He worked on this ambitious portfolio periodically through 1984, traveling across 17 states like California, Colorado, Iowa, North Dakota, Texas, and Wyoming, with assistants and a researcher, Laura Wilson, who helped Avedon arrange 752 portrait sittings. (Wilson also documented the making of the project in her own remarkable photographs and writing.) In total, Avedon printed 124 photographs (some as large as four feet tall) and exhibited them at the museum in 1985. The result was In the American West—a turning point in Avedon’s career. Prior to the project’s completion, in 1983, Vanity Fair ran five photographs from the eventual series, showing coal miners covered in dust and one lone oil worker alongside a feature on Wagner’s operas and an excerpt of Gabriel García Márquez’s Chronicle of a Death Foretold.

“One of the reasons I was drawn to [In the American West] is because my family roots are in the Midwest,” Howard says. “I see a lot of truth in these images.”

Avedon conducting a portrait session for In the American West beside his 8x10 camera.

In a similar fashion to August Sander’s massive undertaking People of the 20th Century, Avedon went looking for “men and women who work at hard, uncelebrated jobs, the people who are often ignored or overlooked,” Wilson wrote. In doing so he joined a whole legion of photographers whose images have come to define broad swaths of middle and western America. But unlike common readings of Sander’s project at the time, Avedon was clear that his version of the West was totally subjective, more of a cinematic panorama than an objective record. He was emphatic that all photographs are filtered through the photographer’s eye, and become their interpretation. Through Wilson, he declared, “This is a fictional West. I don’t think the West of these portraits is any more conclusive than the West of John Wayne.”

Below, Howard reflects on five of his favorite photographs from Avedon’s In the American West.

“I asked if that was makeup, and I was assured that none of it was. [Avedon] set up, and just as workers were coming off their shift, he was getting them in front of the camera. I mean, wow, this is what these workers look like. This is what was in their lungs and their pores. I have cousins and uncles who worked in the oil fields. I never had any idea that they had their days looking like they’d been through what this guy has been through on his shift.”

“The boy with the rattlesnake was the first photo that [Avedon] took where he knew he was going to get at something deeper... He felt like this was almost religious iconography in a way, or mythic. [The subject] was a teenager. As Avedon says, he's a boy turning into a man, and here he's doing this kind of work, so it speaks to a transformation. It also speaks of that region as a very pragmatic region. My own dad, to try to get some spending money [in the ’30s], was hunting and trapping raccoons to sell skins to the junk dealer or possums they could eat. Absolutely just a pragmatic way of life there. So that image always resonated with me. It is also featured in the documentary because it was the proof in [Avedon’s] mind that he was going to be able to identify moments and individuals that would be arresting and mean a lot on an artistic level.”

“The beekeeper is, of course, a stunning image–one that doesn't have that much to do with the American West. I think [Avedon] could have found somebody like that in almost any corner of the planet, but it certainly is fascinating and a tribute to the research team that was working with him to help find him.”

“The maintenance worker who’s dressed up in a really flashy suit, suit jacket, tie and hat, that just looks very familiar to me. I mean, the jacket and all the embroidery on it, the flowers, I never saw anything like that with my family. They didn’t have that kind of flourish. This guy looks like he could be a country western singer or could be trying to be a country western star. But the hat, the tie, the gaze, the posture looks very, very familiar to me. That looks like a hardworking guy getting dressed up on the weekend.”
