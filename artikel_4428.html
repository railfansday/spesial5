Ocarina of Time Remake Director and Producer Credit Younger Developers for Vital Modern Perspective

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Daniel is a lifelong gamer with a love for various genres and gameplay styles who enjoys exploring games and playing from various angles.

The full remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time has launched for the Nintendo Switch 2 today, and perhaps unsurprisingly, the response is quite positive. It’s still an excellent game after all these years, and the modern changes, both in its presentation and gameplay tweaks, improve the overall experience without detracting from the original’s timeless charm.

Hyrule has never looked better as they look in The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time Remake, and critics seem to agree!

It still feels like just yesterday when the original game launched for the Nintendo 64 in 1998, and a lot has changed in the industry since then. Heck, people who weren’t even born yet when that game came out are now working in game development, and apparently, some of those people also worked on the Ocarina of Time remake.

In an interview with The Verge, the remake’s director Hidemaro Fujibayashi and producer Eiji Aonuma, who was also the director on the original game, spoke about the vital perspectives that the team’s younger developers brought to the table in bringing the remake to life, including a few that have never even played the original. It was thanks in part to this next generation of devs that this fantastic meeting of gameplay history was able to take shape.

To paraphrase a bit of wisdom from a better writer than myself, the norms of the gaming industry are kind of like rings on a tree stump. Through various indicators in design, you can trace back the norms and common sense of game development through the ages.

The original Ocarina of Time, while a fantastic game, is a lot different from today’s norms in its visual and mechanical approaches, and needs to be recognized as such to create a proper modern remake. If the only people working on the remake were those who worked on and/or played the original, they would’ve just made the original again. This, as Fujibayashi and Aonuma explain, is why having younger developers on the team was so important.

Eiji Aonuma and the development team for the Ocarina of Time remake discussed how one of the original's infamous dungeons is being improved.

Despite some of them not having played the original, the younger developers on the team understood in the broad sense what made its formula work, and so strove for simple, careful additions and subtractions to bring it a little more in line with modern sensibilities.

“I feel confident that they weren’t necessarily pursuing something new for the sake of novelty when it might be unneeded and clutter the experience,” Fujibayashi said. “They’re looking for elegance. Only what is needed, only what was important in the original, and how to carry that forward as their focus.”

Aonuma and Fujibayashi’s work on the remake alongside the rest of their development team really helped to illustrate to them how much has changed in the game development pipeline since Aonuma’s heyday on the original Ocarina of Time nearly 30 years ago.

“We used to have maybe just one person designing the backgrounds, and that’s something that you couldn’t do nowadays,” Aonuma said. “A lot of the process is not so different compared to the N64 days, but the scale [is different]: There’s a lot more time involved, a lot more people involved, and because of that it can be harder to manage these teams.”

Fujibayashi also worked as the director for the modern, wide-open Zelda games like Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom, which are much different beasts compared to more streamlined, linear approach of Ocarina of Time. Fujibayashi recalls being fascinated with Nintendo’s early approaches to the new conundrums of 3D back in the day, particularly with the advent of the Z-targeting system. “The solutions are something that still stick with me as a foundation for when I create 3D games,” he said.

At the end of the interview, Aonuma reflected on how diversity in game design is what gives the Zelda franchise its staying power, and how this modern take on Ocarina of Time is a good example of its ability to mix things up as much (or as little) as necessary.

“We’ve had opportunities to add to the playbook of Zelda, if you will, and the language of development,” he said. “You get to see the progression of these ideas across the franchise. And now, working with younger staff on this remake, we’re having the opportunity to bring in additional new ideas. I really look forward to the future and what’s possible coming from the new staff.”
