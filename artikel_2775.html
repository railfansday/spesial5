There are 5,100 newly minted Anthropic millionaires—here’s all the stuff they don’t tell you about when you suddenly get rich

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Things are about to get very weird for the thousands of employees of Anthropic who just became, or soon will become, multi-millionaires.

Anthropic's IPO on Nov. 19 turned around 5,100 non-founder "Ants," as the company's staffers are known, into "paper millionaires," and at least 400 will be worth north of $100 million.

Few of us will ever make a million-dollar windfall from an employer. And fewer still will win the lottery or magically land a massive sum from an estranged rich uncle. But don't be too jealous of these employees' gains. Sure, money can buy a lot — and it would be hard to say no if you do strike it rich — but there are a remarkable number of downsides to sudden wealth.

And they may explain why you likely won't see all of Anthropic's newly minted millionaire employees heading for the exits.

First of all, these new moguls won't be able to access all their wealth immediately. The terms of the stock sale outlined in Anthropic's revised S-1 allow employees to cash out up to 10% of the value of their holdings on the first day of trading following the date of the prospectus, but the rest can only be exercised after various vesting periods pass or lock-up terms are achieved, based on the individual's agreements with the company.

Some won't be able to realize all their gains for years. There are many financial institutions willing to lend based on paper gains, or to buy options — but their wealth will also continue to fluctuate with the rise and fall of the company's stock price.

Even after those riches become liquid, research shows that only a small but still significant percentage of employees will, in fact, leave as a direct result of their new wealth. No, they aren't jetting off to the South Pacific to board their new yacht — which is what I would do in my "I just got $10 million" fantasy. Instead, according to a 2017 study from the Federal Reserve, an estimated 17.5% of employees at a newly public company will use their newfound wealth as a financial cushion to join another pre-IPO startup or form another company.

These departures, the study finds, may be particularly painful for Anthropic since the most valuable and entrepreneurially minded employees tend to bolt for this reason.

Experts who deal with suddenly wealthy employees expect that many will keep on working because they love it. "They're successful people in their own right," said David Rust, a financial advisor and co-founder of wealth management firm Quartz Financial in Austin, Texas.

Rust said that of his firm's high-tech employee clients who get sudden IPO wealth, "very few just go play golf every day. They want to stay busy and active, and a lot of them will scale back depending on their age, but very few of them say 'ok I'm done with that.'"

Others will keep working because their payout wasn't life-changing, said Susan Bradley, a certified financial planner and founder of The Sudden Money Institute, a Palm Beach Gardens, Florida-based financial advisory firm that specializes in clients undergoing sudden wealth events.

That's especially true for those whose millions measure in the low single digits. "You can go through $2 million pretty quickly in the world we live in," said Bradley.

A sum north of $1 million might seem life-changing to most of America, but it's chump change in Anthropic's headquarters city of San Francisco, the fifth most expensive city in the United States, with nearby San Jose coming in at third, according to a Q3 2026 Cost of Living report from Council on Community and Economic Research, which tracks metro- and state-level economic data.

In 2025, the Bay Area had the second-highest number of centimillionaires — those with a $100 million net worth or more — of any major metro in the world, according to a study by New World Wealth, a market research firm, in conjunction with Henley & Partners, a global residency and citizenship planning firm. That ranking is sure to get a boost from Anthropic's IPO.

Those who stay at Anthropic may be in for a bumpy ride. A study published in July in Management Science by two researchers from the University of Oklahoma found that employee satisfaction generally tends to crater after an IPO, especially among the earliest employees who had the biggest IPO payday.

The study found that the primary reason for this dissatisfaction is the rules change when a company is public, and the free-wheeling startup energy, decision-making and risk-taking can be dampened by shareholders and SEC rules.

Plus, imagine trying to get an employee base of 96% newly minted millionaires to continue to work super-duper hard. That's bound to cause some intra-company friction, especially if you are a manager who didn't get a big payout.

Another important matter that I don't game out in my $10 million fantasy, and the Anthropic millionaires probably didn't either, is how strange their friends, family and significant others are about to get. What would you do if your husband, girlfriend, sister or best friend was suddenly the richest person you'd ever met in your life?

Those relationships are going to change and not necessarily for the better, said Paul Hokemeyer, a licensed marriage and family therapist and author of "Fragile Power: Why Having Everything is Never Enough." Think of all the times the new millionaires are going to face someone with their hand out, many of whom will be related to them by blood or marriage.

"In my practice, I see a dynamic where the person of wealth gives out of a place of generosity," said Hokemeyer, "and in short order their acts of largesse create unhealthy dependencies and entitlement. Then, when the giver decides to stop or pull back, the recipient becomes a victim and the giver a perpetrator." He advises his clients to make no personal or charitable financial gifts for six months to a year, and not until they've created a specific giving plan.

In general, the best thing experts say that these new millionaires can do to deal with all the weirdness that's about to come their way? Absolutely nothing.

Work with professionals to address the urgent legal, tax and financial matters, of course, but don't peacock, don't buy stuff, don't move, don't quit, don't believe for a moment that you are suddenly super hot based on all the new attention you're getting from the opposite sex. It's all just noise.

Bradley suggests taking a break from any decisions that don't need to be made. "Even if it's as simple as saying, 'This is big. It's a little overwhelming right now, a little more than I expected, and I'm going to take my time.' Just buy some space."

Fortunately, $10 million can buy a lot of space.
