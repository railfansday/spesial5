Archaeologists Found a Mass Grave of 8,000 Ancient Frogs. Scientists Finally Know Who Killed Them.

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

The remains of a yearlong feast had been waiting in a ditch to be found for 1,700 years.

Scientists at Bournemouth University and the Museum of London Archaeology (MOLA) have teamed up to solve the whodunit: 8,000 dead frogs discovered nearly a decade ago in a single ditch in Cambridgeshire, England. The twist? The frogs had been dead for 1,700 years. The scientists have put the mystery to rest by pinning the blame on a group of hungry short-eared owls. Reaching that conclusion, though, doesn’t clear every suspect in the case.

The questions began between 2016 and 2018, when work on the National Highways A14 Cambridge to Huntingdon Improvement Scheme uncovered a ditch filled with amphibian bones. Radiocarbon dating by the University of Vienna placed the frogs in the Roman era. Experts then used a new method of analysis to measure the frogs’ arm bones, which showed that most of the frogs died young. They were eaten in the winter of their first year.

After studying the odd cold case, zooarchaeologist Paul Clarkson of Bournemouth and Vicki Ewens, senior zooarchaeologist at MOLA, published their findings in Environmental Archaeology. Clarkson first compared the damage on the bones with earlier studies of predation and found that birds of prey had likely eaten the frogs. He named short-eared owls and hen harriers as the most likely suspects.

On-site archaeological evidence added a twist, however: the ditch had filled within a single year. How could so many bones pile up so quickly? The research team found that the waters of the surrounding marshy wetlands, including the ditch in question, were retreating during the Roman period. This created ideal breeding conditions for frogs, and more frogs meant more food for predatory birds.

“We suggest natural and anthropogenic environmental changes to explain why this predation occurred in the Middle Roman period,” the authors wrote.

People also changed the landscape. The valley had been heavily wooded during the Iron Age, but snail, pollen, and charcoal evidence showed major changes by the time of the frog massacre. Over time, farmers had cleared large areas of woodland to make grazing land for livestock. This left the frogs with less cover and drew in short-eared owls, which hunt over open grassland.

“We don’t often get an owl’s-eye view of what it was like in the past from archaeological sites,” Clarkson said in a statement from the university. “The mystery of these frogs has been solved—a feast of frogs that were eaten and their bones left behind by a group of owls.”

The owls may not have dined alone, though. Clarkson called the die-off a “short, intensive predation event” that could have drawn a mix of predators, which also fed on shrews, mice, and voles. “Results showed this was the debris left by a community of predators,” the authors wrote.

Human activity may have helped the predators, too. Although people cut down trees to make room for livestock, the researchers said that both the people and their animals were probably elsewhere during winter. That would have made it easier for the owls to roost near the ditch.

In the university’s statement, Ewens said she had “so many theories” about why such a large collection of frog bones turned up there. Working together to answer the question, she added, shows how research on “even the smallest animal recovered on a site can help us to understand not only past environmental conditions but human activity within a wider landscape.”

Clarkson agreed. “These bones not only tell us about life for people living near the Roman Fens, but they also give insights into the natural world—what wild animals were getting up to in the past,” he said, “and what life was like for them.”
