A single drop of coolant could change how data centers cool their chips

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Low-cost water handles steady heat and one costly coolant drop boils on the spikes, a data center cooling idea tested on a chip at Waterloo.

Cooling an overheating computer chip sounds like a job for more cold liquid. In a lab test, a single drop of coolant cooled a simulated chip’s hottest spot faster than a bath of the same coolant.

Engineers placed the drop, just over a quarter of an inch across, on a heated sapphire square submerged in water.

The drop boiled, and within 15 seconds the center of the hot spot had cooled by about 31°F (17°C).

The idea is to spend expensive coolant only where and when a chip needs it, and let low-cost water do the rest.

Sushanta Mitra, a professor of mechanical and mechatronics engineering at the University of Waterloo in Canada, led the work with postdoctoral researcher Tian-Yu Zhang and doctoral student Arnav Banerjee.

The test ran on a heated square of sapphire standing in for a chip, not on a working processor.

“It’s a division of labor,” said Mitra. “Water is excellent at handling steady, everyday heat and the drops take care of sudden spikes.”

Operators of data centers increasingly cool their servers by submerging the electronics in a liquid that doesn’t conduct electricity.

The trouble is that chips don’t heat evenly. AI workloads have pushed data centers toward denser processors that draw more power.

A processor core can run far hotter than the rest of the chip for a few seconds at a time. Such bursts make chips slow down and age faster, and a liquid that simply surrounds the chip responds slowly.

Coolants that boil pull heat away faster, but they’re costly, and boiling is hard to start at exactly the right spot.

Purified water moves heat better than the standard fluids, and with the electronics insulated it can serve as a coolant itself. In still water, though, the currents that carry heat away from a sudden hot spot can be slow to start.

The sapphire square measured 1.2 inches (30 millimeters) on each side and was thinner than a dime. An electric current heated a coating on its surface.

The chip sat under about 1 fluid ounce (30 milliliters) of purified water, less than half an inch deep.

The team squeezed a drop of Novec-7100, an electronics coolant that boils at about 142°F (61°C), onto the chip through a needle. The coolant is heavier than water and barely dissolves in it, so the drop stayed on the chip.

Then they switched on the power. The highest of three settings was about 86 watts, roughly a bright old-style light bulb’s heat packed into an area the size of a postage stamp.

An infrared camera under the chip recorded its temperature 30 times a second, and a high-speed camera filmed the bubbles. Each test ran three times.

For comparison, the team also ran the chip in a tank of pure coolant and in water alone.

At the highest heat setting, the drop made the difference.

Once the chip’s surface passed the coolant’s boiling point, bubbles formed inside the drop while the water around it stayed liquid. Within 15 seconds, the center of the hot spot was some 31°F (17°C) cooler.

A chip in a tank of pure coolant reached a similar peak temperature, but cooled far more slowly afterward. In water alone, the chip kept getting hotter.

“Boiling soaks up a lot of heat and the rising bubbles stir the surrounding water so it carries heat away faster,” said Mitra, who is also executive director of the Waterloo Institute for Nanotechnology.

“We get the punch of boiling, but only where and when it’s needed.”

The chip surface around the drop also ran cooler than it did in water alone, so the drop was helping beyond the patch it covered.

The boiling drop had limits. At the lowest heat setting, water alone kept the chip coolest early on. And until the drop had boiled away, its own footprint ran up to 29°F (16°C) hotter than the surface around it.

Mitra’s lab specializes in wrapping one liquid in a thin skin of another, and the team tried it on the coolant drops.

The team floated a small ring from a plumbing supplier on the water, poured a thin layer of silicone oil inside it, and let coolant drip through the oil. Each drop picked up a silicone skin as it sank to the chip.

The silicone skin changed the boiling. At the middle heat setting, a plain silicone skin delayed boiling by about 1.5 seconds and let the hot spot climb about 14°F (8°C) higher first.

When boiling did start, it happened all at once, as one large bubble, and that drop cooled the chip faster than any other.

Mixing tiny particles of a heat-conducting ceramic, aluminum nitride, into the oil did the opposite. The particles gave bubbles places to start, so many small bubbles formed instead of one big one.

At the highest heat, those skins cut the peak hot-spot temperature by about 18°F (10°C).

Skins have a cost, though. Leftover oil on the chip surface slowed cooling later on, so the wrapped drops did worse than bare ones over a longer period.

Everything so far happened in still water on a heated square of sapphire, not on a processor in a working server.

Real cooling systems usually pump their liquid, and the authors wrote that boiling in flowing water may behave quite differently and needs its own tests.

High-heat runs also varied noticeably from one repeat to the next, though the overall pattern held each time.

Mitra’s team described two ways the idea could work. Sensors could detect a hot spot and deliver a drop to it, or wrapped drops could circulate in the water and boil wherever they land on something hot enough.

Next on the authors’ list are shell liquids that leave less residue, ways to wrap drops inside flowing coolant, and tests under realistic operating conditions.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Colloid and Interface Science.
