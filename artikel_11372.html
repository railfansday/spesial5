Disturbances and climate drive tree elevational shifts

Climate change and disturbances are reshaping forest ecosystems worldwide1,2,3,4, yet their combined effects on species redistribution remain poorly understood. Here we analyse repeated measurements from 44,368 forest inventory plots spanning 1999–2025, with an average remeasurement interval of 10 years, to examine how climate and natural disturbances jointly influence the elevational redistribution of tree species across the western USA. We show that climate warming increased sapling abundance, whereas increasing drought frequency and recent natural disturbances (fire, insect outbreaks and disease) reduced tree abundance, particularly among adult trees. By contrast, legacy fire was associated with increased sapling abundance, with stronger positive responses in angiosperms than in gymnosperms. These influences were distributed unevenly across species’ elevational ranges: upper portions of species’ ranges generally experienced stronger warming, greater increases in drought frequency and more disturbance activity than lower portions, creating elevational asymmetries in stress and recovery. These elevational asymmetries were associated with more pronounced downslope shifts in basal area centres, probably reflecting disturbance effects on larger trees. By contrast, stem abundance centres showed more divergent shifts, consistent with sapling responses to both climate change and disturbance. Our findings reveal contrasting demographic responses to climate change and natural disturbances that shape the elevational redistribution of different species, with potential consequences for forest composition, resilience and long-term stability.

Shifts in the distribution of tree species in response to climate change1,2 and disturbances3,4 can substantially affect forest structure, biodiversity and ecosystem services5,6,7,8. Such redistribution can be characterized by shifts in tree abundance centres, which reveals how populations reorganize within their ranges2,9,10. Given that cooler conditions occur at higher latitudes and elevations, poleward and upslope shifts in species distribution are expected with climate change1,9,10,11,12. However, downslope shifts of tree species in montane ecosystems have also been reported, probably reflecting the influence of non-temperature factors that alter tree abundance and distribution in contrasting ways13,14,15,16. Natural disturbances, such as wildfires, diseases and insect outbreaks, along with subsequent regeneration, can alter stem abundance and basal area (the cross-sectional surface area of tree trunks measured at breast height—1.37 m above the ground) across regions4, influencing latitudinal or longitudinal shifts3. Yet, the roles of disturbances in shaping species’ elevational redistribution remains underexplored8.

Forests in the western USA span a broad elevational range, from near sea level to approximately 3,700 m, near the upper treeline. Over recent decades, these forests have experienced pronounced warming and frequent droughts17,18, which have increased wildfire activity19,20 and insect outbreaks21,22, and altered forest disease patterns22,23. As these disturbances can alter tree demographic dynamics4, elevational variation in disturbance activity may drive the elevational redistribution of tree abundance. Moreover, warming may shift disturbance patterns23,24,25, further influencing tree abundance redistribution. Although previous studies have reported elevational shifts across regions, most have been restricted geographically with inconsistent patterns9,14,26,27,28. A comprehensive and cross-regional assessment using a consistent methodology remains lacking, which limits our understanding of elevational shift patterns and their underlying mechanisms in the context of environmental change.

To fill this gap, we quantified elevational shifts in tree abundance centres of common tree species across individual ecosections (ecoregion sections) in the western USA using remeasured plots conducted by the US Department of Agriculture, Forest Service, Forest Inventory and Analysis (FIA) programme29. As the western USA spans a vast climatic and ecological gradient and encompasses diverse forest types, estimating elevational shifts across the entire region would mask regional-scale differences and could be confounded by species’ geographical redistribution among regions14,30,31,32. We therefore conducted our analyses separately within individual ecosections for each species. By selecting tree species that occurred in more than 100 remeasured FIA plots without evidence of silvicultural treatment within individual montane ecosections (Fig. 1a), we identified 45 species (Extended Data Table 1) across 33 ecosections, yielding 212 species–ecosection combinations. Collectively, they accounted for 68% of total stems and 69% of total basal area across montane forests in the western USA.

In this study, we aimed to address three objectives: (1) to characterize patterns of elevational shifts in tree abundance centres across species–ecosections in the western USA under climate change and natural disturbances; (2) to examine how climate and disturbance jointly influence tree abundance changes and (3) to evaluate how elevational asymmetries in climate and disturbance activity influence the elevational redistribution of tree abundance. We quantified elevational shifts for individual species–ecosections using two measures of tree abundance: basal area and stem abundance. For each measure, the shift was calculated as the difference in species’ elevational centres between two inventory periods (T1, 1999–2013 and T2, 2012–2025), yielding the basal area shift and stem shift. Changes in stem abundance and basal area both reflect demographic processes but differ in their sensitivity to tree size classes. Stem abundance may be influenced more strongly by sapling dynamics (mortality and ingrowth), as saplings are typically more numerous than adult trees. Here, ingrowth refers to the entry of stems into a specified size class by crossing a diameter threshold as defined by the FIA: sapling ingrowth occurs when stems enter the 2.54–12.7 cm diameter at breast height (DBH) class, and adult ingrowth occurs when stems grow beyond 12.7 cm DBH. FIA ingrowth therefore represents the combined outcome of earlier recruitment, survival and growth sufficient for an individual to cross an inventory threshold. Basal area gives greater weight to larger trees and is influenced more strongly by the growth and mortality of adult trees. Thus, the two measures provide complementary information on population change. Because shifts in abundance centre ultimately arise from asymmetric tree abundance changes along species’ elevational gradients, understanding the drivers of plot-level tree abundance change is essential for interpreting the mechanisms underlying these shifts. We then assessed the effects of changes in mean annual temperature (MAT) and drought frequency, together with disturbance factors including fire, insect outbreaks and disease, on plot-level tree abundance changes. Finally, we investigated the elevational asymmetries in these environmental factors, defined as differences between elevational segments above (upper) and below (lower) the elevational centre at T1, and evaluated their associations with ecosection-level elevational shifts using generalized linear mixed models.

Elevational redistribution of basal area and stem abundance varied substantially among species–ecosections, with many showing little change in elevational centres whereas others exhibited significant upslope or downslope shifts (Extended Data Fig. 1). For basal area, shifts were more often downslope than upslope: 42 of 212 species–ecosections shifted significantly (P < 0.05) downslope and 28 shifted upslope, representing 30% and 11% of total basal area across all focal species–ecosections, respectively. When aggregating all species within individual ecosections, 12 of the total 33 ecosections shifted downslope, whereas four ecosections shifted upslope (Fig. 1c). Except for the Nevada–Utah Mountain Steppe (M341), downslope shifts were more common than upslope shifts across ecoregion provinces (Fig. 1c). When aggregating across ecosections by species, 16 species (61.5% total basal area) experienced a significant downslope shift and 11 (11.3% total basal area) shifted upslope. Among the eight most dominant species, six shifted downslope (P < 0.05), including Pseudotsuga menziesii (Douglas fir), Pinus ponderosa (ponderosa pine), Pinus contorta (lodgepole pine), Tsuga heterophylla (western hemlock), Picea engelmannii (Engelmann spruce) and Abies concolor (white fir), whereas none shifted upslope (Fig. 1d).

When measured by stem abundance, shift in elevational centre was more divergent. Specifically, 23 species–ecosections moved downslope and 22 upslope (Extended Data Fig. 1), representing 7.4% and 10.7% of total stem abundance across all focal species–ecosections, respectively (Fig. 1b). Spatially, five ecosections exhibited significant overall downslope shifts, whereas six ecosections showed significant upslope shifts (Fig. 1c). At the species level, only three species shifted downslope and six shifted upslope (Fig. 1d), reflecting divergent elevational responses among ecosections within individual species (Extended Data Fig. 1).

To understand demographic changes underlying the observed elevational shifts, we examined range-edge dynamics by comparing relative tree abundance change between the upper and lower range segments for species–ecosections with significant shifts (Extended Data Fig. 2). Upper and lower range segments were defined as portions of a species’ range above and below the elevational centre weighted by basal area or stem abundance from the initial inventory (T1). For basal area, 61% of upslope shifts and 69% of downslope shifts were associated with consistent changes of basal area in upper and lower segments (either both declining or both increasing, but at differing rates above versus below the elevational centre). For stem abundance, 64% of upslope shifts were associated with declines at lower and increases at upper-range segments, whereas 57% of downslope shifts showed the opposite pattern. This indicates that stem shifts are associated with asymmetric demographic responses between cooler (upper) and warmer (lower) elevational segments, with increase in abundance at one end coupled with decline at the other.

We further analysed the tree abundance change at upper and lower ranges for saplings and adult trees, separately. The asymmetric responses of saplings between range segments aligned with stem-based shifts, whereas those of adults aligned with basal area shifts, indicating that stem shifts were driven primarily by changes in sapling abundance and basal area shifts were driven primarily by changes in adult basal area (Extended Data Fig. 2b). Results were similar when using plots in the uppermost and lowermost 25% of elevations (Supplementary Fig. 1).

As uneven changes in tree abundance along elevational gradients drive shifts in abundance centre, we examined how plot-level changes in tree abundance were associated with climate change and disturbance history. From the recent past (1983–2002, as a baseline) to the study period (2003–2022), MAT in the study region increased by an average of 0.7 °C (Extended Data Fig. 3). Drought frequency, determined based on the 12-month Standardized Precipitation–Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) in July (SPEI12July), generally increased, with the largest increases in southern provinces. Based on FIA records, recent disturbances recorded at T2 (less than or equal to 10 years before revisit) and legacy disturbances recorded at T1 (10–15 years before revisit) showed similar spatial patterns, but legacy disturbances affected smaller forest areas overall (Extended Data Fig. 4).

A generalized linear mixed model fitted across all species–ecosections revealed that climate, disturbance and their interactions were key drivers of stem abundance change (Fig. 2). Increases in temperature and drought frequency exerted modest but opposing influences on stem abundance: warming corresponded to gains, whereas greater drought frequency corresponded to losses. Baseline drought frequency partially mitigated the negative effects of increasing drought frequency on stem abundance, suggesting that forests historically exposed to more frequent droughts were more resistant to additional water stress.

Disturbances exerted strong effects on changes in stem abundance, and these effects differed between recent and legacy disturbances (Fig. 2). Recent fires were associated with the largest reductions in stem abundance, whereas legacy fires increased stem abundance, probably reflecting forest regeneration from earlier fires. Disease had generally weak effects, but legacy disease was associated with stronger negative effects than recent disease, suggesting more persistent impacts of earlier disease events. Conversely, recent insect outbreaks were associated with larger reductions in stem abundance than legacy outbreaks, consistent with partial recovery following earlier outbreaks. Moreover, where previous disease and insect outbreaks had occurred, recent fires were associated with greater declines in stem abundance (Extended Data Fig. 5), suggesting interactive and compounding disturbance impacts.

Fire effects also interacted with other factors including baseline drought, species’ fire tolerance and plant clade (angiosperm or gymnosperm) (Fig. 2). Specifically, fires in historically dry areas were associated with smaller reductions in stem abundance, probably because lower vegetation productivity and fuel availability in dry forests can limit fire severity and associated mortality33. Recent fire effects also depended on species’ functional traits, with fire-tolerant species experiencing smaller declines following recent fires. Legacy fire was associated positively with stem abundance in both plant clades, with substantially stronger responses in angiosperms than in gymnosperms (Fig. 2a,b). Despite some variation, models in individual provinces were largely consistent with the overall effects (Extended Data Fig. 6).

To evaluate potential life-stage differences in responses to environmental change, we also modelled stem abundance change separately for saplings and adult trees (Fig. 2 and Extended Data Fig. 5). Warming primarily promoted sapling abundance but had little influence on adult abundance. Although recent fires consistently caused declines in both saplings and adults, legacy fires showed a positive effect on sapling abundance but a negative effect on adults. This indicates regeneration events after legacy fires, but many recruits are still below the 12.7 cm DBH threshold at plot remeasurement (10–15 years after fire). Disease decreased adult stem abundance; recent disease had little effect on saplings and legacy disease was associated with reduced sapling abundance. Legacy fire effects on stem abundance differed between saplings and adults, and between angiosperms and gymnosperms. Legacy fire was associated with increased stem abundance in saplings, with substantially stronger responses in angiosperms than in gymnosperms. By contrast, legacy fire slightly reduced adult stem abundance, with no significant difference observed between angiosperms and gymnosperms (Fig. 2d,f).

Models of basal area dynamics showed patterns similar to adult stem abundance (Extended Data Figs. 7 and 8), reflecting a strong contribution of large trees to basal area. While MAT change had a marginally positive effect on basal area change (P = 0.056), drought frequency change and most disturbance factors showed negative effects, except for the non-significant effect of recent disease and positive effect of legacy fire. The positive effect of legacy fire was driven primarily by angiosperms, whereas gymnosperms showed relatively weak responses (Extended Data Fig. 7b,d).

To further understand the mechanisms underlying elevational shifts in tree abundance centres, we investigated how climate change and disturbance were distributed across species’ elevational gradients. We hypothesized that, rather than the average changes in environmental drivers across elevations, uneven exposure to the environmental changes between upper and lower range segments drives the observed elevational shifts in tree abundance centres. To test this hypothesis, we quantified differences in changes in climate (MAT and drought frequency) and disturbance extent between the two elevational segments (above and below the elevational centre at T1) for individual species–ecosections.

Overall, trees in upper-range segments experienced greater climate change and more extensive disturbance than in lower segments (Fig. 3). The upper–lower difference in the change in MAT (ΔMAT) was significantly positive for all the ecoregion provinces, indicating stronger warming at higher elevations (Fig. 3a). For drought frequency, lower range segments generally had higher values than upper segments during the baseline period (1983–2002), but this pattern partially reversed during the study period (2003–2022), with more ecoregion provinces showing higher drought frequency in upper segments (Supplementary Fig. 2). Consistent with this shift, temporal increases in drought frequency (ΔDrought) were greater in upper than lower species ranges in four ecoregion provinces, including Cascade Mixed Forest (M242), Sierran Steppe (M261), Arizona–New Mexico Mountains Steppe (M313) and Northern Rocky Mountain Forest (M333) (Fig. 3b).

Patterns of disturbance asymmetry varied by type and period (Fig. 3c–h). Notably, recent fire and disease exhibited significantly (P < 0.05) positive upper–lower differences in more provinces than their legacy counterparts. Specifically, recent fires affected larger proportions of forest area in the upper range in three of the seven ecoregion provinces, whereas the lower range was more affected in only one province (Fig. 3f). By contrast, there was no clear evidence showing the elevational asymmetry in legacy fire. Recent disease affected larger upper-range proportions in three provinces (Fig. 3g), compared with two provinces for legacy disease (Fig. 3d). Both legacy and recent insect outbreaks were more prevalent in upper-range segments in three provinces.

We then modelled elevational shift rate in basal area and stem abundance, with species–ecosections as observational units and upper–lower asymmetries in climate change and disturbance as predictors (Fig. 4 and Extended Data Fig. 9). For basal area shifts, upper–lower difference in recent fire extent and insect outbreak extent showed significantly negative effects (P < 0.01), indicating that greater disturbance extents at one end of elevational range tended to displace population centres towards the opposite end. Elevational asymmetry in legacy fire showed a marginally significant positive effect on basal area shift on average (P = 0.053). The effect differed significantly between angiosperms and gymnosperms, with stronger upslope redistribution observed in angiosperms (Fig. 4a,b). Plant clade also showed a strong main effect, with angiosperms exhibiting more pronounced upslope shifts than gymnosperms under average covariate conditions across species–ecosections.

For stem shifts, upper–lower difference in ΔDrought, recent fire extent and legacy insect extent showed negative effects. Asymmetric warming (ΔMAT) showed a positive but non-significant association with elevational shift (P = 0.19), implying that warming may promote upslope movement weakly but is probably overshadowed by disturbance and increasing drought frequency. Interaction effects further revealed that species with higher fire tolerance were less sensitive to elevational asymmetry of recent fire extent, consistent with trait-mediated resilience to disturbance. Elevational asymmetry in legacy fire extent also showed a positive effect, suggesting that a greater legacy fire extent at one end of the elevational range was associated with a shift of the elevational centre towards the same end, potentially reflecting post-fire regeneration within previously burned areas. This positive effect was marginally stronger in angiosperms than in gymnosperms (Fig. 4c,d). Similar to basal area shifts, the main effect of plant clade indicated that angiosperms generally exhibited more pronounced upslope shifts than gymnosperms.

Across 212 species–ecosection combinations spanning broad climatic and biogeographical gradients in the western USA, many tree abundance centres remained relatively stable despite ongoing climate change and natural disturbance. This relative stability probably reflected opposing demographic processes, with ingrowth and growth promoted by warming and post-disturbance recovery counterbalanced by mortality associated with drought and disturbance. Where redistribution occurred, stem shifts were divergent, whereas basal area shifts were more commonly downslope (Fig. 1). Stem abundance is influenced more strongly by sapling ingrowth, whereas basal area is linked more closely to larger tree dynamics. The discrepancy between stem and basal area shifts suggests a general upslope redistribution of smaller trees relative to larger trees, consistent with previous findings that seedlings tend to occupy higher elevations than adults28. Elevational shifts in tree abundance centre arise from asymmetric changes in abundance along species’ elevational gradients (Extended Data Fig. 2) and therefore reflect demographic redistribution within species ranges in response to environment change.

Our findings demonstrate that both climate change and natural disturbances, along with their interactions, are key drivers of tree abundance dynamics across the forests of the western USA. The strong negative effects of increasing drought frequency align with extensive evidence of drought-induced mortality and reduced growth under water stress34,35,36. In historically drought-prone areas, drought effects on stem abundance were weaker, consistent with evidence that previous or recurrent drought may enhance resistance to subsequent water stress through stress-memory mechanisms37. This pattern was more evident for sapling abundance than for adult stem abundance, suggesting that drought history may alter the balance between sapling mortality and sapling ingrowth more strongly. By contrast, warming exhibited positive effects on stem abundance, driven largely by enhanced sapling ingrowth or reduced sapling mortality (Fig. 2 and Extended Data Fig. 5). This pattern suggests that temperature increases can promote regeneration where moisture remains sufficient38, which often occurs in cooler or higher-elevation sites.

Although fires, disease and insect outbreaks all reduce tree abundance, their effects unfold differently over time. Biotic disturbances such as disease and insect outbreaks caused prolonged declines, as pathogens and pests can persist for years and continue to kill trees39. By contrast, fires triggered abrupt losses in tree abundance followed by gradual increases during post-fire recovery and regeneration40. Our comparison of recent and legacy disturbances is limited to events recorded by the FIA annual inventory, which captures only a relatively recent disturbance history before plot remeasurement. Because post-disturbance recovery can unfold over longer time scales, earlier events not recorded in the FIA disturbance history may also have contributed to tree abundance redistribution but could not be evaluated directly in our analysis.

Post-fire regeneration has an important role in changing stem abundance and elevational distributions, but its effects differed markedly between angiosperms and gymnosperms (Fig. 2). The relatively weak responses of gymnosperms to legacy fires are consistent with recent studies reporting widespread post-fire regeneration failure of gymnosperms in warm, dry and often low-elevation forests in the western USA41,42. When regeneration thresholds are crossed, fire can act as a tipping point: instead of returning to closed-canopy forest, sites may transition to shrubland, grassland or sparse woodland. By contrast, angiosperms showed relatively stronger positive responses to legacy fire, which aligns with the greater capacity for vegetative resprouting of many angiosperms following fires43. Such contrasting post-fire responses suggest that fires may increasingly promote divergent elevational redistribution between angiosperms and gymnosperms.

Although climate change and disturbance pressures drive tree abundance changes, their elevational asymmetry (differences between upper and lower range segments) emerged as an important predictor of the direction and magnitude of species’ elevational shifts (Fig. 4). Processes that reduce tree abundance including increasing drought frequency and recent disturbances can shift population centres away from the affected end of a species’ elevational range, whereas processes that enhance tree abundance such as warming and post-disturbance recovery at one end pull population centres towards the affected end. Under natural disturbance, stronger pressures in upper-range segments across the western USA (Fig. 3) were associated with more frequent downslope shifts in basal area. When plots affected by silvicultural treatments were included, however, basal area shifts were distributed more evenly between upslope and downslope directions (Supplementary Fig. 5), possibly reflecting the greater prevalence of timber harvest at lower elevations8. These results suggest that the elevational distributions can be modified by other elevationally asymmetric influences, including human activities. By explicitly quantifying these asymmetric environmental pressures rather than relying on area-averaged climate trends and disturbance, our approach captures the spatial heterogeneity that shapes demographic processes directly at species’ range segments. Although conceptually intuitive, such asymmetry has rarely been incorporated explicitly into broad-scale assessments of species redistribution. Our findings highlight the need to consider not only regional trends in climate and disturbance, but also how these pressures are distributed across elevation gradients.

Overall, natural disturbances and drought had stronger effects than warming in our models, probably reflecting differences in the time scales of demographic processes, including mortality, ingrowth and growth. Although natural disturbances and drought can reduce tree abundance rapidly through mortality, increases in stem abundance through ingrowth and in basal area through growth accumulate over time. Given the relatively short remeasurement interval (approximately 10 years) in this study, sapling ingrowth may be more evident than adult ingrowth, which may partly explain why adult stem abundance was less sensitive to warming and legacy disturbance than sapling abundance42. These differences in temporal sensitivity may also contribute to the different patterns of stem and basal area shifts. Basal area shifts, which are dominated by larger trees, were more commonly downslope and associated with greater disturbance activity in upper-range segments (Fig. 3). By contrast, stem shifts were more divergent, probably because sapling ingrowth can offset mortality losses more rapidly in smaller trees. Similar demographic differences may also underlie the contrasting redistribution patterns between plant clades. Angiosperms, which generally exhibit stronger recruitment and post-disturbance recovery43, showed greater upslope redistribution than gymnosperms (Fig. 4b,d). This underscores the importance of long-term monitoring, as repeated measurements over longer intervals are essential for capturing the full demographic imprint of sustained warming and disturbance on species redistribution.

Climate change may have exerted indirect effects on forest redistribution by altering disturbance regimes and their elevational distributions19,44,45. Although lower elevations in forests in the western USA were generally drier than higher elevations during the baseline period (1983–2002), drought frequency has increased disproportionately in upper-range segments in recent decades (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2). This increase in drought frequency, together with stronger warming in species’ upper-range segments, may help explain the greater fire activity observed there23,24,46. Consistent with our findings, wildfires in the western USA have advanced upslope at an average rate of 7.6 m per year between 1984 and 2017 (ref. 24). Moreover, elevated winter temperatures have enabled mountain pine beetle (Dendroctonus ponderosae) to survive at higher elevations than previously possible, exposing previously less affected high-elevation forests to greater pest pressure47.

Our study underscores the substantial influence of disturbances, particularly wildfires and insect outbreaks, on tree abundance and elevational redistribution. Recent disturbances were often associated with reduced tree abundance, driving abundance centres shifting downslope. Meanwhile, climate warming and post-fire recovery tended to increase tree abundance, and post-fire recovery was associated with upslope shifts in tree abundance centres. Therefore, the combined pressures from climate change and disturbances place forest ecosystems in the western USA ‘between a rock and a hard place’. Given projected future climate change and increasing disturbance activities globally45, our findings highlight the need for continued monitoring and adaptive forest management strategies to mitigate these impacts and promote ecosystem sustainability and resilience.

The annual inventory of the FIA programme uses a nationally consistent plot design (around 1 plot per 2,428 hectares (ha)) and provides remeasurements with consistent data collection procedures nationwide, enabling robust estimation of changes in forest structure and species distribution. Each FIA plot consists of four 168 m2 subplots on which adult trees (DBH ≥ 12.7 cm) are measured. Within each subplot, a 13.5 m2 microplot is nested to sample saplings (2.54 cm ≤ DBH < 12.7 cm)29. For some plots, additional macroplots are established to measure very large trees. To account for differences in sampling area among microplots, subplots and macroplots, we used the trees-per-acre expansion factors provided by FIA, which standardize tree abundance estimates to a common per-area basis. We obtained forest attributes from the initial inventory period (T1, 1999–2013) and the subsequent remeasurement period (T2, 2012–2025). Because forest management can influence regional patterns of tree density, we excluded plots that showed evidence of recent silvicultural activity, including harvesting, artificial regeneration or site preparation. A total of 44,368 forest plots located within montane ecoregions were selected across 11 western US states (Washington, Oregon, Idaho, Montana, Wyoming, California, Utah, Nevada, Colorado, Arizona and New Mexico). The average interval between measurements was approximately 10 years.

Elevational shifts were quantified within individual species–ecosection combinations, rather than across the entire western US, to avoid conflating elevational shifts with latitudinal shifts. Ecoregion boundaries followed the US Forest Service 2007 ecoregion map48. We selected 45 tree species that occurred in more than 100 remeasured FIA plots without evidence of silvicultural treatment within individual montane ecosections, yielding 212 species–ecosection combinations across 33 ecosections. For each species–ecosection, elevational shifts were quantified as the difference in the stem abundance-weighted or basal area-weighted elevational centres of all trees (DBH ≥ 2.54 cm) between the two inventory periods. The shift rate (metres per decade) was calculated by dividing the elevational shift distance by the mean remeasurement interval of plots where the target species was present. To examine spatial and taxonomic patterns, we further aggregated species–ecosection elevational shifts to the ecosection and species levels. For stem shifts, the mean shift for each aggregation was calculated using stem abundance as a weighting factor, whereas basal area was used as the weight for basal area shifts.

Because ecosection boundaries can intersect species’ elevational ranges, particularly at lower elevations, they may influence the observed elevational shifts by truncating portions of species distributions. To evaluate the potential impact of this boundary effect, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by extending montane ecosections into adjacent non-montane regions using spatial buffers. We applied buffer widths of 50 km and 100 km, which correspond roughly to the average width of counties in the western USA and are appropriate given the spatial uncertainty associated with FIA plot coordinate swapping within counties. Elevational shifts were recalculated under each buffering scenario and compared with the original estimates based on unbuffered ecosections. The overall distribution of elevational shifts remained highly consistent among the 0 km, 50 km and 100 km buffering scenarios (Supplementary Fig. 6). Therefore, we retained the original Bailey’s ecosections as the primary study units because buffering produced little change in the overall patterns.

This study aimed to examine how natural processes, including climate change and natural disturbances, influence tree elevational redistribution in montane forests in the western USA. Because silvicultural treatments often depend on local management objectives, ownership and socioeconomic conditions, we excluded plots that showed evidence of recent silvicultural activity from the main analysis. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the elevational shift calculations with these plots included. This analysis produced more upslope shifts in basal area centres than the treatment-excluded analysis, resulting in a more balanced number of upslope and downslope shifts (Supplementary Fig. 5), possibly reflecting low-elevation timber harvest8. We therefore focused on the treatment-excluded analysis to minimize the influence of silvicultural treatments and better evaluate tree redistribution associated primarily with climate change and natural disturbances.

To test whether the observed shifts in elevational centre were statistically significant, we used a non-parametric bootstrap resampling approach3,49,50. For each species–ecosection combination, FIA plots were treated as the resampling unit, and paired T1 and T2 observations were resampled together with replacement using the original sample size (Supplementary Table 1). In each iteration, elevational centres for both inventory periods and the resulting elevational shift were recalculated. This procedure was repeated 10,000 times to generate a bootstrap distribution of elevational shift estimates. From the distribution, we derived percentile-based 95% CIs and corresponding empirical two-tailed P values. A shift was considered significant when the 95% CI did not include zero. The two-tailed P value was calculated as twice the smaller proportion of bootstrap estimates greater or less than 0, representing the probability of observing a shift as extreme as the empirical value under the null hypothesis of no change.

In the FIA, plots are divided into conditions representing distinct landscape units based on attributes identified by field crews, such as ownership, land type (for example, forest, water) and evidence of disturbance. The proportional area of each condition within a plot is recorded. The FIA database records disturbance events on mapped forest conditions, such as fire, insect outbreak and disease, that occurred since the last measurement in a remeasurement or within the last 5 years for new plots. Only disturbance events affecting at least 1 acre (0.4 ha) in size (including both the plot and adjacent affected areas) and causing mortality or damage to at least 25% of trees were recorded51. Based on the recorded condition proportions, we calculated the total forest area and that disturbed by any of the three principal disturbance agents including fire, insect outbreak and disease in each plot at both the initial inventory (T1; legacy) and remeasured inventory (T2; recent). On average, legacy and recent fires occurred 13.1 and 4.1 years before the T2 remeasurement, respectively. Note that one plot could experience more than one type of disturbance agent, and the area was attributed to each agent based on its specific disturbance, resulting in possible overlap among disturbance agents. For each species–ecosection, we summed forest area and disturbed area from plots using FIA-defined expansion factors with the target species present and calculated the disturbance extent as the proportion of disturbed forest area to the total forest area at both inventories.

For climate variables, we used 800-m resolution monthly temperature data from the PRISM Climate Group Long-Term dataset52 (https://data.prism.oregonstate.edu/time_series/us/lt/800m/) and 4-km SPEI12July from the WestWide Drought Tracker53 (https://wrcc.app/climate/wwdt/archives). The PRISM long-term dataset was used because it is based only on long-term stations of record and is therefore considered more temporally stable than the PRISM All-Network dataset54. SPEI is a drought index calculated as difference between precipitation and potential evapotranspiration over a specified time period55. SPEI12July was used because it is correlated strongly with tree mortality and growth56,57,58,59,60. Drought events were determined as years with SPEI12July < −1.0 (refs. 57,58,61,62,63,64). MAT and drought frequency (number of drought years per decade) were calculated in the recent past (1983–2002) for a baseline and the study period (2003–2022). Climate change was calculated as the difference between these two periods, accounting for potential lags between climatic trends and vegetation responses1,65. For each species–ecosection, climate variables were extracted from all plots where the species occurred and averaged to obtain ecosection-level estimates.

We computed elevational asymmetry for relative tree abundance change (Extended Data Fig. 2), climate change and disturbance extent (Fig. 3) by calculating their difference between upper and lower range segments. Relative tree abundance change between T1 and T2 was quantified for stem abundance and basal area as a symmetric percent change, which places increase and decreases on a common scale (the average of T1 and T2) and avoids the asymmetry of conventional percent change66. Upper and lower range segments are defined as the elevational range above and below the elevational centre weighted by stem abundance or basal area from the initial inventory (T1). We also conducted analysis using plots within the uppermost and lowermost 25% of elevations and found similar patterns (Supplementary Figs. 1, 3 and 4).

Species attributes of fire tolerance, drought tolerance and plant clade (angiosperm or gymnosperm) were obtained from the US Department of Agriculture PLANTS Database (https://plants.sc.egov.usda.gov/). In PLANTS, fire tolerance qualitatively describes a species’ ability to survive or recover from fire and is classified into four levels: None, Low, Medium and High. Similarly, drought tolerance, describing a species’ ability to survive or maintain function under low soil moisture, is categorized into three levels: Low, Medium and High. For the four species lacking trait entries in PLANTS (Juniperus deppeana, Quercus arizonica, Quercus wislizeni and Quercus grisea), tolerance classes were assigned on the basis of expert knowledge. Detailed information on species attributes is provided in Extended Data Table 1.

We used a non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test to evaluate whether the upper–lower differences in climate change and disturbance extent were significantly different from zero (Fig. 2). Fire and drought tolerance classes were converted to continuous variables to facilitate model fitting. Plant clade was included as a categorical predictor using sum-to-zero contrasts (angiosperm = 1, gymnosperm = −1). All predictors except plant clade are standardized to zero mean and unit standard deviation before modelling. Species and ecosection were included as random effects. Because the models included several interaction terms (for example, climate change versus baseline climate), we evaluated collinearity among predictors using variance inflation factor. All predictors variance inflation factors less than 5, indicating acceptable collinearity, and no variables were excluded from the final models. For interpretability, model coefficients and their 95% CIs were later back-transformed from the log response ratio as \(100\times [{e}^{\beta }-1]\) and expressed as percentage changes in tree abundance associated with a 1 s.d. increase in each continuous predictor. Models were fitted for all plots across species–ecosections, separately for each ecoregion province, and for saplings and adult trees. The full models are provided in Extended Data Figs. 5 and 8.

Elevational shifts in stem abundance and basal area of species–ecosections were also modelled using glmmTMB with Gaussian error distribution and identity link. Predictors included the upper–lower difference in climate change, disturbance extent and their interactions (Extended Data Fig. 9). Species identity was included as a random effect. We also evaluated ecoregion province as an additional random intercept, but its variance was negligible and it had little influence on model results. Therefore, only species identity was retained in the final models.
