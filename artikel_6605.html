The Art of the Holiday Tablescape: Four Tastemakers Share Their Festive Creations

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

When it comes to hosting a dinner party, there’s setting the table, and then there’s crafting a tablescape.

British interior designer David Hicks is said to have coined the term—a portmanteau of “table” and “landscape”—in the 1960s. In the 2000s, it was popularized by the celebrity chef Sandra Lee, who once told Vogue that “it’s so much more fun when you organize your table around a theme.” Nowadays, on TikTok, Pinterest, and Instagram, there are countless tablescapes to feast your eyes on, or to inspire you as you prepare to build one of your own. There are even tablescape competitions at county fairs and other venues, reportedly dating back to the 1930s. Crafting the perfect table is no longer just an art—it’s a sport.

With that in mind, Vogue invited four cultural tastemakers, hosting enthusiasts, and culinary professionals—Lisa Przystup, Blanca Miró Scrimieri, Antonio Thomas Jr., and Thư Phạm Buser—to share how they put together their ideal holiday tablescapes. From retro-inspired to nature-filled tables, read on for all their creations.

“One of the most joyful things about the holiday season is that it gives adults permission to play,” says Lisa Przystup, a design writer and author of Once Around the Sun.

When putting together a tablescape, Przystup prioritizes using what she already has in the house, along with what she can gather right outside her door in New York’s Western Catskills, from dried leaves to evergreens and moss. (Though a word of caution: “When it comes to moss, you should take no more than 10% of a single patch, collect only from private land with permission or non-protected areas, and leave the edges intact so the colony can regenerate.”) For the tablescape she made for Vogue, “The idea was to set a table that could work for November and December dinners and be easily updated over the course of the holiday season,” she says. “For example, you could update this with evergreens and more festive plate settings as you get closer to Christmas, or replace the swan with some kabocha squash for something more autumnal.”

Plus, she says, don’t break the bank with your decorations. “Decorations don’t have to cost a ton of money or be perfect. Embrace your inner child and make paper chains, cut out snowflakes, make cotton ball garlands—any and all scrap pieces of paper (grocery bags, wrapping paper, construction paper) can be transformed into a decoration of some sort with the help of scissors, a hot glue gun, tape, and imagination. Also: More is more.”

When the culinary artist Thư Phạm Buser is preparing to host people, she thinks deeply about the reason for the gathering. “Sometimes I invite people because I miss them and want to show them how much I care. Maybe something crazy went down, and we need a four-course tea party to spill, or I’ve just returned from traveling and want to delight them with a little gift,” Buser says. “Feeding people is my love language. It’s a pleasure to think about what dishes each person will connect with in advance.”

In addition to hosting at home, Buser is co-founder and host of Ăn Cỗ, a Vietnamese dinner event series. (The series name means “going to a feast.”) While Buser loves creating a beautiful table, the most important thing is a sense of fun. “I also think this is the year to have some ridiculous fun over the holidays! Give me some cheap props and a silly premise, and I’ll happily turn the night into an outrageous game of charades, karaoke, or riotous gambling with coins. We played a game recently where you have to guess the famous person, but you can only meow in their voice,” she says.

To that end, “I’d also recommend not worrying too much about the little things, but going out of your way to make everyone comfortable,” she says. “Even though I’ve lived in the US for nearly a decade, I’m still such a Viet at heart. Every time someone visits, there are always starter snacks with chili sauce and a fizzy cocktail waiting when they walk in the door, and the dishes will keep coming until they tap out.”

And if people offer to help with dishes? “I say they need to come for dinner three times before they can do so,” Buser says. “It helps with any guilt they might feel and hints that I’d love for them to return soon.”

For the New York City and Annapolis–based digital creator and antique dealer Antonio Thomas Jr., tablescaping and decorating always begins with a dream, no matter how out of reach his vision might seem at first. For his Vogue tablescape, he wanted “to bring the outdoors inside, creating the feeling of dining beneath a tree,” he says.

“I envisioned branches suspended from the ceiling, adorned with pinecones and greenery, almost acting as a natural chandelier,” he continues. Thomas gathered the pinecones for the piece at a local park, selecting the ones that he liked best and putting it all together.

For a table that feels elegant but not overly curated, Thomas recommends mixing and matching, combining different china patterns and objects. “One piece I especially loved was my great-grandmother’s perfume tray, which I used as a centerpiece element to help elevate the table,” he says. In this case, he used an ice bucket as a floral vessel and made his own table linens.

His main piece of advice, though? Don’t get caught up in all the to-dos. “As hosts, we can become so preoccupied with little tasks—whether it’s tidying up, checking on the food, or making sure everything is perfect—that we forget to be present. Your guests are there to spend time with you, and I think being a gracious host means creating an atmosphere where everyone, including yourself, feels comfortable and relaxed.”

When setting the table for a dinner party, Blanca Miró Scrimieri, the Barcelona-based stylist and co-founder of the brand La Veste, goes with her gut.

“I usually start with one element I’m especially excited about,” she says, “whether that’s a tablecloth, a set of plates, some cutlery I found on a trip, or something old I’ve inherited or rescued from a family member. I love using pieces that have a story behind them, so I tend to build the whole table around that one starting point.”

For her Vogue tablescape, Scrimieri began by using an antique yo-yo quilt as a tablecloth. “I wanted it to feel festive and holiday-inspired, without going into traditional Christmas colors. So it became this kind of vintage feast of candied fruit, colorful, nostalgic, a little theatrical, and very joyful,” she says. Scrimieri used her mother’s old sterling silver trays and striped glasses from Laguna~B in Venice, sprinkling in glittery rats and other retro, unexpected objects. The goal? “Almost like a festive table from another era, but with a sense of humor.”

Every year, Scrimieri and her husband, Javi Fondevila, host a traditional Christmas dinner. She’s picked up plenty of hosting advice over the years. “My first tip would be to prepare your table with lots of love and lots of color,” she says. “I think we all need a little more color and joy in our lives, especially during the holidays.” And, she adds, focus on the food. Make sure you have more than enough, and go big on the desserts. Last but not least? Let everyone stay a while.

“I love a very long sobremesa, which is such a Spanish thing. Staying around the table for hours after eating, talking, laughing, playing cards, guessing games, or any silly game that gets the whole family involved. For me, the best gatherings are the ones where nobody wants to leave.”
