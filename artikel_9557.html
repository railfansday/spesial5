The Lies We Heard (and Told Ourselves) About ADHD

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

The world is largely aware of ADHD, but does it truly understand? Jessica McCabe sees lots of room for improvement. Here, the founder of “How to ADHD” reflects on the persistent misconceptions, knowledge gaps, and threats facing people, especially mothers, with ADHD today.

Along the way, McCabe has become a certifiable ADHD spokesperson, a New York Times bestselling book author, and a mother. She has also contributed significantly to the growing de-stigmatization of ADHD and to a still-broiling conversation about how to better recognize and support women with ADHD.

“The more we talk about it, the more shame dissipates,” McCabe said during her recent ADDitude webinar, “The Lies We Were Told About ADHD: An ADHD Awareness Month Conversation.”

When McCabe posted her video, there was no YouTube #ADHD channel and TikTok did not yet exist. Today, social media is bursting with research, analysis, and validation. It’s also wrong more than it is right.

According to a 2025 study, more than half of the claims made in the Top 100 TikTok videos on ADHD were false. Almost 90% of videos were created by non-experts, and only about 20% of ADHD content was deemed clinically useful by trained professionals.1

“The [videos] that are validating and make you go ‘Yeah, that’s me,’ are not necessarily the ones that are accurate,” McCabe said.

She cited a claim circulating on social media that individuals with ADHD lack object permanence, as an example. Object permanence is the understanding that objects, people, and places continue to exist even when they cannot be seen. People with ADHD experience inattention and executive function challenges such as poor working memory, which may contribute to forgetfulness, but that is distinct from poor object permanence.

McCabe recommended checking sources on any health information found on social media. “Google Scholar it,” she said.

From a young age, people with ADHD hear that the frequency and intensity of their emotions are a problem. They’re told they are “too much,” and that their feelings overwhelm and distract others. McCabe says she grew up suppressing her emotions in hopes of combating that narrative. Now, she is working to actively “unlearn” that negative perception of her feelings, and she hopes others can do the same.

“Some of us have forgotten it’s okay to feel. It’s okay to have emotions when you have ADHD,” she said. “The emotions are not actually the problem. What you do with those emotions might be problematic, but by trying not to feel things, that actually creates other issues.”

Acknowledging and creating space for your emotions keeps them from exploding and/or festering — neither of which is healthy.

Executive dysfunction carries a lot of shame and guilt for people with ADHD. Society generally doesn’t recognize that challenges with working memory, organization, and prioritization, for example, are part and parcel of ADHD. Criticism is a constant, and many people add to the pressure by berating themselves with negative thoughts.

“When you don’t have an explanation, you accept the ones that are handed to you by society,” McCabe said. “It looked like I was flaky. It looked like I was uncommitted. It looked like I didn’t actually care about things.”

Executive functioning often fluctuates. Sometimes you can complete a task; other times, that same task feels impossible. In those moments, you may beat yourself up by saying, “I should be able to do this. Why can’t I do this?”

As McCabe shared, sleep quality, personal interest, support systems, and other factors influence a person’s ability to manage ADHD challenges in the moment. Not all of those factors are obvious, or within your control.

“A lot of those things are invisible, so unless you pick it apart and look at it, you don’t know why you’re struggling. You’re just like ‘I shouldn’t be,’” McCabe said. “Anytime I’m like ‘I should be able to do this,’ [I ask] what’s the evidence for that? Have you ever in your life been able to do this without help consistently? No? Then you shouldn’t.”

The effect of childbirth on the ADHD brain is largely unknown due to a critical research gap. What we do know is that women with ADHD are six times more likely to experience postpartum anxiety and five times more likely to experience postpartum depression than their neurotypical peers.

McCabe, who welcomed her daughter in 2024, suffered from postpartum depression. The supports she relied on during pregnancy stopped working and postpartum hormonal changes brought her to a very dark place mentally and emotionally. She was admitted to a partial hospitalization program for postpartum depression, where she received treatment while caring for her child.

As critical research slowly takes shape, McCabe believes strongly in normalizing this experience for mothers and entire families by talking openly about it.

“I think [the outcome] would’ve been different if I didn’t understand this wasn’t my fault that I was struggling,” she said. “Because the idea of what a mom is supposed to look like and what I looked like were so different that I could have piled so much shame on myself. And I think that shame would have made it so much worse. I wouldn’t have reached out for help. I wouldn’t have wanted to tell anybody I was having these thoughts or struggling with these things.”

“For so long we’ve been asking, ‘How do we get people with ADHD functional?’” she said. “Now I think we can ask a new question: How do we get them happy? How do we make it so they can live a good life? What does a good life with ADHD look like?”

Maybe the objective shouldn’t be to never struggle with focus, time management, memory, or emotional regulation ever again, but to use the tools you’ve developed to build a life that looks good and feels comfortable to you.

“Success and happiness to me with my ADHD is like having this powerful stallion that I know how to take care of, ride, accomplish incredible things, have excitement and adventures, handle the rough and challenging moments, while not getting bucked off, trampled, or left behind in the dust,” Katherine, an ADDitude reader, commented during the live event. “It’s still now working to unlearn all of the suppressive things taught through school that wanted to teach me to break the spirit of the stallion, whip it into submission, and then lock it in the stable and feel ashamed of it and what it needs instead of learning how to work and thrive with it.”
