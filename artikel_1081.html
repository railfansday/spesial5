US science academy urges journals to fix errors more quickly

Update: After a delay, the report discussed in this story was released by the US National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine on 24 November. The academies said that the problem was with erroneous references added to the report in response to peer review.

Science publishers take too long to retract or correct research papers, a report by the US National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM) finds. Standards for fixing errors are also inconsistently applied across journals, it says.

There are no data on how long a retraction or correction currently takes, because journals do not generally disclose when they were alerted to underlying problems; the NASEM report says that they should start doing this. On average, according to the Retraction Watch database, retractions occur around 2.4 years after a paper is published. The report recommends that, in most cases, editorial review processes be completed within 12 weeks, and should rarely stretch beyond 6 months — at least when editors are looking at concerns over a single paper.

“The big picture is, the process is too slow,” says Randy Schekman, a cell biologist at the University of California, Berkeley, who chaired the 13-person committee that produced the NASEM report. A prompter and more transparent process would strengthen the reliability of the scientific record, he says.

The report was due to be published online on 19 November but was delayed after journalists at Science noted that it contained some references with fabricated first names for authors, a possible sign that large language models had been used to write or edit the material and that it had been insufficiently checked.

“It’s unfortunate that there were these flawed references in a report about fixing the literature,” says Jennifer Byrne, a cancer researcher and research-integrity sleuth at the University of Sydney in Australia, who first flagged the concern to Science when reviewing the report, which was shared ahead of publication with media outlets.

The report had two main aims: to speed up the process of retraction or correction and to lower the “tremendous emotional barriers” for authors to correct honest mistakes in the record, Schekman says. “Part of the problem is that it’s too rare for a paper to be retracted,” he adds.

It also suggests that journals shouldn’t wait for complaints to reach them, but instead “take proactive steps” to identify errors and integrity issues. These might include journal-wide audits, or regularly monitoring social-media feeds and online platforms where post-publication issues are raised, it says.

Existing guidelines for publishers, such as those from the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) in London, urge only a “timely” response to concerns1, without setting out expected schedules, and don’t suggest that journals scan online platforms for complaints.

“A major theme running through the report is that we have to distinguish between retraction or correction, and research misconduct,” notes Dorothy Bishop, a research-integrity sleuth in Oxford, UK, who was on the NASEM committee. Authors shouldn’t associate a retraction with the stigma of research misconduct, she says. And although journal editors sometimes wait for a university to investigate whether there is misconduct before correcting a paper, they shouldn’t, she adds.

Calls for changing these attitudes aren’t new: journals have long emphasized that retractions can result from honest mistakes. In a 2022 editorial, Holden Thorp, the editor in chief of Science, urged that the processes of issuing corrections or retractions and making a determination of misconduct should be kept separate2, and COPE guidelines already emphasize the point. But “there are still plenty of people who see a correction or retraction as an allegation of fraud”, says Thorp, who was also on the NASEM committee. “Hopefully, this report will accelerate the recognition of the distinction,” he says.
