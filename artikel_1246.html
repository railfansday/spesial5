Poxviruses probably evolved in the digestive system of animals

This is a summary of: Gaïa, M. et al. Closest relatives of poxviruses prevail in human and animal guts worldwide Nature https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11283-z (2026).

Nucleocytoviruses are a major clade of mainly large and giant double-stranded DNA viruses that infect cells with a nucleus: the eukaryotes. Most of them infect unicellular eukaryotes in the environment. Poxviruses (including the virus that causes smallpox) form a distinct group of nucleocytoviruses that infect various animals, including humans1. How poxviruses that infect animals evolved from the wider group of nucleocytoviruses remains unclear. Animals first evolved about 700 million years ago2 and host various unicellular eukaryotes, mostly in their digestive systems3. Such unicellular eukaryotes could have been the hosts of the ancestors of poxviruses. Indeed, the transition of viruses from a unicellular host to an animal host would be facilitated by the virus adapting over time to the conditions in the animal’s gut; however, previous observations have indicated that nucleocytoviruses are typically transient and are therefore infrequently found there. As such, the origins of poxviruses remain unexplained.

DNA sequencing on environmental, human and animal-associated microbiomes produces metagenomes — all of the genetic information from all organisms in a particular environment — that enable researchers to explore the microbial world without having to cultivate organisms. Motivated by discoveries of viruses in the oceans4 in the past few years using a similar approach, we screened a vast library of metagenomes5 using a single marker gene (encoding an RNA polymerase), known to occur in most nucleocytoviruses.

The signal of this marker gene guided the discovery of a diversified clade of DNA viruses with large linear genomes (the egoviruses), which are most closely related to animal-infecting poxviruses (Fig. 1). We detected egoviruses from metagenomes sampled around the world. The overwhelming majority were found in the guts of humans and domestic and wild animals. The most prevalent one in the human gut was found in various continents, suggesting that egoviruses have long been associated with the microbiome of the human digestive system. Viral genes that we found incorporated into the genomes of eukaryotes suggest that egoviruses mainly infect two groups of unicellular eukaryotes: ciliates that aid digestion in ruminants, and metamonads, which have a role in animal immunity and gut-microbiome ecology. These unicellular eukaryotes can engulf solid particles (including microorganisms and viruses) in a process called phagocytosis. Altogether, this work shows that a distinct clade of large DNA viruses infecting eukaryotes is common in human and animal guts, contrary to previous ideas.

Figure 1 | The evolutionary connection between poxviruses and the newly discovered egoviruses. Evolutionary analysis of nucleocytovirus members, including more than 200 egoviruses (purple part of outside ring). The tree covers 1,807 genomes, was built using comparisons of four genes encoding hallmark nucleocytovirus proteins and is rooted with the putative class Proculviricetes. It indicates that egoviruses (the newly identified order Egovirales) and poxviruses (corresponding to the well-studied Chitovirales) are sister clades. Green dots in the tree highlight high confidence in the support for key nodes. Sources of the metagenomes from which genomes were assembled are specified. The gene flux ring summarizes, for each genome, the genes found to be incorporated into the genomes of eukaryotes. High signals for the unicellular eukaryotes Parabasalia (a group of metamonads) and Ciliophora (ciliates) in the genomes of egoviruses suggests virus–host associations. The largest clade of egoviruses specific to the human gut microbiome is highlighted. kb, kilobases. Gaïa, M. et al./Nature (CC BY 4.0).

The origin of poxviruses remains mysterious owing to their distant relationships with other nucleocytoviruses and the lack of evolutionary intermediates. Egoviruses provide that missing link, given their position as the sister clade to poxviruses and their prevalence in the digestive system. Specifically, our results suggest that the common ancestor of poxviruses and egoviruses infected unicellular eukaryotes in the guts of animals before insects and vertebrates diverged from their common ancestor roughly 700 million years ago. This ‘out of the gut’ evolutionary scenario for poxviruses seems to align well with the biology of eukaryotes. Indeed, phagocytosis is a key component of the immune system of animals and serves as the entry mechanism for various DNA viruses. Thus, the ability of the epithelial cells that line the inside of the intestines to perform phagocytosis could offer a mechanistic explanation for how a DNA virus crossed the host barrier from unicellular eukaryotes in the digestive system to animal cells.

Ecological and evolutionary insights gathered thus far on egoviruses have been supported by evidence from environmental genomics. We have neither the microscopic images nor the cultures available for these viruses. To fill knowledge gaps, it will be crucial to perform experiments and develop protocols to not only grow them in association with putative hosts that have already been cultivated, but also gain insights into the connection, if any, between their prevalence and the health and disease of humans as well as domestic and wild animals. — Tom O. Delmont and Morgan Gaïa are at Genoscope, Evry, France.

The authors report the identification and analysis of metagenome-assembled genomes from previously uncharacterized viruses — egoviruses — forming a previously unknown clade basal to poxviruses. The data and computational analysis form a compelling story of great interest, as it provides fascinating insights into the potential evolutionary origin of poxviruses, and reveals new interactions between viruses and some microbes that inhabit the digestive tract of animals.” — Simon Roux is at the DOE Joint Genome Institute, Berkeley, California, USA.

The gut microbiome is an extensively studied ecosystem, owing to its relevance to human health and disease. Microbiologists, virologists, computer scientists and bioinformaticians explore cultured microorganisms and available metagenomes to better understand its ecological and evolutionary facets. Why egoviruses remained elusive for so long despite being an integral component of the gut microbiome can easily be explained by culture efforts and metagenomic workflows being dedicated mainly to bacteria and their viruses. Eukaryotes and their DNA viruses have rarely been the focus of gut microbiome analyses. In the past few years, our team has extensively explored DNA viruses that infect eukaryotes in the oceans, which involved developing screening methodologies to go after the unknowns. Applying our screening methodologies to non-oceanic ecosystems exposed a gut-specific evolutionary signal that led to the egoviruses, unexpectedly shedding light on the origin of poxviruses. — T.O.D.

Delmont and colleagues identify egoviruses, a previously undescribed group of large DNA viruses, related to poxviruses, that are widespread in human and animal digestive systems and probably infect gut-dwelling unicellular eukaryotes. The findings reveal an overlooked component of the gut virome and suggest scenarios for poxvirus evolution. — Editorial team, Nature.
