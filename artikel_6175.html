How to Spend 3 Perfect Days in Naples

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

A fascinating, complex, and multi-layered city, Naples is brimming with contradictions. Dilapidated, neglected neighborhoods share city space with splendid palaces and world-beating museums. The city’s origins go back to the ancient Greeks, yet the contemporary art scene here is buzzing. There is a state-of-the-art metro system designed by renowned artists and architects (no wonder, since the traffic is infamously heavy).

I’ve been visiting for over 30 years and have seen huge changes, most of them positive. Naples today is safer, more tourist-friendly, and cleaner than ever before, yet it remains Italy’s most beguiling city. The eating and drinking scene has evolved, there is a much wider choice of places to stay (with Rocco Forte due to open a hotel here in 2027), and museums and monuments have been spruced up and reorganized. In 2027, the America’s Cup will arrive as well.

You could easily spend two weeks in Naples and not see everything. But if you only have three days, start early and be prepared to pack in as much as possible; you can rest once you get home. Here is my advice for how to make the most of it.

Historic Gran Caffè Gambrinus is a perfect place to start your first day, shoulder to shoulder with the locals at the bar for a shot of espresso and a warm cornetto (only tourists sit on the terrace). Just across Piazza Trieste e Trento lies the imposing dark grey and red Palazzo Reale where you can visit the sumptuous Royal Apartments and ornate Teatrino, and on the other side is the Teatro San Carlo, one of Italy’s most prestigious opera houses; opera buffs can book a behind-the-scenes tour, or even better, tickets for a performance.

From the Toledo metro station, you get full immersion in the Stazione d’Arte, a public art project involving some of the greatest contemporary artists and architects who have transformed Naples’ main underground stations into public galleries. Dominated by Oscar Tusquets Blanca’s jaw-dropping mosaic, Toledo is one of the most impressive—but for just the price of a metro ticket (just over $2), you can tour them all.

A quick walk north on Via Toledo leads to Via della Pignasecca, where you can catch the end of the lively morning market with its troughs of slithering, glistening fish and seafood, piles of seasonal fruit and vegetables, and stalls selling everything from tripe to knock-off designer handbags.

Lunch at no-nonsense Trattoria da Nenella near Piazza Carità is a noisy, fun experience; you’ll probably have to queue, but the deep-fried anchovies and meatballs in tomato sauce are worth it, and prices are rock-bottom.

The Cappella Sansevero is home to Giuseppe Sammartino’s sublime 1753 marble Cristo Velato.

After a shot of the best coffee in the city at Café Mexico in Piazza Dante, you can spend the afternoon exploring the grid of streets around the so-called Spaccanapoli, the heart of Roman Neapolis where ancient monuments rub shoulders with street food stalls, tiny grocers, once-grand palazzi, and shops selling tourist trinkets and religious memorabilia. Piazza del Gesù, at the western end, is dominated by the eponymous church and an ornate roccoco ‘guglia’. Just to the east is the Church and Convent of Santa Chiara with a beautiful Gothic interior and a cloister famous for its bright, 18th-century majolica tiles. Close by is Naples’ most celebrated (and most crowded) sight, the Cappella Sansevero, home to Giuseppe Sammartino’s sublime 1753 marble Cristo Velato.

Back on the Spaccanapoli, nip into Gay-Odin (n. 61) for a chocolate gelato and then continue to the vast Duomo where you will be dazzled by the gilt-encrusted Cappella di San Gennaro and the subterranean church of Santa Restituita and its 4th-century baptistery.

Close by are the chapel of Pio Monte della Misericordia with Caravaggio’s seminal Seven Acts of Mercy and crumbling Piazza San Girolamini where Banksy’s Madonna with a Pistol appeared on the back right-hand wall in 2004. San Lorenzo Maggiore is an essential visit if you want to understand multi-layered Naples; under the 13th-century church, ongoing excavations have revealed the astonishing remains of a Greco-Roman city dating back 2,000 years.

A detour down Via San Gregorio Armeno takes you past shops selling pastori or figurines for Christmas cribs. Much of what you see is mass-produced these days, so for the real deal, visit Marco Ferrigno’s workshop at number 10. Continue to leafy Piazza Bellini, a lively evening hub, and bag a terrace table at Café Intramoenia for a Campari spritz—you’ve earned it.

For dinner, Europeo di Mattozzi is a personal favorite for its echoes of elegant old Naples; the classic food is fantastic (try the sublime melanzane alla parmigiana and the pasta with potatoes and provola cheese), the host Alfonso is charming, and your fellow diners are likely to be mostly locals.

Use Castel dell'Ovoa as your launchpad for a classic Neapolitan passeggiata along the wide, traffic-free seafront drag.

Day two starts where it all began. Parthenope—later Neapolis and later still Napoli—was founded more than 2,500 years ago on the tiny island of Megaris, now occupied by the Castel dell’Ovo and the picturesque fishing hamlet of Borgo Marinari. There’s nothing much to see in the castle, but it’s a good launch pad for the classic Neapolitan passeggiata along the wide, traffic-free seafront drag. It’s lined with restaurants and cafés offering tables in the sun for a cappuccino and magnificent views.

The delightful neighbourhood of Chiaia is a criss-cross of pretty cobbled lanes peppered with independent boutiques, cool bars, and tiny restaurants; Via dei Mille and Via dei Filangeri are known for designer clothes and chic galleries. The handmade silk ties at tiny, old-fashioned Marinella make wonderful gifts, and a counter perch at tiny Pescheria Mattiucci is a good choice for a quick seafood lunch with a glass of something chilled and white.

Depending on the weather (and energy levels), one option from here is to head west beyond Mergellina to the upmarket seaside suburb of Posillipo for an afternoon on the beach; sunbeds and umbrellas can be rented at Bagno Sirena.

Or you can hop on the Funicolare di Chiaia and trundle up to leafy Vomero and the panoramic Certosa di San Martino, a Carthusian monastery complex with an ornate church, several museums, two cloisters, and the famous Monk’s Cemetery decorated with marble skulls. It also has knock-out views over the whole waterfront and the Bay of Naples to the Sorrentine Peninsula. The Friggitoria Vomero—one of the city’s few remaining authentic ‘frying shops’—doles out sizzling snacks from 6 p.m. and is conveniently close to the funicolare stop as you head back down the hill.

Chiaia’s quiet lanes morph into a nightlife hub from aperitivo hour on, filling up with smartly dressed locals and visitors in search of a good night out. Cocktail spot per eccellenza is retro-glam Antiquario where the jacketed barmen mix a stonking Negroni. From here it’s a short stroll to tiny Ristorante Dora, open since 1973, for textbook versions of fish and seafood classics such as spaghetti alle vongole (with local clams). Head back to Chiaia for a nightcap; follow your nose and the party crowd.

The atmospheric burial site of Cimitero delle Fontanelle is a respite from the buzzing streets above.

A no-go area for tourists until relatively recently, the dilapidated, densely populated but intriguing Rione Sanità is Naples in the raw. Breakfast at Pasticceria Popella in Via Arena alla Sanità is a must for their sfogliatelle and cream-stuffed ‘fiocchi di neve’ before having a rummage among the market stalls and tiny shops on Via dei Vergini; at n. 19 is the grand entrance to the famous pale green and cream Palazzo dello Spagnuolo with its celebrated ‘flying staircase’ by local architect Ferdinando San Felice.

These shambolic streets are filled with tooting cars, buzzing vespas, and bewildered tourists, but there are hidden artistic gems too: the 2,400 year-old recently restored Ipogeo dei Cristallini, the atmospheric burial site of the Cimitero delle Fontanelle with its piles of skulls and bones; and the labyrinthine Catacombs of San Gaudioso under the basilica of Santa Maria della Sanità.

You can’t visit the Sanità without a meal at Ciro Oliva’s crazy-lively temple to pizza Concettina ai Tre Santi (booking is essential), where the tasting menus offer an endless succession of slices topped with whatever the pizzaiolo dreams up at the time.

Naples’ Archaeological Museum (aka Mann) houses one of the world’s great collections of ancient art and artefacts, and if you only visit one museum, make it this. Must-sees include the monumental Roman sculptures on the ground floor, the collection of ancient erotica in the Gabinetto Segreto, and the dazzling mosaics, wall paintings, and bronzes from Pompeii and Herculaneum.

Afterwards, check out the brilliant selection of exquisite handmade gloves at factory prices in the workshops of Omega, a family glove business that first opened in 1923, before winding up the day in the surprisingly peaceful oasis of the exotic Orto Botanico gardens on traffic-clogged Via Foria.

For a last blast, Grand Hotel Parker’s on the way up to Vomero is the smart place to go. Cocktails and light bites are served on the rooftop terrace while on the floor below, chef Domenico Candela whips up a storm with his two-Michelin-star take on local cuisine to a backdrop of unforgettable views.
