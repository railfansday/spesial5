Octopus brains challenge what scientists know about learning and memory

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Scientists found a chemical switch that keeps octopus brain connections strong, offering clues to how these animals remember.

Learning changes the connections between brain cells. In animals such as mammals and sea slugs, making those changes last usually requires new proteins.

But octopus brain tissue did something unexpected. Its nerve connections stayed stronger for at least 10 hours, even when researchers blocked the cells from making new proteins.

A gas called nitric oxide may help explain why.

The researchers propose that it keeps the enzyme that produces it active, creating a continuing supply that helps nerve connections stay strong.

Binyamin Hochner’s lab at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem worked with Tal Shomrat’s group at the Ruppin Academic Center.

Ana Luiza Turchetti-Maia carried out most of the experiments, using living brain tissue from common octopuses caught by fishermen off Israel’s Mediterranean coast.

Octopuses solve problems and learn from experience. They have about 500 million nerve cells, giving them the largest nervous system of any animal without a backbone.

A brain region called the vertical lobe helps them learn through sight. It serves a similar role to the hippocampus, which helps people and other vertebrates form long-term memories.

A brief burst of strong activity can strengthen connections between nerve cells in this region.

Scientists call this long-term potentiation, and they think it is one way brains hold on to what they learn.

In 2008, Hochner’s team strengthened connections across the lobe as far as they could go, and the octopuses then struggled to form new long-term memories.

A 2023 map found about 25 million nerve cells in the lobe, most receiving input from just one incoming nerve fiber.

Nitric oxide already looked important: studies in the 1990s found that blocking it disrupted learning through touch and sight.

Later, the team’s tissue tests showed that the enzyme that makes the gas was especially active in the vertical lobe.

For the new study, researchers stimulated incoming nerve fibers with four rapid bursts of 20 pulses each. They then measured the connections’ responses every 30 seconds.

They also used drugs to block nitric oxide synthase, the enzyme that makes the gas. These drugs worked reliably only at much higher doses than researchers usually use.

Scientists often expect nitric oxide to help start the strengthening process. Yet the octopus connections still grew stronger when the blockers were already present.

A thin carbon-fiber sensor placed in the tissue detected a rise in nitric oxide after stimulation.

One roughly calibrated measurement suggested levels about 1,000 times higher than those needed to activate its best-known signaling pathway.

Across five experiments, however, the average rise came later and was less clear.

The researchers suspect that at such high levels, nitric oxide attaches to proteins and changes how they work.

Drugs that targeted its usual signaling pathway did not affect the strengthened connections.

The picture changed when researchers added blockers after the connections had strengthened.

Given 10 minutes later, one drug reduced the strengthened response by an average of 63%, while another reduced it by 82%.

A mirror-image version of a blocker, which cannot block the enzyme, reduced the response by 23%. That smaller drop could have happened by chance.

When researchers washed away the active blockers, the strength returned. One blocker had this effect whether they added it 10 minutes, 90 minutes, or four hours after stimulation.

This suggested that the connections needed an ongoing supply of nitric oxide to keep showing their added strength.

Adding nitric oxide alone did not produce the same lasting effect. A brief dose of a chemical that releases the gas strengthened connections for only a few minutes.

Keeping the tissue in a solution that released the gas caused a large but temporary increase in signal strength.

Prolonged exposure also appeared to damage the nerve fibers.

The team proposes that strong nerve activity starts a loop: the enzyme makes nitric oxide, and nitric oxide keeps the enzyme active.

Hochner said the work shows how different brains can solve a shared problem.

“It is a remarkable example of how evolution can give rise to similar principles of learning and memory through very different biological mechanisms,” Hochner added.

In well-studied examples from other animals, including the sea slug Aplysia, lasting changes in nerve connections depend on new proteins.

Blocking protein production in the mammalian hippocampus prevents the later, lasting phase of strengthening.

The team treated octopus tissue with anisomycin, a drug that blocks protein production, for two and a half hours.

Treatment began half an hour before stimulation, yet the connections stayed stronger for at least 10 hours.

To check that the drug worked, researchers used a glowing marker to label new proteins. Untreated tissue glowed brightly, while treated tissue showed very little glow.

In four lengthy experiments, blocking nitric oxide still reduced connection strength in tissue treated with anisomycin. The nitric oxide system could therefore keep working without new proteins.

When researchers reviewed 11 blocker experiments, they found two patterns. In five, the drug removed an average of 97% of the added strength, acting more than four times faster than in the other six.

After the drug washed away, another round of stimulation made these fast-responding connections about 2.6 times stronger.

It looked as though the earlier strengthening had been erased, allowing it to build again.

In the slower group, the blocker reduced strength by 72%, and further stimulation added almost nothing. Here, the drug seemed to suppress the earlier strengthening without erasing it.

The team thinks the faster response reflected a switch turning off in connections that also started out weaker.

Across the lobe’s five sections, fast responses appeared in the middle, slow ones in the outermost pair, and both in the sections between.

The researchers studied tissue in a dish, not octopuses learning a task.

Most comparisons involved only five or six brain slices per group, and the authors doubt this switch alone explains lasting memories.

Earlier work found that removing the vertical lobe after training did not stop octopuses from remembering learned tasks. The lobe may help animals learn before they store memories elsewhere, where new proteins could still matter.

Cuttlefish may use a different system: their vertical lobe shows no detectable activity of the gas-making enzyme, and connections strengthen on the output side of the circuit.

The team also does not yet know whether nitric oxide keeps its own enzyme active directly or through another enzyme.

Genetic studies show that octopuses and their relatives underwent major changes in how their DNA is arranged.

The authors suggest these changes may have helped turn older mollusk memory chemistry into the nitric oxide switch.

They now want to identify the specific molecular changes involved. That could help explain how this system developed and what role it plays in learning.

The study is published in The Journal of Neuroscience.
