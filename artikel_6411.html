In a 1996 study, researchers reviewed 22,793 poinsettia cases reported to U.S. poison centers and found no deaths, but the plant can still upset pets

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Poison Control says the deadly reputation rests on a child's death over 100 years ago. Tests of about 500 to 600 leaves found no poisonous dose.

Every holiday season, poinsettias go up on tables and windowsills, and so does an old warning: keep them away from children, because they are deadly.

Researchers led by the Pittsburgh Poison Center checked that idea against hard numbers. In a study published in 1996 in the American Journal of Emergency Medicine, they analyzed 849,575 plant exposures reported to the American Association of Poison Control Centers (HRSA).

Poinsettias accounted for 22,793 of those cases. There were no deaths among them.

The researchers wrote that the poinsettia “is a much-maligned plant which is thought by the public and some health professionals to be extremely toxic.”

Their data told a different story. Almost all of the poinsettia exposures, 98.9%, were accidental, and 93.3% involved children.

Most of the people involved, 96.1%, were not treated in a health care facility. And 92.4% did not develop any toxicity related to the poinsettia at all.

“Most patients do not require any type of therapy and can be treated without referral to a health care facility,” the authors concluded.

The study is 30 years old, but the current advice from Poison Control, run by the National Capital Poison Center, says the same thing.

“The poinsettia plant is often considered deadly. That’s wrong,” its guidance states. “Poinsettia can be irritating but it is not fatal if eaten.”

Poison Control says some stories “just won’t go away.” “Over a hundred years ago, a child was found next to a wild poinsettia plant. He died and people blamed the plant. This hasn’t happened since,” it says.

Researchers have even tried to find a poisonous dose. They “just couldn’t, even after reaching experimental doses of about 1.25 pounds (500-600 leaves),” according to Poison Control.

That does not mean the plant is a snack. If children or pets eat it, Poison Control says, they can develop mouth irritation and stomach upset, such as nausea, vomiting or diarrhea.

The sap can cause a skin rash, and Poison Control notes reports of gardeners who work with the plant frequently developing one.

Poison Control describes one real case. On Christmas Day, a 10-month-old put a small piece of a poinsettia leaf in his mouth, and his mother called a poison center.

The specialist told her no problems were expected and advised her to wipe the child’s mouth and give him something to drink.

The plant can also be a choking hazard, so Poison Control recommends placing poinsettias, and other houseplants, where children and pets cannot reach them.

The ASPCA lists the poinsettia as toxic to dogs, cats and horses, and it names the plant’s irritant sap as the toxic principle.

Under clinical signs, the ASPCA describes the plant as “irritating to the mouth and stomach, sometimes causing vomiting, but generally over-rated in toxicity.”

Poinsettias are only one of the season’s concerns for pet owners. Earth.com has looked at how to keep cats safe at Christmas, from toxic trees to dangling decorations, and at festive plants that may be less safe than they look.

The poinsettia is the most popular flowering plant sold in the United States, with more than 70 million sold each year, according to a Clemson Cooperative Extension factsheet last revised in 2015.

It is named after Joel R. Poinsett, the first U.S. ambassador to Mexico. He brought the plant to the Bartram Botanical Garden in Philadelphia in 1828, and a Philadelphia nurseryman, Robert Buist, named it after him, the factsheet says.

The wild plants in Mexico grew 12 to 15 feet tall. The red parts that people think of as flowers are actually bracts, a kind of modified leaf. The true flowers are the small yellow clusters in the center.

If someone does swallow part of a poinsettia, Poison Control’s advice is to call a poison center at 1-800-222-1222, which is free, confidential and available 24 hours a day.
