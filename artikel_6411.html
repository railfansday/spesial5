People lost the same amount of weight drinking water or diet beverages in a two year study

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A two-year study of 493 adults showed that people lose about the same amount of weight whether they drink water or diet beverages.

If you are trying to lose weight, choosing diet drinks over water will not make a huge difference. In a two-year study of 493 adults, participants who drank diet beverages and people who drank water showed very similar weight loss results.

All participants followed a weight-management program and drank at least two 11-fluid-ounce (330-milliliter) servings a day of their assigned drink.

Those still enrolled at two years had lost 10.6 pounds (4.8 kilograms) with diet drinks and 8.2 pounds (3.7 kilograms) with water.

The difference between the groups was not statistically significant. Only 220 of the 493 participants completed the trial.

The SWITCH trial was run at the University of Liverpool in England. Participants lived within 50 miles (80 kilometers) of Merseyside and began their trial drinks between July 2016 and December 2021.

The trial enrolled adults aged 18 to 65 with a body mass index of 27 to 35. They were randomly assigned to water (246 people) or diet drinks (247 people).

Their average age was about 45, and roughly seven in ten were women. The investigators supplied the drinks, which the sponsor paid for.

The diet group chose from 20 branded beverages containing the artificial sweeteners aspartame, acesulfame potassium or sucralose. The water group drank bottled spring or mineral water and was asked to avoid diet drinks.

For 12 weeks, participants attended weekly group sessions led by nutritionists. Monthly sessions followed for 40 weeks, and the final 52 weeks were unassisted.

Advice covered healthy eating and cutting sugar. During the first year, participants were also asked to add ten minutes of daily walking until they reached 60 minutes on six days a week.

The main analysis covered the 220 people with weight data at both the start and week 104. Of those, 117 drank water and 103 drank diet beverages.

Two other analyses that accounted for dropouts also found no significant difference. One used multiple imputation, and the other carried forward each person’s last recorded weight.

After the first 12 weeks, weight loss was equivalent with either drink. Water drinkers had lost 12.3 pounds (5.6 kilograms) and diet-drink users 12.8 pounds (5.8 kilograms), a 2023 report on the trial found.

At one year, diet-drink users had lost significantly more weight in the main analysis, though the gap was not clinically meaningful. By two years, the gap was no longer significant.

Professor Jo Harrold, dean of psychology at the University of Liverpool, led the trial.

“These latest findings show that concerns about sweeteners disrupting appetite or causing weight gain are not supported when tested in a rigorous, long-term randomised trial,” Harrold explained.

Waist and hip measurements fell in both groups, with no difference between them.

Total cholesterol rose more with diet drinks. Blood sugar, measured as HbA1c, and diastolic blood pressure fell more with water, but none of these differences was judged clinically meaningful.

Hunger rose slightly over two years, whichever drink people had. The increase was significant only in the diet-drink group, and the difference between groups was not significant.

Sugar intake fell with either drink, slightly more with diet beverages. Total sweetener intake fell significantly only among water drinkers.

Professor Jason Halford, a co-author at the University of Leeds and the University of Liverpool, pointed to the length of the trial.

“This study provides the long-term clinical evidence that has been missing from international discussions,” Halford said.

In 2023, the World Health Organization advised against using non-sugar sweeteners (NSS) to control body weight. The recommendation was conditional and does not apply to people who already have diabetes.

The guideline covers sweeteners such as aspartame, sucralose, saccharin, stevia and acesulfame potassium.

It drew on a systematic review that found no long-term benefit for reducing body fat. Possible long-term harms in adults included higher risks of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and death.

“Replacing free sugars with NSS does not help with weight control in the long term,” said Francesco Branca, WHO’s director for nutrition and food safety.

The SWITCH authors argue that the guidance rests mainly on uncertain findings from cohort studies rather than randomized trials.

“We hope this evidence informs future WHO guidance and public health policy,” Harrold noted.

The authors partly blame the low completion rate, about 45 percent, on the COVID-19 pandemic, the two-year length and the voluntary final year. That final year was added by amendment after recruitment fell short.

The trial took place at a single site in England, and data on race and ethnicity were not collected.

About 76 percent of participants already drank diet beverages regularly before joining. The trial compared diet drinks with water, not with sugary drinks.

The American Beverage Association funded the research. The team states that it was independent of the funder and fully controlled the analysis and reporting.

The funder could review the manuscript, though final decisions on content rested with the authors. Several authors also report funding from or ties to beverage or food industry organizations.

The study was published in the journal British Journal of Nutrition.
