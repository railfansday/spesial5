The three main types of telescopes—refractor, reflector, and compound—each have their pros and cons. According to NASA, refractors use lenses and are generally easy to maintain, and are great for lunar and planetary viewing as well as stargazing. Reflectors, which use mirrors, offer more aperture (more on that below) for the price, but you require realignment of the telescope's reflective components on occasion in order to get the best focus. Refractors also tend to have a longer focal length.

Compound, or catadioptric, telescopes combine lenses and mirrors for a compact option, suitable for both planetary and deep-sky observing. For beginners, simplicity is key. A small, grab-and-go telescope might be better than a bulky model that’s difficult to transport or set up. Most of the models we looked at are refractors.

Aperture—the diameter of the telescope’s main lens or mirror—determines how much light the scope can gather and, by extension, how much detail of stars and planets you’ll see. Bigger isn’t always better, though. A large aperture typically means a telescope is heavier and more expensive.

Focal length affects how much visual magnification a telescope offers, but more isn't always better, and too much magnification can reduce image quality. For general observation and capturing wide fields of view, telescopes with shorter focal lengths—typically between 400mm and 700mm—are ideal. If you're aiming to, for example, examine planets in really close detail, a longer focal length of 1,000mm or more is usually better (but this is probably overkill for beginners).

Many beginner telescopes come with a basic tripod or mount. In terms of other features, look for included accessories like eyepieces, which control magnification, and finderscopes, which help locate objects in the night sky. Some assembly is usually required, but most entry-level scopes are relatively straightforward. Keep optics clean with a soft brush or air blower—never wipe lenses with your fingers or rough cloths.

Ideal viewing conditions include dark, clear skies and low humidity. Avoid observing right after sunset when the atmosphere is still warming or from areas with excessive light pollution. Use star charts or mobile apps to help locate constellations, planets, and deep-sky objects. With a bit of preparation and patience, you’ll soon be exploring the cosmos with confidence.
