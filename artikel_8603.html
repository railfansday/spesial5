Do the Cheap Energy Upgrades First – They Pay Back in Years, Not Decades

Put your first energy-saving dollars into cheap fixes such as weatherstripping, LED bulbs, a programmable thermostat, and low-flow showerheads. They pay for themselves in about one to three years, while new windows can take decades. Check utility and state rebates before you buy anything.

Rank each upgrade by its payback, the years its savings take to cover its cost. Cheap fixes rank first because they cost little, even when a big project saves more.

Energy Trust of Oregon, an efficiency nonprofit, puts the payback on cheap fixes at one to three years and on efficient new windows and solar panels at more than 10.

A 10-window vinyl replacement averaged $22,073 in the 2025 Cost vs. Value report, and Energy Star says certified windows cut energy bills by up to 13% when they replace single-pane ones. Even on a $3,000 yearly energy bill, a 13% cut is $390 a year, so the job takes more than 55 years to pay back.

Judge bigger projects the same way, using the price after rebates, and only if you’ll own the home longer than the payback. Energy Trust puts attic insulation at four to five years and a high-efficiency furnace, AC, or water heater at six to 10. Energy Star figures a certified heat pump water heater pays back its extra cost in about 2.7 years for a family of four replacing an electric tank. Federal tax credits for efficiency upgrades and solar ended for work done after 2025, but many utilities and some states still pay rebates. Energy Star’s Rebate Finder lists offers by ZIP code.

A project that adds to your home, rather than replacing something, can raise its assessed value. You can appeal a higher assessment yourself or through Ownwell, which charges only when your tax bill comes down.

Do the sealing and insulating first, since Energy Star says better insulation may let you choose a smaller, less expensive heat pump.
