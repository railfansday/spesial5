Facing Fears in Huntington's Disease

Every now and then, I get an automated message in my inbox telling me it’s been a while since I’ve tended to my blog:

Dear Pamela Mallette LCPC, It's been 90 days or longer since you last posted to Living With Huntington's Disease on Psychology Today. We miss your voice in the mix.

Too bad, I think. It’s not that I’m trying to be rude, and it’s not that I have nothing to say (ask anyone). It’s just that I look forward to writing about my bad brain about as much as I look forward to sticking my head in a blender.

Don’t get me wrong: I enjoy the writing process. It’s the subject matter that irks me. Being gene-positive for Huntington’s Disease (HD) means I am facing down one of the cruelest diseases known to humankind, and writing about it can be a real downer. There’s a great weight off when I finish a post, and I feel free to live my life without thinking about it again.

But sooner than I’d like, the email comes along, and with it the invitation to return to the matter of my doom. It forces me out of hibernation and into the spasmodic dance between Avoidance and Confrontation: the Death Tango, as I like to call it—fear and courage, in their epic entanglement within my psyche.

Avoidance, in its protective wisdom, tells me to ignore the emails and let the blog go dark. It’s not worth it. It’s too heavy, and you have enough going on. Spend your time on something—anything—else. Not bad advice. After all, there’s no real pressure, no real consequence, if I don’t write.

It feels like safety to avoid. Unfortunately, ignoring it doesn’t get me very far. I can delete an email or neglect a blog, but I can’t erase my DNA. Nor can I ignore the inheritability of this disease (50 percent), which means my children are at risk. Avoidance would leave all of us unprepared for realities to come, and I’m unwilling to do that.

Plus, as a therapist, I know that facing my reality is probably good for me, or whatever.

So I let Confrontation take the lead, if only because there is no other choice. I follow that valorous fool into dangerous territory, where the different faces of HD lurk around dark corners, each triggering their own primal fears within me.

The most common face of HD is chorea, the involuntary, dance-like motor symptoms that take away a person’s physical autonomy. But it goes further. In later stages, HD breaks you down until you are wheelchair-bound, bedridden, and unable to feed, dress, or bathe yourself.

The loss of physical independence is devastating, but that’s not the worst part for me. The world can be a very unkind place for people with physical challenges, and that’s where the fear sets in. Being on the receiving end of ridicule or humiliation when I am defenseless and vulnerable is literally my worst nightmare.

Then there’s the emotional and psychological decline. Because of damage to the caudate nucleus and frontal lobes, behaviors and emotions start to change as the disease progresses. Once caring people become apathetic. Relaxed people become obsessive and demanding. The gentle become irritable and enraged.

What does this mean for me? Am I not going to be funny anymore? Will I be rude to people for no reason? What if I turn into someone I can’t stand? There’s no telling who I will become as the disease progresses, and my soul shudders.

And of course, there’s the death part. It’s not the dying itself that scares me; it’s that HD, that Biological Robber Baron, claims entire decades as it disrupts the natural lifespan. It most commonly manifests during midlife, such that you become incapacitated in your prime adult living years, and often don’t see your golden years at all.

These are significant periods of time that I have been looking forward to. But if my trajectory is typical, I’m not going to have the future I want. Instead, only the stark sorrow that my best days may be already behind me.

So how do I do this? How do I face the darkness without being overcome by it?

Recently someone close to me told me about a memorial service he’d attended. Though he didn’t know the deceased personally, by all accounts the man being mourned lived an incredible life that touched everyone he encountered for the better.

In memorializing her father, his daughter told of a note she found after his death, something he’d scribbled down and posted on a mirror. It had just three words: “purpose without pressure.”

How simple, and how deep, was this man’s wisdom. He didn’t know it, but with those words he was providing my problem with a solution: balance.

Because just as there is fear, there is meaning—purpose—as well. There’s therapeutic value to voicing the fears, because I don’t often speak them aloud (tends to bring down the mood at parties). And of course, there’s the hope that this writing might encourage someone in the HD community, or that it might help those outside the community to have more understanding and empathy.

Perhaps my greatest purpose is this: If HD is going to take my voice from me, then I will use it as long as possible. Where it destroys, I create. I can try to bring some beauty to this journey, even when all I can say is that I hate it and I’m scared.

So yes, I choose to lean into purpose. But because it’s so grave, I heed the advice of the dead. I give myself permission to honor the enormity of the task by taking my time, coming to it only gently, when I am ready. I write from flow, not force, and I release expectation of the outcome.

Thank you, Peter Vlasses. May you rest in your way, and I in mine.
