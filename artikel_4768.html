Why We Can’t Go It Alone

Updated November 9, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

If a formula for achieving happiness existed, most of us would be following it to the letter. It would also be a global money-making business, marketed in every country. You could buy it online, and perhaps it would even be taught in schools and universities. But happiness doesn’t have a blueprint. It can’t be ordered with free two-day shipping.

So, what is the origin of happiness? Perhaps it comes from finding meaning and purpose in our short existence? Or pursuing a fulfilling career? Embracing spirituality? Is it a result of having love in our lives? Or some other source?

These days, it seems that most happiness-boosting approaches point towards building up your individuality and focusing on you. For decades, we have all been marinating in the self-help ethos that prescribes the inward shift to the self as the only way to foster happiness. Social media, especially, drives that pop psychology viewpoint.

But experts tell us that the biggest influence on happiness (not necessarily the exact cause of it) is being around people and getting along with them. It’s also in the value of our family ties, our intimate partnerships, and our friendships. Therefore, if you are planning to outlive everyone that you know, your social circle needs to consist of more than just your houseplants and a cat. You may need to mix it up a little and find a few human souls.

The 2023 book, The Good Life: Lessons from the World's Longest Scientific Study of Happiness, underlines how significantly our well-being is linked to our human relationships. For example, one of the many studies found that “the people who were the most satisfied in their relationships at age 50 were the healthiest (mentally and physically) at age 80." In other words, healthy relationships and strong bonds help us not only to prosper, but live longer too.

Our ability to adapt to fellow humans and learn how to deal with all the ups and downs that relationships cause is key to contentment. When we engage with other people as our imperfect selves, and we feel safe and accepted, it refutes the illusion that happiness lies only within the self. This speaks to what so many of us consider one of life's decisive objectives: finding someone who embraces us for who we genuinely are. If we are lucky enough to find that, it makes life feel pretty good.

Also, we all know the exceptional joy of connecting with like-minded souls—those who mirror our experiences and share our passions. Sharing moments and activities with others not only reduces loneliness and breeds a sense of universality, but it also makes us feel secure. Having people who can bear witness to our milestones, achievements, and even our sorrows is invaluable.

So even if you like being alone, as humans, we are and have always been social creatures that need each other for survival. We didn’t evolve for thousands of years in a vacuum. Plus, research also shows that in the long run, engaging with others and hopefully finding people to love gives our lives the most significance. It also gives us our identity by reflecting back to us our own humanity.

Psychoanalyst Stephen A. Mitchell, from his book Relational Concepts in Psychoanalysis: Integration, said, “We live in a relational matrix. The person is comprehensible only within this tapestry of relationships, past or present.” In other words, the quality of our relationships clarifies us. Without them, we are inadequate, searching in the dark for a likeness we can only find in our fellow humans. Perhaps when we find the likeness, we find the joy.

Hence, stop looking for the undisclosed blueprint and discover why the ultimate happy formula may not be a solo project—it might be the people right next to you.

But for those of you who are less inclined to place a premium on close and familial connections, here are a few other important factors to happiness that cannot be denied or ignored. Acknowledging and practicing these sustainable aspects of living could additionally help make you more content on a regular basis.

For example, having gratitude for what you have. Secondly, connecting to your own interpretation of God or a higher power. And thirdly, having a sense of purpose and goals.

Look at happiness as a series of actions to exercise every day, not a destination to reach or something you can add to your shopping cart. Remember, humans are not built for separateness; you will be better off sharing some parts of your life with others. Therefore, don’t look at it as an “all or nothing” thing. Find the nuance, the gray area where you can connect and spend time with others without compromising your “alone” time. Isolation is the new smoking.
