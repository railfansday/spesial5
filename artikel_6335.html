The world grows more than enough food for everyone on Earth, yet hunger and food shortages increase each year

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Economists argue that food shortages now come from war and climate shocks, not empty fields.

We do not have a problem growing food. In fact, global farms produce more calories per person every year, even as the human population grows.

The real problem is getting that food to the people who need it. Extreme hunger and local food shortages are currently expected to worsen in at least 13 countries.

Elizabeth Robinson, an environmental economist at the London School of Economics, argues that the planet isn’t short of food. Conflict is the main reason for those limited supplies, she wrote, and climate change is increasingly part of it.

Robinson directs the Grantham Research Institute on Climate Change and the Environment at LSE. She made the case in an opinion article published in the BMJ‘s climate issue. She drew on U.N. hunger reports and climate assessments. with no new data of her own.

In an interview with Earth.com, she said the trouble starts with where the food is. “There is still plenty of food, but often it is in the wrong place, and takes too long to reach people in need,” Robinson said.

The U.N.’s Food and Agriculture Organization and World Food Program identify 13 hotspots where they expect acute food shortages to increase.

Armed conflict and violence affect twelve of them, Robinson wrote, and four face a risk of famine: Sudan, South Sudan, the Gaza Strip, and Somalia.

Beyond the hotspots, around 60 to 70 percent of the world’s most undernourished people live in areas affected by conflict. By one estimate she cited, 85 percent of those facing hunger live in conflict-affected countries.

Fighting cuts off food at every stage. Farmland is abandoned or becomes harder to farm, and people are driven from their homes. Access to markets is restricted, and prices climb past what families can pay.

Where conflict leads to food shortages, more children grow up stunted, Robinson wrote. The U.N. Security Council formally recognized the link between hunger and conflict in a 2018 resolution, and climate change is making that link harder to break.

Hotter years and more extreme weather, including heatwaves and swings in rainfall, cost farmers their crops as well as shifting the distribution of pests and plant diseases, Robinson wrote. Food prices also become less predictable.

An estimated 123.7 million more people experienced moderate or severe food shortages in 2023 because of extra heatwave days and drought months, compared with the 1981 to 2010 average. Robinson took that figure from the 2025 Lancet Countdown report on health and climate change.

Each force makes the other worse. Reports show that food shortages trigger violence and political unrest, and conflict, in turn, deepens hunger.

Researchers broadly agree that climate change is a threat multiplier, Robinson wrote, and studies show that climate shocks worsen hunger in places that are already at war.

Asked by Earth.com what has changed since she wrote the piece, Robinson said the timing is what worries her most.

“I am really concerned that just as a particularly strong El Niño is peaking, and the number of people facing severe hunger is increasing, funding for food assistance is falling,” Robinson said.

That El Niño is expected to strengthen, she said, which will be another climate shock for places where food security is already stressed.

The FAO and WFP have added northeast Nigeria and Somalia to the highest-concern group in their Hunger Hotspots outlook, she said. Haiti has now moved to the very high concern category.

In her view, conflict, climate shocks, El Niño and high input costs are all happening at the same time. The effects will build, she said, because planting and fertilizer supplies are being disrupted.

Robinson isn’t not convinced that the international community is taking the situation seriously enough.

Ukraine has historically supplied a significant share of the world’s wheat, corn, barley and sunflower oil, and Russia’s invasion disrupted each of those markets, Robinson wrote.

In addition, the U.S.-Iran war has disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, which is affecting energy and fertilizer prices. That matters for next season’s harvests in countries that depend on inputs from the region.

Supply is also concentrated. Just five breadbasket countries supply around four fifths of the corn traded internationally. Three crops, rice, wheat and corn, provide over 40 percent of the world’s food calories.

Governments can make shocks worse. After Russia invaded Ukraine, reports indicated that some countries hoarded grain while others imposed export restrictions, just as they had during the 2008 food crisis. Both moves amplify shocks, Robinson wrote.

Robinson’s prescription starts with emissions. Agriculture and the food system account for almost a third of global emissions, she wrote, and cutting them while feeding a growing population won’t be simple.

Shifting toward more plant-based diets in higher-income countries and cutting food loss and waste would lower emissions and leave more food available.

Countries that rely less on trade and more on storage and domestic production can dampen price spikes, she wrote. Cooperation on regional storage may be more achievable than global coordination.

Farmers need climate insurance, drought-resistant seeds and smart irrigation. The most vulnerable households need targeted safety nets, in cash or food, when prices rise.

In the short term, Robinson told Earth.com, keeping food moving through war zones may matter most. “So right now, protecting access routes could well do the most good, but this is very much a short term measure, and not easy,” she said.

“Climate change, conflict, and the resulting increase in hunger that is being experienced around the world, come from choices our governments are making,” she said.

That is her judgment after decades of work on food insecurity, and the BMJ article is a commissioned opinion piece, not a peer-reviewed study.

The open question is the size of the climate share. Researchers have not agreed on how much of conflict-linked hunger is driven by climate change. The 123.7 million figure counts heat and drought alone, not the wars they may feed.

In the short term, support that arrives before a crisis peaks, protecting local harvests or sending cash and food to affected households, can reduce hunger, Robinson wrote.

The lasting fix, however, is bigger: in the medium to long term, “systemic changes are needed across the full agri-food supply chain, from what we grow, and how, through to what we eat.”

“But we need to see far more commitment from governments,” she said. The food exists. Whether it reaches the people who need it before the next shock is up to the governments from whom she is asking for more.

The full article was published in the journal BMJ.
