These ants grow enormous heads, but their antennae break a rule of biology

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Soldier ants grow heads twice as wide as a worker's, yet their antennae span exactly the same sizes, and colony signals help set them.

A bigger animal comes with bigger parts. A big dog has a big head, big paws, and a big tail, and the same goes for nearly every creature biologists have measured.

In one of the most diverse groups of ants on Earth, the soldiers don’t follow that pattern.

Their antennae come in the same sizes as the antennae of the much smaller workers, and the colony’s own chemical signals help shape how big each antenna grows.

A graduate student spotted it by accident under a microscope. Erica Vong, a researcher in Rajendhran Rajakumar’s lab at the University of Ottawa, showed him the antennae of two ants, and the smaller antenna belonged to the soldier.

“Much like when you are getting your favorite latte, both castes have small, medium, and large sizes of overlapping antennae,” said Rajakumar, an associate professor in the university’s Department of Biology.

The ants belong to Pheidole, the big-headed ants. Each colony has two kinds of worker: small minors and much larger soldiers whose heads are about twice as wide.

Soldiers defend the nest and mill seeds, and workers do most of the rest.

Rajakumar’s team measured 100 workers and 100 soldiers of one Florida species, Pheidole dentata. For body size they used the width of the thorax just behind the head.

For the antenna they used its first segment, the long stalk that gives an ant’s antenna its elbow shape.

Soldiers had wider bodies and far wider heads, as expected. Their antenna stalks, though, were the same length on average as the workers’, and the whole antenna followed the same pattern.

The two castes’ measurements overlapped so completely that the team couldn’t tell them apart.

Within each caste the old rule still held. A bigger worker had a bigger antenna than a smaller worker, and the same was true among soldiers.

Pooled together, the numbers showed no link between antenna and body at all, and the trend only reappeared once the castes were split apart.

Queens were the exception, with larger bodies and larger antennae than either worker caste.

To see whether one species was a fluke, the team pulled published measurements for 565 Pheidole species from the Americas.

Across the 565 species, soldiers and workers often had antenna stalks of similar length, even though soldiers consistently had wider heads.

For each species, Rajakumar’s team divided the soldier’s antenna stalk by the worker’s. The most common answer was one, meaning no difference at all, with some species a little above and some a little below.

The same calculation for head width most often came out at 2 to 1, and no species came in under 1.4 to 1.

The team then measured ants from 14 other genera in three subfamilies, using live colonies, specimens from the Cornell University Insect Collection, and photographs from an online ant database.

In species with no soldier caste, and in most that evolved one on their own, antennae grew with the body as usual.

The one exception was a turtle ant, Cephalotes varians, a close relative of Pheidole. The authors suggest the decoupling first appeared in the common ancestor the two groups share.

Whether a Pheidole larva becomes a worker or a soldier depends on how well it eats. A well-fed larva ends up with more juvenile hormone, and a high enough level turns it into a soldier.

Adult soldiers give off a pheromone that pushes the other way, so a colony with plenty of soldiers makes fewer new ones.

The team tested both signals on larvae from lab colonies collected in Gainesville, Florida, and kept at 77°F (25°C) on a diet of mealworms and crickets.

They dabbed some larvae with a synthetic version of the hormone and raised each one with either two adult workers or two adult soldiers, starting with 40 larvae per condition.

The larvae were about a twentieth of an inch (1 to 1.5 millimeters) long, young enough that either fate was still open.

Hormone-treated larvae raised by workers became soldiers with a normal spread of antenna sizes.

Larvae raised by soldiers without the hormone became workers smaller than any found in a natural colony, with antennae to match. The team calls them nanoworkers.

“Social signals and hormones do more than determine whether an ant becomes a worker or a soldier,” Rajakumar said.

“They can also reshape how different traits grow and scale together (or decouple), within castes, between castes, and more broadly across species.”

Larvae that had already passed the point of no return toward becoming soldiers gave the stranger result.

Raised by soldiers, they emerged with soldier-sized heads on worker-sized bodies, and with antenna stalks shorter than those of the smallest natural workers. The team calls these nanosoldiers.

A developing soldier briefly grows a stub of a wing that never becomes a wing, and that stub acts as a signaling center that helps set the soldier’s big head.

When the team switched off a gene that drives that stub, the larvae grew into smaller ants with smaller heads. Their antennae came out at nearly one size, with almost none of the usual spread.

The team has not found a nanoworker or a nanosoldier in the wild. The authors suspect selection weeds them out.

A soldier’s head on a worker’s body may not be properly supported, and an antenna that small may carry too few sensory hairs to do its job.

The authors also allow that the hidden potential could be a side effect of selection on something else, and the question of which is open.

An ant’s antennae are its main contact with the world. They pick up trail and alarm pheromones, touch, heat, humidity, and vibration, and ants use them to tell nestmates from strangers.

The elbow stalk matters too, because an ant swings it to aim the sensitive tip at whatever it is exploring. The stalk’s length sets where that elbow sits, which affects how an ant probes and follows a trail.

A longer antenna carries more sensory hairs, and earlier work found that how an ant responds to an intruder depends on how many of those hairs it has.

So a colony whose members span a range of antenna sizes may span a range of sensitivities as well.

Rajakumar’s team offers one explanation for why the two castes share a range. When a Pheidole colony runs short of workers, its soldiers take on worker jobs, including caring for young.

Soldiers with the same spread of antenna sizes as workers may make that switch more easily. Once the next generation restores the balance, they can go back to defense.

“The antenna is not simply getting bigger because the ant is bigger,” Rajakumar said.

“In these colonies, development preserves a flexible range of antennal forms that may help individuals compatibly respond together to changing environmental demands.”

The authors wrote that the adaptive role of the decoupling is still unclear, and the job-switching idea is a hypothesis they haven’t tested.

Their hormone and pheromone experiments used one species, Pheidole dentata.

Roughly half the treated larvae died before reaching adulthood, which the authors attribute to raising each larva with only two adults instead of a full colony.

Antenna length was measured, not the sensory hairs on it, so whether a soldier’s small antenna senses what a worker’s does is still an open question.

The evolutionary story also rests on a single turtle ant species. And 59 Pheidole species were left out of the genus-wide count because one caste or the other had no published measurement.

Checking the relatives is the next step the authors name.

If antennae turn out to be decoupled across the turtle ants and a third genus, Procryptocerus, the pattern would date back to the ancestor of all three.

Until someone measures those ants, the origin stays a hypothesis.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
