RiverPark Long/Short Opportunity Fund Q3 2026 Commentary

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

The RiverPark Long/Short Opportunity Fund (RLSIX) (the âFundâ) returned 3.93% in the third quarter of 2026, compared to a gain of 2.30% for the S&P 500 Total Return Index.

The modest headline gains belied a challenging macro backdrop. Inflation remained stubbornly elevated. After holding rates steady in July, the Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate by 25 basis points in September, its first increase in three years, and signaled that further hikes may follow. Energy prices added to the pressure as renewed military strikes on energy infrastructure in the Middle East and Russia and constrained transit through the Strait of Hormuz continued to tighten global supply. By the end of the quarter, long-term Treasury yields had climbed to their highest levels in roughly two decades.

Beneath the surface, growth stocks had a more difficult quarter than the headline numbers suggest. The Russell 1000 Growth Indexâs (âRLGâ) 0.92% gain trailed both the S&P 500 and the Russell 1000 Value Index, outpacing only the small-cap Russell 2000, which fell more than 7%. What gains the RLG did produce came, once again, from a small group of mega-cap technology companies, and the index remained concentrated, with semiconductors alone now making up roughly a third of its weight. As a result, the RLGâs quarterly return was driven far more by the swings in a handful of AI-related stocks than by the performance of the typical growth company. Notably, this narrowness came despite a strong fundamental backdrop: S&P 500 earnings grew more than 25% year-over-year in the second quarter, and analysts raised their third quarter estimates as the period progressed.

Within growth stocks, the quarter was defined by the marketâs evolving opinion of artificial intelligence, but the question investors were asking changed. To start the year, the debate centered on whether AI would disrupt incumbent businesses. This quarter, the focus turned to whether the extraordinary sums being spent to build AI infrastructure are backed by real demand. That debate came to a head in late July, when the major hyperscalers reported meaningfully higher capital expenditure budgets, and the market drew sharp distinctions. Microsoft (MSFT), which paired higher spending with clear evidence that cloud demand was absorbing its new capacity, posted its best single-day gain in nearly 20 years. Alphabet (GOOGL), by contrast, sold off sharply after raising its 2026 capex guidance to as much as $205 billion and reporting negative free cash flow, even as Google Cloud revenue growth accelerated to 82%. The same skepticism swept through semiconductors in July, as investors took profits in a group that had run sharply higher in the first half of the year. TSMC (TSM) and Applied Materials (AMAT) both delivered record results during the quarter yet were sold on what we would describe as âbeat-and-worryâ reactions focused on rising capital intensity and China exposure rather than on any deterioration in demand.

The turning point came in late August, when NVIDIA (NVDA) reported revenue that more than doubled year-over-year and guided to approximately 70% revenue growth in fiscal 2028, nearly double what analysts had modeled, answering the question of whether AI infrastructure spending still has room to run. Semiconductors recovered in September, and the AI trade broadened. In our view, however, the most important development of the quarter came from Meta, whose personal AI agent, Muse, reached the number one spot on Apple (AAPL)'s U.S. App Store following its September launch. Muse is the most tangible proof yet that the enormous investment being made in AI can produce consumer products that people want to use, and its inclusion of Shopify (SHOP)'s Shop Pay as a payment option illustrated how AI can serve as a tailwind for the platforms it touches.

These developments reinforce the view we laid out in past letters: for the companies best positioned to capture it, AI is driving new business rather than destroying existing revenue, and the market is increasingly rewarding capital spending that is tied to visible demand. At the same time, the July semiconductor selloff, in which several of our holdings fell sharply despite record results, is a reminder of how quickly sentiment can shift in a group that has had extremely strong performance over the last few years, a risk we flagged in our first quarter 2026 letter.

Overall, our longs contributed 4.64% and our shorts detracted 0.24%. The short book generated gains from our hedges in residential real estate and travel and leisure, areas that came under pressure as interest rates and energy prices rose during the quarter. These gains were offset by losses in our enterprise software shorts, which rallied alongside the broader software sector as fears of AI disruption eased. That change of sentiment benefited software holdings on our long side, such as ServiceNow (NOW), but it also lifted several of the lower-growth software businesses we are short.

In the short book, we continue to focus on businesses that we believe are losing competitive market share, that have business models we believe are flawed or are facing cyclical headwinds (including unprofitable technology, subscale internet media, residential real estate, cyclical industrial and consumer lending). In addition, we use select ETFs from time to time to manage exposure.

We started the quarter 96.86% long, 21.57% short, and 75.29% net. We ended the quarter with less short exposure and more net exposure at 98.45% long, 17.47% short, and 80.98% net.

Microsoft Corporation: MSFT was the portfolio's largest contributor for the quarter, rising 37.8% after a difficult June. The pivotal event was its July 29 fiscal fourth quarter report, which paired better-than-expected results with higher capital expenditure guidance and clear evidence that demand was absorbing the new capacity. Investors' takeaway was that, unlike some peers, Microsoft had successfully linked its AI spending to accelerating cloud demand. Shares continued to advance in August alongside the broader AI recovery that followed NVIDIA's results.

We continue to view Microsoft's Azure and Copilot franchises as key beneficiaries of enterprise AI adoption.

Meta Platforms: META rose 28.8% for the quarter, but the path was uneven. Shares fell sharply after its late-July report, when a weak forecast and continued uncertainty about the monetization of its AI spending left investors unsatisfied. The picture reversed in September, when the launch of Muse, its personal AI agent, drew rapid consumer adoption and reached the number one spot on Apple's U.S. App Store; the stock gained 26.8% for the month, its strongest since 2013, and reached a new 52-week high after the company's Connect conference. Meta also agreed to pay up to $18 billion to resolve a major lawsuit over social media and child welfare, removing a long-standing legal overhang.

Meta's core advertising business remained strong, and Muse provides the clearest evidence to date that its elevated AI investment can produce new consumer products and, over time, new revenue streams.

Apple: AAPL rose 15.2% for the quarter to a record high. The stock held up well during the July AI-capex debate given its comparatively capital-light approach, and its fiscal third quarter results, reported July 30, showed revenue of $109.4 billion, up 16% year-over-year, and diluted EPS of $2.02, up 29%, although an industry-wide memory shortage weighed on the outlook. The iPhone 18 cycle provided a further lift in September: at its September 9 event, the first under new CEO John Ternus, Apple introduced the iPhone 18 Pro and Pro Max, priced $100 higher than the prior generation, and its first foldable device, the iPhone Duo, priced at $1,999 and scheduled for October.

We continue to view Appleâs large installed base, pricing power, and Services franchise as a durable source of earnings, and the product cycle is an additional catalyst, although rising memory costs remain a headwind to gross margin.

Applied Materials: AMAT was the portfolioâs largest detractor for the quarter, declining 29.2% after reaching an all-time high on June 30. The stock fell sharply in July as investors took profits following a run of more than 100% year to date and sold semiconductor names broadly. The companyâs August 13 fiscal third quarter report delivered record revenue of $9.12 billion, up 25%, and record non-GAAP EPS, and guided fourth quarter revenue to approximately $10.25 billion, with advanced packaging growth now expected to exceed 70% for the year, yet shares fell again as investors focused on China falling to roughly 28% of sales from about 35% a year earlier. The stock recovered 11.6% in September as semiconductors rallied.

We continue to view Applied Materials as uniquely positioned across the logic, memory, and packaging equipment markets to benefit from rising AI-driven capital intensity, and we see the decline as a valuation reset rather than a change in the demand outlook.

RingCentral: RNG, a short position, was the second-largest detractor for the quarter as the stock rose 92%. The rally began with the company's July 23 second quarter report, in which revenue of $657 million grew 5.9% year-over-year, free cash flow rose 25% to $180 million, and management raised its full-year outlook for revenue, margins, and free cash flow while increasing the quarterly dividend by approximately 67%. Customers using at least one of its paid AI products grew to approximately 13% of annual recurring revenue, double the prior-year level, and the stock jumped more than 25% the following day. Shares continued to climb through the remainder of the quarter as the broader software sector rebounded on easing AI-disruption fears, with elevated short interest likely amplifying the move.

While we acknowledge the company's improving profitability and early AI traction, RingCentral remains a mid-single-digit grower in a mature business communications market, and we believe the stock's sharp re-rating has outpaced the improvement in its fundamentals.

DocuSign: DOCU, a short position, was the third-largest detractor for the quarter as the stock rose 51%. Shares rallied alongside the broader software sector, which rebounded sharply during the quarter as investor fears of AI disruption eased, and received a further lift from the company's September 3 fiscal second quarter report. Revenue grew 9% to $875.7 million, ahead of consensus, non-GAAP EPS of $1.16 beat estimates, free cash flow reached $295.8 million, and management raised its full-year outlook, including annual recurring revenue growth guidance of 8.5% to 9.0%. Its Intelligent Agreement Management platform grew to approximately $529 million of annual recurring revenue, or roughly 15% of the total.

While DocuSign is executing well on its transition to Intelligent Agreement Management, we believe its core e-signature business is mature and increasingly commoditized, and we expect growth to slow as a result.
