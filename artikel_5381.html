14 key facts about the DEFIANCE Act and Paris Hilton's push for it

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

14 facts on the DEFIANCE Act, from the right to sue and damages to Paris Hilton's push and the House vote that has not happened.

Sexual deepfakes have stopped being an occasional celebrity scandal and become something people produce in bulk. The number of nonconsensual deepfake porn videos online has grown ninefold since 2019, according to one researcher's count reported by the Senate Judiciary Committee. The volume has since moved well past the figures in the policy debate. At least 1.8 million explicit images were shared through the Grok chatbot in just nine days, according to a New York Times analysis reported by ABC News. For a victim, the problem is no longer a single image to report and remove but an unknown number of copies spread across the internet. Few people defend the practice. In a poll published in 2024, 84% of respondents told the Artificial Intelligence Policy Institute that deepfake porn should be illegal, according to TIME. The harm itself is not in dispute.

That broad agreement has not given victims a federal way to go after the person behind an image themselves. The argument in Washington is over which tool fits the harm. Prosecutors could bring criminal charges, platforms could be forced to take images down, or victims could file lawsuits that they control. The DEFIANCE Act would supply the third option, and even it would have a hard edge. The bill would apply in the United States only, according to ABC News. A creator working from another country would fall outside its remedy. Because of that limit, the real test of each provision is what it can do for a victim and what it cannot.

Congress.gov set out to outline what the DEFIANCE Act does and why Paris Hilton backs it, weighing the bill's mechanics alongside its most visible champion, from suing under a pseudonym to the time victims would have to bring a case and the end of the "it's only a fake" defense. Every one of those protections is conditional. The bill has passed the Senate, but the House has not voted, so none of these rights exists yet for anyone harmed by a sexual deepfake. Two questions remain for each provision: whether it fills a gap that existing law leaves open, and whether a lawsuit would work against creators who are anonymous or hard to find.

The DEFIANCE Act would let the person shown in a sexual deepfake decide whether a case goes forward. Under the bill, someone depicted in such an image could file a civil suit in federal court against whoever made or spread it, according to Congress.gov. Rep. Joe Morelle's separate federal proposal, the Preventing Deepfakes of Intimate Images Act, was a criminal bill, according to TIME. Under a criminal law, prosecutors decide which cases to bring. Under DEFIANCE, a victim would not have to wait for her case to become a prosecutor's priority before someone answers for the harm. The bill would also cover more people than the one who made the image. A person who shares or publishes a deepfake made by someone else could be sued, and passing an image along would carry its own legal risk. Anyone who solicits a deepfake could be sued as well, which reaches the person who commissions an image before it exists. A threat to distribute one would be grounds for a suit on its own. A victim facing blackmail could go to court before the image ever spreads.

A federal claim would give victims the same standing in every state. In January 2024, only ten states had criminal laws against nonconsensual deepfakes, and Morelle's criminal bill had seen no action since its introduction, according to TIME. A civil suit in federal court would not depend on where a victim lives or on what her state legislature has passed. The bill's backers present it as a right that belongs to the victim, not as a crackdown run by the government. "Innocent people have a right to defend their reputations and hold perpetrators accountable in court," Sen. Josh Hawley, one of four lawmakers who introduced the bill, said.

A lawsuit can make a person pay, but it cannot by itself remove an image from the internet. A victim could win her case and still find the picture online. Keeping deepfakes off social media will take a commitment of resources from the companies that run those sites, and legislation alone will not do it, according to comments by Nina Brown, a Syracuse University professor of media law and technology, reported by TIME. The bill would give a victim leverage over the person behind an image, but not over the sites that host it.

That leaves an open question: whether a victim can identify and reach the person she needs to sue.

A victim who wins a DEFIANCE Act case could leave court with more than a check, because a judge could also order the images taken offline and make the defendant cover her legal costs, according to CNBC. Those two remedies address a victim's most practical needs: getting the image down and not paying for the fight herself. The bill would also let a victim seek punitive damages, an award that goes beyond repaying what she lost. Those damages penalize the person who made or spread the image. For the bill's best-known advocate, that penalty is the point. "People need to know that there's going to be consequences for their actions," said Paris Hilton, chief advocacy officer at Loti AI. Backers want the money to deter the next creator as well as to compensate the person depicted.

The bill also puts a dollar figure on a win. Damages under the DEFIANCE Act would start at $150,000, according to Bloomberg Law figures reported by Congress.gov. A victim and her lawyer would have that figure in hand when deciding whether a case is worth the time and the exposure of going to court. Taken together, the remedies would make a deepfake expensive for whoever made or spread it, from the removal order through the punitive award.

The bill pairs a way to get the image offline with a real cost for the person behind it.

Under the DEFIANCE Act, a victim of a sexual deepfake would have 10 years to sue, according to Congress.gov and the University of Cincinnati Law Review. That period would not begin on the day someone first posted the image. Under the bill, it would start on whichever comes later: the day the victim discovers the image or the day she turns 18. A deepfake that appears long before its subject learns of it would still be grounds for a lawsuit when she finally finds it.

The two starting points protect two groups of victims. A person depicted as a child would not lose any of her time to sue while she was still a minor. Her period would begin at adulthood, so she could decide for herself, as an adult, whether to go to court. A person who finds an image years after it surfaced would keep the full period from the moment she learns of it. That matters for a harm that spreads through copies a victim may never see until a friend, an employer or a search result turns one up. Under a deadline tied to the first posting, those victims could lose their right to sue before they knew they had a case.

The 10 years would also give a victim room to find a lawyer, gather evidence and weigh whether to go forward. She would not have to make that choice in the first weeks after a painful discovery.

A victim who learns of a deepfake late would not, under this bill, find that she had also learned of it too late.

The DEFIANCE Act cleared the Senate without a single dissent, and the House has yet to vote on it. The Senate passed the bill unanimously on Jan. 13, 2026, according to Congress.gov, with no senator willing to stand against it. The bill has stalled in the House since then. "For 10 months, this critical legislation has sat untouched in the House, just waiting to be put on the floor," Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, a Democrat from New York, said, as reported by Parade. Until the House acts, none of the new protections in the bill is available to victims.

Congress has stalled on this bill before, and earlier versions of it never got through the House. The Senate passed one of those versions unanimously in 2024, according to Parade, and senators passed the bill again in 2026. Each new round of Senate support shows the same agreement again without bringing the bill any closer to law.

The holdup does not run along party lines. The bill has bipartisan support, and its House cosponsors include Rep. Laurel Lee, a Republican from Florida, alongside Ocasio-Cortez, according to Parade. Advocates have therefore turned from winning votes to winning a place on the House schedule. The bill does not need more supporters. It needs House leaders to bring it to the floor.

Until they do, the Senate's unanimous support remains a promise to deepfake victims rather than a right they can use.

Paris Hilton argues for the DEFIANCE Act as a victim first and a spokesperson second. An intimate video of Hilton leaked in 2003, when she was 19, according to Congress.gov. Hilton says that the law offered her no protection then and that the harm had no name. A lawsuit she could file on her own behalf is exactly what she lacked at 19, and it is what this bill would create.

The 2003 leak explains how Hilton came to the cause, but the harm behind her advocacy is happening now and keeps growing. Over 100,000 explicit AI deepfake images of her exist, according to Hilton's own count reported by ABC News, and no one has checked that figure independently. Each new image brings the harm back. Hilton said that "each time a new one appears, that horrible feeling returns, that fear that someone somewhere is looking at it right now and thinking it's real." Her injury comes from what a stranger might believe about each image as well as from the image itself, so it returns with every new copy. That makes her a victim whose case is still open, not a survivor recalling an old scandal.

Hilton has pressed the issue in Washington before. ABC News reports that in 2022 she took her case to officials at the White House under President Joe Biden. She also holds the title of chief advocacy officer at Loti AI. Her return to Washington years after that meeting makes her case harder for lawmakers to dismiss as a publicity stop.

For Hilton, the bill is about the next new image as much as the video that leaked in 2003.

For Paris Hilton, the bill's best-known champion, sexual deepfakes are a flood of images rather than a single fake to report and remove. Loti AI has removed more than 60,000 deepfakes of her, according to Hilton's figures reported by Congress.gov. Removals on that scale are far beyond what any one person could manage alone. No one has said how many remain online, so even a cleanup effort that large has no clear finish line. The removal count deserves some caution, because Hilton is chief advocacy officer at Loti AI and has a stake in how the company's work is seen. Takedowns deal with the images, not with the people who make them. A right to sue is meant to reach those people.

Sexual deepfakes also target women who hold public power, including women in Congress. Research by Hilton's team, as she described it, counted 83 women with sexual deepfakes of themselves online among the 156 women serving in the 119th Congress. No independent count has confirmed that finding. If it holds, a majority of the women in Congress have been targeted by the same kind of image the bill addresses. The harm would then reach inside the institution weighing the bill, not only celebrities and private citizens.

The bill's backers are not asking Congress to imagine a harm that, by Hilton's count, has already reached many of its own members.

A victim of a sexual deepfake can already see an abuser charged with a crime and can make a platform remove the image. She has no federal right to take the creator to court herself. Victims have lived with that gap since the TAKE IT DOWN Act became law in May 2025, according to the NYU Annual Survey of American Law. That law makes publishing intimate depictions, including sexual deepfakes, a crime, but it gives victims no civil cause of action. Prosecutors and platforms hold the controls under the existing law. The DEFIANCE Act would give victims a civil suit of their own, according to Congress.gov. That makes the bill a companion to TAKE IT DOWN rather than a rival, and the case for it rests on what the first law leaves undone.

A takedown moves quickly once a platform learns of an image, but the clock does not start before then. Under TAKE IT DOWN, platforms face a 48-hour takedown requirement that begins when they are notified, the NYU Annual Survey of American Law reports, and someone has to find a copy and report it first. Each removal erases one copy and costs the person who made it nothing. Under the DEFIANCE Act, the defendants would be the creators and distributors of an image. Sites that host the images keep broad protection for what their users post under Section 230, at 47 U.S.C. § 230, so the bill aims at the people behind an image rather than the sites that carry it.

A right to sue gives victims an option, not a fix for every case. Many victims want the image gone and nothing more, and many would rather stay away from police and courtrooms altogether, according to comments by Carrie Goldberg, an attorney and image-based abuse expert at the firm C.A. Goldberg, reported by the NYU Annual Survey of American Law. Victims are best served by having both laws. A takedown serves the victim who wants the image gone, and a suit would serve the victim who wants its maker held to account.

Federal law already offers removal, and the DEFIANCE Act would let a victim go after the person who made the image.

If the DEFIANCE Act becomes law, the Senate's version is the likeliest text to carry it, because it has already passed one chamber. The Senate passed S. 1837 on Jan. 13, 2026, and the House has yet to act on it, according to Congress.gov. Its sponsor is Sen. Dick Durbin, D-Ill., who serves as Senate Democratic Whip and Judiciary Committee Ranking Member, according to the Senate Judiciary Committee. A whip's job is to count votes, and Durbin used that position to move the bill through the Senate. He also had a partner in Republican Sen. Lindsey Graham as co-lead, so senators took up the bill as a measure from both parties rather than one.

The Senate's unanimous vote means that no senator tried to stop the bill, which is a narrower kind of support than the word "unanimous" suggests. The Senate Judiciary Committee reports that no senator objected to its passage. That result shows that no senator was willing to oppose the bill openly. It does not show how hard any senator would work to see it through. For a victim waiting on the right to sue, the clean Senate result signals goodwill but guarantees nothing.

Durbin treated the Senate win as half the job. "Now, let's pass this in the House and make it the law of the land," Durbin said, as reported by the Senate Judiciary Committee. The senator who carried the bill through his own chamber knows that the vote there does nothing for victims until the House votes too.

That leaves the bill's fate with the House, the chamber that has not yet voted.

Victims cannot use the rights in the DEFIANCE Act unless the House passes a bill of its own, and House members have already introduced one. The House version of the DEFIANCE Act carries the number H.R. 3562, according to Congress.gov, which gives readers a specific bill to follow. As a companion bill, it asks House members to take up the same protections the Senate bill contains rather than write a new approach to sexual deepfakes. Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, D-N.Y., is one of its two leaders. Rep. Laurel Lee, R-Fla., leads it with her, so a lawmaker from each party has put her name at the top of the House bill.

That pairing means neither party in the House can treat a deepfake victim's right to sue as the other side's cause. A lead sponsor from each party still sets no date for a floor vote, though. Lawmakers can introduce a bill and stand behind it for months without the House ever taking it up. Backing for H.R. 3562 is already in place, and the decision to bring it to a vote has not been made. Until the House makes that decision, the federal right to sue over a sexual deepfake that the DEFIANCE Act would create remains out of reach for victims.

For anyone waiting on that right, H.R. 3562 is now the bill to watch.

The DEFIANCE Act would not add a new law to the federal books. Congress would instead rewrite one that already exists. Under the bill, the changes would go into 15 U.S.C. 6851, an existing section of the U.S. Code, and would extend it to cover what the bill calls "intimate digital forgeries," according to Congress.gov. With that language, the statute would name sexual deepfakes outright. A victim would then bring her claim under a section of federal law that already deals with intimate images rather than under a statute written from nothing.

The statute Congress would amend is recent. Congress enacted it as Section 1309 of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2022, a large spending package, and the DEFIANCE Act amends that section rather than replacing it with a separate law. Congress is being asked to broaden a remedy it has already enacted, not to design one from the start. That shapes the decision in front of the House. Members would not be debating whether federal law should address intimate-image abuse at all, because the 2022 law already exists. They would be deciding whether that remedy should name forged images explicitly. For victims, the change would place a deepfake claim inside a section of law that has been on the books since 2022, not in an untested new statute.

Until the House passes the change, the 2022 statute stays as written, without the words "intimate digital forgeries."

A sexual deepfake usually damages a victim's reputation, safety and peace of mind, and that kind of harm rarely shows up on a pay stub or a medical bill. Under the DEFIANCE Act, a victim would not have to prove any financial loss to seek damages of $150,000, according to Congress.gov. The harm would count even when it cost her nothing she could measure. A woman who kept her job and lost no income but still has to live with the image would have a claim worth bringing. If damages depended on proven losses, she would have had little to claim.

That sum works as a limit on the standard award, and the difference matters to anyone weighing a lawsuit. The University of Cincinnati Law Review describes $150,000 as the cap for standard liquidated damages, so a court could award that much but not more in an ordinary case. A victim should treat the figure as the most the basic award offers, not as a set check she can expect to collect.

A court could award more when the image is part of a wider campaign against a victim. When a deepfake is connected to stalking, harassment or a sexual assault, the cap rises to $250,000, according to the University of Cincinnati Law Review. The higher limit depends on danger to the victim, not on how far the image spread. The victims facing the most physical risk could recover the most.

Any award is a judgment against a person, and many creators of sexual deepfakes post from anonymous accounts that are hard to trace. Kennedy Aikey, an associate member of the University of Cincinnati Law Review, argues that the bill needs added tools, such as provenance metadata and identity checks on high-risk AI tools, to put the remedy within reach. "Without such measures, the civil remedies promised by the DEFIANCE Act may remain largely symbolic, offering recognition of harm without a realistic path to justice," Aikey wrote. A victim would no longer have to prove loss, but she would still have to find the defendant.

Whether she can name the person to sue matters more than the size of the award, and these dollar limits do nothing to answer that question.

A single fixed award cannot fit every case of deepfake abuse, and the DEFIANCE Act would let victims seek other kinds of compensation as well. A winning victim could recover actual losses and the defendant's profits and could ask a court to order the images removed, according to Congress.gov. Under that remedy, a person who makes these images to sell could be ordered to hand over what the sales earned and would be left with nothing to show for the work. The removal order addresses the rest of the harm. A payment alone would leave the image online, where people could keep finding it and the injury to the victim would continue.

Courts could also punish a defendant on top of repaying the victim. Punitive damages are among the remedies, which means the law would treat a sexual deepfake as wrongdoing to deter rather than as a debt to settle. The cost of the case would not stay with the victim, either. A right to sue means little to someone who would spend more on the lawsuit than a win would return. A victim who prevailed could make the defendant cover her lawyer's bill and the expenses of bringing the case, so her own savings would not pay for holding someone to account.

Instead of one sum for everyone, a court could match the award to what a particular victim lost and what a particular defendant gained.

Under the DEFIANCE Act, calling a sexual deepfake fake would not protect the person who made it. The easiest defense open to a maker is to admit the image isn't real and argue that nobody is harmed when nobody is fooled. The bill rejects that argument: a disclaimer saying the image is fake would not make it lawful, according to Congress.gov. A maker who stamped a label on the picture would face the same lawsuit as one who passed it off as genuine. The injury, in practice, becomes the sexual depiction of a real person without consent, and whether a viewer believes the image is real would not decide the case.

For a victim, the rule removes a fight before it starts. She would not have to prove that anyone mistook the image for a real photograph, and that proof would be hard to supply when the image is openly labeled as fake. The rule also takes away an easy workaround for anyone who would otherwise caption an image "parody" or "AI-generated" and keep sharing it. Under the bill, that caption would offer no legal cover. A victim's case would turn on what the image shows and on whether she agreed to it, not on how a stranger might read it.

A maker's honesty about the forgery would earn no protection under the bill, and only the victim's consent would.

The protection covers only the person who brings the suit. It keeps the plaintiff's identity private, and it says nothing about the identity of the person who made or shared the image. A victim who files under a pseudonym would still have to name a defendant, and many creators of sexual deepfakes post through anonymous accounts. The pseudonym provision gives a victim no help in learning who that person is.

Under the bill, a victim could keep her own name out of the case, but the person who harmed her could stay unnamed as well.
