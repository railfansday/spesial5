Tourists Brave a Muddy Jungle Trek to See a Famed World War II Wreck

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Seven Chinese tourists, a dozen villagers and a couple of journalists forded streams and bushwhacked to the downed plane of Isoroku Yamamoto, the Japanese admiral. Machetes proved handy.

Reporting from Bougainville, Papua New Guinea

Halfway through the morning, I’m being tossed around like a popcorn kernel in the back of a troop carrier playing an eclectic medley of Backstreet Boys and Papua New Guinean jams.

We’re traveling along what feels more like a collection of loose rocks, arranged to be roughly vehicle width, than a road. I’ve lost count of the number of river crossings, including one where the water lapped a few inches from the car’s windows. And this is during a dry spell.

Our caravan is headed for an unlikely tourist destination deep in the jungle on Bougainville Island, an autonomous region of Papua New Guinea in the Solomon Sea.

The piecemeal remnants of a Mitsubishi G4M1 — Betty, as the planes were code-named by the Allies in World War II — have remained for some 80 years at the crash site of Japanese Navy Adm. Isoroku Yamamoto. This is the spot where Admiral Yamamoto, mastermind of the Pearl Harbor attack, archvillain to Americans in the war, a respected and memorialized figure to the Japanese, met his end in 1943.

Bougainville was occupied by the Japanese in early 1942, in the months after Pearl Harbor, as the Imperial Navy pushed into the South Pacific, with Admiral Yamamoto at the helm. In April 1943, Allied forces intercepted and deciphered Japanese communication on the admiral’s travels, and American P-38 Lightning fighters gunned down his plane over southern Bougainville, a feat the Americans triumphantly dubbed Operation Vengeance.
