Stop Playing the End of the Scene

Posted November 10, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

While I was promoting my book, Play Your Way Sane, someone interviewing me said they admired my career trajectory. I had a goal in mind and worked toward it, single-mindedly.

I was taken aback. I may have even laughed because they couldn’t have been more wrong.

After college, I moved to New York City to become an actor. I hit the pavement, auditioned ruthlessly, and landed some film, TV, and theater jobs.

But then I hit my late twenties, and I definitely couldn't pay all my bills on my acting money. I went back to grad school to earn my Ph.D. and become a professor. But the professor jobs didn't pan out.

To pay the bills, I got a job as a teaching artist. One of my first placements was as the full-time English teacher in a public school in the Bronx, and I really enjoyed the work.

I went back to school again. I earned an MS.Ed. and teacher certification and became a full-time middle and high school teacher.

During that time, I had also been working on a book. Eventually, an editor at Simon & Schuster liked my manuscript, and they published my book, Play Your Way Sane. There was no master plan. The goals change and evolve. It’s a moving target. I get out of bed, put everything into whatever it is I’m pursuing, then wake up the next day and take the next logical step.

My career thus far only makes sense in retrospect and because I’ve approached jobs with the improvisational spirit of working with what’s in front of me and taking each logical next step. In Mary Lemmer’s new book, How to Handle Anything, she breaks down four improv concepts we can apply to live a better life—more in-the-moment, next-logical-step kind of life.

Instead of getting stuck on an end goal (in improv, pre-scripting the scene), saying Yes, And means you accept your situation as it develops and then roll with it.

It doesn’t mean liking your current reality. It means acknowledging your reality as it is, then figuring out how to take that next logical step.

Improvisers are conservationists; they use every morsel that’s handed to them to create unscripted scenes. In real life, this means making the most of what you get, instead of focusing on the opportunities you don’t have.

Playing the scene you’re in means accepting reality as it is, instead of complaining about it or living as if you’re in a different reality.

Improvisers aren’t afraid to make bold choices. Sometimes it pays off with a big laugh. Other times, it falls flat. But with big risks come big rewards, and at the very least, we learn from our mistakes.

Stop thinking about your life course as being linear. Life has twists, turns, and a tremendous amount of uncertainty. Improv helps us embrace and accept this uncertainty and focus on the here and now. We don’t have to know how the scene ends. We have to take one step, then another, then the next.
