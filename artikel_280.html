A Journey Through the Absurd Hell of Trump’s Third-Country Deportations

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

The warden kept telling Pheap Rom not to think of himself as a prisoner. Instead, the jailer insisted, Rom and his fellow deportees from the United States were “the king’s guests.”

Here was the warden’s idea of royal hospitality: Each morning at 7:30, Rom—a chiseled, compact, 43-year-old Cambodian man with a pronounced Philly accent—was roused from a bare cell he shared with three other men in Eswatini, a tiny kingdom in Southern Africa. Then he was made to stand at attention as the country’s national anthem, “Nkulunkulu Mnikati Wetibusiso temaSwati,” reverberated through the halls of the Matsapha Correctional Centre. Most meals consisted of a portion of rice splashed with thin broth. It was late 2025, and the weather was warm and wet. For 15 minutes a day, Rom and the other men were allowed to go outside. Before they were locked up for lights out, the anthem played again; thick clouds of mosquitoes kept Rom thrashing all night.

Sometimes Rom would banter with the towering, bespectacled warden, who went by the name Kuenene. If I’m a guest, then why am I being held in a prison with criminals? Rom would ask. Why aren’t I allowed to leave? But Rom knew these exchanges with the warden were absurd, like everything else about his situation.

Before the second Trump administration, the practice of deporting immigrants to countries where they have no ties was a rare and tightly restricted move in US immigration enforcement—reserved mainly for cases where a person simply could not be returned to their nation of origin due to risk of persecution or torture. It has since expanded into a signature White House policy of mass removal. Over the past two years, the administration has inked deals with some 35 nations to accept third-country deportees from the US. The kingdom of Eswatini, which agreed to take up to 160 migrants for a fee of $5.1 million, is one of them. Some of the other destination countries—the Central African Republic, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, South Sudan—are so violent and unstable that they rank on the State Department’s “Do Not Travel” list.

Often making a spectacle of its own transgressiveness, the administration has steamrolled past public outcry, an investigation by Democratic lawmakers, and a warning from the United Nations about a “cascade of human rights violations” resulting from these removals. At least twice in the past two years, US federal courts have ordered the Department of Homeland Security to stop shipping migrants to third countries with insufficient due process. Both times, the Supreme Court has quickly stepped in to let the administration resume.

In September, during one of those brief, court-ordered pauses in removal flights, DHS general counsel James Percival went on X to publish the most comprehensive figures yet on how many migrants have been affected by the practice. He posted a GIF of Taylor Swift mouthing the words “That’s my husband” and pointing excitedly at her wedding ring. Percival’s caption: “How my wife looks at me when I tell her we have deported 25,000 illegal aliens to third nations.”

Rather than slowing down in the face of resistance, in fact, President Trump’s third-country push appears to have inspired imitators. Five European Union countries announced in November that they are working toward a plan to send rejected asylum seekers to a deportation hub in ​Rwanda. In the past two years, Italy has begun sending asylum seekers to Albania. The United Kingdom quietly sent an asylum seeker to Eswatini earlier this year; the man, originally from Sri Lanka, died last month, the government of Eswatini reported. According to local media, the apparent cause was suicide.

Rom’s lawyer, Tin Thanh Nguyen—who is himself the child of refugees from Vietnam—predicts that, if the administration keeps getting away with these expulsions, additional wealthy countries will be emboldened to offload their undesirables to poorer nations for a price. “This is going to be the new model for the West, not only for the United States,” says Nguyen, who also represents three other men currently held in Eswatini and Rwanda. “It’s just kind of out of sight, out of mind for these Western countries.”

But as Rom’s experience reveals, some of these removals may rest on little more than bureaucratic fiction. The US claims it has followed due process with every one of the 25,000 migrants deported to unfamiliar lands. “Their own countries say, ‘We do not want them back,’” US attorney general Todd Blanche told Fox News in early October. Yet in Rom’s case—among countless other Kafkaesque details—his own country never even appears to have been asked.

An American in virtually everything but citizenship status, Rom came to the United States as a 3-year-old refugee from Cambodia. Around 1979, his parents fled their home to escape the horrors of the Khmer Rouge, a genocidal regime that seized power in the wake of Richard Nixon’s carpet-bombing of the country. From there, the family went to a Thai refugee camp—where Rom was born in 1982—and then to a resettlement center in the Philippines. As Rom’s father was selling off possessions to raise money for their ultimate move to America, he was killed by roadside thieves. Rom sat by his side after the attack, his mother later told him, trying to wake his father up.

Rom’s first memories of America are of a cramped, dimly lit bedroom in a small apartment in the Bronx, where he arrived with his mother and six siblings in 1985. “There was just one mattress, and we all slept on that,” he says. “It was a new world to us; everything was new.”

Rom and his family had arrived in the middle of the largest resettlement effort in US history. As America’s chimerical war efforts in Southeast Asia faltered in the 1970s, hundreds of thousands of people began arriving in the US from Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia. Of the nearly 158,000 Cambodians who gained entry between 1975 and 1994, the majority, like Rom and his family, were refugees deeply marked by the atrocities of the Khmer Rouge.

Around 10,000 of them came to be resettled in areas of the Bronx that were blighted by high crime and poverty. Many were placed in dilapidated housing, with limited job opportunities. Rom’s mother, who didn’t speak English, relied on government support checks and whatever extra income she could make sewing and scavenging. As a child, Rom would help her collect cans and carry the overstuffed bags to the grocery store to return for cash.

With the help of an uncle, the family eventually relocated to Philadelphia, where an adolescent Rom was enticed by a newly formed gang—a Cambodian clique within the Crips called the Blue Dragons. “If you don’t want to get bullied,” older members of the gang told him, “you need to link up with us.” Rom was barely a teenager. An older gang member dubbed him Menace, after Dennis the Menace, because he was “funny and bad at the same time.”

By the time he was 18, Rom had done three stints in juvenile detention and was caught in ever-escalating street beefs. At a New Year’s Eve party in 2008, rival gang members jumped him. Rom waited for a day off from his job—caring for abandoned dogs at a local rescue—to seek revenge. He parked outside the gang’s hangout, and when his targets exited, he opened fire from his car. Rom contemplated running when he heard that the police were looking for him but decided against it largely because he had a young daughter. In jail he would be able to see her, even if it was from behind glass. On the lam, he’d have to disappear. He pleaded guilty in April 2010 to multiple charges, including attempted murder.

Rom was a legal permanent resident of the US, a status he had been granted as a refugee. Though refugees are entitled to obtain full American citizenship, Rom had been told he didn’t stand a chance due to his juvenile record, an obstacle that would greatly alter the trajectory of his life.

Under US immigration law since the 1990s, legal permanent residents who commit crimes that qualify as “aggravated felonies”—an expansive and, critics say, somewhat arbitrary legal category—can lose their immigration status and become subject to deportation after they’ve served out their sentences. In a case of what immigrant advocates call “double punishment,” Rom’s conviction for attempted murder meant he would lose his green card. And he knew that in the post-9/11 era, his home country had bowed to US pressure to accept the repatriation of deported Cambodian refugees.

Rom likes to say that prison saved his life. He spent much of his time in lockup reading, exercising, and making cents an hour working in the kitchen to save up for small gifts for his daughter. As he served out his sentence, a generation of shifts in America’s immigration policy flew by—from President Obama’s support of the Dreamers and record-high deportation numbers to Trump’s policy of family separation to Biden’s border crisis to Trump’s return to the White House. But in Rom’s mind, none of that made a difference for him: His fate was already sealed. He was fully prepared to be deported to Cambodia, a country he had never visited, once his sentence was finished.

Rom’s reserved mood as his prison term neared its end confused fellow inmates. “Yo, why are you not happy?” they asked. “You’re about to be released, you made parole.” On November 13, 2024, Rom walked out after serving 15 years. He was immediately transferred to Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) holding, where what he believed would be a straightforward process turned into a bewildering journey through the American deportation machine.

From detention, Rom did what he could to hasten the process of his own removal. He frequently called the Cambodian embassy in Washington, DC, seeking information on his departure process. He was told that his questions should be directed to the Department of Homeland Security. Officers in Pennsylvania, he says, had no updates. They told Rom they were swamped by the number of cases they were trying to handle. At one point, they told him he would be deported to Thailand, his country of birth, which he told them was incorrect. After six months of detention, Rom filed a writ of habeas corpus arguing that he was being unlawfully detained.

He continued to wait for another three months. On September 24, 2025, his daughter’s birthday, Rom saw on a tablet device that his commissary account had been zeroed out. Among ICE detainees, this is a telltale sign that deportation is imminent. The following day, Rom was transferred to Pine Prairie ICE Processing Center in Louisiana. He was moved again a few days later to Alexandria, Louisiana, where the airport hums with obscure charter airlines that ferry deportees abroad. He waited there for another 72 hours before he started, again, asking officers what was happening.

At first, they said they didn’t know, a claim Rom found hard to believe. When an officer checked his case on the computer system and then quickly clammed up, Rom’s suspicions grew. Finally they told him to pack up, and Rom caught a look at the immigration paperwork. It listed Eswatini as the country of deportation. Confused, Rom turned to another detainee: “Hey, bro, is Eswatini another ICE facility in Louisiana?” he asked. The man responded that it was in southern Africa.

Rom ran to the phone and made a panicked call to his sister in Las Vegas. She searched online and confirmed that Eswatini was indeed in Africa. Rom asked her to call the Cambodian Embassy. Another detainee’s lawyer suggested the group request “reasonable fear” interviews: US Citizenship and Immigration Services conducts these interviews through their asylum officers to ascertain if a person has a reasonable fear of persecution or torture. Immigration agents seemed amused by the suggestion, Rom says. What could the men be afraid of? they asked. “Y’all a bunch of murderers,” Rom recalls them taunting.

Rom pleaded with the guards. Eventually, one officer told him and the others that if they agreed to be shackled, he would take down their request in writing. They stepped into the restraints, and the officer set about filling out some paperwork. As they sat waiting, their feet and hands in chains, Rom gave the request for a “reasonable fear” interview one last try. “Hey,” he asked, “you think you are going to give us that interview?” He realized it was “all fake” and that the officers never intended to file anything.

One by one, under heavy guard, the men were loaded onto a small airplane. When the door closed it was “like my coffin is closing,” says KV, another deportee on the flight who asked to be identified by his initials because he remains detained. The plane flew from Alexandria to Puerto Rico. The officials accompanying the men gave them no information. They tried, and failed, to disable the small TVs that showed the plane’s flight path.

The plane carried on to Senegal, where they were delayed as officials sought permission to travel onward. “They were basically smuggling us through Africa,” Rom says. When the flight touched down at King Mswati III International Airport early on the morning of October 6, the runway was lined with dozens of armed military personnel. Rom was loaded into a paddy wagon. As the van turned onto a dirt road, Rom was overcome with fear. Oh yeah, they are going to kill us, Rom thought. They’re definitely going to kill us.

Eswatini, a country about the size of New Jersey surrounded almost entirely by South Africa, is the continent’s last remaining absolute monarchy. In April 1973, King Sobhuza II suspended the constitution and banned all political parties. The ban remains in place. His son, King Mswati III, took power in 1986, at just 18 years old. Challenges to the monarchy have been met with violence. In June 2021, security forces cracked down on pro-democracy protests, killing 46 people, including two children, according to a government inquiry. Civil society organizations say this is an undercount. A leading human rights lawyer and pro-democracy activist was assassinated at his home two years later. No one has been held accountable for his death.

Eswatini has the highest HIV prevalence rates in the world. Poverty in the country is pervasive. A 2024 report from the US State Department describes Eswatini as having “significant human rights issues,” including torture, media restrictions, and the worst forms of child labor. The king is reportedly married to a cohort of wives and lives a life divorced from his country’s brutal reality. At an April event to mark his 40 years on the throne, he held an extravagant party at a luxury convention center that included a performance by Grammy-nominated singer Davido.

The men, none of whom had committed any crime in Eswatini, were deposited in a high-security area of the Matsapha Correctional Centre. Rom’s cell sat behind a large metal door with a barred window painted forest green. When the detainees arrived, the cells were dirty. They asked for cleaning products but were told that none were available. Basic hygiene supplies took four months to arrive. In a dirt courtyard outside, the men had access to a row of concrete sinks to hand-wash their clothes during their 15 daily minutes of outdoor time. One detainee was allowed to retrieve the clothes in the evening.

Rom’s 15-year sentence in the US proved to be a useful experience. He fell back into his workout routine from prison, knocking out 500 pushups a day along with burpees and sit-ups. He read books donated to the prison, churning through the Hunger Games and Twilight series. His lawyer sent him a copy of First They Killed My Father, a memoir about surviving the Khmer Rouge. Rom had expected the weather to cool down at night, but there was little reprieve from the heat. He was given heavy blankets, which meant choosing between sweltering or being swarmed by mosquitoes. He asked jailers the same question he had been asking the guards in the US. “How long am I going to be here?” No one had an answer.

Gradually, the men were granted a few freedoms. They were brought to a mobile store so those without phones could buy them using money from their families. On the way back, they were allowed to eat at KFC and buy two beers apiece. These were the first excursions Rom had taken outside of detention facilities in more than 15 years.

In a post on X, a DHS official had said that the men being deported to Eswatini were “depraved monsters,” and those statements had spread in local media. So Rom smiled and waved, saying hello to the people who stopped to gawk.

The men were permitted to make one phone call a week using WhatsApp. The calls were broadcast in a common room; a prison official would sit nearby, scribbling notes. When Rom wanted to discuss something sensitive with his sister, the two would switch to speaking in Khmer. Local lawyers were barred from visiting. Shortly after Rom was deported, his sister saw a post on Facebook that mentioned Tin Thanh Nguyen, the lawyer in North Carolina. She got in touch, and Nguyen agreed to take Rom’s case.

Thanks to a TV in the common room that was set to the news, Rom was well aware of the controversy surrounding his fellow deportees. He watched a report about one fellow Matsapha inmate from Cuba, Roberto Mosquera del Peral, who had arrived earlier and was on a hunger strike. Each morning, when officers checked Mosquera del Peral’s cell, Rom says, they would shout, mockingly asking if he was still alive.

How Rom and the others came to be trapped in Eswatini is still unclear. According to a report from Reuters, the deal was brokered in February 2025 between Caitlin Piper, a diplomat who was leading the US embassy in the capital of Mbabane, and Russell Dlamini, the country’s prime minister, who sought and received approval from the king. Neither Dlamini nor the acting government spokesperson, Thabile Mdluli, responded to requests for comment. In May, a spokesperson for King Mswati told Reuters that “the king embraced the deal as Eswatini’s contribution to world order.”

Piper has since departed Eswatini. She was most recently based at the US Consulate in Erbil, Iraq, according to social media posts from the consulate. The consulate did not respond to requests for comment on her current position.

In November 2025, more than a month after Rom arrived and four months after the first deportees were deposited at the prison, Eswatini’s finance minister confirmed that he had received a $5.1 million payment from the US. He said the money would be funneled toward Eswatini’s National Disaster Management Agency. The country said it would accept 160 deportees as part of the agreement. The State Department eventually posted a copy of the memorandum of understanding signed between the two countries on May 14, 2025, which confirmed these details. The signature of the US official includes a looping C, but no name is printed underneath. Asked for comment, the Eswatini Press Inquiries Team at the embassy directed questions to the Department of State in Washington. The Department of State did not respond to questions regarding who signed the agreement.

This payment disclosure led the men in detention to charge that they had been trafficked. “You are moving people around, and money is exchanged,” says KV. “So what is that?” If Eswatini got all that money, Rom wondered, why was he having to spend his own on basics like a fan for his cell?

The Southern Africa Litigation Centre (SALC), a human rights NGO, joined several other groups in filing a legal challenge against Eswatini in 2025, aiming to declare the deportation agreement unconstitutional, obtain the terms of the agreement, and secure an order stopping further arrivals until the matter was legally resolved. The initial challenge was dismissed. SALC and the other groups filed an appeal to the Supreme Court of Eswatini in March. (Just weeks before her reported meeting with Eswatini’s prime minister to facilitate the deportations, Piper had addressed an SALC workshop, urging participants “to bring injustice to light, to demand the respect of those in power, to advocate for a world in which our most cherished freedoms are secured.” )

Rom’s personal path to Eswatini appears to have come about through a combination of cruelty and bureaucratic ineptitude. Nguyen points out that Cambodia has been accepting deportees for more than two decades and that the country is generally responsive and professional in issuing the requisite travel documents. He says that the Cambodian government moved to begin rectifying the situation within a week of his contacting them in October 2025.

Nguyen believes that the US erroneously thought Rom should be sent to Thailand, the country of his birth, not citizenship, but never bothered to contact government officials from that country or from Cambodia. Chann Rotana, a spokesman for Cambodia’s foreign ministry, says they also do not understand why Rom was sent to Africa. He adds that the US did not contact the country’s embassy in Washington, DC, regarding Rom’s case before he left the country. Rotana described the deportation to Africa as “strange” and said the embassy only became aware of the situation when Rom’s sister got in touch. Asked about Rotana’s comments, the Department of Homeland Security directed questions to the Department of State, which declined to respond.

“There’s no rhyme or reason as to why any of my clients were picked to go to Eswatini versus the clients that I have who are actually in Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia,” Nguyen says. “There’s nothing that makes them distinguishable.”

After an interview with Cambodian officials, Rom’s case for removal progressed while the other men sat hoping for similar developments. When he was cleared to be sent to Cambodia in early February, he requested that he be escorted by a representative from the UN’s International Organization for Migration as a form of insurance. Rom finally arrived in Cambodia on March 26, becoming the second US deportee freed from Eswatini. (The first, a Jamaican man named Orville Isaac Etoria, was deported to Eswatini in July 2025 and repatriated to Jamaica in September of that year.)

When I met Rom in June, at a restaurant in the Cambodian capital of Phnom Penh, he was dressed in all black. The sleeves of his polo shirt were taut around his thick, tattooed arms. We met within walking distance of S-21, a Khmer Rouge torture and interrogation center that is now a museum dedicated to the genocide that set his family on its path to America. He is contemplative about his journey and speaks softly.

After arriving in Cambodia, Rom enrolled in a creative writing class, hoping to detail his experience. He was taking teacher training courses, hoping to land a job at an international school, and picked up work fixing air conditioners when he could.

The jarring transition to life in Cambodia was made harder when his older sister—who had been his lifeline in detention—died in July. “Im trying to hold it together,” he texted me. In August, Rom sent a string of messages saying he’d gotten a job teaching English to a rambunctious group of third graders.

Once a week, Rom calls the deportees who remain in Africa. There are more now. A few weeks after Rom and I met, Eswatini announced that it had received a fourth batch of 11 deportees. Another group followed. Thirty-two have been forced to the country in total. KV remains imprisoned. “With the holidays around the corner,” he texted in late September, “it feels I’m wasting my life sitting here for no reason.” It is unclear when he will be released.

The fate of all third-country deportees stands to be clarified in the coming months. After at least twice granting the Trump administration temporary stays to keep deporting migrants to third countries, the US Supreme Court will start hearing oral arguments on the ultimate legality of the practice in December.

Shortly after he arrived in Cambodia, Rom noticed that Kuenene, his warden at Matsapha, had added him as a contact on Telegram and WhatsApp. Another Eswatini official requested to connect on Facebook and follow Rom on Instagram. Rom accepted some of the invitations and declined others; he couldn’t tell why they were trying to befriend him. Maybe Kuenene wanted to keep tabs on what Rom might say publicly. “He was one of those guys that tell you what you want to hear, smile at your face, make you believe that he is the most supportive guy in the world, and then he will stab you in the back,” Rom said. “I didn’t trust him.” Before he was released, Rom told the guards and other deportees that he might return to Eswatini to visit as a tourist. In reality, he has no intention of ever returning. One visit as a royal guest was enough.
