Everything that led to Jensen Huang being reinstated as Nvidia’s CEO

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Jensen Huang is back as CEO of Nvidia, just 11 days after his closely watched exit from the company he cofounded—even as he remains mired in controversy. 

In an announcement this morning, the Nvidia board claimed to be “satisfied with respect to Mr. Huang’s candor and ability to lead Nvidia,” following a number of meetings with the newly reinstated CEO. The chairman said the board “believes Jensen is the right person to lead Nvidia,” and that his return atop the company is “in the best interests of our stockholders, employees, partners and customers.” 

There was little mention of the circumstances that led to Huang’s departure, aside from an assurance that the board had conferred with senior government officials and are now “confident that Mr. Huang’s reinstatement poses no national security risk.” The announcement noted that an independent investigation is still underway to probe the fallout from Huang’s extramarital affair with an alleged Chinese spy, and that Huang intended to cooperate. 

Huang’s reinstatement is not entirely a surprise (though it was apparently a surprise to interim CEO Colette Kress, who was not consulted on the decision, per a Bloomberg report). By the board’s own admission, the decision seems to have been influenced by President Donald Trump, who has remained a steadfast supporter of Huang throughout the debacle. But Trump was more explicit about his role in a CNBC interview today, revealing that he spoke directly to the company’s board. “And I convinced them,” he said. “I made the deal.” Trump also noted, “We have to protect our geniuses.”

As Fast Company reported earlier today, Huang’s return underscores his influence as a key player in the AI boom (not to mention his cozy relationship with Trump). Since Huang resigned, there has been a steady drumbeat of concern from the tech community, with prominent figures like Elon Musk and former Uber CEO Travis Kalanick arguing that he should be reinstated. 

At the New York Times DealBook summit last week, Musk argued, “There’s no one that can really replace Jensen,” and likened his resignation to “kicking Michael Jordan off your team when you’re on a winning streak.” (Other leaders at the conference also spoke highly of Huang, from Sam Altman to Anduril’s Palmer Luckey.) In a recent appearance, Kalanick drew parallels to his own experience being ousted from Uber and said, “We gotta bring Jensen back.” Y Combinator cofounder Paul Graham boldly asserted, “I’ve never known the tech world to be more unanimous about anything than they are about Jensen Huang.”

Despite allegations of multiple inappropriate relationships with his own employees—including a direct report—apparently plenty of Nvidia staffers also expressed solidarity with Huang. 

A Wired report last week revealed that some employees immediately urged the board to bring Huang back in internal posts. A widely circulated petition to reinstate Huang as CEO drew more than 200,000 signatures, of which more than 10,000 were from current Nvidia employees—about a quarter of the company’s workforce. Musk, Kalanick, and a handful of other CEOs and business leaders signed the petition.

It didn’t take long for Huang to plead his own case. 

“I’ve seen an outpouring of calls from Nvidia’s employees, investors, business partners and customers for me to return as CEO,” he said last week, hours before the company’s first earnings call without him. “If the board of directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company and America, I would be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO.” He also acknowledged that he “did not live up to the standards expected of everyone at Nvidia” and had made “serious mistakes” in his personal life. At the time, the board continued to insist that there was no plan to bring him back.

But now that Huang is at the helm again, is it back to business as usual? There are all kinds of questions about what his return could mean for Nvidia, and not just in terms of the serious national security concerns raised by his indiscretions. 

The board is still conducting an investigation into the events that led to Huang’s exit, but it could be little more than a formality if he has already been reinstated. (Or perhaps the board will find itself in yet another bind when that investigation surfaces new information.) 

While the board now claims to be convinced of Huang’s candor, it was quite the opposite just two weeks ago, per a Wired report, when his “lack of candor” and “lying” apparently pushed the board to remove him. As Fast Company previously reported, boards usually have little tolerance for CEOs pursuing romantic relationships with their subordinates (and the stock price often takes a dip following such revelations). 

Nvidia also has an unusually flat organizational structure, in which Huang has 60 direct reports, an arrangement that seems to have left the company without a succession plan—while making its CEO even more powerful. 

A New York Times investigation last week raised further concerns over Huang’s leadership at Nvidia, which seems to have created a culture of fear at the company. While many employees remain loyal to Huang—and even seem to revere him—others claim to have experienced ritual humiliation under his rule. More than a dozen former and current employees said Huang frequently used insults and personal attacks to motivate his staff. 

According to The Times, Huang could be particularly creative with his insults, telling an executive that his mother should have aborted him, and in another instance threatening to rip off an employee’s head and “pee on your brain.” 

The report found that Huang’s management style had sparked regular HR complaints—which some employees said weren’t adequately addressed—and that a former HR leader had been fired for calling attention to Huang’s conduct, which he characterized as “the most verbally abusive, threatening and intimidating behavior” that he had seen in his career. (The former employee threatened to sue but ultimately reached a settlement with Nvidia for more than $1 million, The Times reported.)

At the moment, it’s not clear how or whether Nvidia plans to address these issues, along with Huang’s other transgressions. His statement today stops short of introspection or insight into what—if anything—could change upon his return. 

“I am honored to return to Nvidia and lead one of the most consequential technology companies in the world at the beginning of a new industrial revolution,” Huang said in his statement. “I take this responsibility with the greatest humility and renewed commitment.”

It remains to be seen whether Huang will approach the job any differently. In the meantime, since news of his return broke this morning, Nvidia stock is up over 30%.
