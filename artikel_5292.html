The AI boom helped lift retirement accounts, but the Nvidia whiplash showed how much investors’ financial futures depend on a handful of stocks

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Millions of Americans, and their financial futures, may be exposed to the recent AI-fueled swings in the market regardless of whether they’ve ever used a chatbot.

Stocks plunged Wednesday morning before recovering much of the loss. Near the start of trading, the Dow fell more than 1,000 points and the Nasdaq dropped more than 4% as AI chip stocks sold off for a second straight day. Nvidia fell as much as 8%. By late afternoon, the S&P 500 had cut its loss to about half a percent, and the Dow had turned higher.

Wednesday’s swing capped a week in which a single company’s board decision moved the index that holds much of Americans’ retirement accounts. Roughly two-thirds of of Fidelity 401(k) savers keep everything in a target-date fund.

Following a tumultuous few weeks in which Jensen Huang resigned and was later reinstated as CEO of Nvidia, the chipmaker’s stock jumped 31%, added about $950 billion in market value, and lifted the S&P 500 by 4.7%. Still, the S&P 500 is now about 8% below its Oct. 6 record. Last Friday it closed 11.6% below that high, putting it in correction territory, after seven straight losses, its longest losing streak since February 2020.

Stock market concentration has been on the rise for years, and it is now near 35-year highs. Just 10 stocks in the S&P 500 account for about 39% of its total market capitalization, with the five largest stocks—all tech giants—alone accounting for 29%, according to Charles Schwab. That means an American with $100,000 in an index fund tied to the S&P 500 would have $29,000 in just five stocks.

As a result, many investors may think they are putting money into a diversified investment when the health of their portfolios may actually be tied more to the fate of a handful of tech giants than they realize, said Martin Mulyadi, a professor of accounting at Shenandoah University’s College of Business, Engineering & Technology.

“An index fund spreads your money across 500 companies, but it does not spread the risk evenly,” he told Fortune.

For the millions of Americans who have poured their money into S&P 500 index funds through their 401(k)s, this concentration has been a boon in recent years. A booming stock market that has risen just over 50% during the past five years helped push the number of 401(k) millionaires at Fidelity to a record 769,000 in June. Even before the selloff began, that number had fallen some 2% between the second and third quarters, but was still up 15% from a year earlier, according to Fidelity.

Through the third quarter, the vast majority of retirement savers were still investing. About 96% of 401(k) participants at Fidelity either maintained or increased their savings rate during the third quarter, the financial services company revealed in a report this week.

“Despite market volatility, many workers continue to increase their savings, stay invested, and take advantage of workplace retirement plan features designed to support long-term success,” Sharon Brovelli, Fidelity’s president of workplace investing, said in a statement.

Yet the growing dominance of a handful of technology giants means even those who have followed conventional retirement wisdom and have added to diversified funds may be exposed to heightened risk.

A further stock slide could hurt the portfolios of millions of Americans and especially for those closer to retirement: The average Fidelity 401(k) millionaire is 58 years old.

For older Americans, a downturn early in retirement could have implications for when they can afford to stop working and how much money they’ll have to live on once they do.

By selling investments at depressed prices to cover living expenses, retirees face the problem of sequence-of-returns risk. If a falling stock market in early retirement causes retirees to sell their investments at lower prices, that means they have less money left in the market that could later benefit from an eventual recovery.

To try to avoid this phenomenon, Charles Schwab recommends maintaining a short-term reserve of low-risk and more liquid investments that retirees could draw from in a downturn to avoid touching their portfolio.

The increasing concentration of the stock market also doesn’t necessarily mean investors should abandon index funds altogether. Investors should instead consider taking a closer look at the makeup of their portfolio, said Sean McEvilly, the managing partner and founder of Vistamark Investments.

“The practical move isn’t to bail out of stocks. It’s to look at the whole 401(k), since target-date and large-cap funds often hold the same names, and make sure there’s real exposure to small and midsized companies, international stocks, and bonds,” McEvilly told Fortune.

Role: Reporter Marco Quiroz-Gutierrez is a reporter for Fortune covering general business news.
