United CEO Scott Kirby says investors overestimate airline credit-card profits, but filings from United, Delta, and American show substantial loyalty-program cash flows without establishing what the bank deals actually earn.

Airlines are often called credit card companies that operate planes. United Airlines CEO Scott Kirby rejects that idea.

In an October 27 interview on the Cheeky Pint podcast, Kirby noted that over 50,000 passengers fly United daily on credit card miles. Outside analysts see the cash banks pay for those miles, he argued, but ignore the cost to carry those passengers. “Nobody counts the cost,” he said. “All that we disclose is the revenue.”

It's a fair challenge to a popular argument. But the public filings tell a more complicated story. During the pandemic, United, Delta and American showed lenders how much cash their loyalty programs could produce. And American has since told investors what a bigger bank deal could add to earnings.

The key question is how much of that cash translates into genuine profit. Skift previously analyzed the payment fees funding these travel rewards. Now, we look at the other side of the trade.

United Airlines CEO Scott Kirby pushed back on the idea that airlines are credit card companies with planes attached, telling the Cheeky Pint podcast on October 27 that more than 50,000 passengers fly United daily on credit card miles and that analysts count the revenue from selling miles while ignoring the cost of carrying those passengers. The public filings support part of his point and complicate the rest. Delta reported $8.2 billion from its American Express deal in 2025, up 11%, and American collected $6.2 billion from credit-card and other partners. United does not publish total cash received from banks; it reported $2.7 billion in loyalty marketing and partner-benefit revenue in the first nine months of 2026, up from $2.4 billion, and ended 2025 with about $7.8 billion in deferred loyalty revenue. United's own June 2020 MileagePlus presentation showed a bank paying $300 for 15,000 miles, with MileagePlus paying the airline unit $150 for the seat and recording a 50% margin — but that $150 was an internal transfer price between units of the same parent, not the cost of flying the passenger. Award travel has grown meaningfully: 6.1 million flight awards used in 2019, or 7.2% of passenger miles, versus 10.9 million and 10.3% in 2025. Kirby's 50,000-a-day figure implies more than 18 million passengers a year, and United has not publicly reconciled the gap with its filings.
