The Next AI Infrastructure Crisis Will Be About Power

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Hossein Kazemi is CTO at ChargePoint and a technology leader with 25+ years in energy and electrification.

A few years ago, I visited a fleet depot preparing to electrify hundreds of vehicles. The vehicles were ready. The charging infrastructure was being installed. The software platform was in place. What wasn’t ready was the electrical capacity needed to support everything at once.

That experience stayed with me because it highlighted a growing challenge. For years, technology leaders focused on one critical question: Do we have enough compute to support the next generation of innovation? Today, a second constraint is emerging. As AI, transportation, buildings and industry become more electrified, access to power and the ability to manage it intelligently are becoming strategic considerations alongside compute.

Organizations are investing heavily in AI infrastructure while simultaneously electrifying fleets, facilities and operations. Increasingly, these initiatives depend on the same underlying resource: electrical capacity.

As AI moves beyond data centers and into vehicles, robotics, factories and other physical systems, the relationship between technology and energy becomes impossible to ignore.

AI growth and electrification are converging in practical ways. A fleet operator may be adding megawatts of charging capacity while a nearby data center expands its computing footprint. A manufacturer may be electrifying equipment while deploying AI-driven automation. In many cases, these investments compete for finite site and grid capacity.

Meeting future demand will require continued investment in generation, transmission and distribution infrastructure. It will also require smarter coordination of how we use available power. The question is no longer only how much electricity we can generate, but how effectively we can manage it.

Consider the electric vehicle. Most people view EVs primarily as new electrical loads. In reality, they are connected computing platforms paired with large batteries. Unlike stationary energy assets, vehicles move between homes, workplaces, depots and public charging locations, creating a distributed network of mobile energy storage.

This is where intelligent electrification becomes important. Software can determine when vehicles charge, how quickly they charge and how charging demand interacts with available site and grid capacity. Over time, vehicle-to-everything technologies may allow energy to flow both to and from vehicles, creating a more dynamic relationship between transportation and the grid.

The significance of this shift is not simply that electricity demand is increasing. It is that the complexity of managing energy is increasing as well.

For a commercial fleet, success is not achieved by charging every vehicle as quickly as possible. The goal is ensuring the right vehicle has the right amount of energy at the right time while balancing route schedules, electricity costs, site capacity and operational requirements.

We’ve seen this firsthand. A depot may have capacity to charge only a portion of its vehicles simultaneously, despite having dozens or even hundreds returning overnight. The challenge is determining which vehicles need power first, how much energy each vehicle requires for its next route and how to balance those requirements against operational and infrastructure constraints.

Traditional energy management software already performs much of this optimization. AI’s contribution is its ability to analyze larger and more dynamic datasets, identify patterns and adjust recommendations as conditions change. Rather than replacing existing energy management systems, AI can help make them more adaptive as electrified operations become increasingly complex.

This capability becomes even more important as autonomous transportation scales.

The industry has invested tremendous effort in teaching vehicles how to navigate and make decisions independently. Yet autonomy cannot stop at driving. A vehicle that can operate without a driver but still requires human intervention to manage charging and energy availability remains only partially autonomous.

The next phase of mobility will require infrastructure capable of coordinating charging, scheduling, authentication, diagnostics and energy management with minimal human involvement.

One of the biggest misconceptions about electrification is that EVs represent only new demand on the grid. While they certainly increase electricity consumption, they also offer flexibility.

A vehicle parked for eight hours rarely needs all of its energy immediately. Charging can often be shifted to periods when electricity prices are lower, grid demand is reduced or renewable generation is more abundant. Fleet charging can be coordinated around operational schedules rather than occurring all at once.

Bidirectional charging may expand those capabilities further, allowing compatible vehicles to provide energy services in circumstances where the economics, infrastructure and regulatory frameworks support it. However, widespread adoption will depend on factors such as equipment compatibility, interoperability standards, business case validation and continued technological maturity.

What has become increasingly clear to me is that energy can no longer be viewed solely as a utility operating in the background of technology. Power availability increasingly influences where charging infrastructure can be deployed, where fleets can scale and where advanced computing workloads can operate.

That reality is changing the skill set technology leaders need. Understanding software architectures remains essential, but so is understanding energy capacity, load management and infrastructure planning.

The industry often discusses AI and electrification as separate trends. In practice, they are becoming deeply interconnected. AI will increase demand for electricity, while intelligent energy systems can help organizations use available power more effectively. Neither eliminates the need for grid investment. Both reinforce the importance of building infrastructure that is smarter, more flexible and better coordinated.

The most important lesson I’ve learned is that the future is not simply about producing more energy or deploying more technology. It’s about ensuring the systems we build can work together. Compute, transportation, buildings and energy are increasingly part of the same operating environment. Organizations that understand those connections will be better positioned to scale the next generation of AI-enabled and electrified services.
