Best Winter Cycling Gloves for Cold, Wet, and Windy Rides

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

We rode through freezing rain, sleet, and biting wind to find the best winter cycling gloves that stay warm, dry, and secure when conditions are at their worst.

Nothing ruins a chilly, crisp day on the bike faster than frozen fingers. Besides being uncomfortable, numb hands can make it difficult to shift gears or feel your brake levers, turning a cold ride into a potentially unsafe one.

The right pair of winter gloves will keep your hands warm without making it difficult to operate your bike’s controls. But finding that pair isn’t always as simple as checking the temperature rating on the packaging. How warm your hands get, the weather conditions, and the type of riding you do all play a role.

For example, on a 35-degree road ride, you’ll likely want a much warmer glove than you would for a mountain bike ride at the same temperature. Higher speeds on the road mean more windchill, while a slower, harder effort on the trails can leave your hands sweaty in gloves that are too warm. If you ride throughout the winter, it’s worth owning a few different pairs to cover the range of conditions you’ll encounter.

I tested more than 40 glove models in temperatures ranging from the low 60s to below 20 degrees, then narrowed the field to my six favorites. I’ve included recommended temperature ranges for each, but consider those a starting point. If you tend to run cold or have Raynaud’s syndrome, you may want a warmer glove; if your hands sweat easily, a lighter, more breathable option might work better.

Perhaps my all-time favorite version of my all-time favorite glove, DeFeet’s Duraglove has been around for ages and has remained mostly unchanged. It’s very comfortable, has great dexterity, and provides good grip. Plus, it has touchscreen functionality. DeFeet offers two versions of this glove: The plain Duraglove ET and the Duraglove ET Wool Blend featured here, which uses a merino wool blend.

I’ve found this version to be the warmer of the two. I’ve worn this glove into the low 40s during high-intensity rides and stayed comfortable. Because this knit glove is incredibly breathable, it’s still comfortable to wear as the day warms into the mid-to-high 50s. The Duraglove is unsuitable for rainy rides as its knit construction will quickly soak up water.

These Assos gloves feel like they shouldn’t be as warm as they are. But don’t let their thin, stretchy, low-bulk material fool you. These gloves have kept my hands toasty and comfortable into the high 30s, and I have mountain biked in them at near-freezing temperatures with no issues.

They use soft fleece fabric inside and have an outer layer with excellent wind resistance. Assos also applies a water-repellent treatment to manage light rain and spray from damp roads. I’m a big fan of the palm of these gloves. They feature very minimal padding that helps with grip and bar feel. Fit and construction are excellent, with long cuffs to keep cold air out of your jacket, plus touchscreen functionality that works very well. The only knock against these gloves is their price. However, my personal pair is now in its fourth winter with no signs of wear, making these an easy recommendation if you want a good pair of gloves that will last.

Near-freezing temperatures with rain are probably the most challenging conditions to ride in. But for some, it’s unavoidable. Glacier Glove’s Ice Bay is as good as it gets for these conditions. It’s constructed with 2mm-thick, fleece-lined neoprene and is as waterproof as any piece of clothing could be.

These gloves have incredible dexterity given their warmth. The long cuff is easy to tuck under your rain jacket to keep water out. The downside of a neoprene glove is that it does not breathe at all. That’s partly how it keeps your hands warm, but it only works as long as you keep the gloves on. If you take them off, your now-sweaty hands will get quite cold quickly, and your formerly toasty gloves (lacking body heat) will be cold and wet with sweat. Despite these drawbacks, if it’s cold and rainy and I need to be out riding, these are the gloves I reach for.

These pricey gloves are the warmest five-finger gloves I’ve tried that still let me move my fingers. Gore took inspiration from high alpine mountaineering for the design. The outer shell uses a Gore-Tex ePE liner that is highly wind- and water-resistant. For insulation, Gore used a PrimaLoft lining. It features one of the best warmth-to-weight ratios. All of this results in a glove that can be comfortable down to and below freezing, depending on your pace and specific riding conditions.

I’ve used similarly warm gloves from other brands, and they've often been far too bulky to feel useful. These still maintain a good bar feel and let me freely articulate my fingers. While they have touchscreen compatibility, I found them a little too bulky to quickly type out a text message on an iPhone.

We’ve been using Pearl Izumi’s AmFIB Lobster Gloves for more than a decade. And they’re still one of the best gloves for when temps plummet.

The gloves are made with 170g PrimaLoft Gold insulation, AmFIB soft-shell polyester-polyurethane fabric, and Ax Suede Laredo synthetic leather. It has the kind of exceptional warmth you’d get in a mitten. Yet the AmFIBs still allow for ample dexterity so you can operate both your shifters and brake levers. There’s also a gel palm pad for comfort while gripping the handlebar, plus touchscreen compatibility for the index finger and thumb.

The secret to the dexterity is the PrimaLoft Gold insulation, which provides exceptional warmth without adding bulk. Most very warm gloves are also very thick, which makes it difficult to locate the proper shifter and grip your brake levers. This pair keeps my hands warm on long weekend rides and daily commutes, even when temperatures dip below freezing, without getting in the way.

Bar Mitts may look unconventional, but riders who use them swear by their added warmth and control. Designed to work with gloves, these neoprene covers attach to your bar and enclose your bike’s shifters and brake levers. They add significant wind protection and help keep your hands shielded from the elements.

Several Bar Mitt variants are available for road, mountain, and townie-style handlebars. Be sure to check details and confirm fitment with your bar/shifter combo before you order.

Glove fit is subjective. They should fit in the Goldilocks range. If your gloves are too big, you risk losing grip on the handlebar and controls. But a glove that’s too tight can prevent you from comfortably grasping the handlebar or grips.

An easy way to spot a glove that’s too big is if you have a lot of fabric bunching in your palm. It’s always worth trying new gloves on your bike to see how they feel on the bars, since labeled sizing can differ from brand to brand.

If you’re riding in temperatures below the mid-40s or closer to freezing, you want a glove with some insulation. In the most basic definition, insulation is filling used to capture and hold onto warmth. The most familiar insulation for outdoor use is down, often found in puffy jackets. In gloves, you are most likely to find down in very bulky mittens, which are very warm but lack the dexterity for cycling.

Most cycling-specific gloves use synthetic insulation from companies like Polartech or PrimaLoft. These materials are most often used on the back of the hand to protect from wind and minimize interference with the handlebar feel. Thin, grippy material is generally found on the palm. Many brands use leather on the palms for improved grip and durability, so watch out for this if you avoid buying animal products.

For the interior of the glove, look for polyester or Merino wool. You want something that wicks sweat and keeps your hands dry—sweaty hands can quickly turn into frozen prunes.

Gloves with appropriate breathability for the temperature and your riding effort keep your hands warm and dry. The warmer the temps, or the higher your effort, the more breathable you’ll want your gloves.

Regionally, humidity also plays a part in selecting the right glove. If you ride somewhere cool and dry, such as Colorado, where it might be in the 30s with low humidity, you likely will be able to use a lighter pair of more breathable gloves. These can do a much better job keeping you warm because they transfer moisture away from your hands more effectively. If you ride in the Northeast, the same pair of gloves might feel too cold at the same temperature, since humidity is often much higher. For these conditions, you’ll want a glove with better wind and waterproofing at the expense of breathability.

Once you figure out the glove warmth you need for your riding, consider other features before buying. Something I find very important in gloves meant for frigid weather is long cuffs. Cuffs make it easy to tuck the glove under or sometimes over your jacket sleeve, keeping your wrists warm and stopping cold air from entering your jacket.

The palm construction of a glove is also worth careful examination. A common feature is padding. Some riders prefer padded gloves, while others do not. Padding matters less the warmer a glove gets, since the palm becomes bulkier with insulation. For thinner gloves, a seamless palm is the way to go. It minimizes any chance of irritation and improves handlebar feel.

If you ride with a smartphone or have a touchscreen computer, gloves that interact with these devices are great. The less you need to remove your gloves in the cold, the happier your hands will be.

Finally, a soft, large terrycloth nose-wipe section (preferably along the thumb or index finger) is good if you’re a heavy snotter.
