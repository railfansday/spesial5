Jetboil TrailCook 2.0L Stove Review: The Ultimate Stove for Group Backpacking Trips

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Over the years, my backcountry cook setup has run the gamut from no-cook options to cooking for 12+ people on multiday treks across the Sierra Nevada or Grand Canyon.

On solo thru-hikes like the Pacific Crest Trail and Colorado Trail, I have historically used my trusted BRS stove to get the job done. And by job I usually just mean boiling water or, if I’m lucky during resupply, “cooking” tortellini. However, now as a backcountry guide I find myself cooking for other people much more often than just myself.

Cooking as a guide involves much more than boiling a pot of water to rehydrate a meal. Instead of ramen or Knorr sides, we are cooking things like egg sandwiches for breakfast, quesadillas with cilantro salsa for lunch, and backcountry fried teriyaki tofu with mixed veggies for dinner. So with all due respect to my original system, the BRS is not going to cut it out there when I’m trying to fry eggs or crisp up the edges of tofu.

These meals might sound elaborate, but they’re quite simple in the backcountry as long as you have the proper tools: I tested the Jetboil TrailCook 2.0L stove system this summer to see if it could get the job done.

Jetboil has really outdone itself with this one. Highlights include a do-it-all pot that’s large enough to feed the whole group, light enough to carry on the trail, and built with much of the same technology Jetboil users already know and love, plus key improvements over past models.

Straining boiling water into a larger pot while cooking for a large group on the Lost Coast Trail. The TrailCook 2.0L would soon after be used to soft boil some eggs.

MSRP: $199 Weight: 1 lb 6.24 oz or 630 g Dimensions: 17.3 cm x 14.5 cm Boil time:130 sec/0.5 liter water Capacity: 2 liters

While I think the Trailcook is one of the best all-in-one cook systems available on the market today, there is a specific use case for this setup. This system is overkill for a solo thru-hiker, but it really shines when you are cooking for more than two people on a backcountry outing. If your idea of camp cooking goes beyond boiling water and into preparing actual meals for you and your friends in the woods, the TrailCook 2.0L is definitely worth a look.

I took the TrailCook 2.0L system out on several outings during my initial testing period, putting it to work in a variety of group settings. It fed me and my coworkers during a three-day training trip on the Tahoe Rim Trail, helped cook for 18 students over six days in the Gila Wilderness, and performed beautifully while preparing meals for 11 women on the Lost Coast Trail.

Across each of these trips, the TrailCook 2.0L consistently felt like it was the right tool for the job. It never felt bulky or difficult to pack, yet it always had enough capacity to handle the demands of group cooking. What started as a product to test has quickly become an integral part of my kitchen setup on group trips, and I plan to continue taking it into the backcountry long after this review is published.

Boiling water rapidly while students participate in a field lesson before dinner.

FluxRing: This heat-transfer technology is one reason Jetboil stoves are among the best at heating quickly and efficiently.

Handling tabs and strainer: These user-friendly features let you drain the pot without burning your fingers on the hot lid. Hole for more exact pours on one side of the lid and strainer lines on the other.

Handling tabs and lid-lifter protected from heat.

Integrated 6,000 BTU/hr Burner: Powerful and effective. Just 130 seconds to boil half a liter of water.

Folding lockout silicone handles: Super handy for when the pot is full of heavy water or food. There is nothing worse than having to guess if your pot handle is going to hold.

Easy-ignite: The Trailcook lights just like a gas stove at home. The igniter felt intuitive and worked every time I used it. Keeping my fingers crossed it continues to do so.

Igniter with precision heat control features.

Precision heat control: This was a great feature for simmering or otherwise controlling the stove’s heat level! I haven’t seen this in the previous Jetboil versions I have owned, and I definitely suggest choosing a model with this feature included.

Compatible with accessories: While I didn’t test the stove with any accessories, the Summit Skillet will also fit with this system, as will the 1.5L cookpot, giving you multiple cooking surface options.

All-in-one packing: It’s so important to have gear that fits well into your pack. Despite this stove system’s large capacity, it’s so compact and easy to fit neatly into a backpack. The burner goes into a small bag, which can fit inside the pot along with a small (4 oz) fuel canister. The lid can then be put on and the handles folded to keep everything neatly packed together.

Stove with igniter and fuel all neatly held inside pot with handles keeping everything together. Hand shown for scale.

2Lpot: The size of this pot makes it ideal for large-group cooking, such as on my guided backcountry trips. While a second or third pot might be needed depending on group size, this system always boils water the quickest and gets the cooking off to a quick and easy start.

There are a lot of stoves on the market, but what makes a Jetboil a Jetboil is how quickly it can take water from snowmelt to a rolling boil. It has an insanely fast boil time, and that has undoubtedly contributed to Jetboil’s popularity over the years. When you’re cold, hungry, and ready for dinner, those extra few minutes start to matter.

The TrailCook 2.0L is designed for group cooking, and where it really stands out is in its combination of speed, efficiency, and packability. There are plenty of ways to cook for a group in the backcountry. For years, my setup was an MSR WhisperLite, a fuel bottle, and a large pot. That system works, but it also means carrying multiple pieces of gear, finding room for all of them in your pack, and waiting longer for water to boil.

The TrailCook 2.0L simplifies all of that. Everything nests neatly together, and thanks to Jetboil’s FluxRing technology, it boils water and cooks food much faster and more efficiently than the WhisperLite setups I’ve used in the past. It’s also not limited to just boiling water. The improved heat control makes it much easier to simmer, sauté, and cook actual meals instead of simply rehydrating food.

Soft-boiling eggs for the group. 6-minute boil followed by rapid cold dunk. Enjoy!

What impressed me most was how much cooking capacity Jetboil packed into such a compact system. A two-liter pot capable of feeding a group could easily feel bulky or awkward to carry, but I never felt like the TrailCook took up an unreasonable amount of pack space. On trips where I was cooking for anywhere from a handful of coworkers to nearly twenty students, I was consistently glad to have it along.

And while the TrailCook is certainly designed with the backcountry in mind, I think it deserves some credit for front-country camping too. If you’re cooking for two to four people, it offers a much more compact and portable alternative to hauling around a traditional Coleman stove and camp kitchen setup. You give up some cooking surface area, but you gain a lot in ease of use, packability, and speed.

I love knowing that when I get to camp after a long day of hiking or driving, my cook system is together in one place and takes just seconds to get going when it’s time to start dinner.

There are so many features on the TrailCook 2.0L that I love. Below is my love note to each of them:

Locking handles: Glorious. They lock out and feel sturdy and secure while cooking, and I never worried about them folding unexpectedly while moving a pot full of food around camp. A real blessing.

Lid: A design I found myself appreciating over and over again. Having both a pour hole and a built-in strainer makes draining pasta, pouring hot water, or straining veggies incredibly easy. It’s one of those simple features that feels like it was added after years of field testing and experience. It just makes sense.

Strainer side of lid, letting the water out while keeping the yum yum in.

No boil-over: If you’ve ever stood over a stove trying to prevent starchy pasta water from dumping all over your cook setup, you’ll understand why this is such a win. Even when I filled the pot well above the fill line, the contents didn’t bubble out like they did with some older Jetboil models.

Heat pads: The heat pads built into the lid sound minor, but they’re such a quality-of-life improvement. In the past, dumping water out of the lid usually meant grabbing a buff, a shirt sleeve, or whatever happened to be nearby to avoid burning my fingers on the hot metal. With the TrailCook 2.0L, this is a non-issue thanks to the little orange heat pads.

Speed: This thing is FAST. Jetboil has built its reputation on rapid boil times, and the TrailCook 2.0L is no exception. Whether I was melting snow, boiling water for a meal, or trying to get dinner on the table for a large group, I was consistently impressed by how quickly it got the job done. It worked so well that I could boil multiple pots of water on the Jetboil before the first pot finished boiling on my Whisperlite when I had both stoves going at once.

Capacity: It was large enough to comfortably cook for groups — including one trip where I was feeding 20 people — without ever feeling excessively bulky in my pack. That’s a tough balance to strike, and this system nailed it.

Built-in igniter: Backpacking stoves have come with built-in igniters for years, including Jetboils, but they’re often unreliable and break easily. In contrast, this one worked reliably throughout my testing. When everyone is hungry and waiting for dinner, being able to fire up the stove with the turn of a switch is a beautiful thing. Goodbye, “which pocket is my lighter in again?”

Heating rapidly is what Jetboil does best.

Pot support system: If I had one criticism of the TrailCook 2.0L, it would be the pot support system. I like the concept of the redesigned support arms and appreciate that the pot can be easily lifted on and off the stove. However, Jetboil markets the system as “self-centering,” and that wasn’t my experience.

I often found it surprisingly difficult to tell whether the pot was properly seated on the support arms. The grooves on the bottom of the pot are supposed to align with the arms on the stove, but there isn’t much tactile or visual feedback to let you know when everything is lined up correctly. More than once, I found myself double-checking the alignment before feeling confident enough to start cooking.

That being said, once the pot is properly seated, it feels stable and secure. The issue is getting it there. If one of the support arms misses its groove, the pot can sit unevenly, creating the potential for a spill if you’re not paying attention. It’s not a dealbreaker, but it’s one area where I think the user experience could be improved. I would love some external indication that the arms are in the grooves.

Weight: Again, this system shines in a specific use case. For solo hikers, the 2L capacity and 24 oz weight penalty don’t make sense. This isn’t a knock on the stove itself, which is meant for group trips.

The stabilizing arms shown along with bottom pot ring that sits on top.

Is this stove system worth my $199? My simple answer is yes. It has an overwhelming amount of useful, streamlined features and does the job well every single time. If I were in the market for a large-capacity Jetboil system, the TrailCook 2.0L would have my dollar.

Overall, the value-to-spend here is wonderful, but whether it’s worth it to you depends a lot on your use case. Do you need a large-capacity Jetboil system? Would the TrailCook 1.2L be enough capacity for your cooking needs? Do you cook for groups in the backcountry or are you a solo thru-hiker? Let these questions help guide your purchasing decision.
