Van Gogh’s record-breaking ‘Blossoming Chestnut Trees’: the $190m sale and the restitution claim that nearly upended it

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Sotheby’s New York auction of Van Gogh’s Blossoming Chestnut Trees (May 1890) on 18 November

Adventures with Van Gogh is a weekly blog by Martin Bailey, The Art Newspaper's long-standing correspondent and expert on the Dutch painter. Published on Fridays, stories range from newsy items about this most intriguing artist, to scholarly pieces based on meticulous investigations and discoveries.

Van Gogh’s Blossoming Chestnut Trees sold at Sotheby's New York on Wednesday night (18 November) for $190.4m, including fees. Auctioneer Oliver Barker began the sale by stating that “the work is being offered for sale pursuant to an agreement between the seller and the heirs of the Hedwig and Samuel Fischer family”. The Fischer family had sold the painting in 1936.

The auction of the Van Gogh opened at $135m, two telephone bidders coming in. A third bidder, in the saleroom, won the contest after less than four minutes, at a hammer price of $165m. He is the New York dealer William Acquavella, presumably acting for an unidentified client.

Van Gogh’s Blossoming Chestnut Trees (May 1890)

Before the auction, Sotheby’s had not announced a formal estimate range, but instead suggested that the painting should fetch a hammer price of around $180m (before fees). This may have been slightly optimistic, since it was far more than any Van Gogh has ever made. Blossoming Chestnut Trees (Châtaigniers en fleurs) (May 1890) is a fine landscape, although not among his very best. And the other 11 pictures in last night’s auction of the Blaquier-Arrieta collection generally sold for around, or even below, their lower estimates.

The uncertainty last weekend over a possible Nazi-era claim for the Van Gogh may have discouraged some potential bidders, but the $190m (including fees) was nonetheless an astonishing result. This was for an artist who had failed to sell during his own lifetime. Van Gogh completed nearly a thousand paintings in his career, with only a single recorded sale: The Red Vineyard (November 1888, Pushkin State Museum of Fine Arts, Moscow).

Only three artworks have ever sold for more at auction: Leonardo’s Salvator Mundi (around 1500, $450m, Christie’s, 2017), Gustav Klimt’s Portrait of Elisabeth Lederer (1916, $236m, Sotheby’s, 2025) and Andy Warhol’s Shot Sage Blue Marilyn (1964, $195m, Christie’s, 2022).

The previous record price for a Van Gogh was $117m (including fees) for Orchard with Cypresses (April 1888), which Christie’s sold in 2022. The Blossoming Chestnut Trees price was 62% more.

Sotheby’s has been open about Blossoming Chestnut Trees’s Nazi-era (1933-45) provenance. Following our inquiries, we reported in our 2 October blog post: “It was purchased four years later [in 1926] by the Berlin-based publisher Samuel Fischer, and on his death in 1934 it passed to his daughter Brigitte. Although the family was persecuted because of their Jewish heritage, a Sotheby’s spokesperson confirmed to us that, based on recent research, the family was free at all times to dispose of the painting.”

News of a possible Nazi-era claim emerged only five weeks later. Berlin-based Gunnar Schnabel, who describes himself as a “looted art researcher” specialising in the Nazi period, told the New York Times that he first heard about the sale on 7 November, when he was “lying on a sofa in his vacation home”. His letter to Sotheby’s calling for the sale to be delayed was sent on 12 November, just six days before the auction. Obviously this put Sotheby’s in a very awkward situation: should the sale be postponed or go ahead?

In response, Sotheby’s stated: “We are aware of the persecution experienced by the Fischer family. After the Anschluss in 1938, the family fled Austria, and the Gestapo confiscated their Vienna home, library and remaining art collection. But the circumstances around this transaction were different”. The auction house also added that after the war, no claim was ever made for the Van Gogh.

Urgent negotiations were held with the representatives of the Fischer family. The key question at stake was whether their ancestors had been the victim of a forced sale at a lower than market value. By June 1936, when the Van Gogh was sold, the family had just moved to Vienna. They had consigned the painting to the Cassirer gallery in Amsterdam several years earlier, in 1932-33. The price eventually paid in 1936 by the buyer, Paris-based collectors Henriette and Marcel Kapferer, was $22,000.

Comparisons with other Van Gogh sales around the time are telling. In 1935, the year before the Fischer sale, Orchard with Cypresses had sold for $15,000 at the New York auction house Parke Bernet (this was the painting which later fetched a record Van Gogh price in 2022). That makes the $22,000 seem reasonable.

Then there is the Sunflowers (August 1888), which had been sold to London's National Gallery in 1924 for the equivalent of $5,800. Although this sale took place 12 years before Blossoming Chestnut Trees and Jo Bonger, Vincent’s sister-in-law, probably reduced the price for a major museum, it is now regarded as Van Gogh’s greatest masterpiece. This again suggests that the Fischers received a reasonable sum for their picture.

Following yesterday’s Sotheby’s auction, Schnabel told The Art Newspaper that the Fischers had also been supported by the Commission for Looted Art in Europe's co-chair Anne Webber. He added that the family is delighted that Blossoming Chestnut Trees “which hung in the Berlin home of Samuel and Hedwig Fischer for a quarter of a century, has now found a new owner”. They hope this case “may offer some encouragement to other families who have faced persecution during the Nazi era, and that they, too, may find a path toward the recognition of their rightful claims.”

The terms of the 17 November settlement between the heirs of the Fischer family and the Blaquier-Arrieta family remain confidential. Presumably some sort of financial compensation has been paid. The agreement opens the door for the Fischer heirs to make claims for further paintings which had once been in their family if they come onto the market in the future.

Title page of the Leicester Galleries exhibition Artists who Died Young, with an image of Blossoming Chestnut Trees

As a postscript on the 1930s story of Blossoming Chestnut Trees, soon after the Kapferers bought the picture they lent it to an exhibition in London, Artists who Died Young at the Leicester Galleries in 1938. A year after the London exhibition, war broke out. Blossoming Chestnut Trees had been sent to the United States a few months earlier to New York dealers Wildenstein & Co and, after passing through two American private collections, it went to Argentina in 1975, to the Blaquier-Arrieta family. Following the Sotheby’s sale, it remains a mystery where the Van Gogh will ultimately reside.
