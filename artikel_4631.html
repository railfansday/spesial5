Now put names to the adherents to these outlandish ideas. In media appearances, New England Patriots quarterback Drake Maye, along with none other than hometown hero Josh Allen, have expressed skepticism about the moon landing. To be fair, it’s hard to know if they were just playing around. The late NFL star Jared Lorenzen was also a moon landing skeptic, a view he had held ever since watching a conspiracy video in college. Professional basketball stars share the notion, and so have some Hollywood actors.

Moving further out, retired chess grandmaster Garry Kasparov supports the notion that our calendar is missing 300 years of history. To Kasparov and other adherents, written history arose around 800 A.D., not around 5,000 years ago as everybody thinks. The ancient history of Rome and Greece is therefore an elaborate forgery meant to suppress a true chronology of Slavic world supremacy.

And as for folk remedies gone viral—perching atop a steaming pot infused with mugwort, for instance—gynecologists warned, unsurprisingly, that the practice may result in accidental burns. California prosecutors slapped Goop, the lucrative company owned by Gwyneth Paltrow (a proponent of “yoni steaming” and other practices, including inserting jade eggs), with fines over false medical claims.

It is easy to list passionate and strange celebrity beliefs that range from mischief and marketing to misinformation and delusion.

Dan Aykroyd, of Ghostbuster fame, for example, tells the story that aliens have strategically seeded 13 crystal skulls on the planet. (He playfully markets his line of Crystal Head Vodka as “enlightened drinking.”) Kylie Jenner, famous for being from a famous family but not for biochemistry, thinks that jet contrails are really “chemtrails” that have caused honeybee declines. (Spoiler alert: They aren’t, and they haven’t.) Madonna, who perpetually wars with her Roman Catholic upbringing and dabbles in mystical number combinations, wears a red string bracelet as “spiritual protection.” (She claims this is “science.”) Kanye West, who has a publicly acknowledged history of bipolar disorder, famously became convinced that vaccines contain microchips.

In the modern media ecosphere, it is sometimes difficult to separate the “not-serious” from the “un-serious” and make-believe from marketing.

Finding eccentric, far-fetched beliefs is not difficult. It is harder, though, to figure out why we should listen to celebrities’ pronouncements.

After all, we can effortlessly consult qualified opinions from credentialed experts. This is an enormous luxury. With a click, without visiting a library or cracking a book, we can find reliable information in disciplines such as history, cognitive psychology, medicine, ecology, chemistry, astrophysics, mathematics, engineering, and so on.
