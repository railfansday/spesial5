From Raw Sensor Signals to Regulatory-Grade Data: How Air Quality Monitoring Networks Are Engineered

Air quality monitoring looks like a data problem: put a sensor on a pole, read a number, plot it. In practice, it is an engineering problem. The signal you get from a gas or particulate sensor is shaped by temperature, humidity, ageing materials and other gases in the air. Turning that raw signal into data that an environmental manager can defend in front of a regulator takes calibration, quality control, and a well-designed data pipeline.

This article walks through how modern air quality monitoring networks are built, from the physics of the sensor to the API that feeds your dashboards.

Regulatory reference stations are the benchmark. They use certified analysers, controlled shelters and trained operators, and they produce the data that legal limits are checked against. They are also expensive to install and maintain, so a city or an industrial site usually has very few of them.

That creates a coverage gap. Pollution is highly local: a road junction, a loading dock or a stockpile can have a very different profile from a station a kilometre away. Dense networks of compact monitors address this in three ways:

Spatial coverage: more measurement points per unit area show gradients that a single station cannot.

Response time: continuous, short-interval readings from the source area allow alerts within minutes rather than reports after the fact.

Cost of deployment: smaller units with lower power needs can be installed on existing infrastructure, which makes it practical to monitor a fence line or a port perimeter.

The two approaches are complementary. Reference stations anchor the network’s accuracy, and denser monitoring shows where, and when, to look more closely.

“Accurate” is not a useful specification on its own. Several frameworks define what performance is expected, and in which context:

CEN/TS 17660: This European technical specification sets out performance evaluation for air quality sensor systems, in separate parts for gases and particulate matter. Class 1 is the most demanding tier, intended for sensor systems that can be used for indicative measurements.

MCERTS for indicative monitors: The UK Environment Agency’s MCERTS scheme includes certification for indicative ambient monitors, giving buyers independent assurance beyond the manufacturer’s own claims.

US EPA performance targets: The EPA publishes performance targets and testing guidance for air sensors used in supplemental and informational monitoring, with metrics such as precision, bias, linearity and error. Its Air Sensor Toolbox is a good starting point for evaluation protocols.

Independent evaluations: Public test programmes, such as the South Coast AQMD’s AQ-SPEC, compare sensor performance in field and laboratory conditions and are useful for cross-checking any claim.

The regulatory context is also moving. The EU’s Ambient Air Quality Directive 2024/2881 tightens limit values for 2030, bringing them closer to the WHO’s 2021 Global Air Quality Guidelines. For PM2.5, for example, the annual EU limit value drops to 10 µg/m³, while the WHO guideline is 5 µg/m³. Tighter limits mean smaller margins, and smaller margins make measurement uncertainty and good documentation more important. Member states are also expected to make better use of supplementary methods to improve spatial coverage, which opens a clearer role for well-characterised sensor networks alongside reference stations.

For teams comparing options, certification is a practical filter. For example, Kunak AIR Pro is certified under MCERTS as an indicative ambient particulate monitor for PM2.5 and PM10 (certificate CSA MC230418/00), and the manufacturer reports alignment with the Class 1 criteria of CEN/TS 17660. The point is not the product, it is that this kind of independent evidence is what separates a monitoring network from a collection of sensors.

Different sectors use the same technology for different questions:

Industrial fenceline monitoring: Monitors placed around a plant boundary show whether emissions reach neighbouring areas, and combined with wind data, they help identify the source of an episode.

Ports: Ship traffic, cargo handling and trucks create overlapping sources. Distributed monitoring helps separate them and supports mitigation planning.

Mining and quarries: Dust is the main concern. Real-time particulate data lets operators adjust watering, blasting schedules or haulage before limits are exceeded.

Cities: Dense networks complement reference stations to map hotspots near schools, traffic corridors or low-emission zones, and to evaluate the impact of mobility policies.

Data centers: Gaseous contaminants and particulates can affect equipment reliability, and indoor and perimeter monitoring supports facility management and audits.

Before choosing hardware, it helps to answer the following questions:

What is the decision? Compliance reporting, operational control and public information need different levels of data quality.

Which pollutants and averaging periods matter? Match them to the applicable regulation or permit.

What evidence of performance exists? Look for third-party certification, published co-location results, and independent evaluations, not just datasheets.

How is calibration maintained? Ask whether baseline and span corrections can be applied remotely, and how often they are verified.

How are data quality and traceability handled? You should be able to see which readings were validated, flagged, or corrected, and why.

How does the data leave the platform? Check the API, protocols (4G, Ethernet, Modbus), and compatibility with your SCADA, BI, or reporting tools.

What is the siting plan? Height, distance to sources, wind patterns, and power availability all affect representativeness.

Who owns the process? Assign responsibility for maintenance, data review, and escalation before the first unit is installed.

Air quality data only becomes useful when its limits are understood and managed. Teams that treat monitoring as an engineering system, covering the sensor, the calibration, the pipeline and the standards, end up with data they can act on and defend.
