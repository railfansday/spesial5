Inside Gatcombe Park, Princess Anne’s Beloved 730-Acre Country Estate

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

On any given day at Gatcombe Park, Princess Anne’s sprawling 730-acre privately owned estate in Gloucestershire, one might spy the 76-year-old checking in on her prized Highland cows, a scarf tied firmly over her famous bouffant. At the estate’s cutting-edge stables, her daughter, Zara Tindall, may be riding with her daughter and nieces, coaching them as they jump through courses designed by her father Mark Phillips. Meanwhile, Zara’s husband, rugby star Mike Tindall, may be playing beer pong in the “party barn” with his brother-in-law Peter Phillips, while Princess Anne’s husband Sir Timothy Laurence watches rugby in the cozy, cluttered parlor of the estate’s main Georgian mansion.

The scene is that of an idyllic country estate, a beloved family home that is also a working farm. It may also serve as an example to Prince Harry and Meghan Markle as they search for their own private estate in the British countryside. “Being able to take on a place like this—for me, I’ve got to make it work,” Princess Anne once said. “This is not something that comes free, this has got to pay its way, otherwise I can’t stay here."

Since the ’70s, Gatcombe Park has been Princess Anne’s place of refuge from endless royal duties, a place to live the country life she loves. “Every year, month and day, I realise how fortunate and privileged I am to have grown up and spent most of my life in the countryside,” she told Country Life in 2020. “It’s not only the space, appreciating the seasons, the wildlife, the plant life, the arable crops and the livestock, but, most importantly, it’s the people who live and work there and understand the complexity of their environment.”

The estate was the perfect wedding present from the late Queen Elizabeth to her athletic, no-nonsense daughter and her first husband, Mark Phillips. The two Olympic equestrians first met at a party in 1968, although the meeting was not auspicious. “We had to be told that we’d met in 1968 before we remembered,” Anne recalled.

Zara Phillips riding her horse Toytown at Gatcombe Horse Trials, with Princess Anne and Tim Laurence, September 16, 2001.

They later reconnected and found they shared many things, including an obsession with nature, country life, and the world of elite equestrian competitions. “Anne couldn’t marry her horse, so she’s marrying Mark,” the then Prince Charles reportedly joked about his future brother-in-law. Princess Anne approached their upcoming wedding with the same stoicism she did everything. When asked if their relationship could withstand the pressure of publicity and public duties, the Princess was blunt. “Can? It’s got to, hasn’t it.”

The two were married on November 14, 1973, in a lavish ceremony at Westminster Abbey. From the start, Phillips, an active captain in 1st The Queen’s Dragoon Guards, proved to be cut from a different cloth. He refused all the Queen’s offers of titles and was not popular with some royals and their courtiers, with the protective Prince Charles reportedly nicknaming him “fog,” because he was “so thick, according to Kitty Kelley’s The Royals.” British journalist Auberon Waugh (the eldest son of novelist Evelyn Waugh) was even more brutal. He unkindly described him as “Princess Anne’s grinning, speechless husband, who, if you whistle at him, wets himself.”

The entrance hall with a stone floor and decorative pillars, June 1976.

In spite of naysayers, the couple forged ahead and began searching for the perfect country estate (“I’ve never been a city girl,” Anne noted). In 1976, former Home Secretary Lord Rab Butler, suggested the couple check out Gatcombe Estate, his family home outside of the village of Minchinhampton.

The estate featured over 700 acres of woods and cultivated farmland, stables, a fine herd of Hereford-Friesian cattle and a trout lake, “netting an income of between 14,000 and 18,000 pounds a year.” The five-bedroom neoclassical main house, built with Cotswold stone, was originally built in 1774. In 1820, the famous economist Davis Ricardo had the house redesigned. The exterior was covered in roses and wisteria, and the couple were instantly smitten. The interior was not as picturesque, with one friend describing it as “rather dreary, in some disrepair, with suspicions of dry rot.”

But the couple loved it. “We stood on the front steps and looked out at the view; it was quiet, it was secluded, it was peaceful with woods down each side of a long valley,” Phillips recalled. “I thought to myself—well, whatever is inside, we can change, given time. But this we can never change. This is just what we have been looking for.”

Peter Phillips, age 9, on horseback at Gatcombe Park, July 28, 1987.

A week later, Princess Anne took Queen Elizabeth to view the property. In June 1976, it was announced the Queen had purchased the home for the couple for a reported 500,000 to 750,000 pounds at the time. Workers on the estate suggested to reporters that Anne could help on a tractor. “Since the Princess can drive a tank, why not?” one quipped.

There was immediate backlash to the private purchase. “When members of the Labour Party government heard that the Queen was buying her daughter and son-in-law the farm and woodland property at such a price, left-wing members called for the introduction of a wealth tax,” the Australian Woman’s Weekly reported. “They pointed out that last year Parliament voted the royal family an extra $600,000 to help it pay its bills.”

MP Willie Hamilton took it a step further. “They’re gold-plated parasites. The lot of them. All parasites.”

For their part, the palace was tight-lipped, claiming the purchase was “an absolutely private matter.” Lord Butler also scoffed at the reported price, claiming it was not nearly as high.

Ignoring the noise, the couple began working to improve their new estate, insisting they would pay their own way. “People never believe me when I say that we’re just like any other young married couple with a mortgage,” Phillips said. Princess Anne did indeed drive a tractor, and Prince Andrew and Prince Edward were said to come down to help during harvest season. While his wife stayed at her apartment working in Buckingham Palace in London during the week, Phillips worked tirelessly on the farm, “so tired that I fell asleep in front of the television.”

Making good on their dream to turn Gatcombe into an A+ equestrian facility, the couple in 1983 hosted the inaugural Gatcombe Park Festival of British Eventing. The event became a mainstay on the elite equestrian calendar. Held every August, it featured Olympians competing in cross-county (courses often designed by Phillips), jumping and dressage events. Thousands attended the televised program each year, and many more visited at the annual craft fairs and the Gatcombe Food Festival.

Princess Anne with her bull terrier dogs at the British Horse Trials Championships, August 31, 1996.

Phillips found many ways to monetize the estate, even turning brushwood cleared from the courses into paintbrushes. Meanwhile, the couple raised their children Peter and Zara at Gatcombe, where life revolved around horses. “One enjoys riding, but when you get back there’s still half a day’s work to do just to keep the darn things on four legs,” Anne noted, per The Princess Royal by Helen Cathcart. “You have time for nothing else.”

Tindall has noted that she had an “amazing” country childhood on the estate, far from the hustle and bustle of royal life. “It was a great place to grow up around horses, looking after animals—the respect and partnership that you have to build with the animals, it teaches you so much you know growing up and as a kid,” she said.

But this didn’t mean that growing up on the farm wasn’t hard work. The Phillips children (whose parents declined for them to have titles) were expected to carry their weight. “We had to do everything with them,” Zara recalled. “We had to clean our tack. We had to muck them out.”

Zara, who eventually became an Olympic rider herself, recalled that her parents had very different ways of training. “Mum’s very relaxed. She’s like, Just, you know, just do what you do, whereas my father’s like, More of this, more of that, she said. “My father was quite critical... Very rarely you would get a, Yeah, that was good, out of him. So it was always striving to try and get that comment. He was a tough coach.”

There was also tension inside the home. Both Princess Anne and Phillips were unfaithful (Phillips even had a daughter, Felicity, with a New Zealand woman in 1985) and in 1989 they separated. But Phillips did not go far, moving to Aston Farm on the estate and continuing to run Gatcombe’s events (he finally left Aston Farm in 2013). Princess Anne stayed in the main house with her new husband, Sir Timothy Laurence, who she married in 1992.

According to those who have visited the main house, life there is surprisingly “unroyal” and laid-back, filled with bric-a-brac and cozy, worn decor. “Journalists and biographers who visit Gatcombe for the first time are still struck by the informality of the place,” Anne’s biographer Nicholas Courtney told Glocester Live. Gatcombe Park has become a true family compound, with Peter Phillips and his family reportedly having a cottage there. Zara and Mike Tindall have raised their children at Aston Farm since 2013.

Running the estate has also become a family affair. Peter Phillips helped organize the annual Gatcombe Park Festival of British Eventing in later years. Princess Anne would present trophies and Zara would compete, as photographers took pictures of Anne’s grandchildren frolicking.

Zara Phillips, Mia Tindall, and Princess Anne at the Whatley Manor Horse Trials, Gatcombe Park, September 8, 2017.

Farming operations have also continued, a practice Anne clearly relishes. “I have lived at Gatcombe for more than 42 years,” Anne wrote in Country Life in 2020. “We were not looking for a farm, but it has been a real privilege to try to work with what we have. Ours is an organic, extensive grass enterprise, usually complicated by running the horse-trial championships in early August. The woodland is a real mix of trees—mostly beeches, but huge numbers of ash of all ages. Who knows how many will survive, but I feel the naturally selected mix could be an important part of the answer.”

That same year, the official royal website also noted the extensive livestock on the property that included 230 Wiltshire Horn sheep, 14 White Park cows, 6 Highland cows, and 2 Gloucester Old Spot sows. There is also a prized herd of rare Suffolk Punch horses, which Anne is passionate about saving. The estate also includes a kitted out party barn, which includes catering facilities, large screens and bar/lounge areas.

The last few years have been eventful, in both good and bad ways. In 2021, Zara gave birth to her third child, Lucas, on the bathroom floor at Aston Farm. In 2023, the estate hosted their last Eventing Festival, citing rising costs and COVID as the reason.

“The horse trials at Gatcombe and more recently the Festival of British Eventing have been a major part of my life for over 40 years when The Princess Royal and I first had the dream,” Mark Phillips said in a statement at the time. “It’s truly a great sadness that the original model and indeed the sport has changed so much. Since COVID, costs, particularly insurance, have risen so much that the numbers no longer add up. It is an end of an era, the next 40 years of the sport will be different, let’s hope it can be equally special.”

In 2024, Princess Anne was reportedly injured by a horse on the property, which resulted in her being hospitalized for a concussion. But there was happier news in June of 2026, when Peter Phillips and his new wife Harriet Sperling reportedly celebrated their wedding reception at the party barn.

Although Princess Anne still continues with her punishing schedule of official engagements, staying in her London apartment in St. James Palace when she must, it is clear her heart lies at Gatcombe Park, surrounded by her loved ones, beloved animals and the crisp country air. “It’s very normal; there isn’t any standing on ceremony, and she’s the only one with a title,” royal historian Robert Hardman told Hello!. “She’s a very good grandmother. She’s hands-on, but like all grandchildren, I think they can get away with things. She dotes on them.”

In fact, the normally tight-lipped Anne has said so herself. “It’s really nice to come back and just be yourself in an area like this.” Former royal courtier Ailsa Anderson agrees. “Gatcombe is her bolthole, where she is not the Princess Royal—she is Anne.”
