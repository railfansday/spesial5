The next ice age may have begun 2,000 years ago, but humans cut it short

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Buried plants on Baffin Island reveal when a remnant of the Laurentide Ice Sheet began growing again and how human-caused warming reversed its advance.

About 2,000 years ago, the last remnant of the ice sheet that once covered much of Canada began growing again on Baffin Island.

It expanded in bursts, then began retreating around 1960 as warmer summers melted more ice than winters added.

The researchers argue that the regrowth marked the earliest stage of a new glaciation in North America, and that human-caused warming cut it short.

At the peak of the last glacial period, the Laurentide Ice Sheet covered much of Canada and reached into the northern United States.

“People always say that it disappeared, but that’s not true,” said Nicolás Young, a geochemist at Columbia University’s Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory who led the study.

What’s left of it is the Barnes Ice Cap, on the central plateau of Baffin Island in Nunavut. Its bedrock is low for the Canadian Arctic, only about 1,640 feet (500 meters) above sea level.

The ice has lasted this long because it is roughly 2,300 feet (700 meters) thick, enough to lift its surface to where snow survives the summer.

Young’s team wanted to know whether the ice cap had ever been smaller than it is today and then grown back. Gifford Miller of the University of Colorado Boulder was among the co-authors.

The answer matters beyond one ice cap, because models say glaciations over North America most likely begin on Baffin Island.

The Barnes Ice Cap is frozen to the rock beneath it, so it doesn’t slide the way most glaciers do. Instead of grinding the ground as it spreads, it buries whatever is growing there and preserves it.

When the margin retreats, the moss and small plants it killed centuries ago come out of the ice.

Radiocarbon dating estimates when the plants died, giving researchers a way to date the ice’s advance.

Young’s team collected 88 of those plants at or near the 2022 ice edge. About 70% of them died around the year 1800, give or take 200 years. Those were the coldest summers of the Little Ice Age.

Sixteen more came from a half-mile (800-meter) line running out from the ice edge. A drained lake there had let the margin retreat farther than anywhere else.

In an interview with Earth.com, Young said that result surprised the team.

The researchers hadn’t expected the ice cap to have been smaller than it is now at any point in the last several thousand years.

“This unique finding allowed us to unexpectedly use these plants to track the ice cap’s renewed expansion,” Young noted.

The researchers also dated 35 boulders and bedrock surfaces around the ice cap. Rock that is open to the sky builds up a rare form of beryllium at a known rate.

The amount in a surface tells how long it has been uncovered.

All of the surfaces gave the same answer: the old ice sheet had retreated inside its 2022 position about 3,600 years ago.

The oldest plants along the half-mile line died around the year 1000, so the ice was already advancing by then.

To fill the gap, the team turned to 186 radiocarbon dates from plants under smaller ice caps on the same plateau. Not one of those dates is older than the Common Era.

The authors read that as the moment, roughly 2,000 years ago, when the summer snowline dropped onto the plateau and ice began to build up across it.

So the Laurentide reached its smallest size between about 3,000 and 2,000 years ago and then started to grow. The authors count that reversal as the start of the next glaciation.

The ice advanced in pulses during cold spells and was nearing its largest modern extent by about 1800.

By 1960 the growth had stopped. Aerial photographs show the margin retreating from about 1961, and the ice cap has since given up between 650 and 2,600 feet (200 and 800 meters) of ground.

Snow also survives the summer on less and less of it.

Around 1960, about 35% of its surface was high enough for snow to last through the summer. By the early 2000s only 3% was, and since 2010 none of it has been.

There is no longer any part of the ice cap where snow can pile up and turn into ice.

Summer temperatures have been climbing since the late 1700s, and since the early 1960s warmer summers account for almost all of the ice cap’s losses. Those summers track the greenhouse gases people have added to the air.

Between 1750 and 1850, the total rose by only about 12 parts per million of carbon dioxide equivalent. By 1960 it had risen another 50, and by 2024 another 138.

“The expansion and retreat of ice sheets has been one of Earth’s most consistent patterns over the last 2.6 million years,” Young told Earth.com.

About 2,000 years ago, he said, the Laurentide was starting its next phase of expansion, as it should have. Then greenhouse gases ended that expansion in the middle of the 20th century.

In the 1970s, some climate scientists argued that a new ice age was coming, and the idea got a famous airing in Newsweek.

Young said those scientists were essentially correct, even if the media twisted the story. “We did just barely start the next glaciation, but they didn’t know it at the time,” he said.

Behind that growth was a slow decline in summer sunlight at high northern latitudes, the orbital rhythm that paces ice ages.

Greenhouse gases did rise over those thousands of years, by about 20 parts per million. The authors found that was too small to overcome the cooling.

Computer models suggest the next glaciation will not begin for thousands of years. But those models usually define its start as the point where new ice sheets hold enough ice to lower the sea by about 10 feet (3 meters) or more.

Young’s team said that would miss the earlier stage of ice growth that his team considers the start of a glaciation.

A 2025 study built from ocean sediments and Earth’s orbit estimated that the first regrowth of ice in this interglacial would come somewhere in the last 1,800 years or so. The plants on Baffin Island agree.

What the record can’t yet say is exactly when the regrowth began. The plants that would date it are still under the ice, and only continued retreat will bring them out.

The dates so far are archived at the NSF Arctic Data Center.

Young said models of the ice cap’s future have it disappearing completely within the next few hundred years.

Asked what happens once the last piece of the Laurentide is gone, Young said another glaciation is unlikely anytime soon.

“Given the amount of greenhouse gases that are in the atmosphere and that we continue to pump, the next glaciation is a long way off,” he said.

The full study was published in the journal Science.
