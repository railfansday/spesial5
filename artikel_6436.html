Believing you’ll be remembered after death is linked to better mental health

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Believing you'll be remembered after death is linked to better mental health and fewer addictive behaviors, according to new research.

When they think about death, some people picture heaven or another life after this one. Others simply hope to be remembered by family and friends, or through the work they leave behind.

In new research on adults living through a war, only that second hope was linked to better mental health.

Those who expected to live on in other people’s memories reported less depression, anxiety and trouble sleeping, while belief in an afterlife showed no link to those problems.

The associations were generally modest, but they appeared across all the mental health and addictive behavior measures examined.

Psychologists Uri Lifshin and Mario Mikulincer led the work at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. Both are part of the university’s Israel Center for Addiction and Mental Health.

Lifshin’s team surveyed 2,912 Jewish adults in Israel in February 2025, during the country’s war with Hamas, Hezbollah and Iran.

Everyone answered once, so the survey can show which feelings tend to appear together but not which one comes first.

“The feeling that our lives matter and will continue to matter to other people may be an important part of how we cope with uncertainty and the knowledge of our own mortality,” the researchers wrote.

In his 1973 book The Denial of Death, Ernest Becker wrote that much of human behavior is driven by a need to keep the terror of death out of mind. His ideas grew into terror management theory.

The theory holds that people manage that fear by feeling valued, loved and part of something that will continue after they’re gone.

In tests of the theory, reminders of death have made people more likely to choose alcohol over a nonalcoholic drink.

Similar reminders have raised cravings for tobacco and cannabis. They can also increase the urge to gamble in people who already have a gambling problem.

A 2024 review that pooled 104 studies found a moderate link between fear of death and a range of mental health symptoms.

Psychologists have a name for the opposite feeling, the sense that some part of a person will carry on through other people, a legacy or a group. They call it symbolic immortality.

In an earlier study, Lifshin and colleagues linked that feeling to higher self-esteem. Apart from depression, though, no study had tested whether it was linked to fewer mental health problems or addictive behaviors.

Lifshin’s team collected the answers online, in Hebrew, as the fifth round of a larger national study of mental health and addiction. Volunteers were 18 to 70 years old.

The team built the sample to match Jewish adults in Israel by gender, age, how religious they were and where they lived. Of 10,869 people invited, 4,731 agreed to take part.

The team then dropped incomplete, careless and duplicate answers, along with anyone beyond the sample’s quotas, leaving 2,912.

At the center of it all was a single question. “Do you think that you will be remembered by other people after you die (e.g., through your accomplishments or legacy)?”

Answers ran from 1, for certainly not, to 7, for certainly yes. Most people chose answers toward the yes end of the scale, and the average was 4.55.

Volunteers were also asked whether they believed in an afterlife, such as heaven or reincarnation.

Standard questionnaires covered depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress, sleep and emotional control, plus nine kinds of addictive behavior, from problem drinking to social media.

A correlation measures how closely two things are related. Values range from -1 to 1, with zero indicating no linear relationship.

The team adjusted its figures for other differences between people, such as gender, education, politics, military service, relationships, parenthood and afterlife belief.

After that, the hope of being remembered was most closely tied to feeling that life has meaning, at 0.45.

Self-esteem and feeling supported by others each came in at 0.35.

People who expected to be remembered also reported fewer mental health problems. The correlations had magnitudes of 0.27 for trouble managing emotions and 0.26 for depression.

Links of that size reflect a tendency across thousands of people, not a rule for any one person.

Parents, and people in long-term relationships, were slightly more likely to expect to be remembered. Age made no difference.

People who expected to be remembered reported fewer signs of all nine problem habits the survey covered.

The associations were weakest for gambling (0.07) and alcohol (0.08). Internet addiction showed the strongest association (0.18), followed closely by pornography and social media.

“Our findings suggest that feeling connected to others, having meaning in life and believing that something of us will remain may go hand in hand with better mental health and fewer problematic addictive behaviors,” the researchers wrote.

The authors offered one possible reason for the overlap. Other animals can show addictive behaviors, they wrote, but don’t normally develop problem substance use in the wild.

Perhaps, they suggested, that’s because addictions are partly tied to problems only humans face, such as knowing they will die.

The two beliefs did overlap. People who expected to be remembered were also somewhat more likely to believe in an afterlife, with a link of 0.37.

Belief in an afterlife, though, was tied mostly to religion. It was most closely linked to belief in God or a higher power, at 0.79.

Afterlife belief showed no significant association with depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress, sleep problems or emotional control.

It was associated with slightly less problem drinking, cannabis use, pornography use and compulsive sexual behavior, and slightly more problem internet use.

Even with afterlife belief taken into account, the hope of being remembered kept its links. The study doesn’t show that this hope adds anything beyond self-esteem, meaning or close relationships, though.

The authors describe it instead as a broad marker tied to several of those supports.

Which comes first is the biggest open question. Feeling remembered could help protect mental health, but depression or addiction could also weaken a person’s sense that their life will matter, and each may affect the other.

The hope of being remembered was also measured with one question. The authors wrote that a single item may be less accurate and less stable than a full set.

Timing may matter too. The authors suggested that wartime may have made the links stronger than they would be in peacetime or in other countries.

During a war, they wrote, threat, national identity and trauma are likely to be on many people’s minds.

Everyone in the survey came from the general public rather than from people being treated for addiction or mental illness.

Whether a stronger sense of legacy can actually ease depression or addiction is still unknown.

Answering that will take experiments and long-term studies that follow the same people over time, the authors wrote, along with fuller measures of legacy and repeat surveys in other countries.

The full study was published in the International Journal of Mental Health and Addiction.
