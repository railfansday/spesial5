Studio Lambert Is Digging Into The Archives For Its Next ‘Traitors’-Sized Hit

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

One of the interesting consequences of Banijay’s merger with All3Media is that Stephen Lambert, who runs The Traitors producer Studio Lambert, has been reunited with his early work.

Before Lambert, a former BBC documentary producer, founded Studio Lambert in 2008, he was Chief Creative Officer at RDF Media, where he produced series such as Wife Swap and Faking It.

As a result of the merger, which values the company at around $8B, that brings together Banijay’s 130 production companies (including RDF Media, which was folded into Remarkable Entertainment in 2024) with All3Media’s 40 shingles, Lambert and his team, which includes Chief Creative Officer Tim Harcourt and U.S. President Jack Burgess, can now dig through his own archives in search of IP to plunder and shows to remake.

Lambert told Deadline, “We’re excited to be part of a very big group. We’re excited about the fact that we have access to a pretty significant rights library, including a whole lot of shows that we did at RDF that we suddenly have access to.” Watch on Deadline

Studio Lambert already has form bringing back and reinvigorating some of its biggest hits such as The Circle, which started life on Channel 4 in the UK, before becoming a Netflix series and is now streaming on Hulu, and Undercover Boss, which ran for 11 seasons on CBS between 2010 and 2022, and is now in production on a new season for the Skydance-owned broadcast network with Drew Carey as host.

It will look to see whether there’s any new life for franchises such as Wife Swap, which was most recently turned into a Real Housewives vehicle, Faking It, the “modern-day Pygmalion” that ran for seven seasons in the UK and was adapted for TLC in the early noughties, Secret Millionaire, which was remade for both Fox and ABC between 2008 and 2012, and youth-skewing reality show Shipwrecked.

“We’re going through it at the moment, trying to work out what we think is the best thing to do,” Lambert said. “There’s a wealth of opportunity and renaissance moments,” added Harcourt.

The trio are aware that they are “a bit of an anomaly” within the broader Banijay group.

“There hasn’t been quite enough clarity about the fact that we’re not in the country-specific setup. We’re not an American company working to the American system and we’re not a British company working to the British system,” said Lambert. “We’re a bit of an anomaly, but an anomaly that comes from the fact that we’re both British and American, and also we’re not fully owned by Banijay, so that makes quite a big difference as well.”

They’re happy to be part of a big group, but they’re aware that “we hang and fall on our ability to come up with ideas and to sell shows and make them very well.”

“It’s a new outlook for [the parent company], and so far everyone’s been very accommodating of our desire to keep working in the way that has been working for us,” added Burgess.

They work closely with Jane Turton, the former All3Media CEO who is now Banijay’s Deputy CEO.

Turton told Deadline, “Working with Stephen and Tim and the team at Studio Lambert is always exciting. The ideas, the shows, the success and, of course, the personalities are all big. Big bold ambitious ideas developed and produced to the very highest quality. It is a pleasure to be part of the Studio Lambert team and especially so now that we are part of the bigger Banijay Entertainment with its many production territories, deep IP catalogue and expertise in distribution, digital and live.”

Unlike many transatlantic companies, Studio Lambert does not geo-block its ideas with key staff being incentivized to work on projects regardless of whether they land in the U.S. or UK.

This was intentional to allow Studio Lambert entry into the “exclusive” club of producers that are trusted to make big shows. Lambert said that it started with The Circle, which was originally developed for Channel 4 in the UK before becoming a hit among younger viewers and being adapted for Netflix.

“Our effort has been on these big, high-budget shows, which we knew that buyers, both in Britain and America, wanted. In this highly competitive market, [buyers] all know that they need these big, noisy tentpole shows. But they also know that if they order an idea and the people who are pitching it haven’t got the experience of making those very expensive shows, then it’s not just going to be a flop, it’s going to be their careers that are a flop for having ordered it. So, it does mean that they have to go with those people that have got the track record,” he added.

The Circle led to The Traitors, which led to Squid Game: The Challenge, which led to Fallout Shelter: The Experiment.

The company is currently in the middle of shooting The Circle for Hulu, which is streaming three episodes a week, hosted by Tinx and starring a mix of civilians and celebrities from Disney shows such as The Secret Lives of Mormon Wives, Vanderpump Villa and Dancing with the Stars.

“It’s exciting, and fast turn as well. We’re making it one day, cutting it and then it’s on air 48 hours later,” said Harcourt.

It was somewhat of a surprise that Hulu was able to land the rights, given that Netflix had premiered two seasons as recently as 2024. Burgess said that Hulu’s unscripted chief Rob Mills “jumped on the opportunity”. “There was a moment where he could he could do it, and they were instantly on board,” he said. “It was competitive as well,” added Harcourt. “There were other people that were that that were interested. It’s amazing that they’ve been able to do it.”

Studio Lambert’s most buzzy show also features civilians and celebrities, albeit in separate versions.

It has produced four seasons of The Traitors featuring civilians for the BBC, as well as an upcoming fifth one in 2027, and two celebrity versions. In the U.S., The Traitors: New Blood, featuring civilians, just wrapped its first season and was renewed for a second season by NBC, as well as four seasons of the celebrity version for Peacock and a fifth one coming next year.

“A format is always best if it works with civilians because then you know it’s like waterproof,” said Harcourt. “The BBC One audience was never going to accept a bunch of celebrities running around in green cloaks so there [had] to be civilians. Then it success we can do it with celebrities.”

“Conversely, in the U.S., having those tried and true reality stars who brought an audience was really helpful to us on Peacock. It was a platform that had Love Island, but didn’t have a ton of inventory there, and so you’re asking different questions of the audience, and it’s worked really nicely going the other way,” added Burgess.

The Traitors has also been renewed for three further seasons, through season eight, by Peacock. “We’ve already interested our grandchildren. They want to know who’s going to be making the show in 2053,” joked Lambert.

The trio paid tribute to Mike Cotton, who is the creative director of the franchise, and exec produces both the U.S. and UK series.

They also believe there’s plenty of road for the format. “It’s still quite early in its life cycle. I’m sure if 27 years ago we were told that this little show Big Brother, that felt innovative, was still on the air everyone would have scoffed. It’s quite surprising how long the shelf life of IP actually is,” said Harcourt.

In terms of big-budget competitions, the company is also passing go at Netflix with an adaptation of iconic board game Monopoly.

Studio Lambert beat out around 50 rivals in a bake-off to land the right to produce the show, which has a $2M prize. It will see 12 contestants competing on a life-sized Monopoly board.

“It’s a big world. We’ve brought the board to life, and we’ve taken the heart of the game, but changed it for television. I think the changes are brilliant,” said Lambert. “It turns out that a board game that every family ends up squabbling over makes for a really exciting reality game because those emotions are definitely brought to life in this game. Luckily, the board was too big for anyone to flip over,” added Burgess.

Before it became one of the go-to producers for large-scale competition series, the feather in Studio Lambert’s cap was Undercover Boss. The show became a “great utility player” for CBS, which initially launched in behind the Super Bowl in 2010 and went on to air over 130 episodes. The Skydance-owned network revealed in September that it was bringing it back and, this time, with a host in Drew Carey.

“Good shows never die, they just rest,” said Lambert.

The first version of the show launched after the banking crisis and Lambert added that anxiety in the workplace and the American economy got CBS to the point where people would want to watch it again.

“This season has been really fun because you realize just how different people’s work lives are now,” added Burgess.

While the company prioritizes big-budget competitions, it has also struck a number of partnerships that allow it to play in different genres. These include launching Inner Drive Entertainment with Jon Barinholtz and Rob Belushi and bringing Vivian Choi and Jill Goslicky’s MindMeld Entertainment into the fold.

MindMeld focuses on reality shows and docuseries with the pair having worked on Bravo’s Below Deck, while Inner Drive is game show focused.

Burgess said that these teams bring different skill sets. MindMeld, for instance, may be able to take advantage of all of the reality stars that come through the Traitors universe. “I’m really excited about the stuff [these companies] have coming through,” he added.

“It wasn’t that we thought we would help them make big shows like ours. It was more that we thought, let’s [have] our main creative effort concentrate on these big shows. But because we want to grow in all different ways, and we like working with great people, it’s a way of having different kinds of shows away from the main sort of thrust of what we’re doing and by having a relationship with them, we would hopefully help them get stuff away,” added Lambert.

The trio are confident that there are “tons of big opportunities in unscripted in the American market at the moment”.

“I think we’re sort of at the end of a cycle of a glut of dramas that started in the pandemic and I think buyers see value in unscripted and the young large audiences that they can they can deliver,” said Harcourt.

Every seven years or so, a new unscripted format emerges and becomes the lightning rod for others, which is exactly what The Traitors did. “What I hope that the takeaway from that will be is that something big and bold can still be a really big broad hit in unscripted. The focus for us isn’t what’s the next iteration of Traitors, it more what is the next big shape of a show that will be popular,” added Burgess.
