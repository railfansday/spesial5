Bob Woodward Interviews Justice Kagan, Or Does Justice Kagan Interview Bob Woodward

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

Kagan sheds some light on her view about leaks, Dobbs, and the Court.

Last month I wrote about an excerpt from Bob Woodward's new book that reveals sources. Most of the sources are deceased, so there is little harm outing them. But Justices Kagan and Breyer are alive and well. Woodward reveals a remarkable conversation with the former. As Woodward would observe, it is less clear that he was interviewing Kagan than Kagan was interviewing him.

"Well, the conclusion of The Brethren was that the center of the court was in control," I said, meaning neither the left nor the right was in charge. "Yes, I prefer it that way," Kagan said. "That's exactly what it should be." That's not the way it is now, I noted. It is not, Kagan confirmed. How did you get such detail in the book? We had time, I said. Of course, it was a different era in which Scott and I were doing the reporting in the late 1970s. I started to tell her about Scott and how he had really discovered the Nixon Tapes because he found Alexander Butterfield. Not letting me off the hook, Kagan asked again, "How did you do it?" Well, we interviewed five sitting justices and 170 law clerks. We got documentation. That's extraordinary, Kagan said. How did you get the documents? The justices must have approved the clerks giving you the documents? I shook my head. Not that we were aware. You make clerks now sign nondisclosure agreements, I noted. "Yes," she said.

Over the years, I've speculated that Justices have authorized their clerks to leak information to the press. The premise of Kagan's questions is that Justices had authorized their clerks to speak with Woodward and Bernstein. But no, the clerks went rogue. If the clerks acted alone in the 1970s, perhaps they did so in 2022 with the Dobbs opinion.

I told her that Brennan and Marshall, the liberal justices on the court, would not help us. Well, who did? Rehnquist and White, I said. Kagan seemed surprised. I recounted the story of calling Justice Lewis Powell at the court one morning and him agreeing to meet. I said when and he said now. "I was in my pajamas, but told him I'd be there in half an hour." You must be fast, Kagan said. I was then, I joked. I was 79 now and slow.

Justice Kagan clerked for Justice Marshall, who apparently never discussed the sourcing for The Brethren.

Is all of this known about The Brethren? Kagan asked. I didn't have an answer so instead I dived into Blackmun's role in Roe v. Wade and how he had drafted the opinion at the Mayo Clinic library. "I thought the leak of the Dobbs decision was a public service," I told her, referencing the decision that had overturned Roe the previous year. I can't say I agree with that, Kagan said. Why do you think it's a public service? Well, the court took away a right that had been there for 50 years. The public needed to know so there could be a debate. Kagan didn't say anything.

I find it odd that Kagan said nothing in response. This is not the sort of statement that could trigger a recusal. She might have said "that debate nearly got my colleague assassinated." But she stayed silent.

I said from reporting more than 50 years on 10 presidents, I thought there was such a concentration of power in the presidency. Too much power.

She nodded. It's why Trump was able to get away with so much, I said. Kagan did not respond. Then I said, there's too much power in the Supreme Court maybe. She noticeably reacted to that, her head held a little higher, her posture stiffening. Her only reply was, "That's interesting."

She mustered a response here, but not to the leak question. Interesting?

"Are you happy on the court?" I asked her. No, I'm not, given the situation, Kagan replied. I was surprised at her candor.

Kagan likely didn't think she would be quoted.

As we parted ways, she smiled warmly and said she respected my work and I said I respected hers. Late that night I recorded a detailed voice memo of my conversation with Kagan for my assistant to transcribe. It is hard to tell who was interviewing who.

On Saturday, October 21, 2023, Elsa and I attended a dinner for the 50th anniversary of the Saturday Night Massacre hosted by the former Watergate special prosecutors at the WilmerHale law firm in downtown Washington. The main speaker was retired Supreme Court justice Stephen Breyer, who had been a junior Watergate prosecutor. Elsa sat next to Breyer at the dinner before switching seats with me. He had served 28 years on the Supreme Court and told her he was "forced out" so someone younger could take his place.

No one forced him out. He has life tenure. Instead, Breyer surrendered to the pressure. So much for judicial independence. Justices Alito and Thomas are often charged with being MAGA partisans, but they exhibited robust independence from political forces.

Woodward offers this anecdote about the Thomas confirmation hearing:

Later Gray told me it took five years for Clarence Thomas to recover. Justice Thomas's wife, Ginni Thomas, a conservative activist, approached me at a conference in Washington one day. She said after the hearings her husband was never really a whole person again. He hardened. Many conservatives, including attorney general for George H. W. Bush and for Donald Trump, Bill Barr, have said the hearings pushed Thomas to the hard right.

I couldn't find any references to other current justices.

I will read the chapters on The Brethren with interest.
