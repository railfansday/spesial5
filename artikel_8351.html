Bear Canisters Now Required on Appalachian Trail Throughout Georgia

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

The US Forest Service has announced that hard-sided bear canisters will be required year-round on the entire section of the Appalachian Trail through Georgia starting January 1, 2027. The order includes the entire AT in Georgia (miles 0 through 78.3), the AT Approach Trail, all national forest lands within a half mile of the footpath, all connecting trails within the half-mile buffer including the Len Foote Hike Inn Trail, and all AT shelters and campsites within the half-mile buffer.

The Appalachian Trail Conservancy (ATC) has recommended hard-sided bear canisters for years, but they were previously required only in an eight-mile stretch between Neel Gap and Jarrard Gap during the spring, and most hikers chose to avoid camping there to avoid carrying a canister.

Under the new order, canisters will now be unavoidable for any backpacker wishing to sleep overnight on the AT in Georgia.

Map showing the areas affected by the new bear canister requirements

The new requirements specify that overnight hikers carry a commercially manufactured canister made from solid, non-pliable material specifically designed to resist entry by bears.

Standard food hangs and soft-sided food bags such as the Ursack do not meet the requirements. All smellable items must be secured in the canister overnight, including food, food containers, trash, and toiletries.

Hikers should note that the canister requirement applies everywhere in the Georgia AT corridor, including at shelters and campsites with existing food storage facilities. The Forest Service says this blanket requirement  “provides a consistent approach to food storage everywhere along the AT in Georgia,” since dispersed campers often opt to stay at sites that lack dedicated food storage infrastructure.

According to the Forest Service, “the Chattahoochee-Oconee National Forest is implementing the requirement in coordination with Appalachian Trail management partners to minimize human-bear interactions along the trail.”

Bears that get access to campers’ food may begin to associate humans and campsites with an easy meal, potentially leading to dangerous encounters. Food-conditioned bears may ultimately have to be euthanized.

The problem is particularly acute in Georgia, and it’s on the rise. “While only 3.5% of the Appalachian Trail is in Georgia, over 12% of the bear encounter reports on the AT have occurred in the state over the last decade,” said Morgan Sommerville, the ATC’s Director of Visitor Use Management. The organization says the problem stems from increased visitation and overnight visitors failing to keep a clean campsite.

The Forest Service cited a 20-year pattern of increasing human-bear conflict along the Georgia AT in its announcement of the new requirements.

“Protecting the Appalachian Trail in Georgia is the heart of our mission, and that includes the wildlife that call this area home,” said Ashley Luke, President of the Georgia Appalachian Trail Club. “The AT in Georgia sees over a half a million visits each year, including thousands of aspiring AT thru-hikers who arrive each Spring. Our volunteers have seen firsthand the impacts of poorly managed food and trash.”

“The ATC has recommended that overnight campers on the Trail carry a bear-resistant food storage container since 2017 because of how adept bears have become at defeating traditional food hangs and other storage methods,” said the ATC’s Sommerville.

The organization today reiterated its recommendation that hikers use canisters along the entire AT, not just in Georgia. “The ATC strongly recommends that AT hikers use bear canisters no matter where they are on the AT, as canisters have shown to be the most effective, efficient, and reliable method of food storage on the Trail,” the ATC said in an update on its website.
