Where to Donate Used Clothes – 16 Places and What Each One Takes

Give used clothes to the group that needs what you have: work clothes to Dress for Success, coats to a One Warm Coat drive, shoes to Soles4Souls, and everyday wear to Goodwill, the Salvation Army, or a local shelter. Check what each one takes before you go, and get a receipt if you’ll deduct the gift.

In 2018, Americans threw out 17 million tons of textiles, the EPA estimates, and 11.3 million tons of it went to landfills.

Where clothes do the most good depends on what they are. Some groups hand them straight to people who need them, like a suit to a woman headed to a job interview or a coat to a child in winter. Others sell what you give and spend the money on their programs, such as job training or addiction recovery.

Both kinds help, but each suits different things. A blazer does the most good worn by a woman starting a new job, while a bag of everyday T-shirts is exactly what a charity’s thrift store sells to pay for its work.

If you’re still deciding what to let go, sort your closet into sell, donate, and recycle piles first. Clothes too worn to give can go to a retailer’s take-back program for recycling, and pieces worth real money may bring more if you sell them online or on consignment.

You can deduct donated clothes only if you itemize rather than take the standard deduction, listing each deduction on Schedule A. The new deduction for non-itemizers that starts with 2026 gifts covers cash only, and the IRS says clothing doesn’t qualify.

IRS Publication 526 requires each item to be in good used condition or better. You deduct each one’s fair market value, the price it would sell for as it is, and for used clothing, Publication 561 points to what people actually pay at thrift and consignment shops. The Salvation Army’s donation value guide gives low and high estimates for common items.

Starting with 2026 gifts, you can deduct only the part of the year’s charitable giving that tops 0.5% of your adjusted gross income, IRS Publication 505 says. That’s the income your return shows before the standard or itemized deduction. With an adjusted gross income of $80,000, the first $400 you give earns no deduction.

Get a receipt with the charity’s name and address, the date and place, and a description of what you gave. At an unattended bin, keep your own written list instead, with each item’s condition on it. A gift worth $250 or more needs the charity’s written acknowledgment, and if you deduct more than $500 in noncash gifts for the year, you also file Form 8283 with your return.

Check the group in the IRS’s Tax Exempt Organization Search to confirm it can receive deductible gifts, though some eligible groups, such as certain churches, don’t appear in it.

Write down each item and its condition before you hand the bag over. That list, kept with the receipt, is what backs up the value you claim.
