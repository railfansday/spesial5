How a bill becomes law in the U.S., from introduction to veto override

How a bill becomes law in the U.S., from a lawmaker's introduction through committee, floor votes and the president's desk, explained in 10 consecutive steps

The U.S. Constitution gives Congress the power to write federal law, but it also sets strict conditions on that power. Article I, Section 7 lays out a narrow path. A bill has to pass both the House of Representatives and the Senate in identical form. Then it goes to the president, who can sign it, veto it or, in some circumstances, let it take effect without a signature.

Understanding how a bill becomes law helps explain much of what appears in the news about Capitol Hill. A headline about a committee hearing, a stalled vote or a veto threat describes one specific point in a longer sequence. Knowing where that point sits makes it easier to judge what could happen next. It also shows why a proposal that sounds popular can still go nowhere.

The process filters legislation at nearly every stage. Many bills are introduced, referred to a committee and never heard from again. Others clear one chamber and stall in the other. Even a bill that passes both chambers can still be vetoed by the president.

Each stage serves a different purpose. Some stages decide which proposals get attention at all. Others shape the text through amendments and negotiation. A few function as checks, giving one chamber or one branch of government the chance to block what another has approved. The House and Senate also run their floors under different rules. The same bill can move quickly in one chamber and slowly in the other.

The 10 slides that follow walk through the steps in the legislative process in the order a typical bill encounters them. The path starts with a lawmaker formally introducing a bill. It runs through committee work, floor debate in each chamber and the effort to settle differences between the House and Senate versions. It ends with the president's decision and, in some cases, a final vote by Congress to override a veto.

That sequence describes the usual route, not the only one. Congressional procedure includes exceptions that can speed a bill up or let it skip a stage. The Senate can pass measures by unanimous consent. House members can use a discharge petition to pull a bill out of committee. Budget reconciliation runs under its own procedures. Each slide explains one stage on its own terms. A reader can start anywhere in the process.

Every federal bill starts when a sitting member of the House or Senate formally introduces it. That lawmaker becomes the bill's sponsor, the person responsible for it at every later stage.

The idea itself can come from almost anywhere. A president can ask Congress for a new program. A business group, a union, a city government or a citizen advocacy organization can draft language and shop it around Capitol Hill. Executive agencies also send proposed legislation to Congress. None of these outside players can put a bill into the system on their own, though. A lawmaker has to agree to sponsor it.

Drafting usually happens before introduction. Members and their staffs can get help from the Office of the Legislative Counsel. Each chamber maintains its own version of that office to turn policy goals into statutory text. The office works on a nonpartisan basis.

The sponsor can also recruit cosponsors, other members who sign on to show support. Lawmakers who join on the day of introduction are known as original cosponsors. Others can add their names later. A long cosponsor list signals backing, but it does not by itself move the bill forward.

In the House, introduction is a physical act. The member places the bill in the hopper, a wooden box near the rostrum in the House chamber. Members can do this only when the House is in session. In the Senate, a senator typically submits the bill to the clerks at the presiding officer's desk. A senator can also introduce it after being recognized on the floor.

Each bill then gets a number. House bills carry the prefix H.R., and Senate bills carry S. Numbering starts over with each new two-year Congress. That means H.R. 1 in one Congress has nothing to do with H.R. 1 in the next. Any bill not enacted by the time a Congress ends expires. A sponsor who wants to keep pushing it must reintroduce it under a new number.

The Constitution places one limit on where a bill can start. Article I, Section 7 requires that all bills for raising revenue originate in the House of Representatives. The Senate can still propose or concur with amendments to those bills, as it can with any other legislation.

After a bill is introduced, it goes to a committee, where lawmakers assigned to its subject area review it first. In the House, the Speaker refers the bill to one or more committees on the advice of the parliamentarian. In the Senate, the presiding officer makes the referral.

The choice of committee depends on subject matter. Each chamber's rules spell out which committee covers which policy areas. In the House, Rule X $TWTR sets out those jurisdictions. In the Senate, Rule XXV does the same job.

A bill dealing with federal courts or criminal law would typically go to a Judiciary Committee, a panel both chambers have. A House bill on telecommunications or public health might go to the Energy and Commerce Committee. A tax measure in the Senate would usually land at the Finance Committee.

The parliamentarian plays a central role in those decisions. Each chamber has its own parliamentarian, a nonpartisan official who advises on rules and precedents. The parliamentarian's office reviews the text of a new bill and recommends where it belongs. In the Senate, the presiding officer generally follows that recommendation.

A single bill can cover several policy areas at once. The House handles that through multiple referral, which sends the bill to more than one committee. The Speaker usually designates a primary committee and can set time limits for the others. The Senate typically sends a bill to the one committee whose jurisdiction covers most of its subject matter. Senate Rule XIV also lets a senator skip referral and place a bill directly on the Senate calendar.

Referral shapes what happens next. A committee chair largely controls the panel's agenda. That means the chair has wide say over whether a bill receives any attention.

Most bills go no further than committee. A committee can simply take no action on a measure, and that inaction alone can end its chances. A bill that is still pending when a two-year Congress ends expires. Its sponsor can reintroduce it in the next Congress. The new version receives a new bill number and starts over with a fresh committee referral.

A committee often hands a newly referred bill to one of its subcommittees for closer study. Lawmakers there may hold hearings with witnesses, then meet for a markup session where they propose, debate and vote on amendments to the bill's actual wording.

Full committees cover broad subject areas, and subcommittees split that work into smaller pieces. A House panel such as Energy and Commerce handles policy ranging from health care to telecommunications. It divides that territory among smaller groups of members. Each subcommittee works on a narrower slice of policy and builds expertise in it.

The subcommittee chair has wide control over the schedule. The chair decides whether to hold a hearing, which bills get attention and in what order they move. Members of the minority party can request witnesses and offer amendments, but the majority sets the agenda.

Hearings are the fact-finding stage. Witnesses can include officials from federal agencies, academic specialists, industry representatives, advocacy groups and people directly affected by a proposal. Witnesses usually submit written testimony in advance and then answer questions from members. The hearing record becomes part of the bill's paper trail. Hearings are not required, and some bills go straight to markup without one.

Markup is where the text itself changes. The session starts from a base text. That text may be the bill as introduced, or a substitute version offered by the chair. Members then offer amendments section by section. Each amendment is debated and decided by a vote of the members present. Those votes can be taken by voice or recorded by name.

Amendments range from small fixes, such as changing a word or a date, to rewrites of entire sections. A markup can also adopt an amendment in the nature of a substitute. That device replaces the bill's whole text with a new version in a single vote.

When a subcommittee finishes its markup, it can vote to forward the bill to the full committee for further action. Practice varies across the House and Senate. Some committees skip the subcommittee step entirely and hold hearings and markup at the full-committee level instead.

A bill typically reaches the House or Senate floor only after the full committee votes to report it. That vote usually comes with a written committee report explaining what the bill does and why. A bill the committee never reports usually dies there.

The reporting vote follows markup. Committee members decide by majority vote whether to send the measure to the full chamber. They can attach the amendments adopted during markup. A committee may also bundle those changes into a single amendment in the nature of a substitute. That amendment replaces the original text with a rewritten version.

A committee can report a bill favorably, unfavorably or without recommendation. A favorable report is the common outcome.

The committee chair controls much of the committee's schedule. A chair who does not take up a bill can keep it from ever reaching a reporting vote.

The committee report is a public document with its own number, labeled H. Rept. in the House and S. Rept. in the Senate. It typically describes the bill's purpose, summarizes its provisions and lays out the changes it would make to existing law. Members who disagree with the majority can add minority or additional views. In the House, reports generally include a cost estimate from the Congressional Budget Office. Courts and federal agencies sometimes consult these reports later to understand what lawmakers intended.

Once reported, a bill goes onto a calendar of pending business. The House uses the Union Calendar for most bills that raise or spend money and the House Calendar for many others. The Senate places reported bills on its Calendar of Business.

House members have one formal way around a committee that will not act. A discharge petition can pull a bill out of committee once 218 members sign it, a majority of the full 435-seat House. The petition can be filed only after the bill has sat in committee for 30 legislative days. Signatures on discharge petitions have been public since 1993.

A bill that stays in committee does not wait indefinitely. Any measure not enacted by the end of a two-year Congress expires. Its supporters must reintroduce it in the next Congress for it to be considered again.

Before most major bills reach the House floor, the House Rules Committee writes a special rule that decides how long members can debate and whether they can offer amendments. The full House must vote to adopt that rule before debate on the bill begins.

This step has no counterpart in the Senate. It places a formal gatekeeper between committee approval and a floor vote in the House.

A special rule takes the form of a House resolution, numbered with the prefix H.Res. It typically sets a fixed block of debate time and divides that time between the bill's supporters and opponents. It can also waive procedural objections, known as points of order, that might otherwise stall the measure.

The rule's amendment terms come in three main forms. An open rule lets any member offer an amendment that is germane to the bill. A structured rule allows only the amendments the Rules Committee lists in its report. A closed rule bars floor amendments entirely, so the House votes on the bill largely as written.

Members who want changes usually submit their proposed amendments to the Rules Committee in advance. The committee then decides which ones, if any, will reach the floor. That decision determines which changes the full House can consider at all.

The majority party's leadership chooses the majority's members of the committee. That arrangement gives the Speaker significant influence over which bills move and on what terms. Votes to adopt a rule usually break along party lines. If a rule fails, the bill cannot come up under those terms, and debate on it does not begin.

The House typically debates the rule itself for one hour before voting on it.

Some bills skip the Rules Committee altogether. Under a procedure called suspension of the rules, the House limits debate to 40 minutes and permits no floor amendments. Passage under suspension requires a two-thirds vote of members present and voting.

A bill passes the House when a simple majority of members present and voting supports it, as long as a quorum is on hand. Floor debate follows the terms of the rule the chamber adopted for that bill. A winning vote sends the measure to the Senate.

The Constitution defines a House quorum as a majority of members. With all 435 seats filled, that is 218. Much of the amending work happens in the Committee of the Whole House on the state of the Union. This procedural device lets the full membership act with a quorum of 100.

Debate time is usually split between two floor managers, one from the majority and one from the minority. They are often leaders of the committee that handled the bill. Each manager yields minutes to colleagues who want to speak. The rule controls how long this lasts and which amendments, if any, members may offer.

Votes can be taken by voice, by division or by recorded vote. The Constitution lets one-fifth of members present demand that the yeas and nays be entered in the journal. Recorded votes have used an electronic system since 1973. Members insert voting cards into stations on the floor. The first vote in a series stays open for at least 15 minutes.

Before final passage, the minority can offer a motion to recommit. If adopted, it sends the bill back to committee. A tie vote defeats the question. Passage requires more yes votes than no votes.

Not every bill travels this route. Measures considered under suspension of the rules skip a special rule. They allow no floor amendments and get 40 minutes of debate. In exchange, passage requires two-thirds of members present and voting.

Once the House approves a bill, clerks prepare an engrossed copy that reflects every adopted amendment. That certified text goes to the Senate as an act of the House. A bill that began in the Senate reaches the House only after Senate passage. It then moves through these same House steps.

A bill on the Senate floor can pass with a simple majority, but ending debate on it usually takes 60 votes. As a result, the cloture vote, not the final passage vote, is often the harder threshold for legislation to clear in the U.S. Senate.

Senate debate is generally open-ended. Unless senators agree to limits, a bill can stay on the floor as long as members keep talking or keep procedural objections alive. Using that freedom to delay or block a final vote is called a filibuster.

Limits on debate often come through unanimous consent agreements. These agreements can set how long debate runs and which amendments receive votes. A single senator's objection can keep such an agreement from taking effect.

When consent fails, supporters of a bill turn to cloture, the formal process for ending debate under Senate Rule XXII. A cloture motion must carry the signatures of 16 senators. The vote on it takes place one hour after the Senate convenes on the second day after the motion is filed.

Invoking cloture on most legislation requires three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn. With all 100 seats filled, that equals 60 votes. The threshold is measured against sitting senators rather than those present. An absent senator therefore offers no help to the side trying to reach 60.

A successful cloture vote does not end consideration on the spot. Rule XXII allows up to 30 more hours of consideration before the final vote. Passage itself then needs only a simple majority of senators voting. If that vote ties, the vice president, serving as president of the Senate, can cast the deciding vote under Article I, Section 3 of the Constitution.

The cloture rule dates to 1917. For decades, it required two-thirds of senators present and voting. In 1975, the Senate replaced that standard with the current three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn.

Budget reconciliation bills are an exception to this process. They move under limited debate time and cannot be filibustered. They therefore do not need 60 votes to reach a final vote.

Both chambers of Congress must pass the exact same text before a bill can go to the president. When the House and Senate approve different versions, lawmakers have three ways to close the gap. One chamber can accept the other's text, the chambers can trade amendments, or a conference committee can negotiate a compromise.

Acceptance is the most direct route. If one chamber is willing to take the other's version as written, it passes that text. The two chambers are then in agreement, and no further negotiation is needed.

The second route is an exchange of amendments. One chamber amends the other's bill and sends it back. The receiving chamber can agree, propose its own amendment to that amendment, or insist on its position. This back-and-forth is informally known as "ping-pong." It lets leaders settle differences without appointing a formal negotiating panel.

The third route is a conference committee. This is a temporary panel made up of members from both the House and the Senate. The Speaker names the House conferees, and the Senate's presiding officer appoints the Senate conferees. Conferees usually come from the committees that handled the bill.

Conferees from each chamber vote as a separate delegation. A compromise moves forward only when a majority of the House conferees and a majority of the Senate conferees sign the conference report. That report contains the agreed text. A joint explanatory statement accompanies it and describes how the negotiators resolved each difference.

Both chambers then vote on the conference report. Neither the House nor the Senate can amend it on the floor. Members accept or reject it as a whole. If either chamber votes it down, the disagreement stays open. The bill cannot go to the president in that form.

Once the two chambers agree on identical text by any of these routes, the chamber where the bill started prepares a final official copy. That copy is called the enrolled bill. The Speaker of the House and the presiding officer of the Senate sign it. Only then is the measure ready to be sent to the White House.

Once Congress presents a bill, the president has 10 days, Sundays excepted, to act on it. A signature makes the bill law, a veto sends it back, and inaction can produce either outcome depending on whether Congress is still in session.

That deadline comes from Article I, Section 7 of the U.S. Constitution. The clock starts when the bill is presented to the president, not when the final floor vote takes place. Before presentment, the identical text approved by both chambers is prepared as an enrolled bill. The Speaker of the House and the presiding officer of the Senate sign it before it goes to the White House.

Signing is the most direct route. With the president's signature, the measure becomes law. Presidents sometimes sign major legislation at public ceremonies. The legal effect comes from the signature itself, not the event around it.

A regular veto takes a different path. The president returns the bill to the chamber where it originated, along with the president's objections. The Constitution directs that chamber to enter those objections in its journal and reconsider the measure. The president acts on the bill as a whole and cannot approve some provisions while rejecting others.

A president can also decline to act. If 10 days pass, Sundays excepted, while Congress is in session, the bill becomes law without a signature. In that situation, the president's silence counts as acceptance rather than rejection.

The outcome changes when Congress has adjourned. If lawmakers have left and the president does not sign within the window, the bill does not become law. This is called a pocket veto. The Constitution's text describes it as a case in which Congress, by its adjournment, prevents the bill's return.

The difference between the two kinds of veto shapes what happens next. A regular veto puts the bill and the president's objections back in front of Congress, which can try to reverse the decision. A pocket veto leaves no returned bill for lawmakers to reconsider. Supporters of a measure blocked this way have to introduce it again as a new bill and restart the process.

A presidential veto does not have to be the final word on a bill. Congress can override it if two-thirds of each chamber votes to pass the bill again. That is a far higher bar than the simple majority needed the first time.

The Constitution spells out the override procedure. The vetoed bill returns to the chamber where it started, along with the president's objections. That chamber records the objections in its journal and reconsiders the bill. If two-thirds agree to pass it, the bill moves to the other chamber. The second chamber must also reach two-thirds.

The Constitution requires override votes to be taken by yeas and nays. Each member's name and vote are entered in that chamber's journal. Every override attempt is therefore a recorded vote. There is no option for an anonymous voice vote.

If every seat is filled and every member votes, an override takes 290 of 435 House members and 67 of 100 senators. The threshold is measured against members present and voting, provided a quorum is present. The exact number can shift with absences and vacancies. The Supreme Court upheld that reading in Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. Kansas.

If the first chamber falls short, the veto stands. The second chamber then has nothing to vote on. Overrides are rare. A bill that clears both override votes becomes law over the president's objections.

Once a bill is enacted, whether by signature or by override, it receives a public law number. The first part of that number identifies the Congress that passed it. The second part reflects the order in which it was enacted during that Congress.

The new law is first issued as a slip law, a standalone printed copy of the single measure. It is later compiled in the Statutes at Large, the permanent chronological record of the laws Congress passes. Laws of general and permanent effect are then folded into the U.S. Code, which arranges federal law by subject. The Office of the Law Revision Counsel of the House handles that codification.
