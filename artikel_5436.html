Radian Aerospace plans to make its own composite materials for space plane (and for other companies, too)

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

by Alan Boyle on Nov 17, 2026 at 4:00 amNovember 17, 2026 at 4:11 am

On the way to building a space plane, Seattle-based Radian Aerospace is planning a factory to make the advanced materials it’ll need — and it plans to sell those materials to other companies as well.

Radian CEO Richard Humphrey said he envisions the factory, known as Foundry 1, as a state-of-the-art manufacturing facility with a footprint spanning “just under 100,000 square feet.” Six to 12 sites around the country — including sites in the Pacific Northwest — are currently under consideration, with late 2027 as the target for starting production, he said.

Foundry 1 is meant to produce ceramic matrix composites, or CMCs — not only for Radian’s space plane, but also for other applications ranging from gas-turbine components to high-performance braking systems.

“Each of these industries has a bottleneck that’s being driven by a lack of an industrial base to really build these next-gen materials,” Humphrey told GeekWire.

Humphrey and his teammates ran into that bottleneck while developing Radian One, a reusable rocket-powered plane that’s meant to transport cargo and crew to and from low Earth orbit. Securing a reliable supply chain for the required materials proved so challenging that Radian decided to produce its own.

“Defense and space programs need these materials at costs and production rates that today’s supply base cannot deliver,” Humphrey said today in a news release. “Foundry 1 unlocks a substantial commercial opportunity for Radian and a more direct path to revenue. It positions us to supply customers across these industries while advancing the technology that will make Radian One possible.”

Ceramic matrix composites embed reinforcing fibers, such as carbon or silicon carbide, within a lightweight ceramic material. The ceramic provides resistance to extreme heat, while the fibers help the material carry loads and resist damage from cracking.

Carbon-fiber composites have been around for decades, but turning them into products is typically labor-intensive and expensive. To drive down the cost while improving quality, Radian is developing an automated system for producing CMCs. The company says it’s targeting a 50% reduction in CMC manufacturing costs by cutting manual labor, shortening processing cycles and minimizing waste.

Artificial intelligence will play a role as well, Humphrey said. “We see an AI-native base where we can actually learn from each individual piece while digital-twinning them, so each piece ends up with a digital passport,” he said. “We know exactly where every fiber is, the angle of each fiber to the fiber next to it, and then we do in-process CT screening.”

Greg Scofield, an adviser to Radian and former director of the Purdue Hypersonics Laboratory, said Radian is taking an integrated approach to the challenges surrounding CMC production. “Designing fiber placement, thermal processing and inspection together creates an opportunity to improve cost and quality throughout the production line, with benefits for customers across defense and space,” he said.

The payoff could be huge: A report from Polaris Market Research suggests the global market for advanced composites could rise to $126 billion by 2034.

In partnership with NASA’s Glenn Research Center, Radian has already tested a patent-pending composite material called Dur-E-Therm at temperatures exceeding 2,700 degrees Fahrenheit (1,480 degrees Celsius) under conditions that simulate hypersonic flight.

Radian’s next steps are to select the site for Foundry 1 and begin construction while continuing CMC development at partner facilities. That’s a tall order for a small company.

Humphrey said the company currently has about 20 employees working at locations including the University of Washington’s Advanced Composites Center. Back in 2022, Radian announced a $27.5 million funding round that brought total investment to $32 million at the time. Humphrey said Radian has raised additional funding since then, but he wasn’t ready to share updated figures.

So, how much will Foundry 1 cost? “We’re not quite ready to put a pin in the number,” Humphrey said. “It’s fair to say it’s sub — not a lot less, but it’s sub $100 million in terms of total capital to stand this up for what we’re doing.”

He acknowledged that following through on the plan for Foundry 1 will require raising more capital. “It is part of what we do, and we have a really super group of investors that have been behind us for a long time,” he said.

Humphrey said the Radian One space plane remains the focus of the company’s long-term vision — but for now, the company is devoting all of its resources and capital to Foundry 1.

“It’s the bottleneck to Radian One being successful,” he said. “We think that by applying all of our attention to that, it serves the broader market but also serves advancing Radian One. Both of those are hard things, so we’re going to focus on one hard thing at a time.”

Is Radian becoming an advanced materials and aerospace company? Humphrey said that’s “a good way to think about it,” but he emphasized that aerospace still gets top billing.

“I’d rather be launching today than just be building CMCs,” he said. “But building CMCs is what gets us to flying tomorrow. By advancing materials, it’s our single best way to be ready for Space 3.0, if you want to call it that. … At times we have to be adaptable to really move the project forward, and sometimes it goes a little sideways before you go forward. It’s just the way I’ve come to accept it.”
