3D-printed reefs and 6 other technologies trying to save the ocean

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Free daily briefing on global business news.

3D-printed reefs open a selection of seven technologies, from farm-grown coral to satellite fishing maps, that scientists and nonprofits use to restore, clean and monitor the ocean

The ocean's problems are physical, and many of them can be measured. Reefs lose the hard structure that young coral needs to take hold. Plastic drifts into open water and collects in remote gyres. Fishing fleets work far from shore, often beyond easy view of regulators. Water temperature and chemistry shift in ways that scientists track with instruments spread across entire ocean basins.

Engineers, marine biologists and nonprofits have built tools aimed at each of those problems. Some work at the scale of a single reef patch. Others operate across the whole planet. 3D-printed reefs are one example at the small end. They are structures printed from clay or similar materials and shaped to give coral larvae a surface to settle on. They belong to a broader field of coral reef restoration technology that also draws on laboratory and farming methods.

This piece covers seven of those tools. It is a selection, not a ranking, and it does not attempt to list every technology working in the ocean. The seven fall into three broad groups.

The first group restores reefs directly. It includes printed terracotta tiles and a coral-cutting technique called microfragmentation, which is paired with land-based coral farms. It also covers coral IVF, which raises larvae from spawn, and underwater speakers that play the sound of a healthy reef. Each method targets a different stage of reef recovery, from giving coral a place to grow to drawing fish back to a damaged site.

The second group deals with pollution. The Ocean Cleanup, a nonprofit based in the Netherlands, builds floating systems that gather plastic at sea. It also builds river devices that catch plastic before it reaches open water.

The third group watches the ocean rather than repairing it. This branch of ocean conservation tech relies on data. Global Fishing Watch turns ship-tracking signals into maps of fishing activity. The Argo program uses robotic floats to record temperature and salinity below the surface.

None of these tools solves an ocean problem on its own. Each addresses one piece of a larger issue, and several have clear limits. A reef sound study measured fish, not coral. A tracking system cannot see a vessel that turns its transmitter off. The slides that follow describe those limits alongside the methods themselves.

3D-printed reef tiles made of terracotta give young corals a hard, textured surface to attach to on reefs that have lost their natural structure. The aim is to rebuild the physical base a reef needs before new coral can grow on it.

Corals begin life as tiny free-swimming larvae. To survive, a larva has to find a firm surface, attach to it and turn into a polyp. That polyp then starts laying down the limestone skeleton that, over many years, becomes part of a reef. When a damaged reef breaks into loose rubble or is smothered by sediment, larvae have fewer places to take hold.

Printed tiles are designed to fill that gap. A stable tile does not shift with currents the way rubble does. A larva that settles on it is less likely to be knocked loose or buried. Texture matters too. Ridges, grooves and small hollows give larvae more surface area and more sheltered spots than a flat slab would.

That is where 3D printing helps. A printer builds a structure layer by layer from a digital design. This makes it possible to produce irregular, branching or deeply grooved shapes. Casting those shapes in a simple mold would be difficult. The digital design can also be changed from one tile to the next rather than repeating a single form.

Material is the other half of the design. Terracotta is clay that has been fired at high temperature. Potters and builders have used it for flowerpots and roof tiles for thousands of years. It does not release many chemicals into seawater. That trait matters for anything meant to sit on the seabed for years.

Archireef, a group based in Hong Kong, prints terracotta tiles for coral restoration. Reef Design Lab in Australia is another group that produces 3D-printed reef structures.

Printed reef tiles address one specific problem: the lack of a stable surface. They do not by themselves supply coral, cool the water or reduce pollution.

Microfragmentation cuts coral into very small pieces that regrow faster than larger fragments do. David Vaughan developed the technique at Mote Marine Laboratory in Florida.

That speed matters. Many of the corals that build a reef's framework, including brain corals and boulder corals, grow slowly in the wild. A large colony can represent decades of growth. Once disease or bleaching kills one, natural replacement takes a long time.

Older coral gardening methods mostly work with fast-growing branching species, snapping off branches and growing them in nurseries. Microfragmentation takes the cut much further. Pieces small enough to sit on a fingertip are cut from a parent colony.

Fragments taken from the same parent colony share its genetics. When they are placed close together on a reef or a hard surface, they can grow toward one another and fuse. The result is a single larger colony that covers a patch of reef sooner than a single fragment could.

Coral Vita runs land-based coral farms in the Bahamas. Its corals grow in tanks on shore rather than in nurseries anchored on the seafloor. They are raised for replanting onto reefs.

A land-based setting gives operators control over conditions they cannot manage in open water. Staff can adjust water temperature, light and water quality in the tanks. Young corals are also kept away from storms, predators and the marine heat waves that trigger bleaching.

Farm-grown corals still have to reach the reef. Divers carry them out and attach them to the reef surface, where they continue to grow. Cutting one parent colony into many small pieces also yields more plantable units than cutting it into a few large ones.

The method has limits. Microfragmentation increases how much coral can be grown and how fast. It does not change the warming water, disease or pollution that damaged the reef in the first place. Replanted corals face those same conditions once they leave the farm.

Coral IVF is a restoration method that collects the eggs and sperm corals release during spawning, raises the resulting larvae and then releases them onto damaged reef. Peter Harrison of Southern Cross University in Australia led work developing the approach on the Great Barrier Reef.

The method relies on how many reef-building corals reproduce. Many species are broadcast spawners. They release eggs and sperm into the open water, where fertilization takes place outside the parent colony. On the Great Barrier Reef, large numbers of corals spawn within a short window each year. The timing is linked in part to the lunar cycle.

In the wild, most of that output never becomes a new colony. Currents carry eggs and larvae away from the reef. Fish and other animals eat them in large numbers. The larvae that survive must then find a suitable patch of hard surface before they can settle and start building a skeleton.

Harrison's approach steps in at that vulnerable stage. Teams gather spawn from healthy coral and raise the developing larvae until they are ready to settle. They then deliver the larvae directly onto damaged reef areas. The aim is to put far more larvae onto degraded patches than currents alone would bring there.

The first trials on the Great Barrier Reef took place in the mid-2010s. The name coral IVF is shorthand for the fertilization and rearing steps. It borrows from in vitro fertilization, the human fertility treatment. The corals, however, still spawn on their own natural schedule.

Larval restoration differs from fragment-based methods in one basic way. A fragment is a clone of its parent colony and shares the same genes. Larvae come from sexual reproduction, which mixes genes from different parents. A reef reseeded with larvae can carry more genetic variety than one rebuilt from a few cloned colonies. That variety is the raw material a population draws on to adapt over generations.

The method also has limits set by coral biology. Collection is tied to the short annual spawning window. The supply of larvae is seasonal as a result. Larvae that reach a reef still face predators and competition for space after they settle. Newly settled corals are tiny and take years to grow into colonies that add visible structure to a reef.

Playing recordings of a healthy reef through underwater loudspeakers can draw more fish to damaged coral. A study led by Tim Gordon of the University of Exeter, published in Nature Communications in 2019, tested the idea on degraded patches of the Great Barrier Reef.

A healthy coral reef is a noisy place. Snapping shrimp produce a constant crackle. Many reef fish grunt, chirp and drum to communicate with one another. When coral dies and the animals living on it disappear, that soundscape becomes quieter and less varied.

Sound matters because many reef fish spend their earliest weeks in open water. When young fish return to settle, they use sound, along with other cues, to locate a reef. A degraded reef sends out a weaker acoustic signal than a thriving one.

The Exeter-led team used a method known as acoustic enrichment. Researchers placed loudspeakers on patches of degraded reef and played recordings made on healthy reefs. They then compared those patches with similar ones that had no playback. The patches with reef sound attracted more fish than the silent patches.

The approach does not build new structures or plant coral. Its equipment, mainly an underwater speaker, can be moved between sites. That sets it apart from restoration methods that work on the reef's physical framework or on coral itself.

Fish do work on a reef that coral depends on. Grazing species such as parrotfish and surgeonfish eat algae that can otherwise smother coral surfaces. Their return is one sign that a damaged site still supports a functioning reef community.

The study has a clear limit. It measured fish, not coral. Its results do not show that reef sound helps coral recover. Playback acts on the animal community around a reef. Any link to coral regrowth would need separate evidence from studies designed to measure it.

The Ocean Cleanup is a nonprofit based in the Netherlands. It builds equipment that removes plastic from the open ocean and catches plastic in rivers upstream. Boyan Slat founded the organization in 2013.

Its work runs on two tracks. The first deals with plastic that is already at sea. The second tries to cut off the supply.

At sea, the main target is the Great Pacific Garbage Patch. This is a zone of the North Pacific, between Hawaii and California, where floating debris collects. Circular currents in the region, known as the North Pacific Subtropical Gyre, draw plastic inward and hold it there for years.

The name suggests a solid island of trash, but the patch is not one. Most of the debris is scattered across a wide stretch of open water. Some pieces are large enough to see from a boat, such as discarded fishing nets. Much of the rest is small fragments. That spread means any collection system has to sweep a large area to gather a meaningful amount.

The Ocean Cleanup builds floating systems designed to gather this debris. The collected plastic is then hauled out of the water and brought ashore. Sunlight and wave action break floating plastic into smaller pieces over time. Once plastic degrades into microplastics, it becomes far harder to recover.

The second track focuses on rivers. Rivers carry plastic from towns and cities down to the coast. That makes them one of the main routes by which waste reaches the ocean. The Ocean Cleanup builds river-based devices it calls Interceptors. They are placed in rivers to stop plastic before it flows out to sea.

Ocean collection deals with plastic that has already accumulated and can persist at sea for decades. River interception deals with the flow of new plastic. Without that second step, a cleared patch would keep receiving fresh debris from upstream.

The approach also has limits. These systems collect plastic that is already in the water or moving toward it. They do not reduce how much plastic is produced or thrown away in the first place. That depends on manufacturing, packaging and waste management on land.

Global Fishing Watch is an online platform that maps fishing activity around the world using satellite tracking data. It launched in 2016 as a partnership between the ocean conservation group Oceana, the nonprofit SkyTruth and Google $GOOGL.

Its main data source is the Automatic Identification System, known as AIS. Ships broadcast AIS messages by radio. Each message carries details such as the vessel's identity, position, speed and heading. The system was designed so ships could see one another and avoid collisions. Satellites can also pick up these signals. Satellite reception gives a safety tool a second use as a running record of where vessels travel.

Global Fishing Watch takes that stream of positions and looks for signs of fishing within it. A boat heading in a straight line toward port moves differently from one working a fishing ground. By reading those movement patterns, the platform estimates where and when vessels appear to be fishing. It does more than show where boats happen to be.

The result is a public map. Anyone with an internet connection can view it, including researchers, journalists, fisheries managers and government agencies. Fishing on the open ocean often happens far from any observer. The high seas lie beyond the jurisdiction of any single country, and few outsiders see what takes place there.

The map has practical uses. It can show whether boats appear inside a marine protected area where fishing is restricted. It can show fishing activity close to a country's maritime boundary. It can also reveal how fishing effort shifts between regions and across seasons.

The approach has a built-in limit. Global Fishing Watch can only map vessels whose AIS signals it receives. A vessel that switches off its transmitter drops out of the AIS record for as long as the transmitter stays off. A boat fishing where it should not can stay off the map this way.

Coverage also varies by vessel size. Not every fishing boat is required to carry AIS, and many small boats do not. The map shows the activity of vessels that broadcast their position, not the full picture of fishing at sea.

Argo is an international program of about 4,000 robotic floats that drift through the world's oceans. Each float records temperature and salinity as it sinks and rises, building a running picture of conditions below the surface.

The array has been in place since around 2000. Before it existed, much of what scientists knew about the subsurface ocean came from instruments lowered over the side of research ships. Those cruises followed set routes. They could not cover remote waters in every season. Argo floats fill those gaps by spreading across the open ocean and reporting on their own schedule.

A float has no propeller. It changes depth by adjusting its buoyancy, pumping oil between an internal reservoir and an external bladder. Most of its time is spent drifting with the currents at depth. On a typical cycle of about 10 days, a float descends to roughly 2,000 meters. It then rises to the surface while its sensors take readings. At the surface, it sends its data by satellite before sinking again.

Temperature and salinity together set the density of seawater. Differences in density help drive the large-scale currents that move heat around the planet. Tracking both measurements over time shows where the ocean is warming and where it is turning fresher or saltier. It also shows how those changes shift circulation.

The data are shared freely with researchers and forecasting agencies. Weather and seasonal forecasts draw on them, as do climate models and studies of sea level rise. Warmer water expands. Heat stored in the ocean therefore contributes directly to rising seas.

Newer floats carry sensors for ocean chemistry, including dissolved oxygen. These biogeochemical floats can also measure properties such as pH and nitrate. Oxygen readings help researchers track low-oxygen zones that affect fish and other marine life.

Argo's role is measurement rather than repair. The long-term record from the floats gives researchers a baseline for tracking ocean warming, acidification and oxygen loss.
