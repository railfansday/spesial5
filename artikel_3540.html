Dua Lipa Talks About Her Book Club, and Her Favorite Authors Join Her for a Photo

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

It’s the 41st week of this year, and Dua Lipa has already read 22 books. Next up is Sufiyaan Salam’s novel, Wimmy Road Boyz, a coming of age story about a British Pakistani friend group. But fresh on her mind is Careless People: A Cautionary Tale of Power, Greed, and Lost Idealism by Sarah Wynn-Williams, which Lipa has just finished, logging the accomplishment in the Notes app on her orange iPhone.

“Do you know about it?” she asks me, eager to recommend the whistleblower’s memoir. “She’s getting censored because she started working at Facebook very early on and she dishes it all out.”

PAGE TURNERS Lipa at Albertine Books in October with seven of her hand-selected Service95 book club monthly reads. Shirt by Gucci; jewelry by Bulgari.

It should come as no surprise that Lipa, the internet’s favorite hardworking “vacanza queen,” gets most of her reading done while traveling. “I can read in cars. I don’t get sick. It’s kind of a flex,” she says. When we meet in New York in early October, Lipa has just returned from a trip to Madrid, where she performed with Shakira, doing what she humbly refers to as her “day job,” and Paris, for a Chanel show. No matter the destination, for work or play, Lipa will always toss a book—no e-readers—in her bag. “I love the feeling of a book. I love when all the pages get all fucked up a little bit,” she says.

Dressed in an off-duty uniform of blue jeans, a black Chanel sweater, leather boots, and a black Gucci purse big enough to stash a hardcover or two, Lipa, the 31-year-old British Albanian singer who has emerged as a leading literacy advocate, is gearing up for the London Literature Festival, which she guest-curated this year.

Her reading habit is impressive. Not just because she is a jet-set Grammy Award–winning pop star and half of a newlywed power couple (she and husband Callum Turner famously bonded over finishing the same chapter of Hernan Diaz’s novel, Trust) whose Instagram suggests that she exists in a perpetual state of glamour, but because, according to a recent statistic, more than half of American adults read approximately zero books in a year. “Sad,” Lipa says when I give her this stat.

Since 2021, Lipa has been using her spare time to run Service95—a website, newsletter, book club, and podcast—described as a “cultural concierge.” Through the platform, Lipa offers bespoke recommendations and publishes writing on a wide range of topics from style, travel, and wellness to politics and activism. It’s also where, since Service95’s inception, she has hosted in-depth conversations with authors from around the world and across genres. With dozens of podcast episodes under her belt so far, she’s quickly learned what it’s like to be on the other side of an interview. “I’ve done so many interviews myself and I’ve had to answer the same question over and over again. I’m quite sensitive to the fact that I don’t want to put the author in the same position of having to repeat themselves,” she says. “It’s one of my favorite things that I do. I feel like I get so much out of the conversations.”

Today, at the Villa Albertine, a French cultural institution on the Upper East Side, she’s brought her bookshelf to life, gathering seven of her favorite writers—memoirists, novelists, journalists, a National Book Award and Booker Prize winner—for a class photo. Standing together in one room, beneath a mural of a constellation, the stars align: Douglas Stuart, Brit Bennett, Patti Smith, Roxane Gay, Jennifer Clement, Patrick Radden Keefe, Min Jin Lee, and Lipa, all looking like the English department of your wildest dreams. Sitting next to Smith, the author of Just Kids, Lipa, one of the biggest celebrities in the world, appears to be starstruck. In a rare role reversal, Lipa is the fangirl, whipping out her phone to commemorate the occasion with a group selfie.

Here, her impact in the literary world is undeniable—and so is her range.

Between takes, Smith inquires about how many languages Lipa speaks—English, Albanian, some Spanish, and she’s learning French and Italian, she says. The Just Kids author quips that she only knows how to ask where the “biblioteca” is. “That’s kind of all you need,” jokes Keefe, author of Say Nothing, who tells me he often encounters young readers who were introduced to his work via Service95.

READ THE ROOM Dua Lipa and authors take a selfie at Albertine Books. Lipa’s shoes by Prada; jewelry by Bulgari.

Watching all of these literary titans in conversation with each other, it’s impressive to see them all embrace Lipa as an advocate of their work. “I’m sure that there are lots of other things she could be doing with her time that would be far more profitable than championing books,” says Bennett, author of The Vanishing Half. “It’s amazing that she does this out of a genuine love for reading.”

“It’s fun work. It’s good work,” insists Lipa, who is happy to disprove naysayers who think she just “holidays all the time.” “I’m having a good time, and I get all my work done, and I do what I want to do, and then I go on holiday, and I’m happy with that.” It’s what keeps her motivated: “Sometimes being underestimated gives me extra fire in my belly.”

In 2023, when Lipa launched her book club, which has racked up nearly 1 million Instagram followers, she joined the likes of Oprah Winfrey, Reese Witherspoon, Kaia Gerber, Dakota Johnson, Emma Roberts, and Jenna Bush Hager. But the saturation of the celebrity book club market gave her no hesitation. “If people feel a little bit inclined to just go and pick up a book and read something, we’re all doing our job,” she says. “I don’t care if someone’s going to go to this book club or the next one. As long as they’re reading, I’m happy.”

But Clement, author of Widow Basquiat, considers Service95 to be in a league of its own. “It is not simply curating individual titles; it is curating perspective,” she says. “Almost as if, taken together, the books themselves are forming an arc, a larger story unfolding through the choices.”

Lipa works closely with her book club director, Maria Padget, but selects Service95's monthly reads herself, guided not by what’s trending but instead, as Lee, the author of Pachinko who calls Lipa an “exemplary literary citizen,” puts it, by honoring “her intellectual curiosity,” often highlighting marginalized voices, and not absorbing literature through a strictly Western lens.

“When she looks at her readers, she doesn’t think what would be easiest for you to enjoy, or what would comfort you or challenge you the least,” says Stuart, author of Shuggie Bain. “Instead she signals, go on, you can read this, you should read this; read this challenging book, read this banned book, and see what they want to deny you, see what you can achieve and have access to what would make you more powerful!”

In the face of a burgeoning literacy crisis, global book bans, rampant censorship, and the rise of artificial intelligence, Service95 is a venue for Lipa to educate herself and her readers on global issues—encouraging an open dialogue and discourse. “I don’t shy away from it. I also like the fact that I do it on my own terms,” says Lipa, who calls activism an “important pillar” of Service95.

As a result, she’s spoken to Margaret Atwood about the fight for female reproductive rights and discussed sexual violence and intersectional feminism with Gay, author of Bad Feminist. “The book had already been out for more than a decade, so it was quite a thrill to see her introducing the work to a new audience,” the essayist says. Lipa has also pondered the impacts of AI with Nobel Prize laureate Kazuo Ishiguro and provided required reading on Israel and the war in Gaza. She’s even joined a reading group at a women’s prison in Surrey and opened the Manifesto Library—stocking banned books inside the Livraria Lello in Portugal.

“The whole goal was to get people to take a breather, learn about the thing that’s happening, and not to be afraid to ask questions or not be right all the time,” she says.

Lipa, who admits to using the Opal app, which limits her to an hour a day on social media to post her famous photo dumps, is hopeful Service95 can also serve as an antidote to doomscrolling and AI. “Not only are we declining in reading and not picking up a book, but the fact that you can have these cheat codes that completely bypass the whole point of the learning or the ingesting of information,” she says. “It’s just going to make us stupider as people.”

She credits her mother, Anesa, for her love of reading, reminiscing on their weekends spent at a London bookshop. “I basically would just sit there and read for hours and hours and hours. I loved it,” she says, rattling off the names of authors from her childhood, like Jacqueline Wilson and Malorie Blackman and the Mr. Men and Mary-Kate and Ashley Olsen series. Reading is formative to who she is as an artist; it makes her a better songwriter, as she tells me, but it is also fundamental to who she is as a person. “I feel like we get a better understanding of ourselves, of our friends, of our family members, of the people around us, what they might be going through, what’s happening in their life by just picking up a book,” says Lipa.

When I bring up the recent claim from influencer Alix Earle that she had never read a book (apart from maybe Dr. Seuss), Lipa considers the topic thoughtfully and speaks without passing any judgment, generously suggesting the possibility that maybe, as a public figure, Earle said this as a protective measure, to shield her from further prodding and criticism. “Books are so personal, I think people also get intimidated,” she says. “Books should just be enjoyable. No one’s expecting anything from the person reading it. You don’t have to overanalyze it. It’s just supposed to make you feel something.”

And true to form, she offers a book recommendation: “I love The Girls by Emma Cline. I think that’s a cool gateway drug book.”

For Lipa, it all harkens back to searching for that feeling. She returns to her recent interview with Ishiguro, the Klara and the Sun author, who drew parallels between pop music and literature and their ability to foster connection. “He said the heart has so many rooms, so many things that make us, and AI can’t do that,” she says. “There is something really encouraging about that, that some things can't be replicated. Our real humanness and the love we feel, the loneliness even, it can make us feel even more alive and more human.” Perhaps Lipa’s book club is an act of resistance, but it’s also, to borrow a line from her last album, an act of radical optimism.
