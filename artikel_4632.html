The Ache of Feeling Overlooked

Updated November 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

We never really know what someone else is carrying in their backpack of rocks. One of my former colleagues, a brilliant consultant, springs to mind. She was highly experienced, calmly present under pressure, and one of those people a hospital comes to depend upon without anyone saying as much.

Younger doctors would seek her out after shifts and, though she never gave much overt advice, when she spoke they paid attention. Usually she listened carefully, asked a question, or helped them see what had just happened from a new perspective. She was also the last person who would have wanted a fuss made of her.

At some point, she started to change. Nothing dramatic, and she still came to work and took care of her patients as diligently and professionally as ever. If you'd asked me then whether anything was noticeably wrong, I am embarrassed to admit that I would probably have dismissed it. On reflection, I can see that she'd stopped staying behind after teaching sessions to chat with students. She also spoke less in meetings and our general conversations became shorter. She was responding to other colleagues differently too, but we were all over-extended and too preoccupied to rally round.

I assumed she was tired. Nothing unusual about that in a hospital, after all. I never asked her directly what was going on, so I don't know. Maybe she had problems at home, or just decided to work less, and I would be doing her a disservice if I invented a reason for her withdrawal all these years later. What haunts me is that I noticed the change and didn't stop to ask why. She left the hospital soon after.

Doctors spend years learning to observe people. We study skin color, breathing, posture and movement. We become experts at listening to how people speak and notice when their account doesn't quite fit what we are seeing in front of us. Sometimes a tiny detail changes the direction of an examination.

I'm less certain that we are equally observant of the people working alongside us. Hospitals are notoriously busy places, and when someone continues to show up, doing good work and causing us no interruptions, our attention naturally goes elsewhere. This is particularly the case with competent people, and when we are so cocooned in our own work with all its decisions and distractions, it's not so easy to notice when a colleague interacts less or keeps their door closed. Any one of these signals could mean absolutely nothing, but if you know someone well enough to spot their behavior has changed, why not ask? I wish I had.

Years later, I came across a study by Naomi Eisenberger, Matthew Lieberman, and Kipling Williams that made me reconsider that colleague. The researchers used brain imaging to study what happened when people experienced social exclusion during a virtual ball-tossing game. They found activity in some of the brain regions also involved in the distress of physical pain.

The study was published in Science in 2003 and has since generated a good deal of research and discussion. A brain scan can't tell us what my colleague was experiencing, of course. I cannot say whether she felt personally excluded, though the study piqued my interest in something I had previously underestimated: how much being included matters to us.

You don't have to be physically alone to be lonely. I've known several people who have spent their lives elbow-deep in friends, family and colleagues, but who still feel disconnected from others. In a large hospital, and I'm sure in other workplaces, it's perfectly possible to speak to dozens of people every day, without enjoying a conversation where someone asks how you are and genuinely wants to hear the answer.

I used to believe that recognition was mainly about acknowledging exemplary work, but I'm no longer sure that's accurate. Praise has its place, and most of us like to hear what we did well, but in the difficult phases of my own life, the times I've felt truly noticed have been thanks to interactions that are much more subtle. Somebody maybe remembers a joke or a fact I told them weeks ago, or a colleague checks in when I'm unusually quiet. Occasionally a friend will hesitate and ask again after enquiring how I am and receiving the classic response 'Fine.'

None of these moments of recognition would appear in an organizational strategy. They cost nothing and can take less than a minute, but they tell us something valuable: someone has put their own busyness aside long enough to truly care.

Healthcare organizations spend time and money figuring out how to best care for patients, as they should. I wonder if we forget to look sideways at the people providing that care. Who pays attention to them? The people I worry about most aren't necessarily those who announce they're struggling. Often it's the person who just gets on with the job without complaining, who continues to do an excellent job while gradually becoming harder to reach.

I still have no idea what happened to the consultant I worked alongside all those years ago. I only know that I noticed her withdrawal and attributed it to tiredness. If I found myself in the same situation today, I hope I would be attentive enough to knock on the door of her office, sit down and ask how she was, and actually wait long enough for the answer.

Eisenberger, N. I., Lieberman, M. D., & Williams, K. D. (2003). Does rejection hurt? An fMRI study of social exclusion. Science, 302(5643), 290–292.
