One of my favorite psychologists and the founder of humanistic psychology, Carl Rogers, saw empathy as one of “the most delicate and potent tools” we have as humans, and definitely not a weakness. The ability to take another person’s perspective and understand their thoughts, feelings, and experiences captures the fundamental meaning of empathy. We can also categorize empathy into two types: cognitive and emotional. In the simplest terms, emotional empathy means feeling what someone else feels; cognitive empathy means understanding what they feel.

The best part of empathy is its accessibility. We are naturally inclined toward empathy, but sometimes we turn it off or shut it out because it we may feel too much and become overwhelmed. It doesn’t have to immobilize us. If that were the case, therapists would never leave the house. We can learn to empathize with and feel for others while still setting boundaries that allow us to get on with our day-to-day lives.

Empathy can serve three key functions in our lives:

(1) Empathy expands our imaginative capacity.

(2) Empathy creates an ethical understanding with another person, creating trust.

(3) Empathy promotes change, especially in therapy, through creating insight.

Creative empathy is essential to our daily life because it enhances our ability to generate new ideas, knowledge, and ways of being in the world. Empathy-building activities in the classroom help students improve their creativity and critical thinking. Empathy can make meaningless activities significant and fun. Empathy helps us see problems from different angles, which can help creative performance.

For instance, a screenwriter must imagine not just what a protagonist wants, but how every character experiences the same moment differently. By empathizing with the different characters’ fears, hopes, and insecurities, a movie avoids clichés, creates richer, more complex characters, and connects better with audiences.

On the other hand, cognitive empathy enhances our ability to understand and express emotions and ideas. Research has found a relationship between cognitive empathy and almost all areas of creativity except science and engineering, where logic and reason dominate. The relationship between creative achievement and empathy shows that cognitive empathy has significant positive effects in visual arts, creative writing, inventions, scientific discovery, and the culinary arts.

Anatomy of a Fall (2023) is a French film directed by Justine Triet that is an excellent watch for tapping into your creative empathy. If you don’t know the Oscar-winning film, it is an almost Hitchcockian-like thriller that blurs the line between truth and fiction. Sandra Voyter (Sandra Hüller) is a writer who is put on trial for the death of her husband, whom she claims committed suicide.

As you watch, try these three exercises to tap into your creative empathy:

1. Shift your perspective: Think about how someone else might interpret this situation and Sandra’s character differently.

2. Change your perspective: As you watch Anatomy of a Fall, examine everyone’s thoughts, beliefs, and motivations rather than their emotions. Ask, “What is Sandra trying to protect? What does she believe about truth?”

3. Dive into unfamiliar perspectives: Instead of asking if Sandra is guilty, ask, “What does each character believe happened? Why would that version feel necessary to them?”

Creative empathy isn't just a luxury for screenwriters, but it's something we all need and crave. We need it to stay flexible, resilient, connected to others, and feel alive. In everyday life, as a parent, you empathize with your teenager’s need for independence rather than control; you may impose fewer rules and reach creative agreements—not punishment or lecturing. In that way, you let them develop the autonomy they need to succeed in life and set compassionate boundaries to help them achieve it.
