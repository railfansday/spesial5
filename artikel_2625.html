Karl-Anthony Towns is so much more than the Knicks’ best player

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

In New York, it is customary for an athlete to have distractions. How do you hide in a city that adores you? How do you hide in a city full of people counting on your performance to get them from the capitalistic ills of living in New York? So many of our great athletes — everyone from Ruth, Clyde Frazier, Reggie Jackson, Sugar Ray Richardson, Derek Jeter, Alex Rodriguez, Carmelo Anthony — have had off-the-court news show up in Page Six, on TMZ, on the internet, those times when the fame and notoriety in New York becomes too showy, causing discomfort for even the most New York of New York fans. I remember when Stephon Marbury was in the paper constantly, diminishing the goodwill that came from being raised in Coney Island, blending a conflicting cocktail of bitterness towards the press with a heroic sense of duty to working class New Yorkers. Sometimes it was because of his arguments with Isiah Thomas, on the team plane, cursing him out for his lack of loyalty; sometimes it was his inappropriate liaisons with interns. If you are a New York athlete and you don't end up in the newspaper or the gossip columns for merely existing in a way that would not anoint you into a private club of sainthood, then I rebuke your non-existent personality.

The glory of this New York Knicks team, the absolute greatness that exists within the halls of Tarrytown and Madison Square Garden, is that for the first time in my lifetime, we're cheering for a Knicks team that isn't dramatic. It's led by Jalen Brunson, of course, who is a businessman with a sharp crossover, sophisticated at blocking out chatter from elsewhere until he is a champion, in front of a microphone at City Hall about to finally speak about his haters. That deadly professionalism is what the team has become since the fateful day we first signed him from Dallas in free agency. Yet, and this is something that I don't think I realized until the start of the season, the player that I am most proud of for embodying these virtues is Karl-Anthony Towns. Towns, 31, has been the team's best player, by far, and considering that I saw Brunson put the team on his back in the game-clinching game, I don't say that fact lightly. KAT, however, has been the best big man in the world so far. Right now, he is averaging 21.3 points per game and 14 rebounds. Those are terrific numbers, but it is the fact that he leads the Knicks in plus minus (+/-) that is the most surprising part of the season so far. Plus, he's been silencing any critics.

Before the season started, the Knicks and Towns were in talks for a contract extension. The two sides were apart on the numbers and because the Knicks have a rule where they don't negotiate during the beginning of the season, the season started before Towns was able to re-sign for more money. I'm not too concerned. It feels like it'd be a depressing longshot if the Knicks didn't re-up with Towns. In the wake of the championship that the Knicks won, Towns took a point forward role that was completely different from the role that he started the season playing. Controlling the ball at the top of the key, waiting for the offense to set, waiting for those sequences where players grunt and grind off of screens, Towns would be steady, attacking as soon as he saw someone open or he saw a lane to gallop into. Towns became an example of why the bullishness about the Knicks turned out to be a real, tangible thing. Their ace was Brunson but the force, the light saber, was Towns, emboldened to play a style of basketball it would have seemed like a no-brainer to have always allowed him to play.

The games from that run are firmly indelible. The game four against the Hawks, the first time this new offense was employed, when he had a triple double; the first game against the Spurs, where he took it to Wemby, supplying Brunson's thirty points with eighteen points. However, maybe a little less important, but still quite necessary, Towns's personality — unabashedly emotionally intelligent, gentle, and strong — won people over. One of the more meaningful parts of the title celebration was seeing Mayor Zohran Kwame Mamdani connect with Towns, two ethnic New York men, one in politics, the other in sports, connecting with whimsy and joy. Somehow, all of the disses, the fake macho act that his opponents tried to do to him, went out the window. He won, and his way of being won. He's not alone. He's all of us. Why did it ever become popular to pretend like greatness meant being an asshole? Why did it ever become common for folks to be angry while being great? Towns, as a player, is so unique for his emotional gifts, for his understanding of the game. It isn't like he is a soft player, he is rarely getting pushed off the block by anyone. The performance of masculinity, one that is popular for big men of the previous generation, do not apply to Towns.

Last year, I wrote about this. "Towns is an earnest star. His instincts lead him to show emotion on the court, emotion that allows for the most traditional kinds of men to question his mental toughness", I wrote. This piece has aged well, thank you to Towns's overall play and spirit during the Knicks playoffs. His championship has given him newfound respect that I wonder if he would have if we had failed to defeat the Spurs. What if part of the reason that Towns is as universally accepted as he has ever been is because he won the championship? Ring culture, despite the fact that I often dabble in that practice, can be deceiving. Luckily with Towns, it is earned. He won playing a role that Draymond Green hasn't played in years; he won before Jimmy Butler did.

This year has been more of the same. A lesser player and person than Towns would have sulked about the dissolution of the contract talks, but Towns hasn't let it get to his head. He was fantastic against the Pistons three weeks ago with a 20 and 20 double-double, against the Wolves on Thursday night, and against the Raptors, extending his streak of double-doubles to fourteen games. You haven't heard a whisper of discontent out of Towns. Nothing about the lack of respect from the contract, nothing about the fact that he has been the Knicks's best player so far this season, nothing about himself. No one would blame him if he did that. Instead, he has been excellent on the court, adding to his already great legacy with the Knicks, busting lesser big men off the dribble, finishing through hard contact. Malika Andrews said Towns has been the best player in the Eastern Conference and I think she is right. His new trick is his slim body: knowing that he is getting older, Towns lost weight, cutting down on his carbs in the offseason. His body is built to last, all the way through the postseason. Right now, the Knicks are still 9-1 to win the championship, the fourth best odds in the league. When April rolls around, I'd gamble on everyone else catching up to Towns's energy. Until then, we'll rely on him to carry us to the postseason, relying on his steadiness and smile to ground us during a grueling title defense.

Soon, in ten years, we'll be talking about Towns being one of NBA's history's most underrated players, Dirk Nowitzki with somehow less adoration, and a player who helped bring a title back to New York — victory mixed with tears of joy, glory mixed with urban swagger. For this season, a parade down Chambers Street and its surrounding FiDi blocks could happen again. Karl-Anthony Towns is a champion, and when there is will, there is a way.
