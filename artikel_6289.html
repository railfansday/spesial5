Benjamin Franklin mocked the bald eagle in a 1784 letter and praised the turkey, but never asked for it to be the national bird

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

The letter, never mailed to his daughter, was not published in full in English until 1817. The bald eagle became the national bird by law only in 2024.

A popular Thanksgiving story holds that Benjamin Franklin wanted the turkey, not the bald eagle, to be the national bird of the United States. It is a good story, but it is not what Franklin actually wrote.

The story traces back to a letter dated January 26, 1784, and addressed to his daughter, Sarah Bache. In it, he poked fun at the eagle on the badge of the Society of the Cincinnati and said a few kind words about the turkey. In that letter, he never proposes the turkey as a national symbol.

According to the editors of his papers, Franklin never even sent the letter to his daughter. And the bald eagle itself was officially designated the national bird by law only in 2024, more than two centuries later.

When Franklin wrote the letter, the bald eagle was already on the Great Seal of the United States. It had been adopted for the seal on June 20, 1782.

In the winter of 1783 to 1784, Franklin, the American minister, was too ill to travel and isolated at Passy, in France, according to the editors of his papers.

On January 26, he saw the founding document of the Society of the Cincinnati, a hereditary order of American army officers. John Paul Jones had sent it to him along with a pamphlet attacking the society.

The letter that Franklin wrote that same day was, in the view of the editors of The Papers of Benjamin Franklin, a satire meant as a public essay.

Addressing it to his daughter, with whom, the editors note, he never corresponded about politics, made it look like a private note.

He took aim at the idea of passing honors down to children. He objected to “an order of hereditary Knights, in direct opposition to the solemnly declared sense of their country.”

He even did the math. In nine generations, he wrote, a member’s share in his descendant would be “but a 512th part.”

The birds come up near the end, when Franklin turns to the society’s badges, which had been made in France. “To me they seem tolerably done; but all such things are criticised,” he wrote.

Some critics, he reported, disliked the Latin mottos. “Others object to the bald eagle, as looking too much like a Dindon or turkey,” he wrote, using the French word for turkey.

Then Franklin gave his own opinion. “For my own part I wish the bald eagle had not been chosen as the representative of our country,” he wrote. “He is a bird of bad moral character. He does not get his living honestly.”

He described the eagle sitting on a dead tree, “too lazy to fish for himself,” while it watched another bird, which he called the fishing hawk. When that bird caught a fish and carried it toward its nest, he wrote, the eagle “pursues him, and takes it from him.”

He went further. “Besides he is a rank coward,” Franklin wrote. The “little king bird not bigger than a sparrow attacks him boldly and drives him out of the district.”

Only then does Franklin praise the turkey. He wrote that he was “not displeased” that the figure on the badge was not recognizable as a bald eagle and looked more like a turkey.

“For in truth, the turkey is in comparison a much more respectable bird, and withal a true original native of America,” he wrote. Eagles, he noted, “have been found in all countries, but the turkey was peculiar to ours.”

He did not spare the turkey entirely. He called it “a little vain and silly,” though “not the worse emblem for that,” and “a bird of courage” that would attack a grenadier of the British guards who dared to invade its farmyard “with a red coat on.”

Nowhere in the letter does Franklin ask for the turkey to replace the eagle on the seal or anywhere else. The comparison is part of a joke about a badge.

According to the editors of his papers, Franklin showed the letter only to his grandsons before sending it, around March 1784, to the abbé Morellet in France to translate it into French. Morellet advised against publishing it. Franklin agreed and promised it would not appear while he was alive.

A partial French translation came out in Paris in 1790, months after Franklin’s death. The full English text was not published until 1817, when William Temple Franklin included it in his edition of Franklin’s writings.

In other words, the letter that so many people now quote was almost unknown in Franklin’s lifetime.

The bald eagle’s official title came with a bill that Sen. Amy Klobuchar of Minnesota introduced on June 20, 2024, exactly 242 years after the eagle was adopted for the Great Seal.

The Senate passed it by unanimous consent in July 2024, and the House approved it by voice vote on December 16, 2024. It was signed into law on December 23, 2024, as Public Law 118-206.

The law adds a new section to the U.S. Code. It reads: “The bald eagle (Haliaeetus leucocephalus) is the national bird.”

In its findings, Congress noted that the bald eagle is unique to North America and that federal, state and local governments, nonprofit groups and individuals had all contributed to its recovery.

Even so, eagles still face problems, including the lead poisoning that is common in eagles.

The turkey’s history with people goes back much further than Franklin. Scientists have found that turkeys were domesticated at least 1,500 years ago.

The information was obtained from Benjamin Franklin’s letter to Sarah Bache of January 26, 1784, as published by the National Archives in Founders Online from The Papers of Benjamin Franklin, the record of S.4610 on Congress.gov, and the enrolled text of the bill published by the U.S. Government Publishing Office.
