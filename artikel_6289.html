What can your face reveal about your health? AI finds new clues

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

AI tools can estimate age and assess cancer survival risk from facial photographs, revealing health clues that may go beyond a patient’s age.

A patient’s appearance can offer doctors clues about frailty and overall health. But those impressions are difficult to measure consistently.

Researchers have developed two artificial intelligence tools that turn facial photographs into estimates of age and survival risk.

The survival tool sorted cancer patients by their risk of death.

In the first test group, patients it put in the highest-risk quarter were more than three times as likely to die as those in the lowest quarter. That gap survived adjustment for the usual clinical factors.

Both programs grew out of one base model built at Mass General Brigham in Boston.

Raymond Mak, a radiation oncologist, is the study’s corresponding author. Hugo Aerts, who directs the system’s Artificial Intelligence in Medicine program, is a co-senior author.

“As the population ages, physicians need better ways to estimate physical reserve and frailty before recommending intensive treatments such as radiation, surgery or systemic therapy,” Mak said.

“Our goal is to make face photograph-based health assessment more quantitative, consistent and scalable so it can support personalized treatment decisions rather than replace physician judgment.”

The base model, called FAHR-Face, was trained on more than 40 million face photographs from a public collection, with no labels attached.

It learned by having patches of each photo hidden and then reconstructing them, which forces a program to pick up how the parts of a face relate to each other.

The team then tuned that base for two jobs.

FAHR-FaceAge learned to estimate age from facial appearance, allowing researchers to explore whether looking older or younger than one’s chronological age relates to health.

It trained on ten public photo collections balanced so that no age group dominated.

FAHR-FaceSurvival learned from 34,363 photographs of cancer patients treated at the Harvard-affiliated hospitals, paired with whether and when each patient died.

“The key technical advance is that FAHR-Face learns a general representation of the face from more than 40 million images, which can then be adapted to distinct health outcomes such as biological age and survival,” Aerts said.

On a public set of photos of presumed healthy people, the age program missed the true age by 5.1 years on average.

A comparable program trained from scratch on the same photos missed by 6.9. The group’s earlier FaceAge model had been built mainly for patients 60 and older; this one spans the whole lifespan.

The team then asked what it means when the program’s estimate runs ahead of a person’s real age.

Among 38,183 cancer patients treated with radiation at the Harvard hospitals, a third looked at least five years older than their age, and 17% looked at least ten years older.

Patients who appeared five or more years older than their age had a 21% higher risk of death over the follow-up period than those who didn’t.

That held after adjusting for age, sex, race, cancer type, and treatment. Patients who looked five or more years younger had a 17% lower risk.

The gap was starkest in the first three months. Among patients the program judged more than 20 years older than their real age, 21.3% died within 90 days. Among those it judged at least ten years younger, 6.4% did.

The survival program skips age altogether and assigns each photo a score from 0 to 1.

The team tested it on photographs of 3,820 patients whose images had not been used to train it.

The scores rose with the seriousness of the cancer, from a median of 0.13 for benign and earliest-stage cases to 0.45 for cancer that had spread.

At the extremes, the scores separated patients sharply. Of 122 patients scored between 0.9 and 1.0, 64 died within 90 days. Of 975 scored at or below 0.1, 20 did.

The two programs relied on different parts of the face.

The survival tool weighed the area under the eyes and around the bridge of the nose. The age tool weighed the creases from nose to mouth, the forehead, and the temples.

Their outputs were only weakly correlated, and each kept its link to survival when both were in the same model.

“To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration that an AI model can directly predict cancer survival from a simple photograph,” Aerts said.

Lifestyle left marks the age program could read. Heavy drinking and drug use went with looking older, and smoking showed the clearest pattern.

Current smokers looked older than former smokers, who looked older than people who had never smoked.

The estimates held up across the things that vary in a real snapshot.

Pose, lighting, facial expression, and makeup made little difference, and most cosmetic surgeries didn’t shift the estimate. Rhinoplasty did, by a small amount.

Both programs were then checked on 4,892 patients treated at Maastricht University‘s radiotherapy clinic in the Netherlands, and both kept their link to survival.

Combining the two scores predicted survival better than either alone.

The survival analyses focused on patients treated at a few centers in wealthy countries.

Whether the scores mean the same thing in other diseases or other parts of the world is untested.

The age tool stratified risk less sharply in Black and Hispanic patients than in White and Asian patients, and the authors wrote that this residual bias remains a risk.

No patient’s treatment was changed by a score. Whether these photo-based scores help doctors select treatments or improve patient outcomes remains unknown.

The authors called for prospective trials before the tools enter routine care.

Mass General Brigham has filed a provisional patent on the next generation of these algorithms, with Mak, Aerts, and lead author Fridolin Haugg named on it.

The team has also opened a public study in which anyone can upload a photo and receive a research readout of their face age.

Those images will be used to test the models on a wider range of people.

The full study was published in the journal The Lancet Digital Health.
