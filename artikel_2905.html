Why Third-Party Risk Management Is Breaking In The Era Of AI

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

The security industry has never quite gotten the approach to third-party risk management (TPRM) right.

For as long as I can remember, TPRM has sat within the GRC function for most companies. For many teams, an annual questionnaire or a SOC 2 report was good enough to check the box, because vendors’ security postures would look about the same in October as they did in January.

That world is long gone. Vendors and Nth parties have emerged as one of the primary attack vectors for malicious actors. The common thread between the most notable headlines is third-party risk that nobody was monitoring in real time. The proliferation of AI-enabled attacks is only making the problem worse. ​

Mythos surfaced more than 2,000 previously unknown software flaws in seven weeks, with subsequent reporting placing the total above 10,000. Research also projects that the median time from vulnerability discovery to a weaponized exploit could fall below one hour by the end of 2026. The traditional human-led review cycle can’t defend this expanding attack surface that’s in constant flux.

The vendor ecosystems within enterprise environments are larger and more tangled than ever before. Traditional TPRM programs were built to track direct relationships. But the risk surface now extends far beyond the vendor named in the contract.

A company might approve a customer support platform, for example, only to find that the real exposure sits several layers deeper. The platform connects to a third-party analytics tool that relies on an AI model provider, which in turn uses a cloud infrastructure partner that becomes the source of a new vulnerability. None of those relationships would be visible in the original vendor review, yet any one of them can create exposure.

Security teams are tracking more tools, vendors and downstream dependencies than TPRM programs were designed to manage. The problem is only getting worse as the C-suite pushes teams to deploy AI tools in the pursuit of growth and efficiency. TPRM programs weren’t built to see that far down the chain, and it’s impossible for analysts to dig into the increasingly intricate web of third and Nth parties.

In other words, security teams are using human-paced processes to manage machine-paced environments.

The annual questionnaire produces a record of assessment. A SOC 2 report provides useful evidence about controls at a moment in time. A security rating can surface signals about external posture. Each input has value, but they have a dangerous commonality: They’re all looking back in time.

None of these data points provides a continuously current view of whether a vendor, product, AI system or downstream dependency is creating exposure today. Teams cannot make decisions based on stale, outdated data and expect to protect their business from current or emerging threats.

In response to this problem, some security teams might think the best idea is to speed up the existing process, but the underlying workflow is fundamentally broken. What actually needs to change is what the third-party risk program is built to produce and measure.​

​Instead of treating the initial assessment as the final word on a vendor, security teams need to keep asking whether the relationship has changed as that vendor’s role and access have changed. Maybe the vendor now handles more sensitive data, supports a more critical business process or has added integrations and dependencies that were not part of the original review. Any of those changes can alter the risk profile.​

Vendor concentration risk is another area that is easy to miss. Several important vendors may rely on the same software component, AI model or infrastructure service. Looking at those vendors one at a time can make each relationship appear manageable, while the shared dependency creates a much larger exposure across the business.​

Teams also need to be clear about what should trigger an escalation. A new dependency, a major vulnerability or a change in how the business uses a vendor may signal the need for more scrutiny. Deciding in advance who owns that reassessment and what information they need can make it much easier to respond when conditions change.​

It’s the context that surfaces from this new approach to TPRM that will help security teams make better decisions. In practice, that means understanding how a vendor touches the business. A payroll provider, identity platform, customer support tool and experimental AI app all create different kinds of exposure. The exact same vulnerability can introduce very different levels of risk depending on what data the third party impacted touches, what workflows they’re embedded in and your organization’s ability to keep operating if that provider is disrupted.​

The questions CISOs should be asking now are different than they were just a few years ago. How current is our view of our most critical vendors? What are our weakest links across our entire supply chain? What concentration risk do we have from fourth or fifth parties? Do we know where AI capabilities have entered the vendor ecosystem? Can we connect a new vulnerability to the exact business processes and data that may be exposed? Are GRC and security operations working from the same live picture, or are they still managing risk on separate clocks?​

This is where TPRM is heading. ​A function that has historically centered on point-in-time compliance reviews is transforming into a continuous security discipline. The Mythos era has accelerated that shift by introducing autonomous offensive capabilities that move faster than manual review cycles can keep pace with. Security teams need the same continuous visibility and increasingly autonomous defenses across the software supply chain.​​​
