B2B Companies Should Stop Letting B2C Set Their Digital Commerce Roadmap

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Jary Carter is CEO and Co-Founder of Oro Inc., the company behind OroCommerce, a B2B eCommerce platform for manufacturers and distributors.

​For years, B2B companies have taken their cues on digital progress from consumer commerce. Amazon was treated as the model that B2B would eventually have to follow, with every buying experience starting to look more like Amazon.

As the CEO of an e-commerce platform who works closely with B2B partners, I can tell you that never happened. Amazon became another channel, not the blueprint for B2B commerce.​

Now agentic purchasing is getting similar treatment. B2B companies have seen this pattern before: A consumer trend gets treated as the future of commerce before anyone asks how well it fits B2B.

So what happens when that thinking starts setting the digital commerce roadmap?

Consumer commerce tends to treat less human involvement as progress. Search, buy, check out, done.​

B2B buyers want plenty of independence, too. But taking the seller out of the experience can take valuable context out with them. ​

For many B2B companies, the seller’s knowledge of the customer is part of the competitive advantage. Digital commerce should build on that advantage, not erase it.​

One specialty distributor we work with learned this the hard way. Its first e-commerce launch struggled so badly that leadership questioned whether digital selling made sense for the business at all. The team later described the experience as “anonymous e-commerce” because it separated buyers from their account context and the people who knew them.​

On its second attempt, the company brought account management straight into the digital experience. Customers could see their dedicated rep inside the portal, while sales and service staff used impersonation to handle onboarding, guide first orders and troubleshoot on the fly.​

Buyers still get the independence they wanted. The difference is that going digital no longer cuts them off from the people who know their account.​

A retail-style roadmap can make the seller look like friction to remove. In B2B, digital commerce should do the opposite: Give the buyer-seller relationship a place to continue online.

The same problem shows up in how commerce teams define flexibility. The conversation often stays at the experience layer: change the storefront faster, launch a new journey, test a different way to buy.​

Useful. But B2B companies usually discover the limits of their technology somewhere else.​

Can you bring an acquired business onto the same setup when it has a different ERP? Add a region with its own catalog and pricing rules? Change an approval process or introduce a new sales model without rebuilding half the system?​

A company can have a highly flexible front end and still struggle with changes like these. Being able to redesign the buying experience quickly doesn’t mean the commerce setup can adapt quickly to the business behind it.​​

For B2B, flexibility is tested when the business changes, not when the homepage does. The roadmap has to account for that.

AI is the newest version of the same pattern. Retail attention has centered on shopping agents, conversational discovery and AI that can research or complete a purchase.

Those ideas may have a place in B2B, too. They just aren’t automatically the best place to start.​

In B2B, AI can play a different role: help sellers know their buyers better, surface customer and deal information faster and take repetitive work out of the sales process.​

While my company’s platform offers AI features, which certainly shapes my view on this topic, the shift I’m pointing to is about identifying where AI makes the most sense in B2B rather than which tools to adopt. ​

More than 75% of B2B orders still arrive through email, PDFs, spreadsheets, voicemail or handwritten notes, according to Distribution Strategy Group. Their research found that AI-based order processing can deliver productivity gains of 20% to 30%. Gartner also found that AI is already saving sellers an average of 4.8 hours per week.​

Some of the highest-value AI in B2B may sit far from the storefront. That is why copying the retail roadmap can send investment toward the most visible use cases instead of the most valuable ones.

Watch what B2C does with search, usability and convenience. But it should not decide what progress looks like for your B2B business. ​

The next priority should come from the processes your company needs to improve, the relationships it needs to strengthen and the markets it plans to enter. Use retail for inspiration. But don’t adopt its story. B2B has a more interesting one to tell. ​
