Levenza Launches Personal AI Agents for Enterprise With 300 Clients And Zero VC Funding

Personal AI agents became the breakout consumer technology of 2026. Meta's Muse reached 5 million US downloads in 22 days, against 56 days for ChatGPT, while Instinct raised $1 billion at a $10 billion valuation in September, a month after it was valued at $2.5 billion. The AI agents market is forecast to grow from $7.84 billion in 2025 to $52.62 billion by 2030, a rate of 46.3% a year.

The office has been slower to catch up, even though the average worker now receives 117 emails and 153 Teams messages a day. Levenza, a San Francisco company that has signed more than 300 customers including Oliver Wyman, Goodwin and Hg Capital without raising outside capital, has made its personal AI agents available to enterprises worldwide. It has also acquired Fluid Inference, an applied AI lab, so that people can talk to their agents by voice.

Investors and analysts expect agents to spread through business software at speed. Gartner expects 40% of enterprise applications to include task-specific AI agents by the end of 2026, up from under 5% in 2025, while the wider agents market is forecast to grow almost sevenfold by 2030 (our arithmetic). An AI agent, in plain terms, is software that does not stop at answering a question but goes on to take actions for a person, such as sending an invite or booking a room.

Consumers showed how fast that idea can spread. Muse held the top spot among free iPhone apps in the US for 12 straight days, while Instinct's valuation rose about fourfold in roughly a month on the back of an agent that books travel, pays bills and makes calls for its users.

Work is a harder setting for the same idea. A personal agent at home answers to one person, while an agent at a law firm or consulting firm has to deal with shared calendars, senior partners whose time comes first and executive assistants who handle requests for several people at once.

Microsoft's workplace data shows how much of the day now goes to coordination. The average worker receives 117 emails and 153 Teams messages a day, while the heaviest users are interrupted about 275 times a day, roughly once every two minutes. Nearly a third of meetings now span more than one time zone, up 8 points since 2021, while 57% of meetings are ad hoc calls without a calendar invite.

Much of that load lands on assistants. There are about 3.5 million secretaries and administrative assistants in the US, including 489,300 executive assistants, while many professionals have no dedicated support at all and do their own scheduling between client work. Every hour spent chasing replies and moving meetings is an hour that a consultant, lawyer or banker does not spend on the work clients pay for.

Companies are under pressure to prove that their AI spending pays off. Only 13% of CIOs in a Gartner survey of 2,002 technology leaders said their organization had realized significant or transformational value from AI, while 58% said they face more pressure to deliver cost improvements from it.

Admin work is one of the easiest places to measure a return, because the task is frequent, the time it takes is known and the result is either done or not done. Levenza users report saving up to three hours a day, which comes to about 690 hours a year over 230 working days, or roughly 17 working weeks. More than 95% of professionals using Levenza rate it highly, with many reporting feeling happier and less stressed at work.

Levenza's agents run inside Teams, Slack and Outlook, so a firm does not have to adopt a new platform. A professional can ask in plain words for a client meeting to be arranged. The agent will then inspect calendars, coordinate with assistants, resolve conflicts and book the time. The agents also book rooms, liaise with travel agents, submit timesheets and keep each person's to-do list, picking up commitments from meetings, chats and email and working through open tasks in the background.

Over time, each agent learns the preferences of the person it works for. Levenza's forward-deployed engineers, staff who work on site with each customer, adapt the agent to how each firm operates, including whose time takes priority and which requests go through an executive assistant. The company's website lists SOC 2 Type 2 and ISO 27001 certifications, along with single sign-on and activity logs for enterprise control.

Levenza has signed more than 300 customers, including Oliver Wyman, Kearney, Goodwin, Baird, Hg Capital and Spencer Stuart, while also securing multiple million-dollar enterprise contracts. It has grown profitably without raising outside capital, mostly through word of mouth with almost no marketing. Its net revenue retention is 170%, which means that customers who signed a year ago now spend 70% more in total, after any losses, while its gross revenue retention of 99% shows that almost no revenue walks out of the door.

Those figures sit well above the norm for software companies. The median private business software company in SaaS Capital's 2025 survey of more than 1,000 firms reported net retention of 101% and gross retention of 91%, which puts Levenza 69 points and 8 points ahead. Retention at that level usually means that a product spreads inside a customer after it lands, as more partners, teams and offices ask for it.

Levenza acquired Fluid Inference in September to build an agent that people can talk to as naturally as another person. The lab builds open-source models that hear, see, decide and speak, including FluidAudio, a speech toolkit for Apple devices that has trended at the top of GitHub. Its speech and system one models have been downloaded more than 4.7 million times and are used in more than 60 apps, including FluidVoice, VoiceInk and Spokenly. Fluid Inference was also a launch partner for NVIDIA's Nemotron speech models and has worked with Intel to bring speech and language models to AI PCs, while its founder Brandon Weng has joined Levenza as Head of Engineering.

Voice is already part of the product. Someone driving to work can call their agent for a briefing on the day ahead, work through urgent emails and rearrange meetings without opening a laptop, or dictate directly into applications. Travel booking and expense management launch in 2027, widening the range of admin work professionals can hand over.

Levenza grew out of Haefa Mansour's years at Bain, where she saw how much time professionals and executive assistants lost to routine coordination. Mansour, who holds a PhD from UC Berkeley, founded the company two and a half years ago with Austin Tackaberry, a former engineer at Databricks and Uber. She serves as its CEO.

The pair chose to fund growth from customer revenue while much of the AI industry raised large venture rounds. That choice kept the company largely out of public view until this launch, with a business that was already profitable before its wider market debut.

Levenza plans to build a network of agents that integrate deeply with enterprise systems and take ownership of larger goals, moving past single requests toward whole workflows. The long-term goal is a workplace where every professional has a personal agent, while executive assistants spend more of their time on the strategic work that depends on judgment and relationships.

Consumer agents have shown how quickly people adopt AI that acts on their behalf. With hundreds of millions of people at work spending hours each week on coordination, the company that brings the same experience into the office has a market far larger than the one consumers opened up this year.
