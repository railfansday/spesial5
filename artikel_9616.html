Why Topscan.me Stopped Counting Vulnerabilities and Started Scoring Its SLA

The Security Score on Topscan.me's dashboard used to move the wrong way when a team looked harder. Switch a workspace from monthly scans to weekly, or finally point the scanner at the staging box nobody had touched in two years, and the number at the top of the screen would fall. Security had not changed that morning. Only the visible part of the estate had grown, and the model read visibility as damage.

The company rebuilt the model earlier this year. The technical detail here comes from Topscan's Chief Product Officer, Aleksandr Melnichuk.

The original Security Score was a deduction model in the plainest sense. Start at 100. Subtract a weight for every open finding, scaled by severity. Subtract more for anything already past its remediation deadline. Cap the total so a large backlog could not drive the figure to zero, and display the result as a 0-100 health number that a CTO could put in front of a board.

The logic is hard to argue with in the abstract. Fewer open vulnerabilities is better than more, and critical ones should weigh more than low ones.

Once it ran on real workspaces, the score turned out to move when nothing had changed. Import a set of AWS assets, or run a discovery pass that surfaces subdomains from a campaign site nobody remembers owning, and the score drops the same day. Those vulnerabilities were there the whole time. Finding them is progress, and the model logged it as decline.

Worse, it rewarded not looking, and this is the part that decided the rewrite. If open findings cost points, the cheapest way to hold a good score is to scan less often and leave the awkward hosts off the target list. Nobody sits down and plans that. The incentive is there anyway.

And it could not tell whether anyone was keeping their promises. A workspace with 200 findings, every one of them inside its remediation window and moving, was charged for all 200. A workspace with a dozen findings that had been ignored for six weeks was charged for twelve, plus the overdue surcharge, so the team doing the work was charged for far more findings than the team with the shorter list.

"A score that drops when you scan more is not measuring security. It is measuring how much you have admitted to," says Melnichuk. "We had shipped a score that paid people to look away. Once you notice that, you cannot really leave it alone."

One rule carries the whole model: a finding resolved inside its deadline costs nothing at all, not even a small deduction.

The score only starts to drop when a finding passes the deadline attached to its severity, and from that day the penalty grows for as long as the finding stays open. Severity sets how heavy the penalty is, so a critical past its date costs considerably more than a low. Once a rescan no longer detects the finding, Topscan marks it fixed, the penalty stops and the score climbs back.

The deadlines are the defaults listed on the Topscan.me vulnerability management page: critical in 7 days, high in 30, medium in 60, low in 90. The timer starts at first detection, not at the most recent scan and not at the moment somebody created a ticket. A finding still detected in later scans keeps its original deadline.

Consider a workspace with 140 open findings across a wide estate. The oldest was first seen nine days ago and carries a 30-day window. Nothing is past its date. That workspace scores 100.

A second workspace has eleven open findings, three of them critical and first detected five weeks ago. It loses points, and keeps losing more until someone fixes or snoozes those criticals.

The scanning incentive flips along with it. Detection is free now. Pointing the scanner at a forgotten host costs zero points on the day you find whatever is running on it, and hands you a full remediation window to deal with it.

Goodhart's law is the standard warning here: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. In vulnerability management**,** it takes a very specific shape. If the metric punishes overdue work, and a team has a finding they genuinely cannot fix this month because the vendor has not shipped a patch or the deployment window is two weeks out, then the metric is quietly pushing them to close the ticket and move on.

So the model needs a legitimate way to say "not now", or it will get an illegitimate one.

The model also declines to charge teams for things that are not their work. Informational findings with no CVE attached are classed as noise and never counted. A finding marked as a false positive drops out of scoring and out of SLA tracking entirely, though if a later scan sees it again it returns to the current list, on the theory that something on the target may have changed since the last time a human judged it.

"Every metric a team is measured on has a cheat code, and the design question is which one you hand them," Melnichuk says. "I would much rather someone press snooze with a date on it than close a ticket that is not fixed. One of those keeps the finding on the board."

Melnichuk's list of what the score misses starts with coverage, which the score says nothing about. A workspace scanning three assets out of forty can hold a perfect 100 while being blind to most of its own perimeter. Topscan chose not to patch that hole from inside the score, on the grounds that folding coverage into the same figure would have made both halves unreadable. Scan coverage sits next to the score as its own number: targets scanned in the last 30 days against total targets.

"You can get a hundred by scanning three things and fixing them on time. That is the obvious hole in it," Melnichuk says. "We left it visible instead of hiding it in the formula. A single figure that quietly folds in everything stops being checkable, and a number nobody can check does not survive contact with an auditor."

Severity still comes from CVSS. A CVSS base score describes how bad a flaw would be if exploited, and on its own it says nothing about whether anyone is exploiting it on infrastructure like yours. The product surfaces a separate exploit-known flag for findings with a working public exploit, and in day-to-day triage that flag deserves more weight than a CVSS band does. The wider industry is moving the same way. CISA's BOD 26-04, issued in June 2026, revoked the older directive that tied federal civilian agencies' patch deadlines to CVSS severity and replaced it with a decision table built from four facts about each finding: whether the asset is publicly exposed, whether the CVE is in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, how automatable the attack is, and how much control a successful attack hands over. EPSS, which estimates the probability that a CVE will be exploited in the next 30 days, pushes the same way. Topscan's score has not caught up with that yet: it weighs severity and lateness, and exploit-known findings are flagged separately.

Two decisions in this story go further than the arithmetic.

The first is choosing what the metric charges for, because that is what its users will optimize whether or not anyone meant them to. Topscan's charges for missed promises. Discovery is free, and so is a backlog that grew because somebody finally looked. By Melnichuk's account that single choice changed more behavior than any weighting adjustment did.

The second is designing the exception before shipping, before somebody games the metric. A deferral path bolted on later tends to arrive as a workaround with a feature name. The pause exists because a real team, on a real Tuesday, will have a finding they cannot fix yet and a number they are being judged on.

The count did not disappear, incidentally. Open criticals and highs still sit on the dashboard, one panel over, where they tell you what to open first. They no longer set the headline number.

The current model is described in Topscan's help center. Quotes and the account of the earlier model come from an interview with Aleksandr Melnichuk, Chief Product Officer at Topscan.me.
