Should You Buy Dogecoin While It's Below $0.10?

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

The world's original meme-cryptocurrency is fighting an uphill battle to deliver positive returns.

Dogecoin (DOGE -4.53%) has spent most of 2026 trading below $0.10. It's hovering at around $0.07 as I write this on Nov. 10, representing a decline of more than 90% from its 2021 record high of $0.73. However, the cryptocurrency's significant decline doesn't necessarily mean it's cheap.

Dogecoin was created as a joke by two friends in 2013, so they didn't put much thought into giving it an actual purpose. Practically every rally since then has been fueled by pure speculation, which simply isn't sustainable, hence the coin's steep decline from its all-time high.

But developers are working on new innovations like DogeOS, an application layer where people can build decentralized software. This could revive the ecosystem and create organic demand for Dogecoin, so should investors buy it while it's still under $0.10?

An asset can't rise in value over the long term without a sustainable source of demand. If it isn't attractive or useful to consumers, businesses, or investors, then the asset will eventually run out of buyers, and its value will collapse. That is exactly what happened to Dogecoin after 2021.

Although Dogecoin can technically be used to make payments, only 2,400 businesses around the world are willing to accept it in exchange for goods and services (according to Cryptwerk). Its extreme volatility would make cash-flow management very difficult, so it's no surprise, but if consumers can't spend Dogecoin at their preferred stores, they have no reason to own it.

However, the team of developers behind the MyDoge self-custodial digital wallet created DogeOS to give Dogecoin some actual utility. It's still in the testing phase, but it will allow people to create a wide range of decentralized applications, from games to crypto exchanges to lending marketplaces, and Dogecoin will play a central role in their operation.

DogeOS is compatible with the Ethereum (ETH -2.72%) virtual machine, the software engine that powers Ethereum's smart contracts. It gives DogeOS the same app-building functionality as Ethereum, which is the world's most popular platform for developing decentralized software.

But when people use an app that was created on the DogeOS layer, they will have to use Dogecoin to pay fees instead of Ethereum's native cryptocurrency, Ether. These fees are commonly called "gas," and they cover the computing costs required to process actions in the given app. As a result, DogeOS could create a new source of organic demand for Dogecoin.

As promising as DogeOS sounds, it won't solve Dogecoin's biggest problem. New coins have always been issued through a process called mining, which involves people using computers to solve complex mathematical problems for the right to validate transactions on the blockchain. It's a critical process for maintaining a secure system of record, so validators are rewarded with payment in Dogecoin to encourage further participation.

The world's largest cryptocurrency, Bitcoin, also relies on mining, except with one key difference. Bitcoin's supply is capped at 21 million coins, so once all of them have been mined, no new coins will enter circulation.

Dogecoin has no such cap. Although a maximum of 5 billion coins can be mined each year, there is no end date, so its circulating supply will grow forever. In theory, this will steadily erode the value of each existing coin over time.

There are two main reasons I think Dogecoin will continue trending lower, despite innovations like DogeOS. First, there isn't much reason for people to use the new application layer, because they can just build decentralized software on the proven and trusted legacy Ethereum network instead.

Second, any potential value created by those who do adopt DogeOS might be erased by Dogecoin's growing supply. Personally, I've never come across an asset with an unlimited supply that increased in value over the long term, and I don't think this particular cryptocurrency will buck that trend.

There are 156.7 billion Dogecoins in circulation as I write this. If we assume 5 billion more enter circulation every year, then supply will double over the next 31 years or so, which could slash the current price of $0.07 per coin in half.

In summary, a beaten-down cryptocurrency isn't necessarily a cheap cryptocurrency, and I think investors who buy Dogecoin today are likely to suffer losses.
