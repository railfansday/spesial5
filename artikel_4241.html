10 Best Soulslikes With Fluid Movement Mechanics

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Ronald is a video game journalist with more than 10 years of experience in the industry, working on several of the most important Spanish websites.

Soulslikes represent a subgenre of ARPGs with quite distinct characteristics, including various constraints intended to establish their aggressive and challenging nature.

Features such as combat that prioritize functionality over spectacle, as well as limited and mundane traversal methods, are a constant among Soulsborne games' descendants, precisely because they exploit these limitations to create immersive experiences.

It's an incredibly delicate balance because, if poorly executed, it leads to clunky campaigns where the mere act of moving from one place to another can become tedious, though there are also exceptions that prove the rule.

Therefore, in order to shed light on these anomalies and draw on the contributions of titles with a lighter approach to the genre, here are the ten best Soulslikes with fluid movement mechanics.

Since I first played it, I think I've been one of Elden Ring Nightreign's most fervent critics, as it's a title that constantly contradicts me to the point of including it in a list of Soulslikes despite being developed by FromSoftware.

However, if there's one thing I not only can't complain about, but which I sincerely find masterful within the genre, it's the way it agilizes movement to a maximum. It gives each playthrough a dynamism uncommon in the genre.

Thanks to the characters' speed, their ability to climb walls, the spirit streams to jump to incredible heights, and the Spectral Hawk flight, which allows you to hover over areas with remarkable accuracy regarding your landing spot, simply wandering around is quite satisfying—a statement I can't make about almost any other title of this kind.

Is it cheating to include a game from the original influences' roots, which is also more of a roguelite, in this list? Perhaps, yet this is the first time the study has taken movement so seriously, so it deserves the mention.

Phantom Blade Zero is a fusion of traditional action-adventure elements with ideas borrowed from both Soulslikes and Metroidvanias, creating an extravagant yet highly satisfying cocktail.

The game boasts a staggering array of flashy weapons whose visual grandeur is breathtaking, especially in the hands of an ultra-powerful protagonist who is capable of putting those tools at the movement's mercy.

With the ability to sprint at high speed, swing on chains to cross gaps, run along walls, and use a grappling hook to reach high points, this title refuses to simply block doors on one side but instead strives to add depth to the exploration.

Unfortunately, most of these mechanics are prompts that can only be activated at specific, controlled locations, but they are more than enough to make Phantom Blade Zero a more dynamic game than average in this regard.

Speaking of middle ground, Nine Sols takes Sekiro: Shadows Die Twice's combat system and Hollow Knight's perspective, platforming, and exploration to create one of the decade's most wonderful indie games.

While it leans towards one or the other inspiration depending on the context, it's incredible how Yi soars across the maps once you've unlocked all of his movement tools, which are simple in concept but incredibly rewarding in execution.

Surely, it's nothing more than double jumps, dashes, and kicking enemies' skulls to get airborne, but it's enough to perfectly complement his prodigious combat mechanics.

Even if Nine Sols had terrible traversal, it would still be great because of how it shines in all the other elements, making the polished movement mechanics all the more admirable.

Especially considering it was released when Soulslikes were still in their infancy, Salt and Sanctuary was a major surprise, perfectly adapting the genre's formula to the 2D side-scrolling perspective.

With a wealth of secrets hidden behind the movement abilities you progressively unlock, it's a title that knows how to make platforming satisfying and imbue it with meaning beyond simply moving from point A to point B.

When you first change the gravity orientation, for example, you realize Salt and Sanctuary isn't just a great action RPG with challenging bosses and outstanding worldbuilding, but a game that understood that part of the Dark Souls trilogy's charm lay in how it transformed each new tool into a way to reinterpret the levels.

Years have passed, and I still believe it's the purest and most outstanding 2D Soulslike among those that don't venture too far into other genres, as few have captured the essence of their inspiration as well as this one.

Team Ninja is among the developers that have placed the most emphasis on the Soulslike genre, and while Rise of the Ronin isn't their most outstanding iteration, it certainly stands out when movement is the key factor.

Based on its open-world concept, the game boasts one of the most inventive arrays of traversal tools the genre has ever seen, offering a horse, a grappling hook, and a glider to facilitate the discovery of its expansive map.

If you compare it to direct peers like Ghost of Tsushima, it might fall short, but if the conversation turns to previous titles from the studio, such as Nioh, or games from FromSoftware, like the aforementioned Sekiro: Shadows Die Twice, the tone shifts.

Ultimately, Rise of the Ronin combines a myriad of ideas that will appeal to some and deter others, though the fact that it brings with it a form of exploration that avoids relying solely on walking and fast travel is undeniable.

If you’re still waiting for the next FromSoftware game, check out these ten Soulslikes that most people haven’t played but definitely should.

Shattered - Tale of the Forgotten King is a fascinating title due to its attempt, although often erratic, to combine 3D combat with side-scrolling platforming, which is a unique approach even outside Soulslikes' spaces.

The combat is present and functional enough to sustain the experience for the hours it lasts, yet what truly catches the eye is the movement, as it feels like Journey thanks to its flow and smoothness.

In addition to its perspective shifts, which alternate between combat and 2.5D traversal, moving through its open world on a hoverboard feels compelling, mainly because of how unexpected it is to encounter such a mechanic in this type of game.

Indeed, there isn't much to do once you're in free-roaming areas, but since this article focuses on movement mechanics, Shattered - Tale of the Forgotten King keeps its place among the most distinguished in the genre.

While the first game in the series was pure action-adventure, and the second shifted towards open-world RPG elements, Darksiders 3 changed course once again, this time aiming to be both a Soulslike and a Metroidvania.

By merging the level design and combat system of the former with the progression and backtracking mechanics of the latter, Gunfire Games created a phenomenal symphony that truly draws on its hack-and-slash heritage.

Darksiders 3 embraces grandeur, allowing you to transform into diverse avatars to double jump and burn through barriers, glide on air currents, become an energy ball to cling to walls, or even walk on water.

If you add to all these different ways of traversing the use of Fury's whip to swing from platform to platform, the only logical conclusion left to draw is that Darksiders 3 is a marvel when it comes to moving around.

Another Crab's Treasure is among the most creatively designed Soulslikes we've had the pleasure of enjoying this decade, and its movement mechanics are anything but an exception.

The game's levels are extremely vertical and branching, so it needed movement methods that could handle its demanding platforming sections with jumps, climbs, glides, and directional grappling hooks that take you from one ledge to another in a matter of seconds.

It's no wonder the game has become a favorite among speedrunners; its open areas combined with its fluid maneuverability create the perfect breeding ground for exploring the limits of gravity and Krill's stamina.

It has incredible bosses, extensive customization, a remarkable audiovisual component, strong thematic cohesion... Another Crab's Treasure is a complete package, and thankfully, Aggro Crab didn't leave platforming out of the equation.

After Hollow Knight established the Soulslite variant as an alternative for those who didn't want to fully commit to the formula, Hollow Knight: Silksong further solidified its fresh take on the genre.

The loss of resources upon death, save points that also serve as locations to modify your character, pattern-based bosses, cryptic lore... We all know the points of convergence, but where it truly breaks away is in its movement.

Even when compared to pure platformers, Hollow Knight: Silksong possesses a unique grace when it comes to traversal, with an incredibly agile Hornet who's capable of using her abilities in unique ways to facilitate her journey through Pharloom.

Grabbing ledges, bouncing off environmental elements, flying on air currents, leaping between walls, using your needle to propel yourself forward... The array of abilities available during the game is incredible and, above all, very satisfying, especially when you learn to chain them together and run without anything or anyone stopping you.

Nevertheless, beyond the games made by FromSoftware itself or titles that only tangentially touch on the genre, the purest example of a Soulslike with the most fluid movement mechanics is Valor Mortis.

Coming from Ghostrunner, One More Level leveraged its undeniable aptitude for first-person platforming and created an entire world around it, giving us an adventure where you jump, dash, swoop, and grapple with impressive ease.

Valor Mortis is already a striking project for its combination of such divergent concepts as stealth, first-person shooting, and ARPG combat based on parries, though adding such natural platforming sections on top of all that is simply incredible.

I spent the entire campaign amazed by the simple fact that Soulslikes don't move this fast and seamlessly, much less in sequences where you're speeding around in a minecart or running across platforms that are collapsing because a giant monster is attacking you with its tentacles.

Valor Mortis is a game that can be criticized for many things, but not for its movement. In fact, this is an area where it excels so much that other games should strive to emulate its talent.
