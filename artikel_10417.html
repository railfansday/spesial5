Women’s AI leadership is bigger than the data shows

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Every study on women in AI seems to say the same thing. Women are disproportionately impacted yet are hugely underrepresented in AI roles, not making the decisions that have such an impact on the world. The latest comes from LinkedIn, whose recent research found that women accounted for just 26% of U.S. AI hires in 2025, against 50% of hires into non-AI occupations. Across 27 countries, women hold only 13% of C-suite AI leadership roles at AI companies. Thus, they make up a ridiculously small percentage of the best-paid jobs in the economy. 

That’s very worrying. The people designing these tools and running these companies decide what the technology is for, whose tasks get automated, and how the value is captured. If women are left out at every stage, the products and the profits will reflect it. The gender gap will get bigger because of that. 

But after reading some of these studies, I’ve started to wonder if they don’t also have a few blind spots of their own. In denouncing women’s absence from AI, we may fall into the old habit of downplaying what women actually already contribute because it happens in places we don’t count and fail to see.

I interviewed Sarah Steinberg, who leads global public policy partnerships at LinkedIn, at the Reykjavík Global Forum this week. Steinberg told me LinkedIn’s research on AI leadership shows women are more likely to hold jobs exposed to AI and less likely to have AI skills. 

But do the numbers fully capture women’s AI contribution? She agreed we may be underestimating women in AI leadership. And we had just been in a session in which many women leaders from across the globe had talked about AI from another angle: regulating it, contesting it, limiting its costs for communities and the environment. These women show that many are involved in initiatives shaping the future of AI.

A narrow definition of AI work

The first blind spot is in the definition. LinkedIn counts someone as “AI talent” if they hold an AI-classified occupation or list two or more AI engineering skills. There may be a will to also count “implied skills”, i.e. for people in occupations that assume AI expertise even if they haven’t tagged it on their profile, but by and large the lens remains mainly technical. Women’s share of C-suite roles held by AI talent at AI companies is said to be 13%. But it leaves out government and civil society as well as leaders within the AI industry who don’t hold technical titles. 

Skills raise the same question. LinkedIn separates AI literacy, meaning the use of tools like ChatGPT or GitHub Copilot, from the engineering skills needed to build the technology. Asked where AI governance or policy expertise fits, Steinberg said she didn’t know whether it is counted at all, “because it’s not necessarily a technical engineering skill or a tool that you would be using.”

We have seen this before. The previous generation of tech jobs was measured with coding as the yardstick, and women who ran product, policy, or trust and safety teams barely showed up in the count.

Decisions made from the outside

If leadership means making decisions that change the course of AI, many of those decisions are now made outside AI companies. On Nov. 3, voters in 10 U.S. states weighed in on 41 local ballot measures related to data centers, nearly all of which aimed at limiting construction. Voters approved nearly all of them.

And women are prominent in these fights. A survey found that 55% of women strongly oppose data centers, compared with 43% of men. In Prince George’s County, Maryland, Taylor Frazier McCollum’s petition gathered more than 25,000 signatures and led to moratoriums that blocked a $5 billion project. In New York, State Senator Kristen Gonzalez sponsored a bill imposing a one-year moratorium on permits for data centers drawing more than 20 megawatts, which the legislature passed in June.

The revolt is likely to change the industry. It is “forcing a lot of AI leaders to have to reflect on what they have been doing that is clearly alienating the public,” Steinberg said. When companies arrive in towns and ask local politicians to sign nondisclosure agreements, on the assumption that residents would welcome a transformative technology, “what they’re finding is that the benefits that people are experiencing from this technology are not outweighing the negatives that having a data center in their community would present,” she added. Data centers do create some jobs, she added, but “whether that is at a level that is worth it relative to the downsides is up for communities to decide.”

Europe tells a similar story. Margrethe Vestager coordinated the EU’s work on the AI Act, and Lucilla Sioli, who led its drafting at the European Commission, now heads the EU AI Office. In 2024, Fortune reported that women headed more than 50 % of the 30 authorities enforcing EU data rules.

It’s true that some of the best-known critics of the industry, like Timnit Gebru, did not choose to stand outside the AI industry: they were pushed out. Women end up regulating and contesting AI because public-sector, legal, and community careers are more inclusive, while the best-paid technical jobs remain largely in the hands of tech bros who perpetuate a culture that actively filters women out.

The women we miss

Another blind spot is usage: women may use AI more than they admit. In an experiment reported in Harvard Business Review, 1,026 engineers reviewed the same piece of Python code. When told the author had used AI, they judged that person 9% less competent, even though they rated the code itself no differently. Women paid a heavier price: a 13% drop in perceived competence, compared with 6% for men. Because of this “hidden penalty of using AI”, any measure of AI skills based on self-reporting is likely to miss all the women who choose not to mention their use and their skills. 

Steinberg also pointed to the skills employers now want. Demand for AI literacy grew 70% year over year on LinkedIn, but the other most sought-after skills are interpersonal: communication, negotiation, conflict management. She is bullish on women’s strengths there, but she warned that “we haven’t really figured out the code” to train, evaluate, and promote people on those skills. Until we do, that contribution will remain mostly invisible too.

The case for counting differently

None of this is an argument for complacency. Women should be building AI tools and running AI companies, where decisions about capital and products are made. A 13% share of C-suite AI leadership roles is a disaster. And LinkedIn’s data can help better fight this fight. Because it tracks how people move from job to job, it shows where women drop off, and the answer varies by industry. In sectors where women are well represented, the biggest drop comes between vice president and the C-suite. Where they are scarce, it comes at the first manager level. In STEM, it happens in the first year after graduation, between the degree and the job. 

But a picture limited to engineers at AI firms makes women look absent and passive, even though many are shaping AI through ballots, legislation, regulation, and everyday use. If we could map the gap across industry, government, and civil society, we would have a better baseline, and it would probably show that women already have more say over AI than all the studies suggest.
