Aggression experience and observation drive shared behavioural and neural changes

The ability to observe the social behaviour of others and use observed information to bias future action is a fundamental building block of social cognition1,2,3. Aggression observation activates hypothalamic neurons relevant for aggression control4,5, but whether observation and experience engage common circuit mechanisms that promote a new shared behavioural policy is unclear. Here we develop a strategy to perform large-scale cell-type-specific recordings across subcortical networks for social behaviour generation and learning. We record longitudinally while animals train through direct aggression experience or observation, then probe shared differences in behaviour and neural activity in a novel aggression context. After training, we detect unique signatures of a shared adaptive behavioural strategy that is absent in animals without training. During aggression observation, observed behaviour recruits widespread activation across subcortical networks for behavioural generation and mimics activity evoked by experienced behaviour, and shared neural changes persist into the novel aggression context. Multiregion modelling reveals shared changes to a core aggression network across both experience and observation, including a widespread reduction in inhibition, which we find is necessary for adaptive behavioural change. Together, this demonstrates that experience-like activity during aggression observation can recruit a shared inhibitory plasticity mechanism that biases behaviour toward adaptive proactive behavioural strategies.

A major open question is whether experience and observation modify behaviour through a shared neural mechanism to generate common adaptive behavioural strategies. Models of social learning posit that social behaviours, like other behaviours, can be acquired or refined as a result of either direct experience or observation2,3. Social observation may be particularly important when the costs of learning through direct experience are high, as in aggression.

Albert Bandura’s classic ‘Bobo doll’ experiments3, which demonstrated that children can learn aggressive behaviour through observation, highlighted that even behaviours that are considered part of an innate social repertoire can be highly influenced by social observation. In humans, exposure to violence is a risk factor that predicts an increased likelihood of future violence, suggesting that these effects can be long-lasting and generalize to new contexts6,7, but the underlying mechanism remains unclear. In animal models, chronic exposure to aggression has been shown to predict future aggression8. Moreover, in mice living in groups, where exposure to aggression is both frequent and behaviourally relevant, individuals attend to the fights of others and modulate their own aggression levels9,10. Previous work has identified a set of proactive behaviours, including self-defence and vigilance, that predict resilience and emerge through direct repeated aggressive social contact11,12. However, whether these adaptive behavioural strategies can also emerge after social observation and whether behavioural changes share a common neural mechanism is not known.

Behaviour ‘mirror’ neurons have been described in cortical structures across a variety of species13, and recent evidence has shown that neurons in a key hypothalamic area for aggression14 (the ventromedial hypothalamus, ventrolateral area, VMHvl) can also be recruited during vicarious observation of aggression4,5. However, whether this property is widespread within larger subcortical networks for social behaviour generation and whether this activity is critical for updating future behaviour in novel contexts is unclear. The social behaviour network (SBN) is a highly conserved network of largely subcortical brain regions (hypothalamus, amygdala and midbrain) that is conserved across vertebrates15. The SBN is highly interconnected, with many key regions in the core aggression network of the hypothalamus and amygdala sending and receiving both excitatory and inhibitory projection to and from other circuit loci16,17. Furthermore, this network is tightly connected with networks for appetitive and aversive learning that have been shown to promote and reinforce specific defensive strategies during naturalistic behaviour that lead to persistent affective states11,12,18. Although signals for appetitive learning, including dopamine release in the NAc, have been shown to respond vicariously to the actions of others in non-social contexts19, it is unclear whether these are activated by observing social actions during naturalistic social behaviours.

While several previous studies have looked at the synaptic or hormonal basis of aggression experience within a subset of regions within the larger aggression network20,21,22,23, how these changes influence large-scale neural coding across time or are replicated during aggression observation has not been explored. Previous work has implicated several inhibitory projections in the SBN as being critical for the gating and generation of aggressive motivational states and actions24,25,26,27,28,29. One hypothesis is that inhibitory networks for aggression control exert less influence after aggression experiences, leading to new behavioural policies that favour aggression preparedness and other resilience-associated behaviours. Similarly, aggression observation could result in shared behaviour policies by co-opting changes to an inhibitory-control mechanism.

Here we track the evolution of neural activity in a network for social behavioural generation and learning across experience. We find that both aggression experience and observation lead to a shared resilient behaviour strategy in a novel context that includes increased proactive aggressive and vigilance behaviours. Aggression observation evokes widespread activation across the SBN, with subsets of regions preferentially encoding different axes of observed behaviour. Moreover, we observe a widespread reduction in inhibition across the SBN, with these networks gradually becoming more biased towards excitation across either experience or observation. Importantly, these changes persist to novel contexts with observers exhibiting experience-like patterns of activation across the space of behaviour. Using a model-inspired perturbation strategy, we show that reactivation of key subcortical inhibitory regions after aggression observation reverts observers back to a naive, less-proactive behavioural state.

To test whether aggression experience and observation similarly update aggression-control policies, we devised a behavioural train and test protocol (Fig. 1a). Individual mice were first trained through direct experience or aggression observation or were provided with no previous exposure, then tested in a novel aggression context (Supplementary Video 1). Aggression-experienced mice (the EXP group) were given opportunities to practice aggression in a safe and easy-to-win context (8 days, three 10 min sessions per day) by giving them access to a novel, submissive mouse in their homecage. Mice that observed aggression (the OBS group) were placed onto a platform above similar aggressive interactions such that they could receive multimodal cues from exposure to this fight but could not interact with the behaving animals below. The non-social control group (the NON group) was given access to a mouse toy in their homecage for sessions of matched duration. After the 8 day training regime, all of the groups were then tested on an identical probe test: a novel aggression context with a more difficult opponent (hereafter, hard fight). Hard fights comprised three brief (4 min) interactions with a similarly sized mouse with previous aggression experience. To quantify behaviour across these train and test sessions, we used a combination of supervised classifiers, fine-grain aggression annotation and unsupervised clustering (Fig. 1b).

We tracked the pose of all individuals and converted pose to a series of individual and social behavioural features11,30. We trained a supervised behavioural classifier on tracked pose to detect attack frames across the training dataset (Extended Data Fig. 1a) and evaluated with a held-out dataset. We observed that, across the training epoch, the EXP and OBS groups experienced or observed many instances of aggression (Fig. 1c) at relatively stable rates across training in both groups (Extended Data Fig. 1b–d). In the hard fight, while the total amount of attack did not significantly differ between the groups, the composition of that conflict was significantly different across groups (Fig. 1d), with both the EXP and OBS groups showing increased initiated attacks with earlier attack latencies and fewer attacks received with lower latencies relative to the NON controls (Fig. 1e–h). The NON controls, which neither observed nor experienced aggression, exhibited behaviours previously associated with stress susceptibility11,12, including more fleeing behaviour (Extended Data Fig. 1e). These data show that both aggression experience and observation promote adaptive resilience-associated behaviours11,31, including more proactive aggression and resistance when attacked.

To detect differences in behavioural strategy beyond attack, we performed unsupervised clustering of behaviour across training and the hard fight11,32,33 (Fig. 1i,j). To generate a common behavioural embedding, we performed nonlinear dimensionality reduction of the behavioural features, then performed density-based clustering of this low-dimensional behavioural embedding. Across all data, clusters could be mapped to interpretable behaviours (Extended Data Fig. 2), including a series of aggression preparatory postures, investigative behaviours, as well as distal vigilant and attentive states (Fig. 1j). We found that a number of clusters defined by their low proximity between the animals contained attack behaviours and a number of aggression preparatory behaviours, while more distal clusters represented timepoints of social disengagement (Supplementary Video 2 and Extended Data Fig. 1f). We then projected subsets of data into this embedding by group and looked at differences in occupancy and transition structure (Fig. 1k–o). Mapping the transition structure between clusters revealed a conflict supercluster, with an increased likelihood of transition between aggression and aggression preparatory behaviour (Fig. 1m). To directly compare behavioural differences between the EXP, OBS and NON cohorts, we compared differences in both the cluster occupancy (Fig. 1l) and behaviour transition matrix (Fig. 1m) during the hard fight. We found significant group differences that are shared by the EXP and OBS groups relative to the NON control mice (Fig. 1n,o and Extended Data Fig. 1g–j), indicating the emergence of shared changes to an aggression-control policy. Relative to the NON control mice, both the EXP and OBS mice spent significantly more time in several key aggression preparatory conflict clusters, which represent a gradient defined by velocity and body overlap (Extended Data Fig. 2c), and significantly less time in inattentive asocial/avoidance clusters. We compared whether OBS behavioural strategies were more EXP-like or NON-like and found that the overall behavioural similarity between EXP and OBS was greater in cluster occupancy and behavioural transitions (Fig. 1q–t) and contained more shared axes of variance. Overall, these data suggest that both aggression experience and observation bias behaviour towards similar behavioural patterns in a novel aggression context.

To test whether observation of aggressive behaviour was required for shared behavioural change, we exposed male mice to social but non-aggressive male mice (the XPO group), then compared the behaviour of these socially exposed animals during the hard fight (Fig. 1p). We found that the identity of the exposure group could be decoded from either the occupancy, persistence and transition of behaviour in the hard fight (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b), and the behaviours performed by aggression observation and non-aggression exposure groups in the hard fight were distinct (Extended Data Fig. 3c,d). Furthermore, hard fight behavioural patterns of XPO observation mice overlapped more with the behaviours of NON group mice (Fig. 1p–t and Extended Data Fig. 3e,f) than with the behaviours of OBS and EXP group mice. This indicates that observation of aggression is critical for experience-like behavioural change and exposure to non-aggressive behaviour in males is insufficient to produce adaptive behaviour change.

We next explored whether changes in the activity of the SBN underlie shared behavioural changes across aggression experience and observation. We developed a two-colour multifibre recording strategy34,35 that enabled us to record neural activity longitudinally at network-wide scale across training and the hard fight (Fig. 2a). We targeted an extended SBN with custom thin-fibre arrays, including regions in the hypothalamus, amygdala, midbrain and forebrain (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 4). We also targeted two signals previously implicated in both social valence and appetitive and aversive learning during naturalistic aggression contexts: the lateral habenular (LHb)12 and dopamine in the nucleus accumbens (NAc-DA)11. As previous work has implicated inhibitory projections in a core aggression circuit as a gating mechanism for aggression preparation and action17,25,26,36, we developed a strategy to map both putative excitatory and inhibitory signals simultaneously at each node in the network (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Video 3), except for the NAc. We used a Vgat-ires-cre mouse to target putative inhibitory neurons (Vgat+) and non-inhibitory/putative excitatory populations (Vgat−) using a dual-colour viral strategy, and recorded dopamine release in the NAc using a genetically encoded dopamine sensor37. To test whether observers were attentive to attacks, we trained a supervised classifier with a convolutional network on the spatiotemporal features of the raw video of the observer watching aggression during the training period (Extended Data Fig. 5a,b). The classifier was trained to identify instances of attentive behaviours compared with other inattentive behaviours, including rearing, grooming and turning (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 5c–e). We found that attention could be robustly predicted, and that observers increased the time spent in attentive behaviours across the training epoch (Fig. 2f and Extended Data Fig. 5f) and spent more time in attentive states relative to the XPO group (Extended Data Fig. 5f,g). We observed that observers were more likely to attend during attack than during control timepoints and exhibited an increased probability that they were in an attention epoch after attack onset (Fig. 2g and Extended Data Fig. 5i–k). Finally, we found that neural activity in observers was significantly more active during attended attack than during unattended attack (Fig. 2h and Extended Data Fig. 5l,m).

We next compared the response profiles during attack across the SBN between individuals experiencing and observing aggression. To compensate for trial-to-trial differences in shifting attention and for discrepancies in the onset of classifier aligned attack, we filtered inattentive trials and performed a network-wide time-shifting procedure38. This procedure fits a tensor-wide population-specific template and produces trial-to-trial shifts relative to classified attack using a narrow window (Fig. 2i and Extended Data Fig. 6a). We identified a shared network-wide moment of activation followed by a sustained post-attack response in many regions across the SBN (Fig. 2j) and observed a significant sustained increase in activity aligned to attack in the vast majority of signals, even without time-shifting (Extended Data Fig. 6b). We observed no significant attack-aligned activity in any signal in the NON mice, even after time-shifting (Fig. 2j (grey)). Importantly, this procedure does not produce spurious response peaks aligned to synthetic attack timepoints, or matched behavioural timepoints of high-velocity movement without aggression (Extended Data Fig. 6c,d). Overall, we observed an attack-aligned activity profile in the OBS mice that resembles the brainwide response in EXP mice, with sustained responses in a number of key inhibitory regions with known roles in aggression control, including the VMHvl, MeA, BNST and vLS24,25,26,27,28,29.

Decay constants fit to attack aligned responses in activated regions during observed aggression in the OBS group were significantly lower than the constants fit to aggression in the EXP group (Extended Data Fig. 7a,b), indicating that the signal may not persist during observation to the same degree as during action. The decay time in inhibitory regions was slower than for corresponding excitatory signals (Extended Data Fig. 7c,d), suggesting that persistence of inhibition is common to both experienced and observed aggression.

Previous work has demonstrated that performing attack increases dopamine release in the NAc11,39, while being attacked increases the response of LHb excitatory neurons12. Consistent with this, we observed that the profiles of NAc-DA and LHb in EXP mice are highly biased towards NAc-DA (Fig. 3a,b), both at the level of the individual and during simultaneously recorded signals at the single-trial level (Fig. 3b). To test whether neural signals vicariously reflect unique features of observed attack that could account for trial-to-trial heterogeneity, we examined whether neural signals preferentially encoded actions and aggressor identity. We explored in an unbiased manner whether neural signals were differentially modulated by the actions performed by the aggressors (Fig. 3c) or the subordinate male (Fig. 3d). We first compared neural activity defined by aggressor action (the most vigorous high-velocity attacks compared with the least vigorous low-velocity attacks, Fig. 3c) and tested whether individual signals would be sensitive to this difference in observed action. To quantify this, we trained a separate decoder for each neural population and rank-ordered the decoder accuracy performance. Second, we compared neural activity defined by subordinate action (the fastest high-velocity escape trials compared with the slowest, low velocity trials; Fig. 3d). We found that NAc-DA was the only signal (after multiple-comparison testing) that reliably discriminated between both aggressor and subordinate action, suggesting a unique role in vicarious action representation. The LHb was not significantly modulated by observed action and performed no better than chance. These data demonstrate that appetitive but not aversive teaching signals encode information about observed actions of both individuals.

To more directly test whether activity in observers vicariously reflected the behaviour of both individuals being observed, we recorded neural signals during attack where there was no subordinate individual, only an aggressor (Fig. 3e). To perform this experiment, we used optogenetics to facilitate a simulated attack on an inanimate object, rather than a live subordinate conspecific. In the observed aggressor, we expressed ChR2 in the VMHvl of Esr1-cre male mice and implanted a stimulation fibre over the VMHvl. We recorded the neural signals of observers as they watched either conspecific aggression or stimulation-evoked attack on a toy mouse (Fig. 3e), and trained a per-signal decoder to discriminate between real and simulated attacks. Similarly, we found that NAc-DA but not LHb was sensitive to the absence of the actions of subordinate individuals. Moreover, hypothalamic regions, including the VMHvl, PMv and AH, were significantly more robustly activated by the real attack than the simulated attack (Fig. 3e).

Finally, we tested whether neural signals across the SBN would be sensitive to other features of aggression beyond action. Recent work has demonstrated that neural activity in the MeA is modulated by familiarity in observed aggression40. To test whether other signals in the SBN would be sensitive to the identity of the observed individual, we generated familiarity by allowing the aggressor to attack the observer before observation (Fig. 3f). We then recorded these observers while they watched either the familiar aggressor or a novel aggressor attack a novel subordinate mouse. We found that previous familiarity with the aggressor significantly amplified observed attack response and that familiarity is widely encoded across the SBN, including the LHb (Fig. 3f). Notably, NAc-DA was not significantly modulated by familiarity, suggesting a unique role for this signal in the encoding of vicarious action, but not familiarity.

For each neural signal, we quantify the similarity between the EXP-to-OBS and OBS-to-NON groups using cosine distance to indicate the similarity of neural-behaviour maps across groups. We found that, across both excitatory and inhibitory signals in SBNs, the activity in OBS mice is significantly more similar to EXP mice than to NON mice, with hypothalamic inhibitory regions exhibiting the most similarity during both the training phase and during the hard fight (Fig. 4e,f). We observed similar patterns by comparing neural responses tuned to individual behavioural features (Extended Data Fig. 8). These data indicate that, in addition to promoting experience-like behaviour, aggression observation can generate experience-like neural profiles at the brain-wide and behaviour-wide scale (Fig. 4g) and that changes persist into novel social contexts.

We predicted that aggression experience and observation enabled similar adaptive behavioural strategies through a shared plasticity mechanism during training that is absent in the NON group. To test this, we compared neural activity during the hard fight and found both EXP and OBS individuals exhibited significantly reduced inhibition relative to the NON controls across a broad subset of recorded regions including the VMHvl, PMv, AH and vLS (Fig. 5a). Notably, we did not observe significant differences across groups in any excitatory signals, suggesting that broad changes in network-wide inhibition, rather than excitation, underlie the capacity for differences in behavioural strategy. We observed a similar decrease in inhibition in neural signals aligned to conflict in the hard fight (Extended Data Fig. 9a,b). To test whether these differences from NON mice reflect shared changes in inhibition, we trained a linear classifier to discriminate EXP from NON mice and projected OBS activity onto the learned axis (Fig. 5b,c). We predicted that a shared reduction in inhibition in the SBN might result in a relaxation of inhibitory control to the VMHvl, a key hypothalamic site for aggression control14,24,25. To quantify this changing relationship, we modelled the VMHvl signal as a linear combination of ongoing activity from associated excitatory and inhibitory regions in the SBN (Fig. 5g). We partitioned the contributions to the explained variance of the VMHvl into shared and unique variance using banded regularization41 (Fig. 5h). Using this approach, we trained a separate model for each day of training, including the hard fight. We observed a decrease in the unique variance explained by several inputs in the EXP models that is not present in the NON models (Fig. 5i), indicating that, across time, the VMHvl activity is less influenced by specific inputs. Importantly, during the hard fight, we observed a similar significant drop in the unique variance of several inhibitory signals in the EXP group compared with in the NON group, including the inhibitory PMv, AH, MeA and vLS, which are shared by the OBS group (Fig. 5j). Importantly, we did not observe a similar decrease in unique variance with excitatory signals during the hard fight.

Our modelling approach enabled us to identify a key set of inhibitory control regions that show changes in observers that mimic the changes in experienced individuals (PMv, MeA, AH and vLS). We tested the causal contributions of this activity on changes in behavioural strategy after observation of aggression. We predicted that reactivation of these neural signals after observation would reverse the behavioural change induced by aggression observation. To perform the reactivation experiment, we expressed excitatory DREADD-Gq42 bilaterally in the suite of model-identified subcortical regions in Vgat+ Cre mice and waited 3 weeks for viral expression (Fig. 5k,l). We allowed these DREADD-Gq (OBS-Gq) mice and a matched control cohort (OBS-control) to observe aggression across the training phase, then tested behaviour in the hard fight. Both the OBS-Gq and OBS-control cohorts received injections of synthetic ligand clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) 30 min before the hard fight, which reactivated suppressed inhibitory regions in the OBS-Gq group but not in the control group. Using both supervised and unsupervised strategies for behavioural analysis in the hard fight, we found that the inhibition-reactivated OBS-Gq cohort delivered significantly fewer attacks and received more attacks than the control cohort (Fig. 5m), consistent with a reversion away from proactive social behaviours to more reactive aggressive strategies. Finally, both the distribution of occupancy across behavioural clusters and the structure of transitions between them was significantly more NON-like in OBS-Gq mice than in OBS-control mice (Fig. 5n,o), demonstrating a reversion back to a naive behavioural state. Overall, these data demonstrate the necessity of the inhibitory reduction in mediating adaptive behaviour changes after observation of aggression, and indicates a common mechanism by which observation can generate behavioural policy similar to aggression-experienced individuals (Extended Data Fig. 10).

Here we show that both aggression experience and observation recruit a brain-wide network of excitatory, inhibitory and valence signals, and that changes to this activation pattern predict a shared adaptive behaviour-control policy in a new aggression context. First, both groups (EXP and OBS) show behavioural signatures of resilience, including increased initiated attacks and decreased latency to attack in the novel context—a classic signature of aggression instigation23,43,44. Both groups exhibited a new shared strategy consisting of two interpretable behavioural signatures: first, individuals in the EXP and OBS groups spend more time in aggression preparatory postures and similarly transition between aggression-preparatory clusters, indicating their readiness to fight. Second, both groups spend less time in distant inattentive states in which they might be vulnerable to attack. We interpret these behavioural changes as a common ‘be ready’ defensive strategy. Notably, behaviours that involve direct social contact show greater change after direct experience than after observation, suggesting that there may be some limits to what can be changed through observation alone.

While aggression mirror neurons have been previously reported in a single hypothalamic population (oestrogen- and progesterone-receptor-expressing VMHvl neurons, a subset of excitatory VMHvl neurons4), our data suggest that the representation of aggression during observation may be a network-wide phenomenon, activating multiple cell types across an extended aggression-control network. The current data are consistent with models of embodied social cognition, in which observation of aggression might activate neural populations associated with aggressive arousal, physiological signalling and reward monitoring, rather than merely encoding a reflection of an action or fear state.

We found that NAc-DA, a signal known to be involved in encoding action value45 and vicarious non-social reward19, vicariously responds to observed actions during naturalistic social behaviour and encodes differences in the behaviour of the aggressor and subordinate animal. Moreover, the response during a simulated attack is substantially reduced when the subordinate is absent. These data suggest that the observer may not automatically take the point of view of the aggressor and, instead, may learn vicariously from both the animal being attacked and also from the aggressor. This mix of perspectives is consistent with previous work using ‘witness defeat stress’ that shows that individuals exposed to aggression may develop long-term social policies and suggests that vicarious learning may emerge from attending to the actions of both the aggressor and the animal being attacked46,47,48. Rather than solely supporting a model of appetitive reinforcement during aggression, these data may suggest a broad form of goal emulation49, whereby the observer may learn a strategy (avoid being attack-vulnerable) that requires a vicarious reflection of outcomes from both participants. We find that a signal previously associated with aversive learning, the LHb, is not modulated by differences in observed actions and is instead amplified by familiarity with the observed individual. Changes in LHb signalling in bystanders observing a familiar conspecific in pain have also been observed50, suggesting that familiarity may modulate vicarious punishment signals.

Across training, we reveal shared changes in network-level plasticity across the training phase that are common to both experienced and observed mice and are not present in the non-social group. These changes support a network-wide relaxation of inhibition during critical social interactions and demonstrate that these changes persist into a new aggression context. In particular, we observe a shared motif across the core hypothalamic aggression circuit, whereby inhibition becomes less predictive of neural signals in the VMHvl.

Here we find direct circuit-level evidence for the decreased influence of inhibition in aggression-control circuits that emerges after repeated experience or observation, and demonstrate that these changes can generalize to novel and uncertain contexts. In ethological settings, this plasticity may have evolved to facilitate adaptive self-defensive strategies in situations in which aggression levels in nearby conspecifics are high, giving observers a leg up in promoting self-protection. However, these data may also shed light on the underlying circuit mechanism by which extreme levels of aggression exposure in humans may lead to long-lasting maladaptive behaviours.

Animals undergoing fibre photometry recordings were subjected to longitudinal resident–intruder experiments under varying conditions. For 8 consecutive days, animals undergoing longitudinal aggression experience (EXP) received unconstrained access to three different male BALB/cJ mice across three separate 9 min sessions, each preceded by a 1 min baseline without social stimuli. These interactions took place in the homecage of the experimental animals (Optimice Auto-Water Cage Bottom with SE Lab Group cage, 34.3 cm length × 29.2 cm width × 15.5 cm height).

For longitudinal observation (OBS), a cage extender made of black acrylic and clear plexiglass was built to match the dimensions of the homecage, along with a compartment in the front of the cage extender designed to hold each observer mouse (10 cm length × 13 cm width × 17 cm height). The observer platformed compartment was separated from the cage floor through a clear plexiglass barrier with multiple horizontal cutouts allowing for visual and olfactory access of the cage bottom. Every OBS mouse was paired with a unique CD1 male mouse, with the latter undergoing 8 consecutive days of three back-to-back resident intruder sessions in its own homecage. For each day, each observer mouse was placed in the observer compartment, and the cage extender housing the compartment was placed on top of the paired animal’s homecage. Before initiating resident–intruder sessions, OBS mice and the partnered CD1 mice were allowed up to 15 min of habituation while separated by the plexiglass barrier. Next, each resident–intruder session consisted of 9 min of social interaction between the paired CD1 mouse and a different male BALB/cJ mouse, each preceded by a 1 min pre-intruder baseline.

The control animals undergoing non-social experience (NON) were longitudinally exposed to an inanimate toy resembling a mouse over the same timespan as the EXP and OBS mice, and across the same number of sessions per day. Note that, while throughout the training period OBS individuals did not engage in social behaviour, photometry recordings were still performed in these individuals to mirror any bleaching-related signal decays present in EXP and OBS individuals after longitudinal imaging. Animals were assigned to the EXP, OBS and NON groups randomly. Blinding during data collection was not possible because group identity was evident from the experimental configuration.

On day 9, each animal from each group was subjected to the hard fight, which consisted of three back-to-back 4–5 min resident–intruder sessions in their homecage, preceded by a 1 min solitary baseline. In each session, each animal received unconstrained social access to a unique undefeated CD1 male screened a priori for aggression against other CD1 males. Two EXP animals, inadvertently exposed to CD1 aggressors outside the scheduled training protocol, were excluded from hard fight behavioural analyses to avoid confounding protocol-driven effects with this uncontrolled exposure (final behavioural, n = 10); neural data from these animals were retained for all neural analyses (final neural, n = 12), as the exclusion was motivated by behavioural interpretability rather than data quality.

For comparison of familiar and novel males during observation, the mice were first defeated in their homecage by a unique CD1 male that had been previously screened for aggression against other CD1 males. This defeat session consisted of a 9 min social interaction preceded by a 1 min pre-intruder baseline. Afterwards, each defeated animal was placed in the observer compartment and was allowed to watch four consecutive resident–intruder sessions. Two of these resident–intruder sessions of the familiar CD1 aggressor while the other two were a novel CD1 male that the observer had not previously interacted with. All four resident–intruder sessions consisted of 9 min of social interaction between the novel or familiar CD1 mouse and a different male BALB/cJ mouse, each preceded by a 1-min pre-intruder baseline. The order of the novel and familiar sessions was randomized for each observer. Subsequent neural analyses were performed only for the first session of each type.

For simulated attack observation, we unilaterally expressed ChR2(H134R)-mCherry in the VMHvl of Esr1+/− CD1 male mice. After viral expression, we screened for optogenetic stimulation evoked attack on an inanimate object (toy mouse)53. To assess whether the observers would respond differently to authentic attacks on a subordinate (like the OBS group) compared with a simulated attack on a toy mouse, the observers were presented with trials for authentic and stimulated aggression. The session structure consisted of a 1 min pre-stimulation baseline followed by 5 min during which optogenetic stimulation was delivered intermittently to evoke attack-like motor sequences toward the toy. Immediately after the toy session, the same observer animal then watched a standard resident–intruder session in which the same Esr1+/− resident received a live male BALB/cJ intruder, preceded by a 1-min pre-intruder baseline. This within-animal design permitted direct comparison of observer neural responses during optogenetically evoked aggression toward an inanimate target versus naturalistic aggression toward a live conspecific, while controlling for the identity of the observed aggressor.

Social interactions were recorded using customized multi-camera systems, including top and side cameras (40 Hz, FLIR, Spinview). These were controlled with a data acquisition device (DAQ) and associated software (Measurement Computing), which further coordinated the FP3002 CMOS camera in synchrony. The top camera was centred 50 cm above the homecage of experimental mice for each recording session, while the side camera was placed at a height of 30 cm and within 3 cm of the observer compartment. Black paper-chip bedding was used for every recording and miscellaneous items such as food pellets were cleared from homecages before recording. For behavioural analysis, we used a top-down camera, as this is reflective of what the observers viewed from their raised platform.

For supervised classification of attack behaviours during the training period, the ground truth was determined by manual annotations of videos scored with BORIS54 (v.9.3.2). Only attack and fighting behaviours were annotated, defined as rapid forward movements from the resident toward the intruder mouse, such as chases or lunges. The number of annotated frames included 29,805 attack frames out of 759,074 total frames across 49 videos. For hard fight sessions, behavioural annotation of specific behaviours was performed manually and carried out blind to the group identity of the behaving mice. Five behavioural categories were annotated. The first two categories captured aggressive behaviours initiated by the experimental subject: attacks delivered without resistance, defined as forward lunges or chases toward the CD1 aggressor that evoked no retaliatory response (the CD1 fled or remained passive); and attacks delivered with resistance, defined as attacks that evoked active defence from the CD1 aggressor in the form of counter-biting or physical pushback. The next two categories captured the analogous conditions from the perspective of receiving aggression: attacks received without resistance, in which the experimental subject was attacked by the CD1 aggressor and did not retaliate or push back; and attacks received with resistance, in which the experimental subject actively defended itself against the CD1’s attack. The fifth category captured fleeing behaviour by the experimental subject, defined as rapid locomotion away from the CD1 aggressor following an attack, approach or investigation.

SLEAP software (v.1.2.2) was used to predict resident and intruder positions across social interactions. To do this, 6,305 frames from 141 social interaction videos were labelled for tracking of resident and intruder body positions. These 6,305 frames were divided into training and test sets with an 80:20 split. The following points for both resident and intruder animals were labelled and subsequently tracked for all social interaction videos: nose, head, neck, left ear, right ear, trunk, left hindpaw, right hindpaw, left forepaw, right forepaw, tailbase, bottom segment of tail, middle segment of tail, upper segment of tail and tail tip. Training of the SLEAP model was performed over 93 iterations using a bottom-up approach with custom parameters (initialized with Unet and batch-size of 4).

To define the posture of resident and intruder mice, frame-to-frame pose data were converted to the following behavioural features:

Intruder body length: the Euclidean distance between the head of the intruder and its tailbase.

Resident body length: the same as above but for the resident.

Intruder body orientation: the angle spanning the centroid, head and nose of the intruder.

Resident body orientation: the same as above for the resident.

Intersection over union of resident and intruder: the overlap between the resident and intruder bounding boxes by dividing the area of their intersection by the area of their union.

Intruder head speed: the instantaneous distance in intruder head position (every 4 frames or 100 ms).

Resident head speed: the same as above but for the resident.

Intruder head speed lagged at ±3 s: the sum across lags of intruder head speed, computed within a ±3 s lag window.

Resident head speed lagged at ±3 s: the same as above but for the resident.

Intruder centroid speed: the instantaneous distance in intruder centroid position (every 4 frames or 100 ms).

Resident centroid speed: the same as above but for the resident.

Intruder centroid speed lagged at ±3 s: the sum across lags of intruder centroid speed, computed within a ±3 s lag window.

Resident centroid speed lagged at ±3 s: the same as above but for the resident.

Intruder to resident head-centroid angle: the angle spanning the intruder’s neck, intruder’s head and resident’s centroid.

Resident to intruder head-centroid angle: the angle spanning the resident’s neck, resident’s head and intruder’s centroid.

Intruder to resident head-to-tailbase distance: the Euclidean distance between the head of the intruder and the tailbase of the resident.

Resident to intruder head-to-tailbase distance: the Euclidean distance between the head of the resident and the tailbase of the intruder.

Between centroid distance: the Euclidean distance between the centroids of the resident and intruder.

Between head distance: the Euclidean distance between the heads of the resident and intruder.

Distance-related features were converted from pixels to centimetres using custom software based on our behaviour camera’s stereo parameters and pixel depth information.

To standardize feature processing across all sessions, the mean and s.d. of every feature for every recorded session were pooled and subsequently averaged. Features for each session were processed as follows: (1) removal of outliers 3 s.d. away from the session mean; (2) smoothing through a Gaussian filter of 0.275 s; (3) linear interpolation of missing values; and (4) z-scoring using the mean and s.d. pooled from collating features across sessions. The collated mean and s.d. values for every feature were used for z-scoring in individual sessions to capture a wider variance in feature dynamics as opposed to unit variance, which may reflect data fluctuations that are not scalable across samples.

To identify attack behaviours across training period videos, we trained a supervised multilayer perceptron (MLP) on SLEAP-derived feature data using the Keras library (Keras and TensorFlow v.2.11.0). Supervised classification was applied specifically to training period sessions, in which aggressive interactions are initiated exclusively by the resident experimental subject against a subordinate BALB/cJ intruder, yielding unambiguous one-sided attack bouts. Given the large volume of training period recordings across hundreds of videos, automated classification was necessary to make behavioural annotation tractable at scale.

A sequential model was constructed consisting of (1) an input layer shaped after the behaviour feature set; (2) two hidden layers interleaved with batch normalization layers to normalize activations from the preceding layer; and (3) an output layer with a sigmoid activation function for binary classification. The input and hidden layers each consisted of 512 units with ReLU activation functions; the output layer consisted of a single unit. The target matrix was a binary indicator for hand-annotated attack labels pooled from 49 videos, totalling 29,805 attack frames versus 729,269 non-attack frames. The feature matrix included the 19 pose-derived features described above for each video frame, along with 19 additional lagged features capturing the temporal dynamics of the existing feature set, including the instantaneous distance between resident and intruder centroids.

As non-attack frames far outnumbered attack frames, attack frames were oversampled through a synthetic oversampling technique for time series data. This involved generating synthetic samples for both attack labels and accompanying feature matrices by randomly sampling with replacement and combining these synthetic samples with the original data to balance each class distribution while preserving the underlying temporal structure. The resulting distribution totalled 699,464 attack frames versus 729,269 non-attack frames. This distribution was divided into training and validation sets with a 67:33 split, used for model development and internal performance assessment.

To evaluate generalization to entirely new data, model performance was additionally assessed on a held-out test set drawn from videos outside the original annotation pool (totalling 13,747 attack frames versus 433,936 non-attack frames). The performance on this held-out set yielded a balanced accuracy of 0.92; the confusion matrix is reported in Extended Data Fig. 1a. Subsequently, probability distributions for attacks were generated for every training period social interaction video. The probability threshold for classifying a frame as attack versus non-attack was set to 0.25; attack bouts were additionally required to span at least 200 ms of consecutive frames and to be separated from adjacent bouts by at least the same minimum duration.

Supervised classification of behaviour during training period sessions was not performed for NON mice, as individuals in this group were not engaged in social interaction. Instead, behavioural labels for NON animals were generated through a yoked control design in which labels from a randomly assigned EXP or OBS individual were paired to each NON individual for the corresponding recorded day and session.

All behavioural and neural analyses aligned to attacks during the hard fight were derived exclusively from hand-scored annotations rather than supervised model predictions. Hard fight sessions involve bidirectional aggression between the experimental subject and a CD1 aggressor, rendering one-sided supervised classification inappropriate. Hand annotations for hard fight sessions were performed blinded to group identity and segregated into the five behavioural categories described above (attacks delivered not resisted, attacks delivered resisted, attacks received not resisted, attacks received resisted and experimental subject fleeing).

To comprehensively characterize social behaviour in an unbiased manner, we adapted previous unsupervised behaviour classification strategies involving the reduction of high-dimensional behaviour features to a low-dimensional embedding and the definition of behaviours as high-density clusters within this embedding. To achieve dense clusters, we embedded the 19 behaviour features described above into a two-dimensional embedding using UMAP. To generate this embedding, an importance sampling approach was taken. This approach increased the likelihood of generating a final version of the embedding featuring rare or nuanced behaviours. Importance sampling involved a total of two rounds of UMAP reduction. In the first round, 80,000 video frames were uniformly sampled from 504 social interaction videos. As aggression can often happen briefly and therefore be under-represented in the total pool of feature frames used for reduction, an additional 20,000 feature frames aligned to aggression labels according to our supervised attack classifier were randomly added to the frame pool. This total pool of 100,000 feature frames was next embedded into a two-component UMAP manifold (umap from umap-learn, v.0.5.5, n_neighbors=50, min_dist=0.1), which was further binned into a 200 × 200 histogram, smoothed with a 2D gaussian kernel of 3.5 s.d. and parcellated into 21 clusters with watershed (skimage.segmentation.watershed). As UMAP is nonparametric, a separate model was implemented to learn the mapping from the original 19-dimensional feature space to the new 2D UMAP space. An MLP (sklearn.neural_network.MLPRegressor, hidden layer size of 1,000 × 500 × 100 × 50 units) was used for this purpose, allowing the data from every video frame to be represented in UMAP space and therefore fall into any of the 21 clusters previously defined.

To generate the final UMAP embedding, over 20,000 frames were sampled from the 21 clusters generated previously. Specifically, 2 random frames from each cluster and for each recorded session were pooled together for a total of 21,168 frames (2 frames × 21 clusters × 504 videos = 21,168). From these sampled frames, the 19-dimensional feature set was embedded into 2D UMAP space. Next, the full set of video frames were mapped into this final UMAP manifold using another MLP. The resulting embedding was turned into a 2D histogram, smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of 2 s.d. and divided into 13 final clusters with watershed. All behaviour analyses involving behaviour clusters are performed using this final UMAP segmentation. Note that unsupervised classification of NON mouse behaviour during the training period was not performed as individuals in this group were not socially interacting; instead, cluster labels for individuals in this group were generated through a yoked control design whereby labels from a randomly assigned individual in the OBS or EXP groups were paired to each NON group individual for each recorded day and session.

The expression of UMAP clusters was quantified through the occupancy (fraction of time in cluster relative to whole recording session) or persistence (mean number of consecutive frames comprising a segment of a behaviour cluster) of each cluster, in addition to each cluster’s probability of transitioning to every other cluster on a frame-to-frame basis.

To quantify the transition structure of behaviour clusters (Fig. 1m), pairwise transition probabilities were calculated as the average number of times the first cluster transitions into the second cluster. For each session, a 13 × 13 matrix containing pairwise transition probabilities for all 13 clusters was constructed. A global transition matrix was then generated by averaging the transition matrices of each individual from each group from both training period and hard fight sessions. To evaluate above-chance significance in pairwise transitions, we implemented a surrogate significance test. We first calculated the empirical transition matrix \({T}_{ij}\in {R}^{k\times k}\), where k is the number of states, from UMAP behaviour state cluster labels. Tij denotes the probability of transitioning from cluster i to cluster j. We then constructed a null transition matrix T0 that preserves the diagonal elements of T but replaces all off-diagonal entries with uniform probabilities, such that \({T}_{{ij}}^{0}=\frac{(1-{T}_{{ii}})}{(k-1)}\) for i ≠ j. Using T0 and the empirically fitted state duration distributions, we generated W = 104 surrogate sequences with 105 samples each and re-estimated transition matrix \(\hat{T}\) for each sequence, yielding a null distribution for each off-diagonal element. Let uij be the mean of \(\hat{T}\) across the N surrogates. The two-tailed empirical P value is calculated as \({p}_{{ij}}=\frac{(1+{M}_{{ij}})}{(N+1)}\), where Mij is the number of surrogates with \({| \hat{T}}_{{ij}}-{u}_{{ij}}| \ge | {T}_{{ij}}-{u}_{{ij}}| \). Effect sizes are reported as \({D}_{{ij}}=| {T}_{{ij}}-{u}_{{ij}}| \), the absolute deviation on the include-self transition scale. All 156 off-diagonal cells were tested, and P values were corrected for multiple comparisons at FDR = 0.05. Finally, the resulting global transition matrix was sorted through unsupervised hierarchical clustering (scipy.cluster.hierarchy.linkage) using the complete linkage method and Jensen–Shannon (JS) distance metric, with optimal leaf ordering to enhance the interpretability of the dendrogram. The resulting dendrogram was visualized with a colour threshold of 0.8 to distinguish clusters.

Two complementary representations of this transition structure were used in downstream analyses. First, an aggregate transition weight was computed by pooling pairwise transitions between clusters found to individually differ in occupancy between groups relative to NON (Fig. 1n (clusters 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 11 and 13)), and summing these pairwise weights within each origin-category-to-target-category pairing according to the behaviour category assignments established in Fig. 1m and Extended Data Fig. 2 (conflict, investigative, asocial), yielding a single aggregate transition weight for each of the nine possible category-to-category transitions (Fig. 1o). Second, the full transition matrix (all 156 off-diagonal pairwise transitions, without category aggregation or cluster filtering) was row-normalized, such that each row summed to 1 and represented a valid probability distribution over target clusters conditional on the origin cluster; this row-normalized matrix was flattened to a single vector per individual per session and used as the transition-probability input to the JS divergence analysis described below.

To assess whether behaviour occupancy, persistence or transition probabilities were predictive of group identity in the hard fight, a leave-one-session-out cross-validated approach (sklearn.model_selection.LeaveOneOut) using support vector machine classifiers (sklearn.svm.SVC) was used. For each session, three kinds of vectors were generated: (1) a 13 × 1 vector in which each element represented the average occupancy each cluster; (2) a 13 × 1 vector in which each element represented the average persistence of each cluster; and (3) an 81 × 1 vector containing the flattened pairwise transition probabilities for 9 UMAP clusters identified as social behaviours. For each vector type above, a separate leave-one-out cross-validation procedure was implemented. Here a new classifier was iteratively constructed in each fold consisting of 68 vectors of occupancy, persistence or transition probabilities as the training set and a single held-out vector for the classifier to predict on. The balanced accuracy score of all held-out predictions was subsequently saved. Finally, to assess statistical significance of this leave-one-out cross-validation strategy, a null distribution was created by shuffling group identity labels 1,000 times and repeating the cross-validation process, recording the accuracy score for each shuffle. The P value for each model’s performance was computed as the proportion of shuffled accuracy scores greater or equal to the test held-out accuracy score.

Statistical significance of the balanced accuracy score assessed against a permutation null distribution as described above.

We developed a deep neural network to classify observer behavioural states directly from raw video frames, consistent with previous machine learning approaches to behavioural classification55,56. The model processes short video clips (20 frames, 0.5 s at 40 fps) and outputs probability scores across seven behaviour labels: attention, grooming, scratching, still, investigate, rearing and turning. The model architecture consists of two sequential components: a spatiotemporal encoder and a classifier (Extended Data Fig. 5a), which is consistent with common machine learning solutions in animal behaviour55,56. The model is trained with Pytorch library (v.2.5.1).

The encoder is a four-layer 3D convolutional neural network that transforms raw pixel values into high-level feature representation. Each layer applies 3D convolutions with kernel size 3 × 3 × 3, followed by batch normalization, ReLU activation, max pooling (2 × 2 × 2) and a spatiotemporal attention module (described below). Channel dimensions double at each layer (64 → 128 → 256 → 512), while spatial and temporal dimensions are reduced through pooling, progressively capturing higher-order representations of posture and movement. The final feature map is reduced to a 512-dimensional vector using global average pooling. The classifier is a three-layer feedforward network (512 → 256 → 128 → 7) with dropout regularization (p = 0.3) between layers. Output logits are converted to class probabilities through softmax, and each video segment is assigned to the behaviour label with the highest probability.

A key component of the encoder is a spatiotemporal module applied after each convolutional block, which is adapted from CBAM57 for video data. The attention module allows the network to dynamically weight the importance of different spatial regions and specific frames within the data segment through separate stages explicitly. First, a channel attention step computes a global descriptor for each feature channel with adaptive average pooling, passes it through a bottleneck (reduction ratio = 8) with a ReLU nonlinearity, and projects back to the original channel dimension. The resulting sigmoid-activated weights selectively amplify informative feature channels while suppressing less relevant ones. Second, a spatiotemporal attention step applies a 3D convolution across the channel-weighted feature map to produce an attention map, followed by sigmoid activation. This map assigns larger weights to behaviourally relevant regions (for example, hind limbs during scratching, the head during grooming) while suppressing background areas (Extended Data Fig. 5b).

The model was trained end-to-end using the AdamW optimizer with different learning rates per component: the classifier was trained at a fixed rate of 3 × 10−4, while the encoder learning rate was warmed up from 7 × 10−5 to 3 × 10−4 over 20 epochs following an exponential schedule. This strategy allows the feature encoder to adapt gradually while the classifier learns to interpret its evolving representations. Models are trained for 300 epochs with an effective batch size of 256 using cross-entropy loss term with label smoothing (α = 0.1) until validation loss plateaued. Gradient clipping (max norm = 1.0) and mixed-precision training (FP16) were used during training for stability and efficiency. All of the models fully converged, reaching approximately 86% validation accuracy and 92% training accuracy. The model snapshot with the best validation accuracy was used for inference and downstream analysis.

To improve generalization and encourage the model to learn body-orientation-invariant representations, we applied stochastic augmentations in the spatial and temporal domains during training. Each video clip was randomly assigned to one of four augmentation modes: spatial only (30%; random rotation up to ±45°, random erasing of 2–33% of image area and random cropping), temporal only (30%; frame-order reversal), combined spatial and temporal (30%) or no augmentation (10%). Validation and test samples were processed without augmentation.

We manually annotated observer behaviour across multiple video sessions with BORIS (v.9.3.2)54, labelling each frame with one of seven behavioural labels: rearing (upright posture with front limbs contacting the chamber walls); turning (fast reorientation of body heading within the chamber); investigate (active locomotion or head movements not directed toward the perforated wall); still (stationary idle posture not oriented toward the perforated wall); grooming (rhythmic front-limb movements directed at the head or ears); scratching (rhythmic hind-limb movements directed at the rear of the body); and attention (close proximity to the perforated wall with head oriented toward the barrier slits). The full dataset contains around 18,000 frames in total (rearing, 6%; turning, 5%; investigate, 7%; still, 10%; grooming, 22%; scratching, 8%; attention, 43%).

For model evaluation, we used fivefold cross-validation with 20% frames in validation and 80% frames in training set, stratified by labels. For main figures and downstream analyses, the seven classes are merged into four major categories: exploratory (rearing, turning, investigate), idle (still), self-directed behaviours (grooming, scratching) and attention (attention). Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC), with metrics reported as the mean ± s.d. across five validation folds (Extended Data Fig. 5c).

For step 3, specifically, we fitted a multilinear regression (sklearn.linear_model.Ridge) for each individual photometry signal using as predictors all calcium-insensitive control signals from 415 nm optical excitation taken from the same recorded session59. The regularization strength (alpha) for each fitted model was determined individually for each session through a grid search. To identify a population-wide single timepoint for observed attack alignment on a trial-by-trial basis, a shift-only time-warping approach38 was used (Extended Data Fig. 6). This approach addresses temporal uncertainty arising from variability in the timing of neural responses relative to classifier-defined attack onsets. Moreover, moment-to-moment fluctuations in attentional state introduce further latency variability in neural engagement with each observed attack. Traditional PETHs aligned to attack onset therefore average across this temporal jitter, potentially obscuring consistent neural dynamics that are present but variably shifted across trials.

Time-shifting was implemented using the ShiftWarping algorithm from the affinewarp package (v.0.20.0), extended through a custom RobustTimeWarping subclass that adds artifact trial detection, boundary shift correction and template-based alignment of held-out animals. The algorithm learns a single scalar time shift per trial that translates the neural signal forward or backward in time, without stretching or compressing temporal structure. Optimization proceeds iteratively: beginning from the trial-mean as an initial template, the model alternates between (1) finding the optimal per-trial shift that minimizes reconstruction error relative to the current template; and (2) updating the template as the mean of all shift-corrected trials. This loop is repeated for a fixed number of iterations (specified per group below) until convergence. Critically, a single time shift is determined jointly from the activity of all simultaneously recorded neural populations; brain regions are never shifted independently. Before model fitting, trials exhibiting mean z-scored activity exceeding a specified threshold during an early artifact window (frames 0–40, corresponding to the pre-attack baseline period) were excluded. This step removes trials where residual activity from immediately preceding attacks contaminates the baseline, which would bias the learned template toward elevated pre-event activity. For the OBS group, a threshold of 4 z-score units was applied; for the EXP group, this threshold was set permissively high (effectively disabled) given the lower rate of such artifacts in directly experienced aggression.

EXP group: attack-aligned neural signals (−6 s to +6 s, window size = 480 frames at 40 Hz) were extracted for all animals in the EXP group across all training period sessions. Trials containing any NaN values due to missing fibre placements were removed before model fitting. A RobustTimeWarping model was fit to the resulting clean tensor (trials × time × 23 populations) with a maximum allowable shift of ±0.05 × window duration (that is, ±0.5 s; maxlag = 0.05), no smoothness or L2 regularization, and a single optimization iteration. For animals with one or more missing brain regions due to incorrect fibre placement, the learned template from the full-cohort model was used to infer optimal per-trial shifts through a separate align_to_template procedure. In this procedure, missing regions were temporarily filled with data from the nearest available region to enable shift optimization, then restored as NaN in the final time-shifted output.

OBS group: aRobustTimeWarping model was fit to clean OBS trials (NaN-free) from seven of the nine observer animals, with the remaining two animals collected after the primary model was fit. The model was fit with maxlag = 0.18 (±2 s maximum shift), smoothness regularization scale = 0.1, warp regularization scale = 0.1, L2 regularization scale = 0.1, artifact threshold = 4 z-score units over frames 0–40 and 5 optimization iterations. After model fitting, the learned template was used to align the two held-out animals’ clean trials by align_to_template. Animals with one or more missing brain regions due to incorrect fibre placement were handled using the same nearest-available-region substitution procedure described for the EXP group, with missing regions restored as NaN in the final time-shifted output. Time-shifted data from the primary fit and held-out animals were concatenated into a single combined OBS tensor for all downstream analyses. Observer attention state labels (ATTN+/ ATTN−) were propagated through the same trial indexing pipeline to enable subsequent attention-filtered analyses.

NON group: NON animal neural signals, aligned to timepoints derived from OBS attack labels (yoked design), were time-shifted by applying the same fitted OBS model template via fit_transform with identical hyperparameters. This ensures that any temporal structure revealed in OBS signals under the same model reflects genuine signal structure rather than an artifact of the alignment procedure. For neural signals recorded during attacks toward a toy versus a live conspecific, and during familiar versus novel conspecific encounters, the OBS training period model was reset and re-fit to the primary OBS dataset, and the resulting template was used to align these held-out datasets through align_to_template. This ensures that temporal alignment is governed by the same aggression-specific template derived from observer neural dynamics rather than from the held-out behavioural contexts themselves.

Validation controls: to confirm that the learned time shifts reflect aggression-specific neural dynamics rather than generic movement statistics or arbitrary temporal structure in attack bouts, three control conditions were implemented. In the first (synthetic) control, attack labels were replaced with synthetic sequences generated by a Markov chain (order = 10) trained on empirical attack data from 20 randomly selected sessions, preserving the statistical structure of attack timing (transition probabilities and bout durations) while destroying the temporal correspondence between labels and neural activity. The model learned state transition probabilities from the observed attack label sequences and additionally modelled the empirical distribution of attack bout durations, sampling bout lengths from this distribution during sequence generation to preserve realistic bout structure. A target attack rate of 0.1 was applied to match the approximate overall attack frequency observed in real sessions. To ensure a fair comparison between real and synthetic attack-aligned signals, synthetic trial counts were matched to real trial counts on a per-animal, per-day basis by randomly subsampling synthetic trials where the number of synthetic trials exceeded the number of real trials; sessions in which synthetic trials were fewer than real trials retained all available synthetic trials. Neural signals from OBS animals were then time-shifted using these synthetic labels under the same model.

In the second (speed) control, attack labels were replaced with binary indicators of high resident centroid velocity (threshold: >2 z-score units), with any overlap with true attack periods removed. The same OBS model was applied to signals aligned to these speed events. In the third (yoked) control, the OBS model was applied to NON animal neural signals using attack time indices derived from real OBS sessions, testing whether the model produces spurious structure when applied to neural data from animals that never observed aggression. In all three controls, the resulting time-shifted signals and their aligned PETHs were compared against those from real OBS attack trials.

PETH computation: after time-shifting, PETHs were computed for each neural population and experimental group by averaging time-shifted signal tensors across trials, either per-animal (yielding a time × populations × animals array) or across all animals (yielding a time × populations array) pending the type of analysis. Baseline correction was applied by subtracting the per-trial or per-animal median activity during a pre-event window (−5 s to −3 s before attack onset) from each timepoint. For analyses of ATTN+ versus ATTN− trials, time-shifted OBS trials were filtered by requiring a minimum of 5 s of ATTN+ occupancy within the attack window (200 frames out of 480 at 40 Hz) before averaging.

Hard fight: conflict-aligned neural signals during the hard fight were time-shifted using the same RobustTimeWarping procedure described above, with a more conservative parameterization than that used for training-period alignment (center_shift = True, maxlag = 0.05, that is, a maximum allowable shift of ±0.05 × window duration). This approach was adopted for two reasons: first, hard-fight conflict bouts encompass a mixed composition of behaviour types (attacks delivered and received, resisted and not resisted; Fig. 1d), and a tighter, centred shift constraint helps ensure consistent alignment across this heterogeneous trial composition rather than allowing the model to drift toward any one behaviour type; second, the hard fight yields substantially fewer trials per animal than the training period, and a less permissive shift window reduces the risk of overfitting the learned template to a sparse trial set. To test whether population neural activity in OBS animals carries information about the observer’s behaviour, we trained logistic regression classifiers on summary features extracted from windowed neural activity and evaluated their ability to classify behavioural categories. Neural recordings were segmented into 1.5 s windows (60 frames) with a 0.75 s stride (50% overlap), and only windows in which a single behavioural category occupied ≥90% of frames were retained and labelled with that category. Within each window, we extracted 10 summary statistics from the activity of each neural population (maximum, 90th percentile, 10th percentile, median, 10% trimmed mean, s.d., mean squared amplitude, s.d. and mean absolute value of the first-order difference, and s.d. of the second-order difference), yielding a 190-dimensional feature vector per window across the 19 recorded populations.

Decoding was performed separately for each animal and behavioural category as a binary one-versus-rest classification. For each animal and category, a logistic regression classifier with L2 regularization (C = 1) and balanced class weights was fit to features standardized using training-fold statistics. We evaluated model performance with stratified group fivefold cross-validation, in which windows were assigned to folds in blocks of 50 consecutive windows so that temporally adjacent, overlapping windows were not split between training and test sets. Cross-validation was repeated 10 times with different fold assignments, and the positive-class F1 score on held-out windows was averaged across folds and repeats to obtain one score per animal and category. We compared two decoder designs: an all-regions decoder trained on the features of all 19 populations, and a best single-region decoder. For the latter, a separate single-region decoder was trained on the features of each of the 19 populations, and for each animal and category the population whose decoder achieved the highest F1 score averaged across the 10 cross-validation repeats was selected; the F1 score of that decoder was reported. Chance performance was estimated by repeating the all-regions decoding procedure on randomly permuted labels (100 permutations per animal and category) and averaging the resulting F1 scores. Classifiers, feature standardization, cross-validation and F1 scoring were implemented in scikit-learn.

To identify the populations that contributed most to the all-regions decoder, we computed SHAP values61 using the linear explainer from the SHAP package. For each animal and category, the decoder was refitted within the block cross-validation scheme described above, and interventional SHAP values were computed on held-out windows using the training fold as the background distribution. The importance of each population was quantified as the mean absolute SHAP value across held-out windows and that population’s 10 features, averaged across folds and repeats.

To assess whether attack-aligned neural activity in individual SBN populations carries information about the context, social target or identity of the observed aggressor, we trained logistic regression classifiers (LogisticRegression from scikit-learn) on single-region mean z-scored ΔF/F activity and evaluated their ability to decode binary condition labels on a trial-by-trial basis. For each decoding analysis, the feature for each trial consisted of the mean z-scored ΔF/F of a single neural population computed within an active attack window after subtraction of a pre-attack baseline median. Classifiers were trained and evaluated separately for each recorded region, and all analyses were restricted to regions that showed significant attack-aligned activity in OBS animals (identified through the PETH comparison framework described above).

Given the sparsity of trials per animal in certain experimental conditions, decoding was performed across all trials pooled from all animals rather than on a per-animal basis. For each region, a logistic regression classifier with balanced class weights was fit using a pipeline that included standard feature scaling. Regularization strength and penalty type (L1 or L2) were selected by inner grid search using stratified k-fold cross-validation (sklearn.model_selection.StratifiedKFold). The number of folds for the outer cross-validation loop was determined empirically by evaluating classifier performance across a range of fold counts and selecting the value that maximized mean cross-validated accuracy, subject to a minimum of nine samples per fold. The final classifier was re-evaluated using the selected fold structure, and per-fold balanced accuracy scores were stored for subsequent significance testing. To determine whether decoding accuracy was significantly above chance, a one-sample t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to the distribution of per-fold scores against a chance balanced accuracy level of 0.5 (one-sided, testing whether mean accuracy > 0.5).

Authenticity of aggression (conspecific versus toy target): observer animals watched either naturalistic aggression toward a live BALB/cJ intruder or optogenetically evoked attack-like behaviour directed toward an inanimate toy by an Esr1-cre animal expressing ChR2 in the VMHvl. Neural signals were time-shifted using the OBS training period model before feature extraction.

Trials falling at or below the 10th percentile of resident attack speed were designated as low-vigour trials, and trials at or above the 90th percentile were designated as high-vigour trials.

Attack effectiveness—intruder flight speed: the same trial-sorting and classification procedure was applied using the z-scored peak centroid speed of the intruder following each attack, as an index of attack effectiveness. Trials at or below the 10th percentile of intruder flight speed were designated as low-effectiveness trials, and trials at or above the 90th percentile were designated as high-effectiveness trials.

To characterize the temporal dynamics of attack-aligned calcium signals across recorded neural populations, several decay metrics were computed on a per-animal basis from baseline-corrected mean PETHs. For each animal and each recorded population, the mean z-scored ΔF/F signal was computed by averaging across all time-shifted, attack-aligned trials within the training period, yielding a single mean PETH trace per animal per region. All of the subsequent decay metrics were derived from this trial-averaged trace. The peak of each PETH was identified within a search window spanning timepoints −0.5 s to +0.5 s around attack onset. To improve robustness against single-frame transients, a 3-frame (0.075 s) uniform smoothing filter was applied to the trace before identifying the peak location; however, the actual peak value used for all metric calculations was taken from the unsmoothed trace at the identified peak frame. Traces of which the detected peak fell below the noise floor threshold were excluded from all decay metric computations for that region and animal, and the corresponding metric values were set to NaN. For each animal and region with a valid peak, t1/2 was defined as the time elapsed from the peak frame until the signal first crossed 50% of its peak value in the post-peak portion of the trace. To achieve a sub-frame temporal precision, linear interpolation was applied between the last frame above and first frame below the half-target value. If the signal never reached 50% of its peak value within the post-peak analysis window (frames 0 to end of trace from peak), the trial was flagged as non-decaying and the t1/2 value was set to NaN rather than assigned the maximum window duration, to avoid inflating decay time estimates.

To compare the similarity of population-level neural representations across behavioural states between groups, the mean z-scored ΔF/F of each recorded neural population was computed separately for each of the 13 UMAP behavioural clusters, yielding a 13 × 1 activity vector per population per session. Cluster membership at each timepoint was determined by the unsupervised behavioural labels assigned during UMAP segmentation. If no timepoints were assigned to a given cluster within a session, the corresponding vector element was set to zero. Note that activity vectors for OBS mice during the training period were drawn from neural time series filtered for positive attention states (see the ‘Supervised video-based classification of observer behaviour’ section).

where A and B represent mean activity vectors for two different individuals. Cosine distance measures divergence in the orientation of activity vectors rather than their magnitude, making it well-suited for identifying populations of which the behavioural representations share a similar structure regardless of overall signal scale. A cosine distance of 0 indicates identical vector orientation and therefore maximum similarity, while larger values indicate greater divergence. For training period sessions, activity vectors were mean-centred before computing cosine distance. Specifically, for each session and each neural population, the per-cluster activity vector was centred by subtracting its mean across clusters. Although cosine distance is intended to be invariant to vector magnitude, it is sensitive to differences in the origin of the activity vectors—that is, if two populations share the same representational structure but differ in their absolute baseline level, the vectors will point in different directions in high-dimensional space, inflating the apparent distance between them. Such baseline differences are expected when comparing activity vectors across contexts in animals performing radically distinct behaviours. Furthermore, these differing baseline conditions can shift the absolute level of neural activity, giving the activity vectors different effective origin points. Mean-centring removes this global offset, ensuring that cosine distance reflects differences in the shape of the representation across behavioural states rather than differences in baseline. All 13 UMAP clusters were included in the activity vectors for training period analyses. To further account for the temporal variability in neural responses during the training period—as evidenced by the trial-by-trial time shifts required to align attack-period neural activity (see the ‘Time-shifting of attack signals’ section)—pairwise distances during the training period were computed using a soft cosine distance measure rather than standard cosine distance. The matrix S was constructed by grouping clusters into three functional categories—aggressive (clusters 1–4), investigative (clusters 5 and 7–9) and asocial (clusters 6 and 10–13)—and assigning within-group similarity of 0.5 and between-group similarity of 0.0, with a diagonal of 1.0. This formulation reduces the penalty assigned to activity vector differences that fall within the same functional cluster group, reflecting the expectation that temporal jitter in neural responses may displace activity across closely related behavioural states without representing a true representational difference. As the temporal alignment of neural activity is more reliable during hard fight sessions (where all animals are engaged in equivalent social interactions and the behavioural context is more constrained) standard cosine distance was used for hard fight comparisons and mean-centring was not applied.

For each session, neural population and pair of animals, cosine distance (or soft cosine distance, for the training period) was computed between the corresponding 13 × 1 activity vectors. Distances were stored in a sessions × populations × OBS animals × other animals array. For statistical comparisons, this matrix was averaged jointly across the sessions and other-animals dimensions to yield a single cosine distance value per population per OBS animal, separately for the OBS-EXP and OBS-NON comparisons. After this reduction, each OBS animal contributed exactly one cosine-distance value per population for its comparison to EXP and one for its comparison to NON, constituting a paired sample (both values are derived from the same animal).

To assess whether the multi-region pattern of neural activity during social behaviour distinguishes EXP from NON animals and to determine where OBS animals fall along this axis, a LDA classification approach was applied to population-level activity vectors aligned to social behavioural clusters during the hard fight, implemented using scikit-learn. For each animal and each session, the mean z-scored ΔF/F was computed for each recorded neural population across all frames assigned to social behavioural clusters (conflict clusters 1–4 and investigative clusters 5 and 7–9), yielding a per-session, per-population scalar value. A social engagement index was additionally computed for each session as the difference between mean activity during social clusters (1–5, 7–9) and mean activity during asocial clusters (10–12), providing a single value per population per session that captures the relative modulation of each population by social versus non-social behavioural contexts. These values were averaged across sessions to yield one feature vector per animal, with each element corresponding to a single recorded neural population. Feature matrices were constructed separately for the full population set, excitatory-only and inhibitory-only subsets. An LDA classifier with automatic shrinkage regularization (solver = ‘eigen’, shrinkage = ‘auto’) was fit on EXP (label = 1) and NON (label = 0) animals using each of the three feature subsets. To characterize the unique and shared contributions of each recorded SBN region to VMHvl excitatory population activity, we used an automatic relevance determination (ARD) regression framework with permutation-based variance decomposition41,62,63, applied separately for each behavioural cluster, session and animal. For each animal, neural activity across all sessions was concatenated into a trial × region × epoch array using a standardized formatting pipeline. A fixed set of eight predictor regions was defined a priori (indices corresponding to VMHvl inhibitory, lPAG excitatory, PMv excitatory, PMv inhibitory, AH inhibitory, MeA inhibitory, BNST inhibitory and vLS inhibitory populations), chosen to span the recorded SBN while excluding the target region (VMHvl excitatory). The target variable y was the VMHvl excitatory population activity vector for a given epoch, and the predictor matrix X was the corresponding activity matrix of the nine predictor regions. Epochs or clusters in which any predictor region contained exclusively NaN values, or in which fewer than 80 timepoints were available within a given behavioural cluster, were excluded from analysis. For activity corresponding to OBS mice in the training period, neural activity for each session was filtered for positive attention states a priori.

For each behavioural cluster c within each epoch e, an ARD regression model was fit using threefold cross-validation (KFold, shuffled). ARD regression was selected over standard ridge regression because it places independent Gaussian priors over each regression coefficient, enabling automatic pruning of irrelevant predictors while providing stable coefficient estimates under multicollinearity. Predictions from held-out test folds were concatenated to produce a full out-of-sample predicted time series, and the out-of-sample R2 was computed against the true target. Negative R2 values were clipped to zero. The final cross-validated R2 was stored as the full-model performance score for that cluster and epoch. Regression coefficients were averaged across the three folds to produce a single weight vector per predictor region.

Unique variance (permutation-based knockout): for each predictor region, unique variance was estimated by comparing full-model performance against a knockout model in which the values of that predictor were randomly permuted within the test set at prediction time, leaving the trained model weights intact. This procedure was repeated across 100 permutation iterations per predictor, and the mean knockout R2 was computed across iterations. To test whether the unique variance (ΔR2) attributable to each predictor region changed systematically over the course of training, per-session unique variance values were modelled as a function of training day using a LME model, implemented in statsmodels (mixedlm). For each recorded region and each experimental group (EXP, OBS, NON) separately, a model of the form value ~day + (1|animal) was fit, with the day of training entered as a fixed effect and the animal identity as a random intercept, to account for repeated measurements within animals across the 8-day training period. The day coefficient from each converged model was tested against zero using a Wald test. For excitatory predictors, for which no a priori directional hypothesis was specified, this test was two-sided. For inhibitory predictors, consistent with the a priori hypothesis that inhibitory unique variance declines over the course of training, a one-sided test was used: the two-sided P value was halved when the estimated slope was in the hypothesized (negative) direction, and set to 1 − half the two-sided P value otherwise, preventing an effect in the direction opposite the hypothesis from being reported as spuriously significant.

To characterize how the behavioural features were encoded in the neural activities across the network, we fit independently for each feature and each neural signal a linear Gaussian generalized linear model (GLM) with a smoothing regularizer that is chosen based on cross-validation. The input to the model is a one-hot vector representation of the feature value, which was binned in 20 total bins. The fitted GLM weights reflect the tuning curve for that specific region and feature, with positive weights suggesting an increase in activity and negative weights a decrease. To test for group differences in tuning, we used a leave-one-animal-out procedure: for each held-out animal, group-specific tuning curves were fit to the remaining animals, and the cross-validated R2 of the held-out animal’s neural activity was computed using the tuning curve of its own group. For each group, feature and region, a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to determine whether R2 was significantly greater than (P < 0.05). Only feature–region pairs for which at least two of the three groups (EXP, OBS, NON) showed R2 significantly above 0 were analysed further. For these pairs, per-group tuning curves were averaged over the lowest five and highest five bins of the feature, and each pairwise group comparison was performed using a two-sided independent-samples t-test to assess differences in tuning at the extremes of the feature range.

After the completion of our photometry and behavioural studies, animals were deeply anaesthetized by injection of a ketamine and xylazine cocktail, and perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) dissolved in 1× PBS. Brains were extracted and post-fixed in 4% PFA for 12–24 h.

For a subset of animals (5 out of 12 EXP mice), after PFA post-fixing, brains were cleared using a previously described iDISCO+ protocol64. Cleared brains were then imaged with light sheet microscopy at ×1.1 or ×1.3 magnification, and these images were registered to a common reference atlas as previously described65. We chose to register to the Paxinos Atlas66 to map fibre tip placement to anatomically defined sub-areas of each SBN location, such as the lateral division of the periaqueductal grey or the NAc core and shell structures. Fibre tip locations were manually identified in registered light-sheet images and plotted together in the common Paxinos coordinate frame. Experimenters were blinded to group identity during histological verification.

For another subset of animals (7 out of 12 EXP, 7 out of 9 OBS and 6 out of 8 NON mice), after PFA post-fixing, the extracted brains were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose and embedded in optimal cutting temperature mounting medium (Fisher Healthcare Tissue-Plus OCT compound, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for freezing over dry ice. Cryosections of the frozen tissue (30 μm slices) were made and stamped directly onto glass microscope slides. The slices were washed with PBS, dried and then coverslipped with a mounting medium (EMS Immuno Mount DAPI and DABSCO, Electron Microscopy Sciences, 17989-98, 180418), and sealed with clear nail polish around the slide edges. After at least 12 h of drying, the slides were imaged with a digital robotic slide scanner (NanoZoomer S60, C13210-01, Hamamatsu).

Fibre placements for all recorded animals are summarized in Extended Data Fig. 4, with coronal reference sections adapted from the Allen Reference Atlas66.

To assess the causal contribution of inhibitory populations to observation-induced changes in aggressive behaviour, a separate cohort of Vgat+/− (OBS-Gq) or wild-type littermates Vgat−/− (OBS-control) animals expressing hM3Dq-mCherry in MeA, vLS, PMv and AH underwent an observation training regime consisting of five consecutive days of three resident–intruder observation sessions per day, using the same observer compartment setup described above. On the sixth day, the mice were subjected to a hard fight session against a CD1 aggressor screened a priori for aggression against other CD1 males. At 30 min before the hard fight, each animal received an intraperitoneal injection of clozapine-N-oxide (CNO; 1 mg per kg; Hello Bio) and was returned to its homecage to allow for sufficient drug bioavailability before the CD1 aggressor was introduced. Behavioural outcomes, using unsupervised clustering and annotation classification of fight outcome during the hard fight were compared between OBS-Gq and OBS-control cohorts. Note that assignment to OBS-Gq and OBS-control was determined by genotype; both groups received identical viral injections and CNO. Experimenters were not blinded to genotype during CNO administration of the hard fight.

All statistical tests were run in Python (v.3.9.23) using numpy (v.1.23.0), pandas (v.2.3.1), matplotlib (v.3.9.4), seaborn (v.0.13.2), scipy (v.1.13.1), scikit-learn (v.1.6.1) and statsmodels (v.0.14.5). Unless otherwise noted in the corresponding figure legend or Methods section, statistical analyses were conducted using biological replicates (individual animals) as the unit of replication. The exception is analysis of trial-level neural-behavioural covariation (for example, decoding of trial-by-trial resident attack speed, subordinate speed; Fig. 3b–d), for which the trial is the appropriate unit of analysis given the trial-level nature of the scientific question being tested.

Before any group comparison, samples were classified as independent or related based on experimental design: distinct animals assigned to different groups were treated as independent, and repeated measurements from the same animals across timepoints or conditions were treated as related.

Sidedness of tests (one-sided versus two-sided) was determined a priori based on directional hypotheses specified before the corresponding analysis was performed, and is stated explicitly in the Methods section and figure legend for each analysis in which a one-sided test was used. In the absence of a specified a priori directional hypothesis, tests were two-sided by default.

For comparisons between two independent groups, normality was assessed per group using the Shapiro–Wilk test (scipy.stats.shapiro). For normally distributed data, equality of variance was assessed using Levene’s test (scipy.stats.levene); group means were compared using Student’s t-test (scipy.stats.ttest_ind) when variances were equal, or Welch’s t-test (scipy.stats.ttest_ind, equal_var=False) when variances were unequal. For non-normally distributed data, the Brunner–Munzel test (scipy.stats.brunnermunzel) was used in place of the Mann–Whitney U-test as the primary nonparametric two-sample test. The Brunner–Munzel test was preferred because, in contrast to the Mann–Whitney U-test, it does not assume equal variances or distributional shape between the two groups being compared and remains valid under heteroscedasticity and unequal sample sizes—both common features of this dataset, where group sizes frequently differ due to histological exclusion criteria. In the rare cases in which the Brunner–Munzel test statistic was undefined (for example, due to zero within-group variance), the Mann–Whitney U-test (scipy.stats.mannwhitneyu) was used as a fallback. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Bonferroni or FDR (Benjamini-Hochberg; statsmodels.stats.multitest.multipletests) method as specified in the corresponding Methods section and figure legend.

For comparisons between two related samples, the normality of the paired differences was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. A paired t-test (scipy.stats.ttest_rel) was used for normally distributed differences, and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test (scipy.stats.wilcoxon) was used otherwise.

For comparisons across three independent groups (EXP, OBS and NON), normality was assessed per group via the Shapiro–Wilk test and equality of variance via Levene’s test. For normally distributed data with equal variance, a one-way ANOVA (scipy.stats.f_oneway, equal_var=True) was used, followed by post hoc Student’s t-tests. For normally distributed data with unequal variance, Welch’s ANOVA (scipy.stats.f_oneway, equal_var=False) was used, followed by post hoc Welch’s t-tests. For non-normally distributed data, a Kruskal–Wallis test (scipy.stats.kruskal) was used, followed by post hoc Brunner–Munzel tests (with Mann–Whitney U-test as a fallback, as described above). Post hoc pairwise comparisons were corrected using the Bonferroni or FDR method as specified in the corresponding figure legend.

For the single analysis involving three related conditions (day 1, day 8 and the hard fight, within the EXP group; Extended Data Fig. 1d), a Friedman test (scipy.stats.friedmanchisquare) was used in place of a repeated-measures ANOVA, followed by post hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, corrected using the FDR method.

For determining the difference between two flattened distributions (NAc DA/LHb bias), a two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (scipy.stats.ks_2samp) was conducted.

For assessing whether cross-validated decoding accuracy differed significantly from chance (0.5), one-sample t-tests (scipy.stats.ttest_1samp) or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (scipy.stats.wilcoxon) were applied to the distribution of per-fold accuracy scores pending normality, tested one-sided for above-chance decoding, with FDR correction across regions where applicable.

Where LME models were fit (for example, to test for a trend in unique variance across the training period; see ‘Decomposition of shared and unique variance in VMHvl activity across behavioural states’ section), convergence was assessed using up to six optimizer/estimation-method combinations in sequence (L-BFGS, Powell and conjugate gradient, each under both restricted and full maximum likelihood), with the first converged fit retained; models for which no combination converged were excluded from the corresponding analysis. Convergence outcomes and the specific method used were logged for every fit as a diagnostic check on model reliability.

Where session-level or pooled data were evaluated by permutation testing (for example, pairwise transition-probability significance across UMAP clusters, decoding accuracy against chance and against group identity; Extended Data Fig. 1g–i), the number of permutations and null-construction procedures are described in the corresponding Methods section.

Where multiple comparisons were corrected within a defined family rather than across all comparisons in a panel, family membership was established a priori and is stated explicitly in the corresponding figure legend. Family structure varied by analysis, reflecting the specific directional hypotheses under test. For behaviour cluster-level comparisons, clusters were divided into two a priori families—social (clusters 1–5 and 7–9) and asocial (clusters 6 and 10–13)—anticipating opposite directions of effect between the two. For Fig. 2, families were defined within each experimental group (EXP, OBS) individually. For Fig. 3, families were defined by valence (NAc-DA, LHb) and excitatory/inhibitory population identity. For Fig. 4, families were defined by behavioural context (training period, hard fight). For Fig. 5, families were defined by cell type (excitatory, inhibitory) for all ARD-based analyses. Where a figure legend does not specify a family structure, correction was applied either within a single planned comparison (no correction required) or across the full set of comparisons presented in that panel.

Schematic summarizing the relationship between inhibitory SBN ensemble activity and behavioural state across experimental groups. The x-axis represents the inhibitory ensemble state, indexed by population activity level and/or VMHvl coupling strength. The y-axis represents aggression preparedness, ranging from avoidant to aggressively prepared. NON animals, which lack social experience, occupy a high inhibitory ensemble / low preparedness state. OBS and EXP animals shift rightward and upward along both axes following observation and direct fighting experience respectively, reflecting reduced inhibitory tone and increased aggression preparedness. Reactivation of inhibition returns OBS animals toward the NON-like state, demonstrating that inhibitory ensemble activity is both necessary and sufficient to gate aggression preparedness in a novel aggression context.
