If you own the asset, you should own the project data

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Owners often inherit thousands of closeout files but lose decision context stored in contractor systems. A persistent owner-controlled record improves operations, diligence, portfolio learning and future AI analysis.   AI Summary

Real estate owners spend years making decisions before a shovel hits the ground, then decades living with the consequences of those decisions after construction is complete. Yet during construction, something unusual often happens: The most complete version of the project can end up living inside systems controlled by a project team whose role is temporary.

That is not an argument against general contractors or the technology they use. Contractors need their own systems to execute the work — to manage RFIs, submittals, drawings, schedules, changes, commitments, field activity and the hundreds of other workflows required to deliver a project. The issue is what happens to all of that information when the project ends.

For the owner, construction is only one chapter in a much longer relationship with the asset, and the information created during that chapter should remain just as durable as the building itself.

By the time a general contractor mobilizes, an owner may already have spent months or years developing the project. There has been an investment thesis, underwriting, capital planning, site diligence, entitlements, early budgeting, design development, consultant coordination, procurement decisions, lender requirements and investor approvals.

Each of these steps creates information and, more importantly, context: why one design option was selected over another, why the budget moved, which assumptions drove the underwriting, which risks were identified early and which decisions were deferred into construction.

Then the project moves into delivery, and the volume of information accelerates. Drawings are revised, RFIs are answered, submittals are reviewed, contracts are executed, schedules change, pay applications are processed and change orders are negotiated. Decisions are documented across meetings, email, project-management software and individual files.

When construction ends, the asset’s operating life continues. The asset moves into operations, warranties begin to matter, capital projects follow, systems get replaced, spaces are renovated and new teams inherit the property. The asset may later be refinanced, sold or redeveloped. The asset’s history needs to remain accessible through changes in project teams, operating staff and ownership. The way that history is maintained should reflect that reality.

During construction, it is completely reasonable for the general contractor to operate its own project-management environment; in many cases, that is where the day-to-day execution record is most complete. The problem begins when that environment also becomes the owner’s only durable record of what happened.

At turnover, owners can receive thousands of files — drawings, warranties, manuals, RFIs, submittals, schedules, photos, meeting records and closeout documents. Technically, the information was transferred, but information and usable history are not always the same thing.

A folder can tell you what the final drawing was; it may not tell you why the design changed. A change-order file can tell you what was approved; it may not clearly preserve the sequence of decisions that created the change. A final budget can tell you where the project landed; it may not preserve how the forecast evolved along the way.

The distinction matters because the value of project information is not simply in possessing the files. It is in maintaining enough structure and context to understand what happened and why. When that continuity breaks at turnover, the next team has to reconstruct the story.

The answer is not for owners to dictate every piece of software used by every project participant — that would create a different problem. General contractors, architects, engineers, consultants and trade partners all have tools and workflows that support their roles, and those systems are important to project execution and should continue to be.

What the owner needs instead is a persistent layer of information that remains under its control throughout the asset lifecycle. That record should begin before construction, remain active while the project is being delivered and continue after turnover. During construction, information can move between the owner’s environment and the systems used by the project team through workflows, integrations, APIs, exports or agreed reporting processes.

In practice, that means being able to access, export and reuse the agreed project record without depending on a former project participant’s account. Owners should define the required records, their format and responsibility for maintaining them before delivery begins.

The goal is not to duplicate every click made inside a contractor’s software. It is to make sure the information that matters to the long-term owner does not disappear when the temporary project organization dissolves.

Not everything created during construction has equal long-term value. The enduring record should focus on the information required to understand how the asset became what it is.

That starts with the financial history: approved budgets, commitments, forecast changes, contingency usage, major cost movements and the financial impact of significant project decisions. It also includes the decision history — major approvals, design changes, scope decisions, responsible parties and the supporting information behind those decisions.

Then there is the technical project record: drawings, specifications, RFIs, submittals, schedule information and other documentation that explains what was designed and ultimately built. Finally, there is the turnover record — as-builts, warranties, manuals, punch and closeout documentation and the information future teams will need to operate, maintain or modify the asset.

The important point is not the specific software used to store each item. It is continuity. Five years after completion, someone working on the property should be able to understand the decisions made five years earlier without starting an archaeological dig through old inboxes, shared drives and archived contractor accounts.

Construction information is often treated as though its usefulness peaks at closeout. For owners, the opposite may be true. A complete development and construction history can become increasingly useful as the asset ages: operations teams can use it to understand equipment, warranties and design intent and asset managers can use it when evaluating future capital work.

The same history also supports investment and portfolio decisions. Development teams can compare budgets, scopes and outcomes against previous projects, and executives can benchmark performance across a portfolio. Future project teams can understand why earlier decisions were made instead of repeating the same analysis, and the information can also support underwriting, refinancing and disposition diligence by making the history of the asset easier to understand.

This becomes even more valuable for owners with multiple properties. One project record is useful; a consistent record across 20, 50 or 100 assets begins to create institutional knowledge. Patterns become visible, assumptions can be tested, budget performance can be compared and recurring problems can be identified — lessons learned on one project can actually influence the next one. The organization’s experience stops resetting every time a project team changes.

Artificial intelligence makes this conversation more important, but not because every owner needs an AI strategy for every construction workflow today. The larger issue is much simpler: AI becomes more useful when it has access to complete, structured and trustworthy information.

An owner might want to understand why similar scopes repeatedly generated changes, how contingency usage varied across developments or which underwriting assumptions proved wrong. Answering those questions requires more than a collection of final documents; it requires a traceable history of decisions and outcomes.

If each project’s information is fragmented across contractor platforms, consultant systems, shared drives, inboxes and archived files, an AI model faces the same problem a human does: the information may technically exist, but reconstructing the story becomes difficult. The owners that preserve a consistent project history today are not simply improving document retention — they are building the information base from which future analysis becomes possible.

There is an important distinction between controlling a project and controlling the enduring record of a project. Owners do not need every contractor to abandon the systems that help them build effectively, and they do not need to centralize every workflow or capture every administrative action performed during construction. What they do need is continuity over the information that explains the asset they ultimately own.

The owner owns the building. It carries the financial consequences of the decisions made during development, operates the asset after the project team leaves, funds the next capital program, and answers the questions of the next lender, investor, buyer or development team. The information describing how that asset was conceived, financed, designed, built and changed should be treated accordingly.

Construction teams move on, and ownership can change. The project record should remain with the asset, available to the people responsible for its future.
