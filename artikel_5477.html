The debt ceiling explained: 10 things to know about the U.S. borrowing limit

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The debt ceiling explained in 10 parts, from its 1917 origins and extraordinary measures to the X-date, credit downgrades and proposed workarounds

The U.S. government puts a legal cap on its own borrowing, even though Congress has already passed the spending and tax laws that make the borrowing necessary. That cap is the debt ceiling. The gap between what lawmakers have committed the country to pay and what the Treasury Department may borrow is the source of recurring fiscal standoffs in Congress.

The debt ceiling is also called the debt limit. It is a dollar figure set in federal law. Once total federal debt reaches it, the Treasury cannot borrow more until Congress raises or suspends the cap. Tax revenue keeps arriving, and obligations under existing programs keep coming due. What disappears is the ability to cover the difference by borrowing.

The stakes reach well beyond Congress. The central question in every standoff is what happens if the U.S. defaults, meaning it cannot pay all of its obligations on time. Treasury securities are held by investors, banks and governments around the world. Each deadline draws attention far outside the U.S.

The U.S. credit rating has also changed in recent years. All three major rating agencies have lowered the country from their top grade: Standard & Poor's in 2011, Fitch Ratings in 2023 and Moody's $MCO in 2025. The first two downgrades followed debt limit fights. Fitch listed repeated standoffs among its governance concerns.

This is the debt ceiling explained from the ground up. The first slides cover what the cap is, what it does not do and how it grew out of a World War I-era borrowing law. A fourth slide looks at which kinds of federal debt count against the limit. Two more explain the extraordinary measures Treasury uses to buy time and the X $TWTR-date, when that time would run out.

The rest of the piece traces three recent turning points: the 2011 Budget Control Act, the 2023 Fiscal Responsibility Act and the 2025 increase under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. It closes with proposed alternatives, from a legal argument built on the 14th Amendment to a platinum coin to abolishing the limit outright. None of those ideas has been tested.

The debt ceiling is a cap, written into federal law by Congress, on the total amount of money the Treasury Department may borrow. Only a new law, passed by both chambers of Congress and signed by the president, can change that figure.

The statute sits in Title 31 of the U.S. Code, at section 3101. It sets a single dollar figure for the government's borrowing as a whole. It does not set separate caps for individual programs or agencies.

Borrowing fills the gap between what the federal government collects and what it pays out. Treasury raises that money by selling securities to investors. In any year the government runs a deficit, it has to borrow to cover the difference.

The debt limit runs on a separate track from the budget. Congress decides how much agencies can spend through appropriations bills, which fund the government one fiscal year at a time. Programs such as Social Security and Medicare pay benefits under permanent law and do not need yearly approval. Tax law determines how much revenue comes in. The debt ceiling is a separate vote on whether Treasury may borrow to cover the result of those decisions.

The House has at times tried to link the two tracks. Under a procedural rule known as the Gephardt rule, adopting a budget resolution automatically produced a matching debt limit measure in the House. The chamber has dropped and restored that rule several times.

Changing the limit takes ordinary legislation. A debt limit bill can move on its own or inside a larger package. In the Senate, a standalone bill can face a filibuster, which takes 60 votes to end. A budget reconciliation bill can pass the Senate with a simple majority.

Congress has two main ways to change the cap. It can raise it, replacing the old dollar figure with a higher one. Or it can suspend it, setting the limit aside until a fixed date. Either way, the new room to borrow lasts only until debt catches up with the higher figure or the suspension period ends.

Raising the debt ceiling does not approve a single dollar of new federal spending. The obligations it covers come from spending and tax laws Congress has already passed. The limit decides only whether Treasury may borrow to pay for them.

The distinction comes from the order in which Congress makes its decisions. Spending choices are made elsewhere in the legislative process. Congress passes annual appropriations bills that fund agencies such as the Defense Department. It also keeps permanent laws on the books that set benefits for programs including Social Security and Medicare. Those programs pay out without a yearly vote.

Revenue is set the same way. Tax laws determine how much money flows into Treasury from income, payroll and corporate taxes. When those laws together call for more spending than taxes bring in, the government runs a deficit. Treasury covers that gap by selling securities to investors.

The debt limit enters only at that final step. It does not change how much an agency may spend or how large a benefit payment is. It governs whether Treasury can raise the cash to honor commitments already written into law.

A common comparison is a household credit card. Raising the debt limit resembles paying off charges already made on the card, not approving new purchases. The analogy has limits. A household that hits its card limit can stop buying things. The federal government's obligations keep arriving under laws that remain in force until Congress changes them.

Those obligations span the full range of federal activity. They include salaries for military personnel and civilian employees, payments to Social Security recipients, tax refunds and interest owed to holders of Treasury securities.

The power behind both sides of this arrangement sits with Congress. Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution gives Congress the authority to borrow money on the credit of the U.S., along with authority over taxes and spending. The debt limit is a separate statutory vote layered on top of those earlier decisions.

Future borrowing is shaped through the budget itself. Changing tax rates or reducing appropriations alters future deficits. Declining to raise the limit does not erase obligations that already exist.

The U.S. debt limit traces back to the Second Liberty Bond Act of 1917. That wartime law let the Treasury borrow to pay for World War I without returning to Congress to approve every bond issue. The single overall cap that exists today came later, through the Public Debt Act of 1939.

Before 1917, Congress typically authorized federal borrowing one issue at a time. A law would spell out the purpose of a loan and often its terms, such as the amount, the interest rate and the maturity. That approach suited a federal government that borrowed only occasionally and in modest amounts.

World War I changed the scale. The U.S. entered the war in April 1917. The cost of raising an army and supplying allies far outran tax revenue. Congress needed a faster way to raise money than passing a new bill for each sale of bonds.

The Second Liberty Bond Act, passed that September, gave Treasury Secretary William Gibbs McAdoo broad authority to issue bonds and other debt within limits Congress set. The government sold these Liberty Bonds directly to the public through large national campaigns. Ordinary households bought them alongside banks and businesses.

The 1917 law did not create one overall ceiling. It set caps on particular kinds of borrowing, and Congress adjusted those separate limits by category in the years that followed. The arrangement moved routine borrowing decisions from lawmakers to the Treasury. Congress still decided how much could be borrowed within each category.

The Public Debt Act of 1939 replaced that patchwork. It folded the separate limits into a single aggregate limit on federal debt. That change gave the Treasury freedom to choose the mix of bills, notes and bonds it issued, as long as the total stayed under the cap. The modern debt ceiling grew directly out of that 1939 framework.

That history explains why the ceiling works as a cap on total borrowing rather than a vote on individual loans. Lawmakers have raised, extended or suspended the limit many times since 1939, but the basic structure set that year remains in place.

Nearly all federal debt counts toward the debt limit. That covers money the government owes to outside investors and money it owes to its own trust funds, such as Social Security. The ceiling applies to the combined total, not to either piece on its own.

The first piece is debt held by the public. Most of it consists of marketable Treasury securities sold at auction. Treasury bills mature in a year or less. Treasury notes mature in two to 10 years, and Treasury bonds mature in 20 or 30 years. Savings bonds sold to households also fall into this category.

The buyers of that debt range widely. They include individuals, banks, pension funds, mutual funds, state and local governments, foreign governments and the Federal Reserve. Securities held by the Federal Reserve still count as debt held by the public, even though the Fed is part of the broader federal system.

The second piece is intragovernmental debt. Some federal programs collect dedicated revenue, such as payroll taxes, and credit it to trust funds. When a trust fund takes in more than it pays out, federal law directs it to invest the surplus in special-issue Treasury securities. These securities are not sold on the open market. Holders include the Social Security trust funds, Medicare's Hospital Insurance Trust Fund, the Civil Service Retirement and Disability Fund and the Military Retirement Fund.

Because both pieces count, the limit can bind even when debt held by the public sits well below it. Debt held by the public is the measure the Congressional Budget Office typically uses in its budget projections. The figure that matters for the ceiling, known as debt subject to limit, is larger.

The balance between the two pieces shifts over time. When Social Security collects more in payroll taxes than it pays in benefits, its trust funds buy new special-issue securities. That pushes debt subject to limit higher even if Treasury borrows nothing new from investors.

The reverse happens when a trust fund draws down its balance. The fund redeems its securities to pay benefits, and Treasury generally borrows from the public to raise that cash. Intragovernmental debt falls and debt held by the public rises. The total counted against the limit changes little.

The word nearly matters. Treasury reports debt subject to limit in its Monthly Statement of the Public Debt, and that figure differs slightly from total public debt outstanding. The gap comes from small technical adjustments, such as accounting for the discount at which Treasury bills are sold.

Extraordinary measures are accounting steps the Treasury Department uses to keep paying the government's bills for a period after federal debt reaches the statutory limit. They create temporary room under the cap. They do not raise the limit or give Treasury any new borrowing authority.

The tools work by reducing the amount of debt that counts against the limit. A large share of federal debt is owed to government trust funds and retirement accounts, which hold special Treasury securities. When Treasury stops issuing new securities to some of those funds, the debt subject to the limit falls or holds steady. That opens space to sell securities to the public and keep paying obligations.

The Thrift Savings Plan's G Fund is one of the main tools. The Thrift Savings Plan is the retirement savings plan for federal employees and members of the uniformed services. Its G Fund invests in special-issue Treasury securities. During a standoff, Treasury can suspend new investments in the G Fund.

The Civil Service Retirement and Disability Fund is another source of headroom. That fund pays pensions to retired federal civilian workers. Treasury can suspend new investments in it as well. Federal law also allows Treasury to redeem some of the fund's existing holdings ahead of schedule during these periods.

The process has a formal starting point. The Treasury secretary declares a debt issuance suspension period. That declaration is the legal trigger for using the retirement fund measures. Treasury notifies Congress when it begins.

Two smaller tools sit alongside the retirement funds. Treasury can suspend reinvestment of the dollar balance in the Exchange Stabilization Fund, which it uses for operations in currency markets. It can also halt sales of State and Local Government Series securities, which state and local governments buy to manage the proceeds of their own bond sales.

Federal law requires the affected funds to be made whole afterward. Once the limit is raised or suspended, Treasury issues the securities that were held back. It also credits the interest those securities would have earned. The restoration is designed so the funds do not lose money because of the maneuver.

The measures are finite. How much headroom they provide depends on the balances in the funds involved and on the pace of the government's tax receipts and spending. Once they are used up, only Treasury's remaining cash stands between the government and missed payments.

The X $TWTR-date is the day the Treasury Department would run out of both cash and extraordinary measures. After that point, it could no longer pay all of the federal government's obligations on time. The X-date is a projection, not a fixed deadline, and it moves as money flows in and out of the Treasury.

The X-date is a later and more consequential point than the day the debt limit is reached. Once debt hits the ceiling, Treasury can keep paying bills for a period by drawing down its cash balance and using accounting steps. The X-date marks the point at which those resources are gone.

No one can pin down the day in advance. It depends on how much the government collects in taxes and how much it pays out, and both totals swing from week to week. Federal cash flows are uneven. Large outflows such as Social Security benefits, Medicare payments, military pay and veterans' benefits cluster at particular points in the month. Tax receipts also arrive in bursts rather than at a steady pace.

The calendar plays a large role. Individual income tax payments due in mid-April can lift Treasury's cash position sharply. Quarterly estimated tax payments due in June add another inflow. A stronger or weaker tax season than forecast can move the X-date earlier or later.

Treasury keeps its operating cash in the Treasury General Account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. It reports daily receipts, outlays and the account balance in the Daily Treasury Statement. Forecasters use those reports to track how fast the cushion is shrinking.

Three sources have produced estimates during past standoffs. The Treasury secretary has sent letters to Congress warning when the department expects to exhaust its measures. The Congressional Budget Office and the Bipartisan Policy Center, a Washington, D.C.-based think tank, have published their own projections. Those estimates have shifted when tax receipts and spending ran higher or lower than expected. They have sometimes been presented as a window of possible dates rather than a single day.

The federal government has never reached the X-date. What would follow has therefore never been tested. Treasury would still have cash arriving each day, but not enough to cover every payment due. That gap would force decisions about delaying payments to bondholders, beneficiaries, contractors or federal employees. No law sets out an order for making those choices.

The 2011 debt ceiling standoff ended when President Barack Obama signed the Budget Control Act on Aug. 2, 2011. Three days later, on Aug. 5, Standard & Poor's lowered the U.S. credit rating from AAA to AA+. It was the first time S&P had ever downgraded the country.

The dispute ran for months. Republicans won control of the House in the 2010 midterm elections, while Democrats kept the Senate and the White House. The two sides negotiated over whether an increase in the borrowing limit should be paired with deficit reduction, and on what terms. Talks stretched into the summer while Treasury relied on extraordinary measures to keep paying the government's bills.

Treasury had identified Aug. 2 as the day its borrowing authority would run out. The deal was signed on that same day, leaving almost no margin before the government risked missing payments.

The Budget Control Act raised the debt limit in stages. It also set caps on discretionary spending that ran through fiscal 2021. A third piece created the Joint Select Committee on Deficit Reduction, a panel of 12 lawmakers split evenly between the two parties. Its job was to propose further deficit reduction for Congress to vote on.

The law built in a penalty for failure. If Congress did not enact a committee plan, automatic across-the-board spending cuts known as sequestration would take effect. The committee announced in November 2011 that it could not reach an agreement. Sequestration began in March 2013.

S&P based its decision on two main concerns. It judged that the deficit plan fell short of what would be needed to stabilize the country's medium-term debt path. It also pointed to the brinkmanship of the standoff as a sign that U.S. policymaking had become less stable and less predictable. S&P paired the AA+ rating with a negative outlook, signaling that a further cut was possible.

AAA is the top grade on S&P's scale, and AA+ sits one notch below it. The other two major agencies did not follow in 2011. Moody's $MCO kept its Aaa rating but assigned a negative outlook, and Fitch affirmed its AAA rating. That left the U.S. with a split rating among the big three agencies for years afterward.

Markets reacted unevenly. U.S. stocks fell sharply in the first trading days after the downgrade. Demand for Treasury securities held up, though, and Treasury yields declined.

The Fiscal Responsibility Act, signed June 3, 2023, ended that year's debt ceiling standoff by suspending the borrowing limit until Jan. 1, 2025. The law also set caps on federal spending. Fitch Ratings then lowered the U.S. credit rating from AAA to AA+ on Aug. 1, 2023.

The act came out of negotiations between President Joe Biden and House Speaker Kevin McCarthy. The House passed the bill on May 31, 2023. The Senate approved it on June 1, and Biden signed it into law on June 3.

The act did not lift the cap to a new dollar amount. Instead, it switched the cap off for a set period. That approach is called a suspension, and it works differently from a straightforward increase. An increase writes a higher number into law. A suspension removes the number entirely until a chosen date.

While a suspension is in effect, the Treasury Department can borrow what it needs to cover the government's obligations without running into a fixed ceiling. When the suspension ends, the limit does not return to its old level. It resets to the amount of debt outstanding on that date.

That reset folds any borrowing done during the suspension into the new ceiling. On paper, a suspension looks like a pause. In practice, it works as an increase. Lawmakers avoid voting on a specific dollar figure, yet the limit still rises by whatever the government borrowed in the interim.

The spending caps applied to discretionary spending — the portion of the budget Congress sets through annual appropriations bills. The act set those caps for fiscal years 2024 and 2025.

Fitch had placed the U.S. on rating watch negative in May 2023, while the negotiations were still underway. Its downgrade arrived after the standoff was over. In explaining the decision, Fitch pointed to an erosion of governance, including the repeated standoffs over the debt limit. The agency also cited expected fiscal deterioration and a high and growing government debt burden.

At the time of the Fitch decision, Moody's $MCO still rated the U.S. Aaa, its highest grade.

A rating of AA+ sits one notch below AAA and remains investment grade. The downgrade did not signal a default. It reflected Fitch's judgment that the U.S. no longer met its standard for the highest rating it assigns.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, raised the federal debt limit by $5 trillion, to roughly $41.1 trillion. The increase restored Treasury's borrowing room after the cap had returned in January 2025 at roughly $36.1 trillion.

That January figure was not a number lawmakers chose. The Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023 had suspended the limit until Jan. 1, 2025. When the suspension ended, the ceiling came back at the level of debt then outstanding. The limit and the debt were the same amount. Treasury had no fresh capacity to issue new debt from the moment the cap returned.

Treasury began using extraordinary measures in January 2025 to keep meeting the government's obligations. Those accounting steps postponed a breach but did not add any room under the cap. A lasting fix required Congress to pass a new law.

That law was a broad tax and spending package. It extended individual tax cuts first enacted in the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, alongside many other fiscal changes. Congress passed it through budget reconciliation. That procedure lets the Senate approve certain budget legislation with a simple majority. Reconciliation bills cannot be filibustered. As a result, the debt limit increase did not need the 60 votes most Senate bills require to move forward.

The 2025 change was an increase, not a suspension. A suspension sets a date when the limit comes back. An increase sets a dollar amount instead. The next deadline therefore depends on how fast federal borrowing climbs toward roughly $41.1 trillion, not on a date written into the statute.

The increase came after another change to the U.S. credit rating. Moody's $MCO lowered its U.S. rating from Aaa to Aa1 in May 2025. Moody's had rated U.S. government debt at Aaa since 1917. It had also been the last of the three major agencies to keep the U.S. at its top grade.

Three main alternatives to the debt ceiling have been proposed: invoking the 14th Amendment, minting a high-value platinum coin and repealing the cap entirely. None has been put into practice. Each remains a proposal rather than settled law.

The constitutional argument rests on Section 4 of the 14th Amendment, ratified in 1868 after the Civil War. Its text says the validity of the public debt of the U.S., authorized by law, shall not be questioned. The same section bars the U.S. and the states from paying any debt incurred in aid of insurrection against the country. Supporters of the 14th Amendment approach argue that a statutory cap cannot force the government to miss payments on debt Congress has already authorized. In their view, the limit would become unenforceable once Treasury could no longer meet those obligations.

No court has ruled on that argument. The amendment's text does not mention a borrowing cap. The statute that sets the debt limit remains in force.

The platinum coin idea comes from a provision of federal law, 31 U.S.C. 5112(k). That section gives the Treasury secretary authority to mint platinum coins and leaves their specifications to the secretary's discretion. Proponents argue this would let Treasury strike a single coin with a very high face value. Under the proposal, Treasury would deposit the coin at the Federal Reserve. Treasury keeps its main operating cash account, the Treasury General Account, at the Fed. It would then spend the resulting balance instead of issuing new debt. Treasury has never minted such a coin.

Abolishing the limit is the third option. The debt ceiling exists in statute, so Congress could repeal it through ordinary legislation. Borrowing would then follow from the spending and tax laws Congress passes, without a separate vote on the total amount the government owes.

Denmark offers a point of comparison. The country has a statutory debt limit, but it is set high enough that it does not bind.

The U.S. has followed a different path. Congress has kept its limit and adjusted it over time through increases and suspensions.
