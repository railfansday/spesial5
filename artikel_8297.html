Osprey ThrULite Pro 55L Review: Ultralight Comfort From a Backpacking Giant

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Osprey is making a serious push into ultralight backpacking with the new ThrULite Pro series. Long known for its comfortable, feature-rich backpacks, the company is now targeting the ultralight market, long dominated by smaller manufacturers.

The ThrULite Pro comes in 20-, 40-, and 55-liter versions, with the 55L weighing just 1 pound, 11 ounces. That weight is competitive with packs from brands like Hyperlite Mountain Gear, Zpacks, and Gossamer Gear, yet the pack still retains some of the comfort-focused design Osprey is known for.

I tested the ThrULite Pro 55L throughout Maine during late summer and fall, putting in plenty of hiking and guiding miles on rocky trails and arduous mountain terrain. My regular pack is a Hyperlite Mountain Gear Southwest 55, so I was particularly interested in how Osprey’s new offering would compare.

After several months of use, the biggest surprise was just how comfortable this pack is. There are a few design choices I’d change, but Osprey has put together a compelling ultralight option that deserves a closer look.

MSRP: $340* Weight: 27 ounces | 765 grams Materials: “NanoFly” proprietary nylon/UHMWPE ripstop fabric Volume: 20L, 40L, 55L (I reviewed the 55L) Suspension: Minimalist frame with running vest-style harness Closure: Roll-top

I used the ThrULite Pro 55 on hiking and guiding trips throughout Maine in late summer and fall, including long days on rocky, root-covered trails and mountainous terrain. As a Registered Maine Guide, I regularly carry extra equipment for clients, making comfort and accessibility particularly important.

Conditions were mostly dry, with limited rain that provided some opportunity to evaluate the pack’s weather resistance.

One of the most notable features of the ThrULite Pro is its running vest-inspired shoulder harness, a departure from the traditional straps found on most backpacking packs. The design creates a close-fitting, body-hugging carry that distributes weight comfortably across the shoulders and upper body.

Combined with the minimalist suspension system, the result is a pack that feels remarkably comfortable despite weighing less than two pounds. Osprey has eliminated its familiar trampoline-style back panel, cutting weight while maintaining a supportive carrying system with a slimmer foam and mesh panel.

At just 27 ounces, the ThrULite Pro 55 is firmly in ultralight territory. Osprey uses a proprietary lightweight fabric and strips away unnecessary features, including the traditional top lid or “brain,” to keep weight down.

The pack instead uses a roll-top closure, a common feature among ultralight backpacks. While this reduces weight and simplifies the design, I found the closure a bit awkward to use. It also doesn’t allow the pack to cinch down and compress as tightly as I’d like, particularly when carrying smaller loads.

The ThrULite Pro maintains a relatively minimalist layout but offers plenty of exterior storage. The side water bottle pockets are tall and feature adjustable cinch closures at the top, allowing you to tighten them around bottles or other gear for a more secure hold. There’s also a large, deep rear pocket that provides generous storage for items you want to keep separate from the main compartment, such as a rain jacket or other frequently used gear.

The pack also includes two hipbelt pockets, one with a traditional zipper and the other with an elastic closure for quick access. Unfortunately, both are extremely small, limiting their usefulness for snacks, electronics, or other frequently accessed items. It’s an odd design choice considering how practical the rest of the exterior storage is.

Osprey’s proprietary fabric provides a reasonable degree of weather resistance without the weight of heavier traditional pack materials. During my limited rain testing, the fabric shed water effectively, with precipitation beading up and running off the surface.

That said, it didn’t appear to offer quite the same level of protection as Dyneema Composite Fabric (DCF), which is used in many ultralight packs. I didn’t experience prolonged or particularly heavy rain, so I can’t speak to how well the ThrULite Pro would hold up in sustained wet conditions. For longer trips, I’d still recommend using a pack liner to protect critical gear.

Comfort is easily the standout feature of the ThrULite Pro. After plenty of hiking and guiding miles, I found the pack extremely comfortable, with a stable, well-balanced carry that feels more substantial than its 27-ounce weight suggests.

The running vest-style harness and minimalist suspension work well together, providing a secure fit without the bulk of a traditional backpacking suspension system. Even over long days on Maine’s rugged trails, the pack remained comfortable and moved naturally with my body.

Coming from the Hyperlite Southwest 55, I was impressed by how well Osprey managed to combine low weight with the kind of carrying comfort the brand is known for.

Osprey has managed to bring a 55-liter pack below two pounds without making it feel overly stripped down. At 27 ounces, the ThrULite Pro compares favorably with many established ultralight packs.

The minimalist suspension, lightweight fabric, and lack of a traditional brain all contribute to the low weight. While there are lighter options available, the ThrULite Pro strikes a good balance between weight savings and comfort.

I’ve generally preferred minimalist backpacks, and the ThrULite Pro follows that approach. There aren’t a lot of unnecessary features or complicated adjustments, and the overall construction feels straightforward.

Osprey also brings the advantage of its established warranty and repair program, which could make the ThrULite Pro especially appealing to hikers considering their first ultralight pack.

My biggest complaint with the ThrULite Pro is the hipbelt pockets. Both are so small that they’re almost useless for practical storage.

I like having the option of a zippered pocket and an elastic closure, but neither provides enough room for much beyond the smallest essentials. When guiding or hiking long days, I frequently reach for snacks and other small items without stopping, so accessible hipbelt storage is particularly important.

Larger pockets would add minimal weight while making the pack considerably more functional. It’s a frustrating oversight on an otherwise well-designed backpack.

While I appreciate the weight savings from eliminating the traditional top lid, the roll-top system could use some refinement.

I found it somewhat awkward to close, and more importantly, it doesn’t allow the pack to compress as much as I’d like when carrying a smaller load. Compared with my HMG Southwest, which has a straightforward roll-top and side compression system, the Osprey feels less adaptable as gear volume changes.

It’s not a dealbreaker, but it’s something I noticed regularly during testing.

The ThrULite Pro 55L is an impressive entry into the ultralight market from one of backpacking’s biggest manufacturers. At just 27 ounces, Osprey has created a pack that competes with established ultralight offerings while maintaining the comfort that has long been one of the company’s strengths.

After several months of hiking and guiding in Maine, carrying comfort was easily the highlight. The running vest-style harness and minimalist suspension provide an excellent fit, and the pack feels comfortable over long days on rugged terrain.

As previously mentioned, there are a few things I’d modify: the hipbelt pockets are frustratingly small, and the roll-top closure doesn’t compress as effectively as I’d like. I’d also want more time in sustained rain before making any definitive claims about its weather resistance.

Still, these are relatively minor drawbacks compared with how well the pack carries. For hikers looking to move into ultralight backpacking without giving up comfort, the ThrULite Pro 55L is well worth considering.
