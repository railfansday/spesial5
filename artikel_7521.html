The Supreme Court and profanity

Around 2 p.m. on October 30, 1973, John Douglas was driving with his 15-year-old son when he heard a litany of profanity come over the radio. New York’s WBAI, a nonprofit radio station, was airing a program on modern attitudes toward language. Near the end, the station played George Carlin’s “Filthy Words” routine, which begins with the comedian saying he’d been thinking about “the words you couldn’t say on the public airwaves; the ones you definitely wouldn’t say, ever.” He went on to repeat those words several times, contrasting them with words like “bastards,” “hell,” and “damn,” which were mostly acceptable, and showing how even some of his list of seven were perfectly fine in other contexts. His point was to show the absurdity of profanity.

The station later said it had chosen Carlin’s routine because it was an “incisive satirical view of the subject under discussion,” in the style of other great satirists like Mark Twain or Mort Sahl. Carlin, the station explained, was doing more than “mouthing obscenities.” He was “using words to satirize as harmless and essentially silly our attitudes towards those words.”

Before playing the routine, the host had warned that it included language some listeners might find offensive and suggested they return in 15 minutes if they were inclined toward offense. But about a month later, John Douglas complained to the Federal Communications Commission about the transmission which he said was “garbage” and had been broadcast in the presence of his “young son.” He was the only listener to do so. A member of Morality in Media who frequently listened to the station to “see how far” they’d go, Douglas later acknowledged that he had laughed out loud at the bit but “did not appreciate WBIA’s ‘smart-alecky’ on-air provocations.”

The previous year, Carlin had been arrested after performing an earlier version of the same routine, but a judge threw out the charge. This time, the FCC found the broadcast “indecent,” and put its order in the station’s license file.

That order ended up before the Supreme Court.

Early in its order, the FCC acknowledged that Congress had forbade it from censoring broadcasts. But Congress had also prohibited the broadcast of “obscene, indecent, or profane” language, and the Commission thought Carlin’s broadcast fit the bill. In the FCC’s view, the ban on “indecent” language covered not just obscenity, but also patently offensive language when there was a reasonable risk of children listening. Late at night, when children were unlikely to be listening, the same material might be okay if it appeared in a work of serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value.

Two commissioners thought even that was too permissive. Charlotte Reid believed “this language to be totally inappropriate for broadcast at anytime” and thought the commission’s standard did “not go far enough.” Commissioner James Quello took much the same view. “Garbage is garbage,” he wrote, whether children were listening or not.

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, however, set aside the order in a 2-1 vote, with one judge believing it violated the statutory ban on censorship and the other saying it violated the First Amendment. The FCC appealed to the Supreme Court.

At oral argument, which was held in April of 1978, several justices made their distaste for Carlin’s bit plain. When Pacifica’s lawyer stressed that the station had warned listeners beforehand and aired the monologue as part of an educational program about language, Chief Justice Warren Burger incredulously asked, “This is educational in your view?” And when counsel defended the material’s artistic value, Justice Thurgood Marshall responded, “Well, I’m not an expert, but if that’s artistic, deliver me.” Justice William Rehnquist, for his part, tested the limits of Pacifica’s position with a hypothetical involving a single four-letter word repeated for an hour. Would the FCC be powerless to prohibit that? Justice John Paul Stevens, however, seemed troubled by the potential reach of the FCC’s position, given that the prohibition on indecency could be enforced through criminal law.

One striking exchange concerned the Justice Department’s position to argue separately, and more narrowly, than the FCC. The solicitor general’s office urged that the First Amendment prevented the government from barring particular words outright for a substantial part of the day without regard to context, even if the FCC could constitutionally regulate “indecency” to some extent. Rehnquist sharply asked why the Justice Department was arguing against the FCC, what interest it had in seeking a narrow construction of an act of Congress, and whether the solicitor general, as the government’s lawyer, could ever argue that an act of Congress was unconstitutional.

Justice Lewis Powell also pressed the DOJ on the reasons behind its departure from the FCC, noting that the DOJ had supported the FCC in the court below. “That is true, and that is an embarrassment to us,” Deputy Solicitor General Louis Claiborne acknowledged, suggesting it was because the matter had been handled there by the Antitrust Division rather than the Criminal Division. Another explanation, which he did not mention, was that the administration had changed.

The court ultimately ruled 5-4 that the FCC could constitutionally treat this broadcast as “indecent” under the governing statute, and that the government could constitutionally prohibit indecency even though it fell short of legal obscenity. Stevens wrote for Burger, Justice Harry Blackmun, Powell, and Rehnquist, having apparently struggled with the case and flip-flopped before settling against Pacifica. The Commission had treated the piece as a nuisance, and, as the court had observed in Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co., a nuisance could be as simple as “a pig in the parlor instead of the barnyard.” He concluded that “when the Commission finds that a pig has entered the parlor, the exercise of its regulatory power does not depend on proof that the pig is obscene.” Though the holding expanded the government’s power to regulate speech, Stevens emphasized broadcasting’s pervasive presence in the home and its accessibility to children, the fact the piece was broadcast during the daytime, and the narrowness of the holding. He did not address late night broadcasts or the “occasional expletive.”

Stevens had only three votes for some parts of his opinion. Powell, joined by Blackmun, wrote separately to object to Stevens’ suggestion that the level of protection that speech receives should depend on a judge’s perception of that speech’s value. For them, the combination of repeated, patently offensive language, an afternoon broadcast, and children’s ready access was enough to resolve the case. Powell also addressed the concern that the decision could end up “reduc[ing] the adult population . . . to [hearing] only what is fit for children”: adults could buy tapes or hear the same words at theaters.

Justice Potter Stewart dissented, joined by Justices William Brennan, Byron White, and Marshall. Stewart would have resolved the case on statutory grounds. He read “indecent” to reach only obscene material, and everyone agreed that Carlin’s monologue fell outside that category. His opinion recalled his concurrence in Jacobellis v. Ohio, where he voted to overturn an obscenity conviction for showing a French film with an explicit love scene. Stewart understood obscenity narrowly, but declined to define it, famously observing in that case, “I know it when I see it, and the motion picture involved in this case is not that.”

Brennan wrote a separate dissent, joined by Marshall, that went further. He agreed with Stewart on the statute and said he would ordinarily refrain from addressing the constitutional question, but he found “the Court’s misapplication of fundamental First Amendment principles so patent, and its attempt to impose its notions of propriety on the whole of the American people so misguided,” that he was “unable to remain silent.”

Brennan also questioned the majority’s reliance on protecting children. He read the court’s precedents as allowing the government to shield children from material that was obscene as to them. In his view, Carlin’s routine fell outside that category. Keeping it off the radio during the afternoon cut off access for adults as well as children. And while the majority had invoked parents’ right to raise their children as they saw fit, Brennan thought the FCC had somewhat ironically made the choice for them.

Finally, he remained unconvinced that the court’s reasoning would reach only cases like Carlin’s. Among the things it could sweep in were “literary works, novels, poems, and plays by the likes of Shakespeare, Joyce, Hemingway, Ben Jonson, Henry Fielding, Robert Burns, and Chaucer,” “a good deal of political speech, such as the Nixon tapes,” and even portions of the Bible. In his view, the public, and not the court, should have the power to weed out “worthless and offensive” speech in a marketplace “unsullied by the censor’s hand.”

Brennan and Marshall had joined together in another Supreme Court opinion involving the F-word. In Cohen v. California, they joined Justice John Harlan II’s opinion protecting the right to wear a jacket emblazoned with “Fuck the Draft.” Stewart joined it too (not surprisingly, Burger and Blackmun dissented). Brennan brought Cohen straight into his Pacifica dissent; as he made clear, changing the words can change what the speaker communicates, something that applies especially well to Carlin because the taboo words were themselves the subject of the routine. And Brennan thought telling listeners to buy a record or pay to see Carlin live missed what it meant to hear the routine on the radio.

In closing, Brennan wrote that the majority had only “confirm[ed] Carlin’s prescience as a social commentator.” (It was interesting that Marshall, who sounded distinctly unimpressed by the claim of artistic merit at oral argument, joined him, perhaps especially here.) As Brennan pointed out, Carlin had made fun of the rules about which words you could say on the air, and the court had exemplified the very attitude he was satirizing. To top it all off: Appended to the opinion is the transcript of Carlin’s routine, giving his “indecent” words a permanent home in the U.S. Reports.

At the 2002 Billboard Music Awards on Fox, Cher dispatched the critics who’d spent decades predicting her downfall with “So f*** ’em.” At the 2003 awards, Nicole Richie asked whether anyone had tried getting cow manure out of a Prada purse, then used the F-word to describe how hard it was. The next year, after Bono called U2’s Golden Globe win “really, really f***ing brilliant,” the FCC announced that even a single expletive could be indecent, and it later applied that approach to the two earlier Fox broadcasts.

In 2009, the Supreme Court took up the dispute in FCC v. Fox Television Stations. Writing for a 5-4 majority, Justice Antonin Scalia held that the FCC had adequately explained its change in policy under the Administrative Procedure Act, leaving the First Amendment question open. Justice Clarence Thomas joined the majority but wrote separately to question Pacifica’s holding. On remand, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 2nd Circuit struck down the policy as unconstitutionally vague.

The case returned to the Supreme Court in 2012 and all eight participating justices agreed on the result (Justice Sonia Sotomayor recused herself). Justice Anthony Kennedy’s opinion held that the FCC had failed to give Fox fair notice that fleeting expletives could trigger an indecency finding, but once again left both Pacifica and the First Amendment question open. Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg wrote separately to say that Pacifica was wrong from the start and deserved reconsideration. But while the court has had several cases involving curse words since, it has yet to take up the question directly.

In 2004, the FCC dismissed complaints related to the airing of Saving Private Ryan on the basis that the film’s curse words helped convey the soldiers’ experience. But just two years later, it slapped a station with $15,000 in fines for airing a Martin Scorsese documentary about Blues music that contained profanity. In 2020, the FCC received over 1,300 complaints about Jennifer Lopez and Shakira’s halftime show, though it never took any action against the network. And in February 2026, FCC requested transcripts of Bad Bunny’s Superbowl halftime performance before ultimately finding no violation.

Half a century after Carlin’s seven dirty words, the FCC’s continued and seemingly arbitrary application of its indecency laws shows the prescience of Brennan’s dissent and the need to finally answer the question of whether the Constitution prohibits the “dominant culture” from trying to force “groups who do not share its mores to conform to its way of acting, thinking, and speaking.”

Anastasia Boden is a Senior Attorney at the Pacific Legal Foundation, where she brings civil rights lawsuits that advance the Constitution’s promises of equality and economic opportunity.
