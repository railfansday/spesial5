Beyond Bars and Into Being

Updated November 3, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

Midway through the 2024 film "Sing Sing," a group of men rehearses a play in a classroom at a maximum-security prison in New York (Kwedar, 2024). Some of the actors learned their craft while they were incarcerated. They are alumni of Rehabilitation Through the Arts (RTA), the theater program the film portrays, and on screen they play versions of themselves.

RTA makes a striking claim about what happens after its participants go home: only around 5% return to prison (ABC7 New York, 2022). Nationally, 27% of people released from prison are reincarcerated within three years, down from 35% for those released in 2008 (Council of State Governments [CSG] Justice Center, 2024). The national figure is moving in the right direction. RTA’s figure suggests the arts may be reaching people in ways standard rehabilitation misses.

One possibility is that creative engagement addresses barriers conventional re-entry programs tend to overlook: trauma, a disrupted sense of self, and strained emotional capacity. The idea is promising, and the research behind it is still young.

The past two decades have brought new investment in re-entry, including the Second Chance Act of 2008, which funded education, mental health care, and substance use treatment for people leaving prison (CSG Justice Center, 2024). Attending to these needs is valuable; however, far less attention goes to what years inside do to a person’s mind and body. “The legal system, and incarceration more specifically, produces a legacy of intergenerational trauma and health harms,” says Dr. Rebecca Fix, an Assistant Professor at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health. “Instead of addressing the traumatic circumstances that led many to incarceration, prisons exacerbate those harms.”

In a study of about 4,000 incarcerated men, 44.7% reported physical trauma in childhood (Wolff & Shi, 2012). Current rates of post-traumatic stress disorder are roughly five times more common among men in prison than in the general population, and about eight times more common among women (Baranyi et al., 2018). Experiences in prison typically perpetuate this trauma and distress. Victimization, solitary confinement, and coercion experienced in prison are each associated with PTSD (Piper & Berle, 2019). Among people released after long sentences, researchers have identified a lasting cluster of symptoms that includes chronic distrust, difficulty making decisions, and trouble sustaining relationships (Liem & Kunst, 2013). Poet Reginald Dwayne Betts was incarcerated at 16 and spent 14 months in solitary confinement. His collection Felon traces how that time follows a person long after release (Betts, 2019). These are the very capacities people need to rebuild their lives at home, work, and in their families, and most re-entry services do little to restore them.

Coming home after prison means building a new story about who one is, one in which a person sees themselves as capable, creative, and worthy of a future (Maruna, 2001). Creative work offers a place to start writing that story (Fleetwood, 2020). For example, Jimmy Santiago Baca (2001) entered prison unable to read and taught himself to read and write there, emerging as a poet. Shaka Senghor (2016), who spent seven of his 19 years in prison in solitary confinement, credits literature and self-examination with helping him confront his past.

Making art engages the body and the senses along with language. In one small study of adults outside prison, 45 minutes of art-making lowered levels of the stress hormone cortisol in about three-quarters of participants (Kaimal et al., 2016). In another study, dance movement therapy lowered depression scores by 72% in 12 weeks (Jeong et al., 2005).

Theater and writing give people a place to try on a new identity. On stage, someone the system calls an inmate becomes an actor, a writer, a member of an ensemble, and an audience watches that change happen. Narrative identity research links redemptive life stories to well-being (McAdams & McLean, 2013), and performance may be one way to rehearse such a story out loud (Maruna, 2001). Ashley Lucas (2020), former director of the Prison Creative Arts Project, draws on years of directing theater in prisons to describe how performance builds community and hope in settings designed to strip both away. In Baltimore, Center Stage launched a year-long theater program in 2024 with young people held at the city’s Juvenile Justice Center (Baltimore Center Stage, n.d.). Stella Adler Studio of Acting has been partnering with the Rikers Island Correctional Facility since 2014. Every week across the year, a cadre of trained artists – including teachers, writers, voice and speech experts, movement coaches and directors – visit multiple facilities to reach hundreds of inmates annually. Ensemble work asks for turn-taking, negotiation, and impulse control, too. In an evaluation of California prison arts programs, participants reported gains in emotional control, time management, and self-confidence, and disciplinary reports fell (Brewster, 2014).

An evaluation of the University of Denver Prison Arts Initiative concluded that arts programs can “invoke liberatory experiences for participants” (Littman & Sliva, 2021, as cited in Minnesota Prison Writing Workshop, n.d.), and other studies report fewer depressive symptoms and a greater sense of self-respect and purpose among participants (Minnesota Prison Writing Workshop, n.d.).

While peer-reviewed research conducted in carceral systems is thin, though growing, a meta-analysis of arts therapies in forensic settings, including 11 randomized or controlled trials, found improvements in psychiatric symptoms and in social and psychological functioning (Abbing et al., 2023). In a randomized trial with 200 men in a Chinese prison, group music therapy reduced anxiety and depression and raised self-esteem (Chen et al., 2016).

The punitive model assumes that people choose crime and that harsher consequences will deter them. Research offers little support for that assumption: compared with non-custodial sentences, imprisonment has a null or slightly criminogenic effect on reoffending (Nagin et al., 2009). Scholars and organizers including Angela Y. Davis (2003), Ruth Wilson Gilmore (2022), and Mariame Kaba (2021) call for moving resources away from prisons and toward the people and institutions that help communities thrive.

The arts are one of those investments. “Alternatives to incarceration, including restorative and transformative approaches, ask what people and communities need to heal,” Fix explains. “For many, creative expression is part of that answer, as an alternative to the traumatic carceral environment, though arts programming within prisons can also offer meaningful support where alternatives don't yet exist.” They give people a structured, safe outlet for processing difficult experiences, room to rehearse a new identity, and a way to contribute something others value. The arts also help people who have been incarcerated speak for themselves. Ear Hustle, a podcast about life inside created at San Quentin State Prison by Earlonne Woods, who was then incarcerated there, and artist Nigel Poor, reached a national audience and was a 2020 Pulitzer Prize finalist (The Pulitzer Prizes, 2020).

The arts can be a powerful tool to support the social and emotional health of incarcerated individuals. For the men who learned to act at Sing Sing and then played themselves on screen, creative work was part of how they rebuilt their lives. Far more people leaving prison deserve the same chance.
