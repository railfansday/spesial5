Cumin-laced fizzy sodas are giving cola giants a tough fight in India

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Lahori Zeera will close this year with $115-120m in revenue

It began as an impromptu kitchen experiment.

Saurabh Munjal, Nikhil Doda and Saurabh Bhutna - three cousins in India's north-western Punjab state - were messing about at home post dinner one evening, when Doda casually brewed up a tangy cumin-flavoured fizzy soda using regular pantry ingredients.

They enjoyed its earthy, piquant taste so much that they decided to explore options to sell it commercially.

Cumin-laced sodas had always been sold at street-side stalls in India, but it was a broken market of local companies. Not many had built a carbonated beverage brand around the flavour.

The trio persuaded their families to invest some capital and launched Lahori Zeera in 2017. Zeera is the Hindi name for cumin.

Almost a decade on, the drink has become a bit of a sensation in India, challenging the entrenched monopoly of global cola giants, especially in the all-important mass-market ten-rupee ($0.11; Â£0.078) beverage category - it will close this year with revenues of $115-120m.

Its popularity reflects a growing consumer preference for distinctly Indian flavours and drinks perceived to be more healthy, say analysts.

Lahori Zeera is one of several cumin-flavoured soda brands that have mushroomed across the country over the past decade.

There's Jeeru from Mumbai-based Xotik Frujus, Campa Jeera from billionaire Mukesh Ambani-owned Campa Cola and at least half-a-dozen other variants from regional players including Fizz Jeera Masala from House of Bindu.

Bindu launched their cumin soda nearly 25 years ago, but sales were flat until some years ago, according to Megha Shankar, a director with the company.

"Now it is the fastest growing category among carbonated beverages," Shankar told the BBC. "Its contribution to our revenues has doubled from 20% to 40% in the last five years."

Soaring demand for the product has pushed House of Bindu to widen its footprint across the country, after being largely based in southern India for over two decades.

Parle Agro, external, one of India's oldest consumer brands, also rolled out its own cumin soda brand Dhishoom nationally last year after exclusively selling it in rural markets since 2012.

Online orders for these fizzy drinks, meanwhile, were up 900% this summer according to a report from one of India's online delivery companies.

Cumin is a warm, earthy spice widely used in Indian cuisine

The cumin frenzy is reshaping India's non-alcoholic beverages market, which was valued at $32 billion in 2024 and projected to double by 2033.

According to a report from Kotak Mutual Fund earlier this year, Lahori Zeera, Campa and other smaller challengers - now dubbed as India's "ethnic beverages market" - collectively doubled their share of India's carbonated soft-drinks market to around 15% by September last year, while the combined market share of Coca-Cola and PepsiCo fell from roughly 93% to 85%.

"This is a "Jio moment" for beverages. Just as telecom incumbents bled market share when data prices crashed, beverage incumbents are bleeding volume," the Kotak report said, drawing an analogy to the disruption caused by the entry of Reliance-owned Jio Telecom in India's telecoms market in 2016.

Saurabh Munjal, CEO of Archian Foods which manufactures Lahori Zeera, told the BBC that the company has "always been in a situation where we are sold out each year. We've doubled our capacity to 1.2 million bottles per day last year and are still unable to meet demand".

For decades, the colas have had a dominant presence in India, appealing to the aspirational part of the population as lifestyle products that were cool to consume.

With millions of dollars in marketing budgets splashed out on billboards, Bollywood stars and cricket championships, they sold a very western idea of hydration to India, says Munjal.

But home-grown brands are now mounting a serious challenge with a combination of smart distribution, attractive pricing and a turn towards nostalgic Indian flavours such as cumin, raw mango, kokum fruit and tamarind.

Munjal says that for long, there has been a tendency among consumer brands in India to either westernise Indian products or "Indianise" western products. But with Lahori Zeera, which uses the tagline "apna desi thanda", meaning India's own cold drink, his desire is to change that.

Shankar says her brand has also recently launched a zero-sugar version of their cumin soda to capture the growing market for healthier non-cola drinks.

Global giants like Coca-Cola and Pepsi have long held a monopoly on India's carbonated beverages market

This success notwithstanding, sustaining this winning trajectory will not be easy for these ethnic beverage brands, say analysts. The very factors that propelled their rise could also limit growth.

"The million-dollar question is how big can the cumin-flavoured drink market become. And I suspect it won't become as big as the colas," Rajiv Agarwal, a professor at Mumbai-based SP Jain Institute of Management, who advises family businesses on strategies to succeed against multi-national corporations, told the BBC.

The 10-rupee price point, which accounts for the bulk of sales volumes in India's cumin-flavoured carbonated drinks market, is another double-edged sword.

While it has driven up sales, the soaring cost of plastic, packaging, sugar and other raw material amid the global energy shock could start to hit margins, which are already very thin.

Raising prices, meanwhile, could affect volumes, forcing brands to further reduce bottle sizes, something the cola companies had resorted to a few years ago.

Then there is always the threat of bigger, deep-pocketed players coming in to disrupt the category.

"Competition is extremely aggressive," says Agarwal. And the established global players are better organised in terms of systems, processes, distribution channels and bottling arrangements.

Coca Cola, which had acquired the cumin-flavoured Rimzim brand in the 1990s, is strengthening its play in this category and reportedly doubled production last year.

But the challenges haven't deterred the ambitions of the homegrown players.

"Indian flavours need to find a global spotlight," says Munjal, who is looking to tap the global market in the coming years.

"For generations, we've grown up drinking colas of the world, but I hope the next generations will drink the zeeras of India."
