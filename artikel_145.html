Mushroom lovers in UK told to avoid grow kits owing to invasive species risk

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Popular edible golden oyster classed as high-risk invasive species because of negative impact on woodlands

They have been viewed for years as the ideal Christmas gift for the culinary-minded, but experts have now warned that grow-your-own mushroom kits should be blacklisted from a spot under the tree.

An international group of mycologists, including experts from the Royal Horticultural Society, have said many of these kits, which allow people to easily grow mushrooms at home as a delicious addition to stir-fries and other dishes, contain invasive species.

The edible golden oyster mushroom, a favourite option, has been found growing in UK forests at breakneck speed. It has now been officially classed as a high-risk invasive species because of its negative impact on woodlands.

Scientists have said the golden oyster has established itself in the wild in at least two UK forests, with the most likely pathway of introduction being escape or release from grow kits.

Native to east Asia, the mushroom species has been found to be invasive on other continents as it has wind-dispersed spores that can travel thousands of miles, making it impossible to stop its spread. It arrived in the US in 2012 and has spread across 25 states and approximately 772,200 sq miles (2m sq km). Because it establishes itself so successfully, the species has been shown to as much as halve the beneficial wood-inhabiting native fungi.

Despite its invasive nature, it is still legal to sell the golden oyster commercially, with grow kits widely available online and through large retailers. The RHS no longer exhibits the golden oyster at events, nor does it sell the mushroom in its shops, instead spotlighting native species that can be grown at home including lion’s mane.

Dr Jassy Drakulic, a senior plant pathologist at the RHS, said: “The growing public interest in fungi is something to celebrate, but as they become more widely grown and sold, we must also recognise the biosecurity risks that can accompany them. The escape of the golden oyster mushroom in the UK highlights a significant gap in how invasive fungi are managed.

“Fungi are difficult to track in the wild, living mostly unseen until they fruit, so we need stronger policies to prevent introducing species that can permanently alter native ecosystems. Developing a dedicated strategy for invasive fungi is essential if we are to safeguard biodiversity for the future.”

Invasive species are one of the world’s leading causes of biodiversity loss. A famous example in Britain is the grey squirrel. It was imported from the US in the 1800s and spread a fatal disease known as squirrelpox to the native red squirrels. As a result, red squirrels have become extinct in many parts of Britain.

Aishwarya Veerabahu, a mycologist at the University of Wisconsin-Madison in the US, said: “Every golden oyster mushroom kit sold gives the golden oyster a chance to establish in nature and spread from there. Mushroom kits helped the golden oyster rapidly spread across the US and Canada; in less than a decade, it colonised vast areas of North America, and its full ecological impacts are still unfolding.

“Fungi are the foundation of forests and we’re beginning to understand how invasive species are impacting local biodiversity and disrupting their complex relationships. Golden oysters haven’t spread too far in the UK yet, so there’s a fighting chance of preventing it from becoming invasive. We’re asking people to hold off on buying mushroom kits to protect the UK’s fungal biodiversity and favourite native edibles.”
