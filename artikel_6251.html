Air pollution’s link to blood sugar has a surprising temperature twist

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

A European study links air pollution and temperature swings to higher blood sugar, offering clues to how environmental conditions may interact.

Air pollution is usually discussed as a threat to the lungs, while heat is treated as a seasonal health risk.

In a study of more than 21,000 Europeans without diabetes, particle pollution and temperature variability were linked to higher long-term blood sugar. The association with each depended on exposure to the other.

In modeled scenarios, reducing air pollution was associated with a larger drop in blood sugar under warmer, more variable conditions.

The study found no measurable association between residential greenery and blood sugar.

Nicole Probst-Hensch and her colleagues at the Swiss Tropical and Public Health Institute and the University of Basel led the work.

Each person was measured once or a few times. So the study can show that pollution, temperature and blood sugar go together, not that one causes the other.

The team pooled nine long-running studies from Sweden, the Netherlands, Spain, the Czech Republic, Germany and Switzerland.

Together, they held 33,989 blood samples from 21,259 people, drawn between 1999 and 2021.

Ages ran from eight-year-olds in a Dutch birth cohort to the oldest adults in a Stockholm study.

The largest single group was 5,464 people from the GCAT study at the Germans Trias i Pujol Research Institute (IGTP) near Barcelona.

Each blood sample was tested for glycated hemoglobin, or HbA1c, which reflects average blood sugar over the previous two to three months.

Anyone with a diabetes diagnosis, diabetes medication or a reading in the diabetic range was left out, allowing the researchers to examine environmental associations in people without diabetes.

Each home address was then matched to a year of air pollution, greenery and temperature, from Europe-wide maps built by the EXPANSE project.

Of the three pollutants on the maps, only PM10 showed a consistent link to higher blood sugar.

PM10 includes airborne particles up to 10 micrometers across, including both fine and coarser particles. Nitrogen dioxide showed no consistent association, while the link with the finer PM2.5 was less consistent.

The other signal was how much the daily temperature swung over a year, more than the average temperature itself.

Their statistical method was built to find exposures that act together, and it did. Coarse particles and temperature swings interacted, so the effect of one depended on the level of the other.

The temperature-swing terms held up in nine of ten rounds of re-testing on different slices of the data.

One interaction, fine particles with average temperature, did not, and the authors treat it with caution.

Why would coarse particles matter for blood sugar? Larger particles get trapped in the upper airways, are moved up to the mouth and are swallowed, the authors wrote.

That fits other studies suggesting air pollution may alter gut bacteria.

The team then ran three futures through its model. The first cut coarse particles and nitrogen dioxide by 10 micrograms per cubic meter of air, and fine particles by 5.

These reductions were intended to approximate progress toward World Health Organization guideline levels. In the model, they were associated with lower blood sugar.

The second scenario raised the yearly average temperature by 0.9°F (0.5°C) and widened daily temperature swings by 1.8°F (1°C). The model associated these changes with higher blood sugar.

The third scenario combined cleaner air with warmer, more variable temperatures. Together, these changes were associated with a larger decrease in blood sugar than cleaner air alone.

The researchers expressed these modeled changes in standard deviations, a measure of how much blood sugar varied across the group.

Cleaner air alone was associated with a decrease of about 0.06 standard deviations, compared with about 0.07 under the combined scenario.

That is small for any one person. But most future diabetes comes from the many people at low or moderate risk, the authors wrote, so a small shift across a population matters.

The pattern was stronger in the 28,089 samples from nonsmokers and absent in the 5,900 from smokers.

Smoking causes inflammation of its own, the authors wrote, which may mask the effect.

Trees and parks are the usual prescription for a hot, polluted city. The study measured greenness around each home from satellite images, and the distance to the nearest green space.

Neither was linked to blood sugar, on its own or in combination with pollution or heat.

The links between pollution, temperature variability and blood sugar appeared almost exclusively in cities. In the less urban half of the samples, no stable link appeared.

The authors put that down to higher pollution and stronger heat islands in urban areas.

Natàlia Blay, a postdoctoral researcher at GCAT and a co-author, put the result in a wider frame.

“Including temperature in this approach is particularly relevant in the current context of climate change, where environmental exposures cannot be understood in isolation,” she said.

Diet and physical activity were not included in the models.

Participants had blood sugar measured once or a few times, so the study could not establish whether pollution caused higher blood sugar or whether other differences between people explained the association.

When the team split the group by body weight, the links disappeared in both halves.

The authors suggested that body fat might help explain the association, although this analysis could not establish its role.

The temperature maps began in 2003, so earlier blood samples were matched to later weather. HbA1c reflects only the three months before a blood draw, while the exposures were yearly averages.

Traffic noise, which other studies have tied to diabetes, had no Europe-wide map and was left out.

Whether cleaning the air of a hot city lowers its diabetes rate is the question the scenarios point at but can’t answer.

The authors wrote that noise belongs in the next round of this work, once Europe-wide noise maps exist.

The full study was published in the journal Environment International.
