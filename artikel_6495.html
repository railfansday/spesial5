Fatal cases of Ebola often share a striking pattern

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Ebola may trap immune messages inside infected cells, keeping the body's defenses active longer and potentially worsening illness.

A deadly virus such as Ebola might be expected to kill by shutting down the body’s defenses.

Ebola virus does make proteins that block a key signal the body uses to fight infection. The pattern in patients, however, is the opposite.

People who die from Ebola often show the highest activity in the genes that fight viruses.

Scientists have struggled to explain this for years.

A new study points to the tiny droplets of protein and RNA that Ebola virus builds inside infected cells. These droplets are known as viral condensates, and the cell’s own immune messages become trapped inside them.

Once trapped, the messages are not broken down when they should be.

The defense therefore remains switched on. That prolonged response may be part of what makes patients so ill.

The findings come during a major outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

As of November 17, the CDC reported 11,613 confirmed cases and 5,678 confirmed deaths in the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

The outbreak involves Bundibugyo virus, a close relative of the virus studied here.

Two of them make proteins called NP and VP35. Both attach to viral RNA and to each other, gathering into droplets that have no outer membrane.

During an infection they can take up between one-fifth and one-third of a cell.

Jingru Fang led the work in the Saphire Lab at the La Jolla Institute for Immunology. Fang now works at Princeton University’s Omenn-Darling Bioengineering Institute.

“When birds land in a new place, they make nests where they can raise their babies,” said Fang.

“You can imagine viral condensates as nests for a virus.”

To study the droplets, NP and VP35 were introduced into human kidney cells. The actual virus was left out, and the droplets still formed.

The messenger RNA in those cells was then stained. Messenger RNA carries the instructions a cell uses to make a protein.

Messenger RNA was more concentrated inside the droplets than in the surrounding cell fluid.

In human cells, the buildup came to roughly 40%. Kidney cells from Egyptian fruit bats showed an even greater buildup.

When NP was changed so that it could no longer attach to single-stranded RNA, that buildup fell by about half.

A similar change to VP35 had little effect in human cells. NP appears to play the bigger role in collecting RNA.

That figure covers every message at once, and says nothing about which ones are collected.

The team therefore used a method capable of reading thousands of RNA types at once, across 136 genes linked to Ebola infection and the immune response.

About 90,000 cells were examined, with droplets found in roughly 30% of them. Of the 66 genes measured reliably, all but two were more concentrated inside.

The amount of RNA made little difference. Rare messages collected inside while far more common ones stayed out, indicating that something other than abundance determined where they ended up.

Shape proved to be the better explanation.

RNA is not always a straight strand. Parts of it join together wherever the letters match, folding the molecule into loops and stems.

Messages with fewer folds in their protein-making sections entered more readily.

To test this, the genetic instructions in some messages were rewritten. The new versions made the same protein but folded differently, and those with fewer folds entered more readily.

Ten viruses that carry negative-strand RNA were also examined, including Ebola, measles, rabies and Marburg. In all of them the protein-making regions had fewer folds than the human messages left outside.

The most striking finding concerned interferon. Type I interferon, released when a cell detects a virus, switches on hundreds of defense genes in nearby cells.

After the cells were treated with interferon, the droplets started collecting different messages.

Messages from more than 20 defense genes appeared in droplets.

The rule governing entry also changed. The protein-making section was no longer the best indicator.

Instead the tail of each message mattered more, the stretch that follows the instructions for making a protein.

Immune defense messages often have long, tightly folded tails.

The more folded the tail, the more of that message was found inside. Interferon also increases proteins that attach to RNA, and some of these may help pull immune messages into the droplets.

People who die from Ebola often show stronger activity in their defense genes than those who survive. Even people who recover can show signs of inflammation-related gene activity years later.

Erica Ollmann Saphire, who led the study with Fang, is the president and chief executive of the La Jolla Institute for Immunology.

“These are strong immune responses, but they’re not working well,” said Ollmann Saphire.

Cells normally break down messenger RNA once it has served its purpose. Enzymes such as XRN1 help carry out that cleanup.

Staining showed less XRN1 inside the droplets than in the surrounding cell fluid.

This raised a further question: whether messages trapped inside survive longer because the enzymes cannot reach them.

Messages held in the droplets broke down more slowly than the same messages in cells carrying a modified NP that could not bind RNA. Messages that stayed outside broke down at similar rates in both groups.

“The antiviral response is like a weapon, and cells use degradation as a way to deactivate that weapon,” Fang explained.

Protein production also changed. A protein the cell requires in order to start building other proteins was largely missing from the droplets.

Without interferon, more messenger RNA collected inside but less protein came out of it.

With interferon, both rose: one defense message increased more than sixfold, and levels of two defense proteins rose alongside it.

A final experiment used a weakened Ebola virus, one lacking a gene needed for replication.

A defense message called IFIT3 was found inside the droplets of those infected cells as well.

Bats can carry filoviruses without getting sick. These viruses belong to the same family as Ebola.

Bat genes cannot be read with tools designed for human RNA. Computer models were therefore used to predict how 45 of the collected messages would fold, in bats, ferrets and macaques.

Ferrets and macaques can become seriously ill from Ebola, which makes them useful comparisons.

Within the defense genes, the bat versions had tails that were less tightly folded than the human ones. Ferret and macaque tails were much more like those in humans.

Genes outside the defense group showed no meaningful difference between species.

Fever often comes with an interferon response, so the predictions were repeated at 102 degrees Fahrenheit (39 degrees Celsius).

Messages carrying bat tails then behaved as the folding had predicted. The least folded bat tail kept its message out of the droplets better than the matching human tail.

Bat immune messages may therefore be less likely to become trapped. If they remain outside, cells can break them down at the usual time.

Most experiments used kidney cells grown in a laboratory. The process was not tested in human patients or living animals, and for much of the work NP and VP35 stood in for the complete virus.

The cells were measured at just one point, 24 hours after the proteins were introduced, across 136 genes out of roughly 20,000.

The human kidney cells responded poorly to interferon. Higher concentrations were therefore used for longer periods than other tissues might need.

The RNA folding patterns were computer predictions. They cannot fully show how every RNA molecule behaves inside a living cell.

The bat comparison included only one species. It was chosen partly because scientists have not yet identified Ebola virus’s natural host.

“We don’t know whether this difference can explain everything, but it gives researchers a place to start looking and confirm what we’ve found in additional human or animal samples,” Fang added.

Breaking the droplets apart may be worth pursuing as a treatment, the authors wrote. It could slow the virus and prevent immune messages from remaining active too long.

The principal challenge is achieving that inside living cells without destroying the cells.

The full study was published in the journal Cell.
