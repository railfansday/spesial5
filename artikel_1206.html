Although coronary artery disease (CAD) genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified hundreds of loci, most variants lie in non-coding regions, obscuring the causal gene programmes and cell types driving disease1,2. Here we performed single-nucleus multi-omic profiling of gene expression and chromatin accessibility in human coronary arteries from 44 people to construct a genome-wide single-cell variant-to-enhancer-to-gene map. We identified 11,269 chromatin accessibility quantitative trait loci (caQTLs) across principal vascular cell types, including CAD-associated caQTLs in smooth muscle cells (SMCs). Using dynamic single-cell caQTL modelling3, we show that variant effects on chromatin vary across SMC states. For example, rs658956-mediated suppression of the FGGY-DT enhancer through disruption of an AP-1 motif is amplified as SMCs transition towards a fibromyocyte fate. We next performed genome-wide high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) in 15 human coronary arteries, showing that rs4887091 in the ADAMTS7 CAD locus has allele-dependent effects on CCCTC-binding factor occupancy and loop strength. Integrating Hi-C, Hi-C with chromatin immunoprecipitation and in vitro clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats interference (CRISPRi) nominated AMOTL2 as a distal SMC-specific CAD gene whose loss promotes proliferation and suppresses contractile identity. Finally, arrayed Perturb–seq of GWAS-nominated enhancers converged on extracellular matrix remodelling as a core SMC disease programme. Together, these findings provide a framework for mapping non-coding GWAS variants to cell types, chromatin states and gene programmes in human disease.

Common diseases are driven by a multitude of factors and remain difficult to treat given the lack of a unified disease mechanism4. Coronary artery disease (CAD) is among the most prevalent common chronic diseases, and numerous genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have now identified hundreds of variants linked to CAD risk. Although these results may potentially pave the way for new therapeutic target discovery1,2,5,6, much of the translation from GWAS to therapeutic targets for CAD has been restricted to lipid-lowering drugs7,8. Large-scale clinical studies have demonstrated a persistent residual risk of CAD independent of lipid levels9,10, but we lack drugs that directly target disease pathways in the vessel wall due to an incomplete understanding of causal disease mechanisms. Leveraging human genetics to uncover new lipid-independent biological mechanisms and drug targets in CAD represents a tremendous unmet need and would be a principal milestone in translating population-scale GWAS findings to therapies for patients11,12.

Although population-scale GWAS offer great opportunity for target discovery, most identified variants lie within non-coding regions of the genome, making it difficult to dissect the causal cell types and molecular pathways13. The advent of single-cell sequencing technologies now allows for the profiling of human tissues in health and disease at unprecedented granularity14. Previous studies have used traditional single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) for detailed characterization of cell states in human tissue that drive disease15,16,17,18; however, these studies do not profile the non-coding genome, making it challenging to link GWAS variants to observed molecular changes. More recently, single-nucleus assay for transposase-accessible chromatin (ATAC) with sequencing (snATAC–seq) in human coronary arteries was conducted to link CAD GWAS variants to regulatory elements19. Although this represented an important advance in the field, the lack of complementary transcriptomic profiling rendered it challenging to nominate causal genes in vascular cell types. Furthermore, isolated snATAC–seq precludes the discovery of distal gene regulation as a driver of disease20, and there are currently no studies that profile the three-dimensional (3D) chromatin architecture in human coronary arteries to enable the investigation of these distal regulatory connections.

Herein we performed simultaneous multi-omic profiling of the transcriptome and epigenome in nuclei from 44 human coronary arteries to build a multi-omic enhancer–gene map of CAD. Collectively, these analyses identified CAD-associated regulatory variants and enhancer–gene links in smooth muscle cells (SMCs), motivating focused analysis of SMC state-dependent gene regulation. To expand insight into gene regulation across functionally heterogeneous SMC cell states, we used dynamic chromatin accessibility quantitative trait loci (caQTL) modelling to uncover cell-state-dependent pathogenicity of disease variants. To dissect the role of disease-associated variants in regulating larger scale gene networks, we performed genome-wide Hi-C in 15 human coronary arteries to link variants to distal genes and validated our findings using cell-specific high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) with chromatin immunoprecipitation (Hi-ChIP–seq) and in vitro CRISPRi. Finally, we performed Perturb–seq in human coronary artery SMCs (HCASMCs) to characterize functional mechanisms of repressing disease-associated enhancers and genes. Collectively, we provide a multi-omic map of human CAD by integrating transcriptomic, epigenetic and chromatin architecture to link disease-associated genetic variants with candidate disease-relevant cell types and target genes.

We performed single-nucleus Multi-ome (RNA+ATAC) sequencing in 44 human coronary arteries from non-failing donors and patients with chronic heart failure isolated at the time of heart transplantation (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). After quality control, we recovered 126,804 nuclei from 11 distinct cell types (Fig. 1b,c and Supplementary Fig. 1b–d). Using differential expression of canonical genes, we identified clusters corresponding to all main vascular cell types, including coronary artery SMCs (MYH11, ACTA2, ITGA8), endothelial cells (VWF, LDB2, PECAM1), fibroblasts (LAMA2, DCN, FBLN1) and myeloid cells (F13A1, CD163, MRC1) (Fig. 1b, Extended Data Fig. 1a–c and Supplementary Table 2). We used MACS2 to identify areas of open chromatin (‘peaks’) from the ATAC–seq data (Fig. 1e) and identified 143,743 cell-type-specific differentially accessible peaks (Extended Data Fig. 1d,e). Generally, cell-type clustering was concordant between the RNA and ATAC data. However, gene-expression data enabled the annotation of rarer cell types that were not captured by ATAC-based clustering alone (Supplementary Fig. 1e–g). Using differentially accessible peaks across cells, we also discovered cell-type-specific enrichment of transcriptional motifs such as SpiB and PU.1 in myeloid cells, MEF2B/C/D in SMCs and CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein in adipocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1h). We linked peaks to genes using a new supervised single-cell model called single-cell enhancer-to-gene (E2G) (scE2G)21 (Fig. 1d, Extended Data Fig. 2a–g and Supplementary Tables 3–14; Methods). We found that the non-immune cells had the greatest number of shared enhancers relative to myeloid cells (Fig. 1d) and that all cell types had a similar number of E2G links (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Table 3).

Notably, we found that cardiometabolic disease variants for CAD, systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were enriched in differentially accessible marker peaks specific to SMCs and endothelial cells (Fig. 1f). As a predominantly non-vascular trait, diabetes did not demonstrate enrichment of disease-associated variants (Fig. 1f).

To boost our power for single-cell caQTL (sc-caQTL) discovery, we integrated our Multi-ome ATAC–seq data with available snATAC–seq data from human coronary arteries19 to create a meta-map of 88 samples and 245,562 nuclei across four ancestry groups (Extended Data Fig. 3a–f). We then used RASQUAL to identify pseudobulk caQTLs in four principal vascular cell types (SMCs, endothelial cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts) (Fig. 2a). At the 10% false discovery rate (FDR) threshold, we found 11,269 significant caQTL associations in SMCs, endothelial cells, myeloid cells and fibroblasts (Extended Data Fig. 3g–i, Supplementary Fig. 2a–d and Supplementary Tables 15–26). We further characterized caQTL variants to assess their regulatory potential. Lead caQTL variants were enriched strongly near the peaks they were predicted to regulate, consistent with a cis-regulatory mechanism (Extended Data Fig. 3j). We next overlapped our pseudobulk caQTLs and GTEx bulk arterial tissue eQTLs by cell type and found several hundred shared QTL variants in SMCs, endothelial cells, myeloid cells and fibroblasts (Extended Data Fig. 3i). Furthermore, we found a strong correlation in effect size between our SMC caQTLs and bulk coronary artery eQTLs from GTEx (Extended Data Fig. 3k). Finally, in SMCs we trained a convolutional neural net model (seq2PRINT22) that incorporates chromatin accessibility and ATAC footprinting to predict transcription factor binding probability at a base-pair resolution. We found that caQTL variants within accessible peaks had significantly higher predicted transcription factor binding probabilities compared with all other non-caQTL SNP sites within accessible peaks (P = 8.2 × 10−40, Wilcoxon rank sum) (Extended Data Fig. 3l).

To identify disease-associated caQTLs, we overlapped sc-caQTLs for each cell type with CAD GWAS variants (defined as lead variants associated with CAD at 1% FDR1 and variants in linkage disequilibrium with these at R2 ≥ 0.8 in the European population from 1000 Genomes Project; Supplementary Table 27), which yielded numerous disease-relevant cell-type-specific caQTLs (Extended Data Fig. 3m). For example, we found that rs7182567 was an SMC-specific caQTL that was also a GWAS variant (P = 5.4 × 10−26) and a bulk GTEx eQTL for ADAMTS7 in tibial artery (Extended Data Fig. 4a). Similarly, we found that rs8017642 is an endothelial cell-specific caQTL in the NEK9 locus (Extended Data Fig. 4b), whereas rs2337154 and rs1443120 are fibroblast- and myeloid-specific caQTLs, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 4c,d).

Next, we leveraged the paired RNA data to sub-cluster SMCs into transcriptionally distinct cell states, forming a continuum from ‘healthy’ contractile SMC states (SMC1–3) to de-differentiated ‘disease-associated’ SMC states including fibromyocytes (FMCs) and chondromyocytes (CMCs) (Extended Data Fig. 4e). Notably, we found that ADAMTS7 was expressed most highly in FMCs (Extended Data Fig. 4e), indicating a role in disease regulation. To identify functionally distinct endothelial cell states, we mapped our data to a high-resolution scRNA-seq atherosclerosis atlas23 and identified four transcriptionally distinct endothelial subsets (Extended Data Fig. 4f). Notably, NEK9 was expressed in endothelial cells enriched with endothelium-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) genes such as COL1A1 and FN1 (refs. 24,25) (Extended Data Fig. 4f,g). Pathway enrichment in the EMT population showed increased EMT and stress-response signals (Extended Data Fig. 4h). Next, we created a gene set score comprised of 41 endothelial cell-specific genes thought to be associated causally with CAD26 that showed maximal enrichment in intimal endothelial cells (Extended Data Fig. 4i). Similarly, we created a gene signature of a gene programme26 identified previously as driving CAD pathogenesis in endothelial cells (Programme 8) and found an enrichment of this programme in angiogenic and/or vasa vasorum endothelial cells (Extended Data Fig. 4j).

The CAD disease-associated variant rs658956 illustrates how a cell-type-specific caQTL can impart functional transcriptional changes. The variant is an SMC-specific and fibroblast-specific caQTL and regulates a putative enhancer element (chr. 1: 59169569–59170069), which is linked to the gene FGGY-DT in an SMC-specific manner by scE2G (Fig. 2c,d). Consistently, we found that rs658956 is also a coronary artery eQTL for FGGY-DT expression (P = 3.4 × 10−12) wherein the alternative allele (T) is associated with decreased expression relative to the reference allele (G) (Fig. 2e) and is also predicted to overlie a high probability transcription factor binding site (Fig. 2f). Motif-based transcription factor binding site analysis found that rs658956 is predicted to disrupt a core AP-1 (JUN/FOS) transcription factor binding motif (5′-TGAGTCA-3′) (Fig. 2g). Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) demonstrated a binding shift in the reference allele sequence (containing G) that was not present in the alternative allele sequence (containing T) when these alleles were exposed to nuclear lysate from SMCs (Fig. 2h). Antibody supershift EMSA confirmed that the shifted AP-1 complex band consists of JUN and FOS transcription factors (Extended Data Fig. 4k). Together, these results suggest that the disease-associated variant rs658956 impacts transcription factor binding, thereby decreasing the chromatin accessibility of the enhancer and subsequently reducing FGGY-DT expression in a SMC-specific manner.

In atherosclerosis, SMCs undergo a well-characterized phenotypic switch from a contractile state to a fibroblast-like FMC phenotype16, driven by disease-associated transcriptional programmes. To characterize this transition, individual nuclei were assigned an FMC score based on FMC state marker genes for downstream modelling (Fig. 2i and Extended Data Fig. 5a–d). Notably, this FMC gene score is dynamic with disease across species, disease burden, pseudotime trajectory, in vitro HCASMC stress and transcriptional regulation (Extended Data Fig. 5e–k). To identify state-dependent caQTL effects, we used a Poisson mixed-effects (PME) single-cell model (Extended Data Fig. 6a–c; Methods). Applying this PME model to the lead variants of SMC-specific pseudobulk caQTLs, we identified 712 caQTLs whose effects were dynamic with respect to the FMC score (FDR ≤ 5%) (Supplementary Tables 29 and 30). Notably, eight of these dynamic caQTLs were also disease-associated variants. For example, rs658956 exhibited a significant interaction with FMC score (FDR-adjusted likelihood ratio test (LRT) P value = 2.36 × 10−4) (Fig. 2j,k). A negative interaction term indicates that, as the FMC score increased, the negative association between rs658956 and local chromatin accessibility was amplified (Fig. 2j,k). Using the scE2G map to link dynamic caQTL-related enhancer elements (caPeaks) to target genes, we found that extracellular matrix (ECM) remodelling was the most enriched pathway among linked genes (Extended Data Fig. 6d,e and Supplementary Table 31). Notably, several of these genes linked to dynamic caPeaks were enriched in FMCs (Extended Data Fig. 6f and Supplementary Table 32). Similar state-dependent effects were observed at other CAD loci such as dynamic caQTL variant rs11838776 in the COL4A1 locus, suggesting that COL4A1 may play a role during transition toward an FMC state (Extended Data Fig. 6g–k). Collectively, these dynamic analyses demonstrate that disease-associated variants can exert cell-state-dependent regulatory effects on chromatin accessibility.

Overlapping scE2G with sc-caQTLs provides a high-fidelity set of predicted variants that may be driving disease by directly regulating intermediate molecular traits in a cell-type-specific manner.

Focusing on the 325 genes linked to SMC enhancers harbouring CAD GWAS variants, we performed several enrichment analyses. These analyses found that these linked genes were over-represented in nearest lead genes from CAD and myocardial infarction GWAS, identified GATA2 as the most enriched transcription factor and highlighted collagen and ECM remodelling as key biological processes (Extended Data Fig. 7a and Supplementary Tables 37–39). Correlation analysis between gene expression and FMC score identified FN1, COL4A1, COL4A2, MFGE8 and MYO9B—all known CAD-associated genes implicated in ECM remodelling—among the most highly correlated transcripts (Extended Data Fig. 7b and Supplementary Table 40). Notably, the enhancer harbouring variant rs7246865 emerged as potentially important in disease, acting as both a cell-state dynamic caPeak and pseudobulk caPeak in SMCs with a high-confidence scE2G link (Fig. 3b). We confirmed this predicted link experimentally using CRISPRi in HCASMCs. Repressing the rs7246865-containing enhancer significantly reduced MYO9B expression relative to a non-targeting control (Fig. 3c), consistent with previous functional validation at this locus1. Furthermore, CRISPRi validated scE2G predictions in SMCs for three additional loci near genes LOXL1, NAV1 and PALLD; enhancer repression altered gene expression significantly at each of these loci relative to non-targeting controls (Extended Data Fig. 7c–e).

To further validate scE2G links, we performed arrayed CRISPRi Perturb–seq in HCASMCs, delivering individual sgRNAs to target either the transcription start site (TSS) or the nominated enhancer at eight CAD GWAS loci (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 41). Alongside transcriptional profiling, we measured SMC proliferation by live-cell imaging, revealing that several perturbations (including ADAMTS7, CFDP1 and COL4A1) and significantly reduced proliferation rate, with enhancer knockdowns generally phenocopying their cognate TSS perturbations (Fig. 3e, Extended Data Fig. 8a and Supplementary Table 42). Following quality control, we retained 76,179 cells and confirmed robust, perturbation-specific transcriptional responses, with selective target-gene knockdown, stronger effects for TSS than enhancer perturbations and separation from non-targeting controls (Extended Data Fig. 8b–j).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed locus-specific pathway enrichments: BMP1 and ADAMTS7 knockdowns strongly enriched MYC and E2F target-gene sets, whereas CFDP1 and COL4A1 knockdowns suppressed interferon response pathways, indicating distinct downstream transcriptional consequences across CAD loci (Extended Data Fig. 9a). Consensus non-negative matrix factorization identified six biologically interpretable programmes: inflammation, ECM remodelling, vascular remodelling, interferon response and two proliferative programmes corresponding to S and M phase (Extended Data Fig. 9b,c and Supplementary Tables 43 and 44). Notably, the ECM remodelling programme showed the strongest positive correlation with the FMC module score, with enhancer knockdowns of CFDP1, MYO9B and BMP1 exhibiting the highest mean FMC scores (Extended Data Fig. 9d–h). Programme-level analysis further revealed that COL4A1 and its enhancer were the strongest drivers of the inflammation programme, whereas the MRAS and CFDP1 enhancer perturbations modulated both inflammation and ECM remodelling (Fig. 3f).

Finally, robust rank aggregation (RRA) across pseudobulk DESeq2 profiles revealed a convergent gene programme dominated by downregulation of several collagen genes (COL3A1, COL4A1, COL5A1, COL6A1–A3) and TIMP2, alongside the upregulation of metabolic and stress-response genes (Fig. 3g, Extended Data Fig. 9i and Supplementary Table 45). Pathway enrichment of this convergent programme identified EMT, tumour necrosis factor signalling through nuclear factor κB, and mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 signalling as the top enriched terms (Fig. 3h). Collectively, these findings implicate a shared regulatory framework that governs SMC phenotypic states across independent, disease-associated loci.

To investigate how disease-associated variants may influence broader gene networks through chromatin looping, we performed genome-wide Hi-C in 15 human coronary arteries to construct large-scale chromatin networks (Fig. 4a, Extended Data Fig. 10a–f and Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). To further complement Hi-C in bulk tissue, we performed H3K27Ac Hi-ChIP–seq in cultured human coronary artery smooth muscle cells and endothelial cells. Comparing the number of enhancer–gene connection overlap by method, we found that scE2GMulti-ome and Hi-ChIP yielded the highest number of enhancer–gene connections, with a smaller fraction from bulk coronary tissue Hi-C (Supplementary Fig. 7). We then identified groups of genomic loci that interact preferentially with one another in specific chromatin networks termed ‘mosaics’ (Fig. 4a; Methods). We identified 5,130 mosaics containing 11,494 protein-coding genes. Notably, mosaics harbouring a disease-associated variant exhibited significantly higher structural complexity, containing an average of 25 loops compared with 14 loops in non-disease-associated mosaics (P = 1.7 × 10−17) (Fig. 4b). This approach provides a framework for mapping disease-associated variants to a catalogue of genes regulated distally through interconnected chromatin networks (Supplementary Tables 46–49).

Next, we performed CTCF ChIP–seq in HCASMC to prioritize disease-associated SMC enhancers that are bound by CTCF. Of all CAD disease-associated variants, 498 overlapped CTCF peaks, including 200 within accessible chromatin regions. CAD-associated variants were enriched significantly at CTCF-bound regions above permutation-based expectation (1.25-fold, P = 0.02; Extended Data Fig. 10g).

Among the top-ranking regulatory mosaics, we identified mosaic-1664, which contains the CAD-associated variant rs4887091 located within an SMC enhancer (Fig. 4c). In this mosaic, we found an 800-kb boundary loop exhibiting a strong rs4887091 genotype-dependent increase in contact strength for the alternate allele (C) relative to the reference allele (T) (Fig. 4d). Specifically, the loop strength increased in a dose-dependent manner when quantified across all samples, where there was minimal contact strength in homozygotes (T|T), moderate strength in heterozygotes (T|C, C|T) and highest strength in homozygotes (C|C) (Fig. 4e). Phased genotypes confirmed increased looping strength on the C allele (P = 3.1 × 10−3) (Fig. 4f).

To characterize the regulatory architecture of rs4887091 within mosaic-1664, we identified all genes linked to the SMC enhancer containing rs4887091: Hi-C identified a boundary loop containing ADAMTS7, MORF4L1, CHRNA3, IDH3A, ACSBG1, CIB2, SH2D7 and TBC1D2 promoters (Fig. 4d), whereas scE2G identified ADAMTS7 (the nearest gene) (Fig. 4g). Consistent with ADAMTS7 being a functional target of this regulatory element, CRISPRi Perturb–seq targeting the rs4887091-containing enhancer reduced ADAMTS7 expression in HCASMCs (Extended Data Fig. 8f). CTCF ChIP–seq in HCASMCs demonstrated that rs4887091 overlaps a CTCF binding site (Fig. 4g). Furthermore, motif-based transcription factor binding site analysis found that rs4887091, which changes the reference (T) to alternate (C) allele, is predicted to create a core CTCF binding motif (Fig. 4h). Consistent with this prediction, haplotype-resolved CTCF ChIP–seq demonstrated allelic dosage-dependent increases in CTCF occupancy on the C allele (P = 0.0003) (Fig. 4i). Validation at control loci and orthogonal TaqMan genotyping confirmed the accuracy of the haplotype-resolved CTCF occupancy measurements (Extended Data Fig. 10h–j). Together, these data support a model in which rs4887091 creates an allele-specific CTCF binding site associated with increased local chromatin loop strength, although the effect of this loop alteration on gene expression remains to be established.

Although our scE2G predictions are limited to enhancer–gene interactions within a cis-window, leveraging Hi-C data can link CAD GWAS variants to distal genes. For the loops present in all 15 samples, we catalogued genes linked to a CAD GWAS variant through direct chromatin contact between putative enhancers and promoters and identified 11 such genes: ANTXR1, ARMC12, CNNM2, FKBP5, CAPG, SH2D6, AMOTL2, NUAK2, RGS1, PLCL1 and FOXC2 (Fig. 5a). Among these, ANTXR1, CAPG, SH2D6, AMOTL2, NUAK2 and RGS1 are not the nearest genes. As an example, the CAD-associated variant rs9876658 was linked to AMOTL2 through a direct Hi-C contact. rs9876658 falls within a snATAC–seq peak in SMCs, endothelial cells and fibroblasts (but not myeloid cells) highlighting stroma-specific regulation (Fig. 5b). H3K27ac ChIP–seq showed that rs9876658 overlapped a H3K27ac peak in SMCs but not in endothelial cells (Fig. 5b). Hi-ChIP–seq further identified a direct loop between the rs9876658-containing enhancer and the AMOTL2 promoter in SMCs but not in endothelial cells (Fig. 5b). Collectively, these analyses predict that the enhancer containing rs9876658 functionally regulates expression of AMOTL2 in SMCs but not in endothelial cells.

We found that targeting the AMOTL2 TSS led to a complete knockdown of AMOTL2 expression in HCASMCs and human coronary artery endothelial cells (HCAECs) (Fig. 5c). Importantly, targeting the rs9876658-containing enhancer reduced AMOTL2 expression in HCASMCs but not HCAECs, bolstering our prediction (Fig. 5c). Collectively, these analyses nominate rs9876658 as a causal regulator of AMOTL2 expression in SMCs but not endothelial cells and implicate this gene as a new candidate gene for atherosclerosis through an effect in SMCs. To dissect the functional impact of targeting AMOTL2 in HCASMCs, we compared non-targeting control and AMOTL2 knockdown HCASMCs. We observed disordered cellular morphology as well as decreased calponin (CNN1) and alpha-smooth muscle actin (ACTA2) expression by immunofluorescence in AMOTL2 knockdown cells, suggesting a shift away from the quiescent contractile state (Fig. 5d). We performed bulk RNA sequencing in stressed non-targeting control and AMOTL2 knockdown cells (Fig. 5e). Gene ontology pathway analysis revealed an upregulation of cell-cycle-related genes and a downregulation of cell-motility-associated pathways (Fig. 5f). Consistent with our immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5d), we saw a reduction in contractile SMC gene expression (ACTA2, CNN1, TAGLN and LMOD1) in AMOTL2 knockdown cells relative to control (Fig. 5g). Finally, AMOTL2 knockdown cells had a significantly greater proliferation rate (Fig. 5h). Taken together, these findings establish a functional role for AMOTL2 in SMC proliferation and migration—pathways integral in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis.

Although GWAS have identified hundreds of CAD-associated variants1,5,28,29, translation to therapies remains limited by incomplete understanding of the causal cell types and gene programmes30,31. The emergence of multi-omic single-cell technologies has created a unique opportunity to profile human tissue at unprecedented resolution14,32. Numerous studies have leveraged these technologies to uncover cellular heterogeneity in human tissue across many diseases, contributing to a human cell atlas17,18,24,33. Few studies have mapped common disease variants to causal cell types and target genes directly in human tissue, with previous work limited by unimodal profiling or cell-based systems that lack the native tissue context.

Our study used a new technique—single-nucleus multi-ome (RNA+ATAC)—in human coronary arteries from 44 patients with paired genotyping for unbiased characterization of the transcriptome and epigenome at single-cell resolution. Capturing paired gene expression and chromatin accessibility from the same nucleus allows for more robust peak-gene linkage21 and represents an important milestone for regulatory biology of the human vasculature. We also used a new computational framework—scE2G21—to build an unbiased genome-wide scE2G map in human CAD. Notably, our scV2E2G framework identified a substantial set of CAD-associated enhancer–gene links in SMCs, including 325 linked genes, providing a basis for downstream functional interrogation of SMC regulatory programmes. This systematic approach can be applied broadly across diseases and allows for an unbiased framework to prioritize pathological genes and cell types driving common heritable human diseases.

To nominate functional regulatory variants in a broader cohort, we integrated our data with published human coronary artery snATAC–seq19 to identify 11,269 sc-caQTLs, enabling QTL discovery in cell types that are obscured in bulk analyses34,35. Mechanistic dissection of rs658956 illustrates how non-coding CAD variants can act through cell-type-specific disruption of transcription factor binding and enhancer activity36. The disruption of an AP-1 (JUN/FOS) motif by this variant is particularly notable given the well-established role of AP-1 in SMC phenotypic switching and the recent evidence that AP-1 family members act as pioneer factors governing vascular cell chromatin landscapes37 and carry epigenetic memory of inflammation38. Furthermore, SMC-specific regulation of the lncRNA FGGY-DT highlights the potential contribution of cell-restricted non-coding RNA programmes to cardiovascular disease39,40,41, although its downstream role remains to be defined.

Traditional pseudobulk QTL discovery lacks the ability to discriminate between molecular traits that are influenced by disease state3. Here we used single-cell genomics to define cell-state dynamics in one of the most fluid cell types in the atherosclerotic plaque. In atherosclerosis, contractile SMCs undergo de-differentiation and acquire an FMC phenotype with distinct transcriptional signatures16. We applied a continuous score reflecting SMC to FMC transition to map dynamic caQTLs that are dependent on cell-state. Using this framework, we uncovered rs658956 as a caQTL for FGGY-DT enhancer that is associated with altered activity as SMCs transition to FMCs. This is an application of single-cell-state-dependent QTL modelling in human cardiovascular disease and expands our understanding of gene regulation in heterogeneous cell states.

We applied arrayed Perturb–seq42 to GWAS-nominated enhancers and their SMC-linked target genes, enabling transcriptome-wide functional validation of non-coding regulatory elements and their downstream gene programmes. Critically, the arrayed format preserves non-cell-autonomous transcriptional effects that can be obscured in pooled screens where shared culture conditions confound intrinsic regulatory responses through paracrine signalling, as in a recent CAD variant-to-gene-to-programme framework26. The convergence of several enhancer perturbations on inflammatory and ECM-remodelling programmes is consistent with the established role of said processes in atherosclerosis, including induction of matrix metalloproteinases and collagens43. Together, these data support a model in which CAD variants act through cell-type-specific enhancers to co-ordinately dysregulate inflammation, ECM homeostasis and proliferation during transition from contractile SMCs to pathological FMCs18,44.

Given that the genome is organized as a dynamic 3D structure45, chromatin looping through topologically associating domains (TADs) represents a higher-order regulatory layer governing gene expression45,46. Although Hi-C has been used in cell-based systems and other tissues to map chromatin architecture and connect disease variants to distal genes47,48, large-scale chromatin structures are highly tissue- and cell-type-specific27,48,49 and remain unexplored in diseased human arterial tissue. Here, genome-wide Hi-C in 15 human coronary arteries enabled tissue-specific mapping of chromatin networks and distal variant–gene interactions. Notably, we discovered that rs4887091 creates an allele-specific CTCF binding site with dosage-dependent effects on both CTCF occupancy and loop strength, defining a mechanistically distinct non-coding regulatory mechanism involving 3D genome architecture48. Haplotype-resolved analysis confirmed the allele-specific increase in looping strength50,51, consistent with recent demonstrations that phased Hi-C can resolve allele-specific chromatin structure at disease loci52 and that variants within CTCF sites can alter looping and gene regulation in a dosage-sensitive manner50,51. The linked gene, ADAMTS7, is causally implicated in CAD, with Adamts7 deficiency reducing atherosclerosis and its catalytic activity regulating SMC migration53,54. Although our data do not establish altered looping as the proximal mechanism controlling ADAMTS7 expression, allele-dependent changes to chromatin architecture may represent a previously unrecognized regulatory mechanism at this locus.

Furthermore, our tissue Hi-C framework enabled systematic genome-wide nomination of distal causal genes. We identified 11 genes linked to CAD variants through chromatin contacts, most of which, for example AMOTL2, were not the gene nearest to the disease-associated variant. CRISPRi targeting of the enhancer linked distally to AMOTL2 reduced AMOTL2 expression in HCASMCs but not HCAECs, and AMOTL2 knockdown in HCASMCs increased cellular proliferation and suppressed contractile marker expression, consistent with a shift away from a quiescent state. This phenotype aligns with the known function of AMOTL2 as a scaffold protein55,56 that retains YAP in the cytoplasm to suppress Hippo-driven proliferative transcription, such that its loss de-represses YAP nuclear activity and promotes cell-cycle progression57. The connection between a CAD-associated variant and a Hippo pathway regulator with established roles in vascular cell proliferation and mechanosensing58,59 points to dysregulation of YAP and/or TAZ signalling in SMCs as a potentially underappreciated axis in atherosclerotic disease.

Our study has several limitations. First, nuclear RNA provides more limited cell-state resolution than whole-cell RNA, so we leveraged high-resolution published single-cell data for reference mapping and state imputation. Second, caQTL discovery remains constrained by sample size, cell abundance and allele frequency, limiting power in rarer cell types and variants and complicating interpretation of differences in caQTLs across cell types. Residual population stratification in our multi-ancestry cohort may have also resulted in false positives, although our summary statistics can support future meta-analyses in larger cohorts. Third, multi-ome profiling does not capture post-transcriptional regulation such as alternative splicing. Finally, bulk coronary Hi-C precludes genome-wide cell-type-specific looping inference; we therefore used targeted Hi-ChIP–seq for cell-type resolution at selected loci. Although CRISPRi Perturb–seq supports ADAMTS7 as a responsive target of the rs4887091-containing enhancer, direct allele editing or CTCF motif perturbation will be required to establish whether allele-dependent looping causally regulates ADAMTS7 expression.

In conclusion, we built a multi-omic gene-expression and chromatin-accessibility map of human CAD and provide a comprehensive framework to map CAD GWAS variants to causal genes and affected cell types. Furthermore, we use pseudobulk and single-cell QTL modelling to characterize state-dependent and independent regulatory mechanism of disease variants in human tissue. We used tissue Hi-C to build large-scale chromatin networks and uncover how disease variants impact distal gene regulation. Finally, we integrated tissue -omics with in vitro epigenetic profiling to prioritize and functionally test candidate genes using Perturb–seq and CRISPRi. Collectively, we provide a disease agnostic framework to apply human genetic findings to nominate pathologic cell states and genes driving disease, constructing a comprehensive GWAS variant-to-gene map with genetic- and tissue-level convergence for future mechanistic and therapeutic studies.

We used the scE2GMulti-ome model21 to predict enhancer–gene connections in 11 cell types from 10x Genomics multi-ome data. In each cell type, scE2G scores every candidate element–gene pair (where candidate elements are ATAC peaks within 5 Mb of the TSS of the gene) by integrating several features. Regulatory enhancer–gene interactions were defined as element–gene pairs with a score greater than 0.171.

The scE2Gmulti-ome model is a supervised classifier adapted for multi-omic data from ENCODE-rE2G63. It integrates eight features, including (1) a pseudobulk activity-by-contact score, where 3D contact is estimated by an inverse function of genomic distance; (2) the Kendall correlation across single cells between element accessibility and gene expression; (3) whether the gene is ‘expressed ubiquitously’ and (4) several other measures of genomic distance and chromatin accessibility around the element and promoter. The score threshold of 0.171 was determined as the score yielding 70% recall when evaluating predictions in K562 cells against CRISPRi-validated enhancer–gene pairs.

Similar to previous studies for sc-caQTL discovery19,75, we used RASQUAL76 to identify pseudobulk caQTLs in four main cell types (SMCs, endothelial cells, myeloid cells and fibroblasts). RASQUAL v.1.1 was chosen as our mapping method due to its ability to simultaneously model allelic imbalance and count-based QTL effect to improve fine-mapping of putative causal variants. RASQUAL maximizes power for caQTL discovery by simultaneously modelling allelic imbalance and total read counts in each locus. To generate fragment counts within each ATAC peak regions, we loaded each fragment object and used Signac (v.1.17.1) FeatureMatrix function to generate a cell-by-peak count matrix for all cells within each cell type. This matrix was subsequently aggregated at a sample pseudobulk level using rollup function from the R slam package (v.0.1-56). For RASQUAL’s allele-specific modelling, createASVCF.sh script in paired-read mode was used to generate allele-specific fragment counts at each site with additional BAM flag of -F 1280 to exclude secondary or PCR duplicate reads. For each cell type, only samples with at least 20 cells of that cell type and only peaks with at least five reads on average across all samples were included for the final analysis.

For the caQTL association analysis, all variants within a ±10-kb cis-window of the peaks were tested for association. Total library size for each sample was included as an offset. Age, sex, sequencing site and first four principal components of genotype data (generated from plink --pca) were included as covariates. For multiple testing correction, we used the two-step correction procedure recommended by RASQUAL76, including locus-level correction followed by a genome-wide correction using an empirical null distribution generated using genotype permutations. The caQTL variants were queried subsequently in bulk eQTL datasets using Qtlizer (v.1.22.0)77. Power, MAF-stratified validation, model comparison and genotype-PC sensitivity analyses are described in Supplementary Fig. 2. For locus-level multiple testing, a Q value corresponding to SNP-level FDR was calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and the SNP with the lowest Q value was selected for each locus. For genome-wide correction, an empirical null distribution was calculated from four RASQUAL runs using randomly permuted genotypes; the averaged null distribution was used to obtain Q value cut-offs corresponding to 1%, 5% and 10% genome-wide FDR.

Local perturbation signatures were calculated using CalcPerturbSig from Seurat, subtracting the average expression of the k = 20 nearest non-targeting neighbour cells in PCA space (30 dimensions) to remove technical variation, producing a perturbation assay. The perturbation assay was centred, projected into PCA space using variable features from the RNA assay and visualized by UMAP. Mixscape83 was run on the scaled perturbation assay using RunMixscape with a minimum of five differentially expressed genes, ten iterations and perturbation type set to knockdown. This classified cells as knockdown, escaped or non-targeting based on posterior probability of perturbation. Escaped cells were excluded from all downstream analyses. Knockdown classification rates were computed per perturbation as the proportion of cells assigned knockdown status. Linkage disequilibrium analysis was performed on knockdown and non-targeting control cells using MixscapeLDA on the PRTB assay (ten principal components, log2[fold change] threshold 0.25) and a UMAP was computed on the top linear discriminant analysis dimensions for visualization of perturbation-specific clustering.

Differential expression between KD and non-targeting control cells was performed per perturbation using DESeq2 (v.1.52.0). Cells were assigned randomly to three pseudo-replicates within each perturbation and non-targeting control group, and raw counts were aggregated. Genes with fewer than ten total counts across pseudo-replicates were excluded. Differential expression was tested using a negative binomial model with perturbation condition as the independent variable. Results were filtered at FDR < 0.05.

Pre-ranked GSEA was performed per perturbation using fgsea (v.1.38.0) with genes ranked by log2[fold change] from DESeq2. Gene sets from MSigDB Hallmark and Gene Ontology Biological Process collections were obtained using the msigdbr (v.26.1.1) package (Homo sapiens). Minimum and maximum gene set sizes were set to 10 and 500, respectively, with 1,000 permutations. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method across perturbations.

Pairwise cosine similarity was computed between the log2[fold change] vectors of all perturbations across the union of tested genes. Missing values were replaced with zero. Perturbations were clustered hierarchically on the resulting similarity matrix and visualized as a heatmap using pheatmap (v.1.0.13).

cNMF26,84 was applied to the raw count matrix of Mixscape-classified knockdown and non-targeting control cells. Counts were exported as an AnnData h5ad file and processed using the cNMF Python package (v.1.7.1). Genes expressed in fewer than five cells and all-zero genes were removed before factorization. Factorization was performed over k = 5–10 components with 100 iterations per k value and random seed 42. The optimal k = 8 was selected based on stability and reconstruction error, with a local-density threshold of 0.01 applied during consensus clustering; 99 of 800 (12%) outlier spectra were removed. Six of eight programmes were retained based on coherent gene set enrichment; two programmes dominated by unannotated loci and lacking enrichment were excluded. cNMF usage scores were imported back into R and added to the Seurat object metadata. Programme identity was assigned by over-representation analysis of the top 100 programme-specific genes against MSigDB Hallmark and Gene Ontology Biological Process gene sets using enricher from clusterProfiler (v.4.20.0).

Transcription factor motif enrichment per cNMF programme was assessed by over-representation analysis of top 100 programme genes against Gene Transcription Regulation Database transcription factor target-gene sets from MSigDB C3. Transcription factor activity was estimated using the univariate linear model method implemented in decoupleR (v.2.17.0), with the DoRothEA regulon85 (confidence levels A–C) as the prior network. cNMF gene specificity score vectors (genes × programmes) were used as input. Transcription factors with the highest variance across programmes were retained for visualization.

FMC module scores were computed using AddModuleScore in Seurat on a curated list of FMC marker genes as described above. Stimulated SMC up- and down-regulated gene sets were derived from FindAllMarkers differential expression between stimulated (Zim3_3d3d) and control (Zim3_ctrl) conditions, filtered at FDR < 0.05 and |log2[fold change]| > 0.25. A signed stimulation score was computed as the difference between up- and down-regulated module scores. Spearman correlations between module scores and cNMF programme usage were computed using cor.test. Per perturbation mean scores were compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction. The Spearman correlation between each perturbation’s full log2[fold change] profile and the signed stimulated SMC log2[fold change] vector was computed across shared genes.

RRA86 was applied across all perturbation DESeq2 gene rankings using the RobustRankAggreg (v.1.2.1) R package, separately for upregulated and down-regulated genes ranked by adjusted P value. Genes passing FDR < 0.05 after Benjamini–Hochberg correction of RRA scores were retained as the convergent programme. Each gene was assigned a direction based on the RRA result with the lower score. The top 30 convergent genes by number of perturbations in which they reached significance were selected for visualization. A convergent programme score was computed per cell as the difference between up and down module scores using AddModuleScore. Pathway enrichment of convergent up and down-regulated genes was performed using enricher against MSigDB Hallmark and GO Biological Process gene sets.

We called cohort-specific 3D structures using the AQuA Tools framework90 for 3D genomics. Sample-wise chromatin structures were called in BEDPE format using extract_bedpe. We used union_bedpe to stack and merge BEDPE coordinates coming from various samples into one BEDPE file that contained the union set coordinates of all possible Hi-C connections in the cohort. This allowed for resolving overlapping interactions and outputting a common scaffold. To assign membership (as unique identifiers to column 7) to the stacked loops based on shared overlap of BEDPE anchors, we used cluster_bedpe. This resulting file became the mosaic for the cohort. To assess their functional and disease relevance, we intersected two-dimensional mosaic coordinates with one-dimensional genomic features including genes and snATAC peaks harbouring CAD GWAS variants. Against the mosaic BEDPE, contact values for each sample were queried in inherent and counts per million (CPM) numeric spaces using query_bedpe. All downstream analyses were performed in R.

The protein G Dynabeads were washed and the immunoprecipitated DNA was de-crosslinked at 67 °C for 2 h and eluted (50 mM sodium bicarbonate, 1% SDS).
