An Azzedine Alaïa Exhibition in San Francisco Makes a Case for the Human Touch

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

It’s possible to view “Sculpting Fashion”—the new Azzedine Alaïa exhibition at San Francisco’s Legion of Honor museum—as a kind of benevolent Trojan Horse that has infiltrated big tech’s ground zero with a dazzling demonstration of human capability and physicality. “There’s no one anywhere in the world that can touch him in terms of construction. And no one else makes your body look better,” says model Veronica Webb in a vintage clip included in a film made for the show.

To be clear, the exhibition isn’t “anti” anything. “Azzedine for sure would’ve been fascinated [by AI] because he was fascinated by the telephone,” said Carla Sozzani, president of the Fondation Azzedine Alaïa. The designer, who once said couture necessitated a “state-of-the-art spirit,” did indeed use technology as a tool, working with Italian knitwear specialists on technical fabrics and the like, but what is breathtaking in this show is his manual savoir-faire.

The first American retrospective of the designer’s work, “Sculpting Fashion,” was curated by the Legion of Honor’s Laura L. Camerlengo with Olivier Saillard, director of the designer’s foundation, and it anticipates the tenth anniversary of the designer’s passing in 2017. Alaïa had a relationship with the museum, having visited in 2005 when he received an honorary doctorate from the Academy of Art University.

Before his death, Alaïa established a protocol for exhibiting his work that Saillard and the foundation stick to. The designer had clear, headless forms based on Naomi Campbell’s body created for displaying clothes. They stand on raised plinths, taking on a monumental quality and fixing attention on Alaïa’s construction and fit and materials. In “Sculpting Fashion” this focus is uninterrupted by any of the ephemera—photographs, magazine clippings, etc.—that has become common in fashion displays. What’s interesting here is how, as with “Art and Fashion” at the Louvre last year, garments have taken up residence in the art galleries. A baroque beaded dress in black and red has been placed among religious paintings and under a 15th-century decorative ceiling that was once part of a Spanish palace, and two coquettish looks in celadon green and white keep company with a sofa once owned by Marie Antoinette (and recently acquired by the museum) in a room devoted to the 18th century. Visitors who haven’t come to the Legion of Honor for “Sculpting Fashion” will encounter his work in the first floor galleries; the main exhibition is displayed downstairs.

Grace Jones plays hostess to the show in absentia. The first, spotlit, piece on display is the lilac, hooded jersey bandage dress the performer and model wore when Alaïa picked up two awards at the Oscars de la Mode in Paris in 1985. Behind and surrounding that wonder are other designs arranged in a manner that feels almost Grecian. What’s really on display is the divine feminine as unleashed by an autodidact designer whose work seems to defy time—the result, in part, of his evolutionary, rather than trend-driven, approach to design. “The clothes I make can not be dated,” Alaïa once said. “They are made to last.”

Born in Tunisia in 1935, Alaïa enrolled at the École des Beaux Arts school in Tunis to study sculpture at 15. “When I realized I could not become a great sculptor, I turned myself to fashion,” says Sozzani, quoting the designer in the exhibition film. Alaïa’s twin sister Hafida taught him to sew and over time he became a dressmaker, a practice he’d continue in Paris, where he moved in 1956. Alaïa worked briefly with some of the French maisons, but his real education came from living breathing women, the performers he costumed at the Crazy Horse, friends, models, and clients. He was also tutored by the greats of fashion history. The designer amassed a collection of 15,000 historical garments over his lifetime, some of which he took apart, but most of which he lovingly preserved. Some of these are on loan to the Legion of Honor and are displayed in a separate room from Alaïa’s designs. “He believed true innovation required an understanding of the past,” Camerlengo explains.

Azzedine Alaïa’s Archetypes: Red knit dress, spring 1992 ready-to-wear

The classicism of Alaïa’s work, the sense that it exists in continuity with what’s come before, is especially resonant in this destabilizing era of technological expansion. In the museum’s Rodin gallery where two column dresses are on view, “you can see a sculpture, which is a huge hand, and you see the clothes. It reminds me that Azzedine spent his life doing something with his hands,” says Saillard.

The second room of the exhibition speaks to Alaïa’s relationship with America. In 1982 the designer, then still relatively unknown, was invited by Dawn Mello to do an in-store show at Bergdorf Goodman; it was a raging success. Camerlengo stresses here how Alaïa’s designs spoke to more assertive and sexualized expressions of feminism in the ’80s, following the fitting-in approach women entering the workforce had adopted the previous decade. “After a decade of concealing their bodies in androgynous clothing in male-dominated corporate spaces,” the curator writes, “professional women flocked to Alaïa’s formfitting clothes, made in supple leathers and stretch knits that celebrated the female form.”

Azzedine Alaïa and the United States: Zippered denum dress, spring 1986 ready-to-wear

The two arteries of couture, tailoring and flou, are addressed in the third room. Here, Alaïa’s rather dandy suiting shares space with romantic pieces inspired by the 18th century. Saillard recounted that the designer had moved into a building where Madame du Pompadour (the mistress of Louis XV) had lived, and she was an inspiration for his spring 1992 collection.

All sorts of couture techniques are on display in the next room. While the Legion of Honor’s collection is mainly European, in “Sculpting Fashion” the influence of Egypt and Africa are visible. Camerlengo makes a connection between the designer’s cut leathers and mashirabya, the latticework screens of North Africa, for example.

An Enduring Legacy: Dress worn by Tina Turner, spring 1989 couture

Azzedine Alaïa and Tina Turner, Paris, 1989. Photograph by Peter Lindbergh.

Alaïa was also greatly influenced by the movies, to which his grandparents frequently took him as a child, and the final room is dedicated to all-out glamour. It’s here that the gold bead dress he made for Tina Turner holds court among other stunners, some that pay homage to the ’30s and others that demonstrate why Alaïa was dubbed “The King of Cling.”

“Sculpting Fashion” is on view at the Legion of Honor from November 21, 2026 through April 11, 2027.
