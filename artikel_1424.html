Mycobacterium tuberculosis, the causative agent of tuberculosis, remains the world’s deadliest pathogen1. Despite intensive study, many of its genes remain uncharacterized, which limits the insight that can be gained into tuberculosis biology and constrains drug development. Genetic interaction (GI) mapping is a powerful strategy to define functional relationships between genes by quantifying how the phenotypic effects of combined perturbations deviate from expectations based on single-gene perturbations2,3. Here we develop a dual-sgRNA CRISPR-interference platform that enables high-throughput GI mapping in mycobacteria. We address key technical challenges in library construction and data interpretation by incorporating unique barcodes for each sgRNA pair and implementing a robust statistical pipeline for GI detection. Focusing on essential genes, we generate comprehensive GI maps in the model mycobacterium Mycobacterium smegmatis and the pathogen M. tuberculosis. These maps recapitulate known biology, uncover thousands of previously unrecognized interactions and identify functional connections between essential genes and drug targets. By generating AlphaFold3-based protein–protein interaction predictions4 for all gene pairs in our GI maps, we show that a subset of GIs is likely to reflect underlying physical associations. Validation of selected GIs confirms both novel biological relationships and opportunities for synergistic targeting. Overall, this work establishes a generalizable framework for dual-gene perturbation and defines the architecture of essential-gene networks in mycobacteria, advancing both our understanding of tuberculosis biology and the rational design of combination therapies.

Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), the world’s leading infectious cause of death1, is among the bacteria that have been most extensively sequenced, but the functions and interactions of many of its genes remain poorly understood. This knowledge gap limits our understanding of the genetic foundations of tuberculosis (TB) and hinders the development of effective therapies.

Genetic interaction (GI) mapping is a powerful approach for deciphering the functional relationships between genes2,3. It involves systematically assessing how perturbing one gene modifies the phenotype associated with perturbing a second gene. A GI is defined as a combined phenotype that deviates from the expectation based on single-gene perturbations. Positive interactions occur when dual perturbation produces a milder phenotype than expected, whereas negative interactions—including synthetic lethal effects—result in a more severe phenotype. Applied at scale, GI mapping yields quantitative interaction profiles that serve as high-resolution phenotypes3. These profiles can generate functional hypotheses for uncharacterized genes, reveal surprising roles for genes of known function and identify connections between distinct biological pathways3. Moreover, they can highlight potential synergistic and antagonistic drug–target relationships, informing combination therapies5.

GI mapping in Mtb has historically been constrained by biological and technical challenges, including slow growth, biosafety requirements and limited early genetic tools. The development of high-density transposon mutagenesis libraries combined with hybridization- or sequencing-based readouts (TnSeq)6,7 constituted a major advance, permitting genome-wide identification of conditionally essential genes8 and GI mapping in deletion mutants9. However, such approaches are typically restricted to non-essential genes, excluding most antibiotic targets. CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) has since emerged as a complementary technique10,11,12,13, enabling conditional and tunable knockdown of both essential and non-essential genes. In bacterial systems, dual-gene knockdowns have been used to identify GIs among small gene sets14, and larger-scale efforts have paired individual deletion or knockdown strains with genome-wide libraries15,16. Dual-CRISPRi and dual-TnSeq approaches for comprehensive GI mapping have been implemented in Bacillus subtilis and Streptococcus pneumoniae17,18,19. However, technical limitations have thus far precluded the development of a comparable system in Mtb.

To enable high-throughput GI mapping in Mtb, we developed a dual-single-guide RNA (sgRNA) CRISPRi platform for mycobacteria. Addressing key challenges in library construction and data analysis, we devised a robust experimental and statistical pipeline for high-confidence detection of GIs. Using this platform, we mapped GIs among nearly all essential genes in Mycobacterium smegmatis (Msm) and Mtb, prioritizing this technically demanding class of genes because of their centrality to cellular processes and importance as antibiotic targets. These maps recapitulate known biological relationships, validating the platform, and also uncover thousands of novel GIs. We further performed AlphaFold3 co-folding for all gene pairs (approximately 600,000) represented in the GI maps, identifying GIs that are likely to reflect physical associations and offering a basis for mechanistic hypotheses. Finally, we validated selected predictions from the maps, including drug–drug interactions and roles in the electron transport chain (ETC) for several genes of unknown function. Together, these maps constitute a powerful resource for elucidating Mtb biology and guiding the rational design of combination therapies.

To enable dual-sgRNA CRISPRi-based GI mapping in Mtb, we adapted the Combinatorial Genetics En Masse (CombiGEM) framework20 to the Mtb CRISPRi platform10, assigning each sgRNA a barcode, and using a cloning workflow that preserves sgRNA–barcode linkage and positions paired barcodes in close proximity (Extended Data Fig. 1a). This design permits accurate quantification of dual-sgRNA strain abundance by barcode sequencing, facilitating high-throughput GI mapping.

GI detection for essential genes requires sgRNAs that impose a precise fitness cost: sufficient to reveal interactions, yet not so severe as to mask additive or synergistic effects. Compounding this challenge, the combinatorial scale of dual-sgRNA libraries necessitates selecting only a small number of sgRNAs per gene. To define the optimal fitness cost for GI detection, we performed a pilot screen in Msm using a panel of 16 genes that are predicted to interact. We selected up to 24 sgRNAs per gene (Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table 1) spanning a broad range of fitness costs, cloned a dual-sgRNA library containing all pairwise permutations and performed a GI screen using the workflow planned for larger-scale studies (Extended Data Fig. 1b). The resulting data allowed us to identify the sgRNA strength window that best supports GI detection under these conditions: an approximately 8% reduction in fitness per generation (Extended Data Fig. 1c–h and Supplementary Discussion).

To establish feasibility, we built a comprehensive essential-gene GI library in Msm. Guided by the pilot screen, we selected up to two sgRNAs per gene with reproducible, intermediate fitness costs (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). The library contains 1,524 sgRNAs targeting 787 genes, plus 10 non-targeting (NT) controls, yielding about 2.3 million sgRNA permutations. Target genes included those that are essential for Msm fitness and those that are non-essential in Msm but essential in Mtb; the latter were targeted by the predicted strongest sgRNAs10. The library was cloned in Escherichia coli, validated by deep sequencing (Extended Data Fig. 2a) and transformed into an Msm strain containing a chromosomally encoded Streptococcus thermophilus (Sth1) dCas9 expression cassette. This yielded a pooled library in which each bacterium carries a single integrated guide plasmid expressing two sgRNAs. We subjected this library to competitive growth, tracking dual-sgRNA strain abundance over time by barcode sequencing. Strain depletion was modelled using a piecewise linear regression10, providing a quantitative readout of fitness.

Growth phenotypes were highly reproducible across biological replicates (Extended Data Fig. 2b,c). As expected, strains with two NT sgRNAs showed minimal depletion, single-targeting strains exhibited moderate depletion and dual-targeting strains showed the greatest depletion (Fig. 1b). sgRNA position within the plasmid had little effect on fitness, indicating consistent activity from both expression cassettes (Fig. 1c).

To identify GIs, we compared the observed fitness of each dual-targeting strain to the expected outcome under a null model of no interaction (Fig. 1d,e). Deviations from this expectation were quantified and corrected for floor effects (Fig. 1f and Extended Data Fig. 2d,e), and then aggregated at the gene-pair level to generate a GI score. Gene pairs with greater-than-expected depletion were assigned negative GI scores, and those with less-than-expected depletion yielded positive GI scores (Fig. 1d). To assess confidence, GI scores were modelled as a mixture of typical deviations (normal distribution) and deviations outside the norm (uniform distribution). Gene pairs with interaction probabilities of 0.5 < p ≤ 0.95 were designated likely interactions, and those with p > 0.95 confident interactions.

The Msm GI map revealed nearly 8,500 interactions (p > 0.5), with negative GIs outnumbering positive ones (Fig. 1g–i and Supplementary Table 2). This 2.7% hit rate across gene pairs aligns with previous GI studies3. Most genes interacted with fewer than 15 partners, but a subset exhibited high connectivity, consistent with hub-like behaviour that has been observed in other organisms, such as yeast3 (Fig. 1i). Out of 787 targeted genes, 64 (8.1%) showed no interactions under these conditions. Notably, GI strength did not correlate with gene vulnerability10, confirming that our fitness-based sgRNA selection accounts for gene vulnerability (Extended Data Fig. 2f). sgRNAs targeting the same gene exhibited highly correlated interaction profiles (Fig. 1j), underscoring the reproducibility of measured GIs.

Illustrative examples confirm that the map captures known or mechanistically plausible functional relationships. We observed a strong negative GI between the Hsp40 chaperones dnaJ1 and dnaJ2 (Fig. 1e), consistent with their known synthetic lethal relationship21. Another strong negative GI, between cwlM and murA (Fig. 1k), is consistent with the proposed role of CwlM in binding to MurA, stimulating its activity, and promoting peptidoglycan precursor synthesis22.

With individual GIs providing initial confidence in the dataset, we next sought a global assessment of the functional signal encoded in the map. To this end, we analysed correlations among GI profiles, because profile similarity often reflects similar gene function3.

Pairwise correlation and clustering revealed that genes with related biological functions tended to group together (Fig. 1l). We systematically annotated the tree, identifying 63 functionally coherent clusters containing 2 to 48 genes each. Representative clusters spanned functions including cell-wall synthesis, protein degradation, cell division, cofactor and nucleotide synthesis, the ETC, and protein folding and ribosome rescue (Fig. 1l, top to bottom, left to right). Several observations highlight the biological relevance of these clusters. First, cwlM and murA share not only a strong negative GI (Fig. 1k), but also highly correlated GI profiles22 (Fig. 1l). Second, the two genes most closely associated with the mycolic acid transporter mmpL3—pgfA and ttfA—were previously shown to interact physically with MmpL3 and support mycolic acid transport23,24. Third, the transcription factors whiA and whiB2, which are involved in cell division and act in a co-dependent manner in Streptomyces, clustered with each other and with ftsZ, consistent with their established roles25 as transcriptional activators of ftsZ. Many clusters also contained previously uncharacterized genes, as well as functionally annotated genes with unexpected correlations, suggesting unrecognized biological connections. Barring technical limitations (for example, polar effects), these clusters offer functional predictions by placing genes in the context of their network relationships.

Encouraged by the validity of the Msm map, we next extended our approach to Mtb. Following the same experimental blueprint, we constructed a dual-sgRNA library containing up to two sgRNAs per gene with reproducible, intermediate fitness costs (Supplementary Table 1). The library includes 1,483 sgRNAs targeting 762 genes, most of them essential for in vitro Mtb growth, plus 10 NT controls, yielding about 2.2 million sgRNA permutations. The library was cloned in E. coli, validated by deep sequencing (Extended Data Fig. 2a) and transformed into an H37Rv Mtb strain containing a chromosomally integrated Sth1 dCas9 cassette. Competitive growth and barcode sequencing were performed as in Msm.

As in Msm, growth phenotypes were highly reproducible across biological replicates (Extended Data Fig. 2g,h). Dual-NT, single-targeting and dual-targeting strains depleted on average in the expected order, from least to most (Fig. 2a). sgRNA position within the plasmid had a greater effect than it did in Msm (Fig. 2b), but the effect was modest and deemed acceptable, because our GI model accounts for sgRNA position. The Mtb GI map revealed nearly 6,500 interactions (p > 0.5), with negative GIs outnumbering positive ones (Fig. 2d–f and Supplementary Table 3). This 2.2% hit rate across all gene pairs parallels the Msm map. Most genes interacted with fewer than 12 partners, but a subset exhibited high connectivity (Fig. 2f), and 3.4% (26/762) of targeted genes showed no interactions under these conditions. As in Msm, sgRNAs targeting the same gene exhibited highly correlated interaction profiles (Fig. 2g), and GI strength did not correlate with gene vulnerability (Extended Data Fig. 2k). We observed strong negative GIs between dnaJ1 and dnaJ2 (ref. 21) and between cwlM and murA (ref. 22) (Fig. 2h,i). Further strong negative GIs recapitulated known or mechanistically plausible functional relationships, including (1) transcription factors and their targets (Extended Data Fig. 3a–c); (2) partially redundant enzymes (Extended Data Fig. 3d,e); and (3) functionally related genes within shared biological processes (Fig. 2j and Extended Data Fig. 3f). Reflecting the biological coherence of the data, clustering by GI profile similarity grouped genes with related functions (Fig. 2k).

To test platform reproducibility, we repeated the Mtb GI screen using a slightly higher concentration of anhydrotetracycline (ATc) to ensure saturating induction of dCas9 and sgRNA (Supplementary Table 3). We refer to the original and replicate screens as experiment 1 and experiment 2, respectively. The second screen showed slightly greater positional variability but also more pronounced depletion of single- and dual-targeting constructs (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b), resulting in a wider distribution of GI scores (Extended Data Fig. 4c–e) and a higher signal-to-noise ratio. Key benchmarks were again met, including concordance among replicates (Extended Data Fig. 2l–o), high GI profile similarity among sgRNAs targeting the same gene (Extended Data Fig. 4f) and a lack of correlation between GI score and gene vulnerability (Extended Data Fig. 2p). Most importantly, the replicate map recapitulated the functional information in the original. Summary metrics, including the overall hit rate (2.3%) and per-gene interaction number (Extended Data Fig. 4g), were comparable; specific GIs were reproduced across the two screens (Extended Data Figs. 3g–l and 4h,i); and functionally related genes clustered together (Extended Data Fig. 4j).

GI scores were strongly correlated between the Mtb experiments (R = 0.82; Extended Data Fig. 4k). However, concordance between interaction probabilities was lower (Supplementary Table 3), particularly near the boundary between the null and interaction distributions, where experiment-specific fits diverged. To reconcile these differences, we extended our statistical framework to jointly model GI scores from both experiments (Extended Data Fig. 4k). The model assigns each gene pair a joint probability and, when sufficiently confident, classifies the interaction as negative, positive or discordant. Joint modelling identified confident GIs for approximately 2.5% of Mtb gene pairs (p > 0.95) and likely GIs for 4.4% (p > 0.5).

Given the substantial but incomplete overlap between the two maps, we retained data from both screens. Unless otherwise specified, the individual Mtb GI scores and downstream analyses presented in the following sections are based on experiment 2, and the joint model provides an integrated assessment of interaction confidence across both experiments.

Having established platform robustness in both Mtb and Msm, we analysed GIs involving homologous genes targeted in both libraries. Consistent with comparative GI work in budding and fission yeast26, homologues showed a modestly higher GI profile correlation than did non-homologues (Fig. 3a,b), reflecting a mixture of conserved (for example, Figs. 1e,k and 2h,i) and non-conserved (for example, Extended Data Fig. 5a–c) interactions. A global comparison of GI scores for homologous gene pairs revealed limited correlation across species (R = 0.26; Extended Data Fig. 5d), which increased substantially when the analysis was restricted to gene pairs sharing the same biological process annotation (R = 0.57; Extended Data Fig. 5e and Supplementary Table 4). Likewise, within-process GIs were conserved at a higher rate than the overall set of interactions (Extended Data Fig. 5f). Together, these findings indicate that mycobacteria recapitulate the pattern of GI conservation observed in budding and fission yeast, in which interactions that link genes in the same biological process are preferentially conserved relative to interactions that link distinct processes26.

We next examined the functional organization of Mtb GIs by testing for enrichment of interactions within and between biological processes (Supplementary Table 4). We focused on negative GIs, because they are more abundant in our dataset and more likely to reflect functional relationships, following the precedent in yeast3. Gene pairs comprising genes in the same biological process were enriched for negative GIs (on-diagonal; Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 5g), consistent with limited tolerance for simultaneous knockdown within essential pathways. Negative GIs were also enriched among gene pairs spanning distinct biological processes—for example, genes involved in synthesizing different components of the cell envelope (such as arabinogalactan and lipoarabinomannan), and genes involved in DNA replication and DNA repair (off-diagonal; Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 5g). Beyond these process-level patterns, gene pairs with highly correlated GI profiles were themselves enriched for negative GIs (Extended Data Fig. 4l), consistent with the expectation that genes acting in the same pathway are more likely to share interaction partners and interact with each other3.

To investigate the molecular basis of GIs, we asked how frequently they reflect physical associations. Because no large-scale experimental protein–protein interaction (PPI) map exists for Mtb, we generated PPI predictions using pooled AlphaFold3 co-folding4,27 across the approximately 600,000 gene pairs screened in the Mtb and Msm GI libraries. We identified 15,240 candidate PPIs using a size-corrected interface predicted template modelling score (s.c. ipTM) cut-off ≥ 0.05 (Fig. 3d,e, Extended Data Fig. 6a–h and Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Because size correction systematically reduces ipTM scores, this threshold is necessarily permissive, yet the overall hit rate (2.6%) remains low. Many of these candidates achieve higher ipTM scores when evaluated pairwise with AlphaFold-Multimer28. Consistent with observations in yeast3, gene pairs with negative GIs or highly correlated GI profiles were enriched for predicted physical interactions (Fig. 3f). Nevertheless, many lacked a predicted PPI, because GIs can arise from pathway-, process- or network-level relationships without direct binding. Overall, this resource identifies gene pairs that are both genetically and physically linked, providing a framework for predicting their molecular mechanisms. We present several representative examples spanning both known22,29,30,31 (Fig. 3g–j) and previously unrecognized (Fig. 3k–n) biological connections.

Previous studies have shown that GIs can predict drug–drug interactions5. To test whether this principle holds in our dataset, we analysed GIs among established drug targets.

One of the strongest negative GIs in both Mtb and Msm involved aspC (alanine aminotransferase16,32) and ddl (d-alanine-d-alanine ligase; Fig. 4a). Although previously unknown, this interaction is mechanistically plausible: AspC is crucial for biosynthesis of l-Ala, which is required by Alr (alanine racemase) to produce d-Ala, the substrate for Ddl (Fig. 4b). aspC also showed a negative GI with alr. Alr and Ddl are the targets of d-cycloserine33, a second-line agent for multidrug-resistant TB. Consistent with the GI data, CRISPRi-based chemical–genetic interaction (CGI) profiling of d-cycloserine identified aspC as one of the top sensitizing hits (Fig. 4c,d and Supplementary Table 5). Moreover, a paper published while we revised this one reported synergy between an AspC inhibitor and d-cycloserine34.

Another set of novel, mechanistically plausible interactions involved PptT, a phosphopantetheinyl transferase that activates acyl carrier proteins (ACPs) required for lipid biosynthesis and is an active drug development target35. PptT showed negative GIs with kasA, hadB and hadC (Fig. 4e), which catalyse steps in the FAS-II pathway responsible for mycolic acid biosynthesis (Fig. 4f), and are likewise being pursued as drug targets36,37. Consistent with the GI data, CGI profiling of thiolactomycin, a KasA and KasB inhibitor, identified pptT as a top sensitizing hit (Extended Data Fig. 7a and Supplementary Table 5). Moreover, co-treating Mtb with thiolactomycin and the PptT inhibitor 8918 produced strong synergy in both the H37Rv reference and clinical Mtb strains38 representing diverse lineages (Fig. 4g,h). This synergy was specific: neither thiolactomycin nor 8918 synergized with control drug treatments (Extended Data Fig. 7b–g). Although KasA inhibitors and rifampicin are synergistic39, kasA and rifampicin’s target rpoB did not show a negative GI (Supplementary Table 3). In this case, KasA inhibition is likely to potentiate rifampicin by increasing envelope permeability and intracellular rifampicin exposure, rather than through a target-based mechanism39. Supporting this interpretation, knockdown of rpoB did not sensitize to thiolactomycin in our CGI screen (Extended Data Fig. 7a). These examples show how KasA inhibition can drive synergy through both target-specific and chemotype-specific mechanisms, and how GI mapping can disentangle these modes of action. Combined inhibition of PptT and the HadAB and HadBC dehydratases likewise produced strong synergy (Fig. 4i,j), providing further evidence that negative GIs can predict drug–drug interactions.

To assess whether positive GIs likewise have predictive value, we focused on the pks13–efpA pair. Pks13 and EfpA are essential proteins involved in mycolic acid biosynthesis and lipid translocation, respectively, and both are active targets for TB drug development40,41. The pair showed a strong positive GI (Fig. 4k), predicting drug–drug antagonism, which was confirmed by checkerboard assays of preclinical Pks13 and EfpA inhibitors (Fig. 4l and Extended Data Fig. 7h,i).

As a final proof-of-concept, we examined PknA and PknB, essential eukaryotic-like serine/threonine kinases that regulate Mtb growth and division and are promising drug targets42. pknA and pknB exhibited strong negative GIs with embA and embB (Fig. 4m), which encode arabinogalactan biosynthetic enzymes targeted by the first-line TB drug ethambutol43. Co-treatment with ethambutol and the PknA and PknB inhibitor V49 resulted in borderline synergy in checkerboard assays (Extended Data Fig. 7j), but clear synergy in bacterial killing assays (Fig. 4n). This bactericidal interaction seemed to be specific: pknA and pknB did not exhibit negative GIs with ATP synthase genes (Fig. 4m), and V49 did not enhance killing by the ATP synthase inhibitor bedaquiline44 (Fig. 4n). Although V49 and bedaquiline showed borderline synergy in checkerboard assays (Extended Data Fig. 7k), this might reflect the same uptake-mediated effect proposed for KasA inhibition and rifampicin, rather than a target-based interaction39. Together, these results illustrate how GI mapping can reveal combination effects that are missed by conventional growth-inhibition assays.

Beyond the specific interactions discussed above, the Mtb GI map offers broader opportunities for drug discovery. Strong GIs between currently pursued TB drug targets are rare, consistent with the general scarcity of target-based synergy and antagonism45. Nonetheless, the map reveals thousands of previously unexplored interactions among established and potential drug targets across diverse pathways. We cover several of these interactions in the Supplementary Discussion and Extended Data Figs. 8 and 9. The map also identifies a set of ‘pleiotropic sensitizers’—including ppk, macE, dnaA, rnc, treS, sodA and pknG—that showed strong negative GIs across numerous targets, consistent with findings from our previous CGI studies39. Inhibitors of these pleiotropic sensitizers could potentiate multiple drug classes by globally altering bacterial metabolism, stress responses or regulatory networks, making these genes promising candidates for combination therapy aimed at amplifying the activity of diverse antibacterial agents.

Oxidative phosphorylation—the combined activities of the ETC and ATP synthase—has emerged as an important antibiotic target in Mtb and related pathogens. Clinically validated examples include the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved ATP synthase inhibitor bedaquiline44 and telacebec (Q203)46, a clinical drug candidate that targets complex III. In mycobacteria, complex III2IV2 (Fig. 5a) is the primary terminal oxidase that establishes the proton gradient driving ATP synthesis47. This supercomplex (larger than 700 kDa) consists of 24 protein subunits47 that position 18 redox cofactors to enable electron transfer coupled with proton translocation. In eukaryotes, numerous proteins chaperoning the maturation of complex III and IV have been identified, and defects in these assembly factors cause severe hereditary diseases48. In mycobacteria, the mechanisms of complex III2IV2 assembly and cofactor incorporation remain mostly unknown49.

We identified a cluster of genes of unknown function that strongly correlated with genes encoding subunits of complex III2IV2 and with genes involved in cofactor biogenesis, in both Msm and Mtb (Figs. 1l and 5b,c and Extended Data Fig. 10a). Many of these uncharacterized genes also showed negative GIs with the alternative, partially redundant terminal oxidase, cytochrome bd oxidase (cyt. bd), encoded by cydA and cydB (Fig. 5d,e). Synthetic lethality with cyt. bd is a hallmark of defects in complex III2IV2 in mycobacteria50. We thus hypothesized that these genes contribute to the assembly or function of complex III2IV2. Several lines of evidence support this hypothesis: (1) independent single-strain knockdowns using sgRNAs not included in the screen validated the GI results in both Msm and Mtb (Fig. 5f–i); (2) complementation experiments in Msm ruled out off-target effects and established the functional equivalence of Mtb homologues (Extended Data Fig. 10b); (3) these genes showed negative GIs with each other in Mtb (Extended Data Fig. 10c); (4) AlphaFold3 predictions suggest physical interactions among several of these genes and with CtaD, a complex IV subunit (Fig. 5j,k and Extended Data Fig. 10d); and (5) knockdown of these genes in strains lacking cyt. bd reduced oxygen consumption, an indicator of complex III2IV2 activity (Fig. 5l and Extended Data Fig. 10e–g), and increased sensitivity to Q203 to varying extents (Fig. 5m and Extended Data Fig. 10h).

To extend these findings biochemically, we focused on ms2764, selected for its AlphaFold3-predicted interactions and the tractability of generating a functional 3×Flag-tagged construct with correct membrane topology. To our knowledge, there are no previous studies of ms2764 or its Mtb homologue rv2698, which encode membrane proteins containing a DUF3093 domain that seems to be restricted to Actinobacteria. Affinity purification of Ms2764–3×Flag followed by mass spectrometry (AP–MS) showed co-purification of complex III2IV2 subunits and several other uncharacterized genes in the cluster (Fig. 5n). Motivated by this evidence of physical interaction, we generated an ms2764 knockout and assayed respiratory activity in inverted membrane vesicles (IMVs). As shown previously51, NADH-driven IMV acidification can occur even when complex III2IV2 is inhibited or absent, whereas succinate-driven IMV acidification requires functional complex III2IV2. Similar to IMVs from a ∆qcrB strain, ∆ms2764 IMVs were capable of NADH-driven acidification but not succinate-driven acidification, indicating loss of complex III2IV2 activity (Fig. 5o) and providing biochemical evidence that Ms2764 contributes to complex function.

Closer inspection of these uncharacterized genes revealed that one (rv2235 or ms4311; here renamed surf1) contains a well-conserved SURF1 domain, known in other species to facilitate the incorporation of haem a3 into CtaD (ref. 49). A second gene (rv0102 or ms4702; here renamed ctiP) probably encodes a distant CtiP homologue (25% sequence identity) that, in Corynebacterium, seems to mediate CuB insertion into CtaD (ref. 49). Consistent with this proposed role, ctiP is encoded adjacent to chalkophore biosynthesis genes in Mtb, which supply copper to complex IV under copper-limiting conditions and during infection52. This genomic context suggests that CtiP collaborates with chalkophores to metallate complex IV. rv1342c (ms4900) and lprD are induced under iron starvation53, consistent with the iron dependence of complex III2IV2 activity, whereas rv1457c (ms3118) and rv1458c (ms3119) are located immediately upstream of ctaA, which catalyses the final step of haem A biosynthesis. For the remaining genes, no precedent links them to the ETC. They encode predicted integral membrane proteins (rv2698 (ms2764) and rv1083 (ms5260)) and lipoproteins (lppL, rv2980 (ms2396)). These uncharacterized genes might therefore participate in cofactor trafficking or installation or represent previously unrecognized subunits of complex III2IV2. Although further studies are required to define their molecular roles, these findings show how our GI platform enables hypothesis generation for previously uncharacterized genes.

A large portion of the Mtb genome remains poorly understood. Here, to help close this gap, we developed a scalable approach for mapping genetic interactions in mycobacteria, focusing on essential genes because they underpin core cellular processes and represent the targets of most antibiotics. Across the model species Msm and the pathogen Mtb, we screened more than four million sgRNA permutations and identified more than 15,000 GIs. This constitutes the first comprehensive GI map of essential genes in Mtb—and, to our knowledge, the first such map for essential genes in any organism beyond budding yeast3. Together with other large-scale resources41,54,55,56,57—including transcriptomic and metabolomic compendia, systematic gene knockdown and morphological profiling, curated TnSeq datasets and chemical–genetic profiling—these datasets unlock new opportunities for dissecting mycobacterial biology and guiding the rational design of drug combinations.

Three applications of our maps merit particular attention. First, they can identify interactions between drug targets, enabling prediction of synergistic or antagonistic drug combinations. Second, GI profile similarity can reveal functionally related genes. Third, GI mapping can uncover surprising links between well-characterized genes and distinct biological processes. Although some unexpected associations might reflect technical artefacts (for example, off-target or polar effects), many are likely to reveal genuine biology. For example, the ribonuclease III gene rnc, best known for rRNA maturation58 and CRISPR RNA processing59, clusters with cell-division genes in both Mtb and Msm (Fig. 1l). This association aligns with observations60 in Corynebacterium glutamicum, in which Rnc cleaves the mRNA of the cell-division regulator MraZ.

Shortening the duration of TB treatment remains a central objective in TB drug development. One promising strategy is to exploit interactions between drug targets. GI mapping, already a powerful approach in cancer therapeutics3, offers analogous opportunities for TB. First, GI maps can highlight synergistic and antagonistic target combinations to exploit and to avoid, guiding the rational selection of drug combinations. We validated four synergistic compound pairs (8918 + TLM, 8918 + TAZ, 8918 + ISO and V49 + EMB) and one antagonistic pair (BRD-8000.3 + mCMX410) predicted by our GI maps. We also identified and validated numerous negative GIs that link established drug targets to genes not currently targeted pharmacologically. These findings nominate the corresponding gene products as potential targets for future drug development. GI data can also illuminate the mechanistic basis of drug–drug synergy—distinguishing, for example, uptake-based and target-specific interactions. Moreover, because pairwise interactions might predict higher-order interactions61, these datasets provide a foundation for exploring regimen-level effects. This information represents a key piece of the puzzle, although other factors beyond drug synergies contribute to therapeutic success62. Second, GI maps can help to ‘de-orphan’ potential drug targets by predicting their function—an essential step, because understanding a target’s biochemical role is a prerequisite for target-based drug discovery. Third, GI profiles provide genetic signatures for inferring the mechanisms of action of uncharacterized compounds. A close resemblance between a compound’s CGI profile and a gene’s GI profile could implicate that gene as the compound’s primary target5. Two caveats are worth noting. First, CRISPRi-mediated target depletion is not equivalent to small-molecule inhibition. CRISPRi approximates noncompetitive inhibition; it does not capture the full spectrum of pharmacological mechanisms. CRISPRi perhaps most closely resembles targeted protein degradation, as exemplified by emerging BacPROTACs63. Consequently, GIs probably exhibit only partial overlap with CGIs. Second, drug interactions are concentration dependent, and the fitness effects observed here might not correspond to the concentration window in which a drug interaction occurs.

Elucidating gene function is fundamental to understanding Mtb biology. Although individual pairwise GIs can be informative, they often do not reveal the broader functional relationships between genes. GI profile correlations address this limitation by integrating GIs across gene pairs, enabling the identification of functionally related genes through shared interaction patterns. Indeed, clustering genes by profile similarity in our maps recovered coherent pathways, and placing a gene within a pathway is often a major step towards defining its role. However, pathway assignment alone rarely yields specific molecular hypotheses. To help bridge this gap and clarify the relationship between GIs and PPIs, we generated PPI predictions for all essential genes in Mtb and Msm. We found that genes exhibiting negative GIs, as well as those with correlated GI profiles, are enriched for predicted physical interactions, increasing confidence that these genetic connections reflect real biological relationships. However, the overlap between GIs and PPIs remains incomplete, underscoring that each dataset captures distinct but complementary dimensions of gene function. To illustrate how GI data can help to assign functions to uncharacterized genes, we examined a subset of genes of unknown function whose interaction profiles strongly correlated with those of ETC components. Through orthogonal assays, we confirmed their association with complex III2IV2, providing a first step for further investigation.

Our GI maps were generated using standard laboratory strains and culture conditions. Even in this form, our maps are relevant to clinical Mtb strains, enabling the prediction of strain-specific vulnerabilities. For instance, we previously showed39 that sublineage L1.2.1, which is endemic to the Philippines, Thailand and Indonesia, carries a loss-of-function mutation in whiB7. The GIs observed here suggest that this background is particularly sensitive to inhibition of histidine and alanine biosynthesis16. Beyond these predictions, our maps provide a foundation for GI mapping across diverse genetic backgrounds. Such studies could help to explain why, despite relative genetic homogeneity, Mtb strains exhibit differences in clinically relevant traits, including transmission and response to antibacterial agents64. At the same time, how growth environment reshapes GIs in Mtb remains an open question. Studies in budding yeast indicate that although most GIs are preserved across growth conditions, approximately 5–24% of them are condition specific65. Thus, defining how genetic and environmental contexts rewire Mtb’s GI network will be crucial to understanding its adaptive physiology and identifying condition-dependent drug vulnerabilities.

This work builds on pioneering efforts to map GIs in E. coli66 and budding yeast2,3 and expands on earlier efforts in Mtb9. By generating comprehensive GI maps of essential genes in Mtb and Msm, we provide a rich resource with broad utility for elucidating Mtb biology and guiding the rational design of combination therapies. All data are publicly available through an interactive web server (https://pebble.rockefeller.edu/pages/gi/). These maps lay the groundwork for probing how GIs change across environments—such as under antibiotic pressure or in infection-mimicking conditions. Moreover, this framework can be extended to encompass non-essential genes, ultimately enabling genome-wide, all-by-all GI maps. The experimental and computational strategies described here also provide a blueprint for systematic GI mapping in other pathogens, with the potential to accelerate therapeutic discovery and combat the growing threat of drug-resistant infectious diseases.

There are six main limitations to these GI maps. First, this dataset represents a first-generation GI map in mycobacteria and, as such, inevitably includes both false positives and false negatives. Future iterations incorporating improved library design, expanded replication and refined statistical modelling should increase confidence in individual interaction calls. Second, by design, this map focuses on essential genes, reflecting current experimental limits: extending the present approach to an all-by-all Mtb GI map would require testing around 64 million sgRNA permutations. Such comprehensive maps remain a goal for future work. Third, CRISPRi-mediated polar effects can produce spurious GIs and correlations. This issue is partially mitigated by the fact that genes within operons often participate in related pathways, although it remains an inherent limitation. Fourth, a small subset of genes (about 25 of 787 in Msm) exhibited numerous strong correlations that are unlikely to reflect genuine biological relationships. The cause of these correlations is unclear, and caution is warranted when investigating these genes. Fifth, although we observe overlap in ‘hub genes’ between our GI data and published CGI datasets39, some of these might represent technical artefacts. Finally, dCas9 can act as a physical barrier to DNA replication67, potentially producing artefactual negative GIs with DNA-replication-associated proteins.

With practical constraints limiting the design of GI libraries to two guides per gene, the identification of guides that are least likely to cause off-target knockdown took on new importance. A custom script was applied to each candidate guide to identify off-target matches, defined as instances of perfect complementarity between the 3′ (PAM-proximal) end of the sgRNA sequence and the genome, spanning ten or more base pairs and positioned next to a PAM site other than the intended target. Guides were then stratified into four tiers according to the following criteria: tier 1, no off-target matches; tier 2, no off-target matches targeting the non-template strand of a gene; tier 3, no off-target matches targeting the non-template strand of a gene with a PAM sequence conferring fold repression ≥ 42.2 in a luciferase knockdown assay13; and tier 4, all remaining guides.

Moving from the first tier to the fourth, the risk of off-target activity increases. The focus on non-template-targeting matches reflects previous data establishing the greater efficacy of non-template- versus template-targeting CRISPRi11,13. The PAM strength cut-off of 42.2 was chosen because weaker PAM sequences rarely produce detectable off-target effects, as inferred from depletion of guides targeting non-essential genes with off-target matches in essential genes.

The Msm GI library was designed to target all essential genes, in addition to Msm homologues of Mtb in vitro essential genes. Essentiality was defined as the union of TnSeq calls72 and CRISPRi calls10; in the latter case, essentiality was determined using a mixture model of the estimated maximum depletion rate, βmax, for each gene. This produced a list of 834 genes for which guide selection was attempted.

An automated guide selection pipeline was used to select up to two sgRNAs per gene. For genes essential by CRISPRi, sgRNAs were selected in several rounds, with each round becoming more permissive to increase the chance of identifying suitable sgRNAs. The first round of selection was restricted to sgRNAs imposing a specific fitness cost previously determined in a pilot screen to be optimal for detecting GIs: predicted log2-transformed fold change (L2FC) at 25 generations between –5 and –1 in our reference experiment10. In addition, only sgRNAs without off-target matches (off-target tier 1) were considered. In the second round, sgRNAs were permitted to have intergenic and template-targeting genic off-targets (off-target tier 2). In the third round, the guide pool was further expanded to include sgRNAs within a larger L2FC window (–6 to –0.5) and sgRNAs possessing weak non-template-targeting off-targets (off-target tier 3). The final round of selection placed no restrictions on off-target matches (off-target tier 4). In all four rounds, priority was given to sgRNAs closest to the logistic curve describing a gene’s expression–fitness relationship10.

For genes categorized as non-essential by CRISPRi, sgRNAs with the highest predicted strengths10 were selected. To minimize the potential for off-target effects without excessively constraining PAM site availability, a first round of selection was limited to sgRNAs in off-target tiers 1–3. In a second round of selection, the guide pool was expanded to include sgRNAs in off-target tier 4.

For all genes, the selection of length isomers (sgRNAs targeting the same PAM site) was prohibited. In addition, guides that were computationally flagged as outliers and guides introducing restriction sites necessary for library construction were excluded.

Automated guide selections were subjected to minimal manual curation, which involved (1) the replacement of sgRNAs exhibiting unexpected depletion and (2) the exclusion of genes for which only a single guide in off-target tier 4 could be identified.

The final selections consisted of 1,524 sgRNAs targeting 787 genes. The addition of 10 non-targeting control sgRNAs brought the final guide count to 1,534.

To profile CGIs, an optimized library was designed to target all Mtb genes. This library, designated RLC25, is a combination of two libraries: (1) the previously described library RLC3 (ref. 10), consisting of strong sgRNAs targeting in vitro non-essential genes; and (2) the recently designed library RLC14 (ref. 74), comprising hypomorphic sgRNAs that target in vitro essential genes (union of TnSeq and CRISPRi essentiality calls).

To select sgRNAs for RLC14, behaviour in the reference experiment was used as a starting point, with emphasis on the parameter βe, which models the rate of depletion of a given sgRNA over time10. For each in vitro essential gene, up to 20 sgRNAs were selected. The selection algorithm prioritized depletion rates around βe = −0.14 while also including some stronger and weaker guides. To achieve this, bins representing the 20th percentiles of a normal distribution centred at –0.14 were defined, and one sgRNA was selected from each bin. This approach ensured that most sgRNAs were selected near –0.14 (where the bulk of the distribution lies) while still incorporating guides from the distribution tails. If a bin contained no available sgRNAs, other bins were resampled until a maximum of 20 sgRNAs was reached. In total, 14,354 sgRNAs targeting 748 genes were selected. The addition of 646 non-targeting control sgRNAs brought the total guide count for RLC14 to 15,000.

Combining RLC3 (16,681 sgRNAs) and RLC14 to make RLC25, the final library contained 31,143 sgRNAs: 30,306 sgRNAs targeting 4,005 genes, along with 837 non-targeting control sgRNAs (538 sgRNAs are shared between RLC3 and RLC14). The Msm and Mtb GI libraries were cloned using an approach based on CombiGEM20,75. This involved first constructing a library of barcoded single-sgRNA constructs containing strategically placed restriction sites. These sites enabled efficient derivation of a dual-sgRNA library containing all pairwise sgRNA permutations, with the corresponding barcodes in close proximity to one another to facilitate high-throughput sequencing.

The biotechnology company Genewiz was commissioned to synthesize the single-sgRNA libraries. They received a list of the selected sgRNA sequences, each of which had been assigned a unique 18 base-pair barcode (Supplementary Table 1). Barcode sequences were derived from a published list (https://github.com/hawkjo/freebarcodes)76 after excluding those that would create restriction sites incompatible with downstream cloning. Each sgRNA–barcode pair was cloned individually into the plasmid backbone plRL389 (see ‘Plasmid construction’), with maintenance of restriction sites necessary for CombiGEM cloning. This approach prevents template switching77,78 between the sgRNA and its linked barcode, preserves sequence fidelity and ensures even representation during pooling. In the final constructs, EcoRI and SalI sites flank the sgRNA–barcode cassette, and MfeI and XhoI sites occupy a short spacer region between the sgRNA and the barcode. These restriction enzymes were selected because they recognize distinct sequences but generate compatible overhangs. The crucial region of each construct, encompassing the restriction sites, sgRNA and barcode, was verified by Genewiz using Sanger sequencing. The single-sgRNA libraries were designed so that digestion with MfeI and XhoI yields a backbone library, whereas digestion with EcoRI and SalI generates an insert library. A Bayesian mixture model was used to identify statistically significant interactions. The mixture consisted of two distributions: a normal distribution representing the null hypothesis of no interaction with a prior mean of 0, and a uniform distribution representing the alternative hypothesis. The posterior probability of a GI score belonging to the alternative hypothesis was calculated, and interactions were classified as likely interactions if the posterior probability was greater than 0.5, and as confident interactions if the posterior probability was greater than 0.95. We use a cut-off of 0.5 for likely interactions because this corresponds to the point at which GI scores are more likely to be explained by the uniform distribution than by the normal distribution.

To integrate the evidence from the two independent Mtb screens (experiment 1 and experiment 2) into a single, directly interpretable probability, a joint Bayesian mixture model was fitted to the paired GI scores. For each gene pair, the GI score in each experiment and its standard error (that is, mean GI score and the standard deviation of Δ′ from the Bayesian two-line model) were taken as the x,y coordinates and their measurement uncertainties.

The mixture consisted of five components defined over the range of GI scores: a null component and four interaction components, one for each quadrant. The null hypothesis of no interaction was represented by a bivariate normal distribution centred near the origin, with a correlation parameter, ρ, given a weakly informative prior centred at zero. Allowing ρ to be a free parameter allowed the model to learn any existing correlation observed in the null distribution, owing to each screen using the same library and protocols. Per-pair measurement error was incorporated into the null by adding each pair’s squared standard error to the variance on the corresponding axis and scaling ρ accordingly (see Stan model code for implementation details). This approach incorporates the uncertainty associated with each GI-score estimate, down-weighting imprecise measurements while integrating both the consistency and the precision of observed effects across the two screens.

The four interaction components corresponded to the concordant negative quadrant, the concordant positive quadrant and the two discordant quadrants in which the two experiments disagreed in sign (one positive and the other negative). Each quadrant was modelled as a product of per-axis uniform densities over the range of observed scores, which were winsorized at the 0.1st and 99.9th percentiles to reduce the influence of outliers. Because pairs near a quadrant edge could belong to a neighbouring quadrant owing to measurement uncertainty, the uniform densities were convolved with the per-pair measurement error at the origin. Thus, a pair lying near an axis is assigned partly to each quadrant, and the model identifies the most likely membership from the mixture. The mixture weights were given a Dirichlet(2, 2, 36) prior over the concordant, discordant and null components, placing an 18-fold higher weight on the null, reflecting the expectation that most gene pairs do not interact. Concordant and discordant weights were divided evenly between their two respective quadrants (two discordant quadrants and the concordant positive and concordant negative quadrants). The model was fitted using Stan (cmdstan v.2.35, cmdstanpy v.1.2.4). For each gene pair, the posterior probability of belonging to each component was computed and summarized as the probability of a negative, positive or discordant interaction, and of no interaction. As in the single-experiment analysis, a pair was classified as a likely interaction when the largest of its interaction-class probabilities exceeded 0.5 and as a confident interaction when it exceeded 0.95, and was labelled negative, positive or discordant according to the dominant class.

Distinct colours were assigned to the remaining key nodes and propagated through their descendant branches, producing functionally labelled dendrograms in which leaves inherited the most specific term in their lineage.

Genes were assigned to pathways on the basis of manual curation and AI-assisted literature review (Claude Sonnet 4.5). Gene pairs were categorized as having a significant GI (p > 0.5) or not, and Fisher’s exact test was used to assess the association between pathway pairs and significant GIs. Self-interactions, in which a gene is targeted twice (that is, A + A), were excluded from the analysis. The resulting P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. The analysis was performed twice: once using all gene pairs and once excluding potential polar effects (defined as pairs in which the genes were five genes or fewer apart), to assess how significance changed.

Sequences for selected genes were co-folded with AlphaFold-Multimer28 (v.3) to predict protein complex structures and AlphaFold34 to predict PPIs. All model inputs for Mtb used amino acid sequences from the H37Rv reference proteome (UniProt: UP000001584). All model inputs for Msm used amino acid sequences translated using BioPython from strain mc2155 (RefSeq: NC_008596.1), with the following exceptions. MSMEG5414 in the reference genome was split into proteins MSMEG5414A and MSMEG5414B. Four genes are misannotated in the reference genome as pseudogenes, and their translations were manually included: MSMEG4629:folC and MSMEG1828 from another genome (GenBank: CP009494); MSMEG1843:ahcY from UniProt (accession A4ZHR8); and MSMEG1400:fusA1 from a previously reported sequence88,89. All sequence inputs for Msm can be found in the file msm_essential_proteome.fa in our linked GitHub repository (see ‘Code availability’). AlphaFold-Multimer inputs were formatted as pairs of monomers28. These complexes were predicted using LocalColabFold90 v.1.5.5 (https://github.com/YoshitakaMo/localcolabfold)90 on an NVIDIA L40S GPU and 16 24-GB CPUs. The model was run with three recycles and AMBER relaxation. AlphaFold3 inputs were prepared following a modified pooled AlphaFold3 approach27. Within the bin-packing strategy, smaller protein sequences were prioritized to maximize the number of monomers per pool. In both cases, five predicted structures were generated per input. Predicted TM scores (pTMs) and interface predicted TM scores (ipTMs) for any given gene or gene pair, respectively, were taken as a mean across all models. ipTMs were corrected for combined protein length as previously described27. In Supplementary Tables 2 and 3, columns from pooled inputs on the web server have the suffix or infix ‘_webserver’.

Structures and scores were also generated locally for Mtb using the same pooled inputs as the AlphaFold3 web server. Per-chain multiple sequence alignments were generated with ColabFold v.1.5.590 using MMseqs2 18.8cc5c91 against the UniRef30 (2023-02) and ColabFold environmental clusters (2021-08; comprising filtered BFD, MGnify, MetaEuk and SMAG sub-databases), with GPU-accelerated prefiltering (--gpu 1 --prefilter-mode 1). The output was written directly in AlphaFold3-compatible JSON form (--af3-json), yielding one unpaired multiple sequence alignment per chain. To avoid recomputing identical alignments across the many overlapping pools in this study, sequences were keyed by SHA-256 hash and computed only once; an indexed central cache of all previously generated alignments (about 3,800 unique sequences) was consulted on every job, with cache hits delivering byte-identical multiple sequence alignments and cache misses triggering a fresh ColabFold run that subsequently updated the cache. Co-folding was performed with AlphaFold3 v.3.0.14 executed inside an Apptainer/Singularity container on NVIDIA H100 80GB HBM3 GPUs. For each pool, the per-chain multiple sequence alignments were combined into a single AlphaFold3 input JSON (dialect alphafold3, version 1), populating unpairedMsa from the ColabFold output; pairedMsa was left empty, deferring to AlphaFold3’s internal pairing logic. AlphaFold3 was invoked via run_alphafold.py with --flash_attention_implementation=triton and --resolve_msa_overlaps=false; all other parameters retained their AlphaFold3 defaults. A single random seed (modelSeeds = [1]) was used per input, producing five diffusion-sampled structures per pool from which downstream pTM and ipTM statistics were derived as described above. In Supplementary Table 3, the resulting columns have the suffix or infix ‘_local’.

For both the local and the web-server results, the total number of predicted PPIs for each single protein is listed with the prefix ‘degree’, following notation from graph theory. Each of these columns is suffixed with 1 or 2 to indicate the monomer to which it corresponds. These degrees are calculated cumulatively at three ipTM cut-offs (see Fig. 3f): for example, ‘degree_low_webserver1’ refers to all PPIs predicted for gene 1 with low or higher confidence using the web server.

Compounds were dispensed in a 384-well plate as described above for the MIC assays. For each pair of drugs, all combinations of the two-dilution series were plated, including dilution series in which each drug is present alone and a DMSO control in which neither drug is present. Each combination of concentrations was plated in triplicate. Cultures were grown and plated and the relative growth was measured as described for MIC assays, except that the cultures were seeded at OD600 nm = 0.025. Drug synergy was quantified using the synergyfinder v.3.14.0 R package93. For each checkerboard, mean percentage viability values were used to calculate the Bliss independence score94,95 at each combination of drug concentrations. The resulting scores were then scanned to find the most synergistic area (MSA) and most antagonistic area (MAA). These areas were defined as the 3 × 3 contiguous areas of the plate with the highest and lowest mean Bliss scores, respectively. The mean Bliss scores of the MSA and MAA are reported for each drug combination. Both metrics are reported to avoid bias in calling a drug pair synergistic or antagonistic. Following recommended guidelines96, drug pairs with an MSA mean Bliss score higher than 15 were considered synergistic, and drug pairs with an MAA mean Bliss score lower than –15 were considered antagonistic.

Because we did not have a full panel of concentration combinations, we opted to use the highest single agent (HSA) method of quantifying drug synergy97. In brief, the amount of killing (measured as the change in CFU per ml versus the no-drug control) with the combination of the two drugs is compared to the drug that shows the highest growth inhibition (the HSA) when used alone at the same concentration. The drug pair is considered synergistic if the combination of drugs results in more than 100-fold more killing than the HSA.

The SAINTexpress98,99 software package was used to score protein interactions. In brief, peptide-level counts are summed for all detected proteins for both the tagged and the untagged samples (two replicates per condition). These were then used to obtain the protein-level L2FC of counts in the tagged samples relative to the untagged samples. The SAINTexpress package uses a Bayesian model to calculate the probability of an interaction for each protein and uses these probabilities to estimate the FDR.

IMV acidification assays were performed as previously described51. In brief, the membrane fraction from wild-type, ∆qcrB and ∆ms2764 Msm strains were isolated and resuspended in IMV resuspension buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgSO4, 5 mM 6-aminocaproic acid, 5 mM benzamidine hydrochloride and 20% [v/v] glycerol). NADH was prepared immediately before use by dissolving disodium NADH in water to a final concentration of 4 mM. Succinate was prepared by dissolving sodium succinate in water to a final concentration of 20 mM. A master solution containing ACMA assay buffer (10 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl and 5 mM MgCl2), 9-amino-6-chloro-2-methoxyacridine (ACMA) dye (3.125 µM), IMVs (5 mg ml−1 total protein concentration for succinate-driven acidification; 2 mg ml−1 total protein concentration for NADH-driven acidification) and 10 µM of Q203 (with a final concentration of 2% [v/v] DMSO) or an equivalent volume of compound-free DMSO vehicle was prepared and transferred into each well of the 96-well plate. The plate was incubated in the dark for approximately 15 min at room temperature before the start of the experiment. ACMA fluorescence was measured at 25 °C with a BioTek Synergy Neo2 Hybrid Multimode plate reader (Agilent Technologies), using an excitation wavelength of 410 nm and an emission wavelength of 480 nm. IMV acidification was initiated by the addition of NADH or succinate. Fluorescence was monitored for 20 min before the addition of nigericin in ethanol to a final concentration of 2 µM with 0.02% (v/v) ethanol. Fluorescence recovery was monitored for an additional 10 min. Fluorescence recovery was calculated as the absolute difference between the fluorescence immediately before and 10 min after nigericin addition. All IMV acidification experiments were performed with at least three replicates.
