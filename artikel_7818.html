As a gas, ammonia, hydrogen and nitrogen chemical compound is pungent and dangerous. But in its diluted household form, the nitrogen-based super cleaner is a workhorse in the war on common household dirt and grime. Ammonia is readily available, inexpensive, and potent, so a little goes a long way.

Before you buy ammonia, know that there are various types of household ammonia to choose from. You may see “cloudy” or “sudsy” versions—that’s ammonia with soap added, which is generally used for such domestic cleaning chores as treating stained clothing or scrubbing stovetops. We recommend opting for “clear” ammonia, which is suited to a wider variety of tasks (and you can always add a tablespoon or two of dish soap to make clear ammonia sudsy). You’ll also find ammonia that’s scented with lemon or pine to temper its stinging odor.

The harsh smell serves as a warning, though: Working with ammonia absolutely requires caution. Pure chemical ammonia can cause severe burns and respiratory issues if it comes into contact with skin or is ingested. Even diluted in water, as is recommended for most cleaning purposes, ammonia can still be harmful.

Mixing ammonia and chlorine bleach creates a toxic gas called chloramine. Take great care not to combine them—doing so could have dire consequences.

The most important safety rule to remember is: Never mix ammonia with chlorine bleach. The result is a highly toxic chlorine gas that can produce headaches, seizures, and other symptoms. It’s crucial to keep this in mind if you’re using ammonia with laundry or to clean surfaces. You want to make absolutely sure not to mix it with a detergent or household cleaner that contains bleach.

When using ammonia, be sure to wear chemical-resistant gloves and ventilate the area well. Be sure, too, to store it where pets and children can’t access it. Bearing these safety guidelines in mind, put ammonia to work around the home, as generations have done before us.

To achieve streak-free mirrors, windows, and crystal, mix 1 tablespoon clear ammonia with 2 cups water in a spray bottle. Spritz the solution onto glass items and wipe immediately with a soft, lint-free cloth for windows and crystal; paper towels work fine for mirrors.

To remove stains on a concrete driveway, garage floor, or patio, mix 1 cup ammonia with 1 gallon of water in a bucket and apply it to the stain with a mop or large sponge. Let sit for about 20 minutes, and then scrub the stain with a bristled brush. Rinse the surface with clean water. If the stain isn’t completely gone after the area dries, repeat the process until you’re satisfied with the results.

If gold, silver, diamond, or platinum jewelry has lost its shine, mix 6 parts warm water to 1 part ammonia in a bowl (one that you don’t eat or cook with and add your jewelry. (Never use ammonia to clean pearls or other gems.) After 15 minutes, remove the jewelry with your gloved hand, and then gently scrape away any stubborn grime with a soft-bristled toothbrush (one that’s no longer in use or designated for cleaning). Allow it to soak for another 15 minutes, remove from the solution, and immediately rinse with warm water. Dispose of used ammonia solution down the drain while running the hot water. Polish the jewelry with a soft, lint-free cloth and let it dry.

Stubborn stains on cotton, polyester, or nylon fabrics are no match for a solution of ⅔ cup clear ammonia, ⅔ cup dish soap, 6 tablespoons of baking soda, and 2 cups warm water. Mix in a bowl or bucket and apply generously to stains with a sponge or spray bottle. Let the treatment sit for about 30 minutes, then launder as usual. For tougher stains like blood or grass, treat the stain again with a solution of 1 part ammonia and 1 part warm water.

Note: Do not use ammonia on wool or silk, which can eat through the delicate fabrics. And remember never to mix ammonia with any laundry products that contain bleach.

Tackle caked-on grease on stove burners with this trick. Place each burner in a separate sealable plastic bag filled with ¼ cup ammonia. Seal each bag to keep fumes at bay, and place in a sink or washtub for 8 to 12 hours. Open the bags in a ventilated area, and remove all grease and dirt easily with a quick scrub of dish soap and water. Once the gunk is gone, thoroughly rinse the burners with water to get rid of ammonia residue. Let dry completely before using.

Mix one part clear ammonia and one part hot water in a sprayer or other bottle. Apply generously to the stain by spritzing or with a sponge. After about 10 minutes of dwell time, place a clean old towel over the stain and press with an iron, blasting with steam, for about 20 seconds. Check your progress: The stain should start transferring to the towel. Repeat, with steam ironing and more ammonia solution if needed, until the stain is gone.

Ammonia is tough on every odor from fresh paint to burnt lasagna. Place a small container filled with about 1/2 cup of clear ammonia near an offending smell, being sure to keep the area ventilated, to absorb the nastiness within hours.

Heat the oven to 150 degrees, and set a large pot of water on the stove to boil. Turn off the oven and open the windows to ventilate the kitchen. Place a sturdy container, such as a glass measuring cup or metal bowl, filled with ½ cup ammonia on the oven’s top shelf. Place the pot of boiled water on the bottom shelf. Close the oven and let sit for 8 to 12 hours, then remove the containers of ammonia and water. Open the oven door and air air out for at least an hour before cleaning. Because the grime has basked in ammonia fumes, it should be loosened and easy to remove with a sponge, warm water, and dish soap.

This method is only recommended for electric ovens, because mixing ammonia with gas can be dangerous. If you choose to use this method on your gas oven, make sure that the pilot light and gas lines are turned off.

To clean and disinfect kitchen and bath tiles, mix ¼ cup ammonia with 1 gallon of water in a bucket and use a sponge or mop to liberally apply the solution to the tile. Use a soft-bristled brush to scrub any particularly stubborn areas. Finish up by rinsing the surface with clean water and your sponge or mop.

Ammonia’s strong scent can deter unwanted critters. Fill a saucer with clear, unscented ammonia, or saturate a rag or cotton balls with it. Then place in areas that attract pests, such as near garbage cans where raccoons prowl or beside cracks in the basement where mice enter. The smell will remind the varmints of predators, so they’re likely to catch a whiff and turn away, unharmed. That said, make sure to keep the ammonia deterrent out of reach of kids and pets.
