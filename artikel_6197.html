The Golden Rules of Affordable Family Travel, Illustrated by Mona Chalabi

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

My first Thanksgiving was a lavish 12-person dinner in Connecticut in 2014. I had moved to the US in March of that year and had planned on working from home until a friend generously invited me to go spend the day with her family; an act of unguarded openness that I’ve realized is one of the many things that differentiates Americans from Brits. As soon as we got off the train in Cambridge, Massachusetts, I realized that even though my friend lived in a nasty, overcrowded apartment in Brooklyn, she came from a family that had immense wealth. After dinner, as I helped wash plates in the kitchen, my friend’s mum asked her why she wouldn’t just move into the lovely building they had bought for her. She turned beet red and handed me another dirty plate without looking at me.

Take it from an immigrant: this holiday is full of weird contradictions. Of course, there’s the annual reminder of colonization which requires a careful forgetting of the people who have been colonized. And then there’s the financial aspect of it. At first blush, it seems like the least capitalist holiday of the year—there isn’t the expense of Christmas or Hanukkah gift-giving or the extravagance of Halloween candy. Yet the holiday also marks the start of one of the year's busiest travel seasons when families are taking great expense to reunite, vacation, and explore outside of their every day lives.

As we approach the last Thursday of November, with money very much on our minds, the below well-traveled parents share their hard-won wisdom on pulling off affordable family travel—including how to find treasure in New York City’s Chinatown without paying an arm and a leg, seek out free entertainment in the streets of Oaxaca, and embrace the joys of accessible public transportation in Europe. All of these stories say something about the wealth of the person writing it, whether or not they wanted to reveal that information. I’m obsessed with getting to the truth about someone’s class (it’s why I wrote my book on the subject), and so these family dispatches, which serve as equal parts advice and confession, are a treat I’ll come back to when I’m not busy washing a rich family’s plates. —Mona Chalabi, Pulitzer Prize-winning writer and illustrator

I know the secret to parenting and I’m willing to share it: snacks. Snacks are everything—they’re an activity, a mood changer, and a window into culture for kids. Hit a local farmers market on your first day or if you’re somewhere truly out of their comfort zone even a local convenience store—like 7-Eleven in Tokyo—for something that can feel both familiar and novel. If your hotel room has a refrigerator you can store any pre-cut fruit that you buy, or opt for items that don’t require refrigeration like apples, bananas, and clementines. Bring a lightweight backpack, refill each morning, and watch the hangry meltdowns (mostly) vanish. —Dana Kravis, global executive director, CNT video

Last December in London, we stayed in a quaint Airbnb in Primrose Hill and explored different neighborhoods on foot. When our kids, then eight and three, got tired, we hopped on a double-decker bus and headed upstairs, hoping for front seats. Suddenly, getting home became an amusement park ride: Christmas lights, bustling streets, panoramic views. The kids loved it; as a photographer, I loved the vantage point. London caps daily bus fares when you use the same card or phone, and children under 11 ride free. We’ve done this in Singapore, too. Our rule: skip the sightseeing tour (or Uber) and make public transportation part of the family adventure. —Tanveer Badal, photographer

Breakfast is where family vacations are made, or broken. My moody teenagers can’t be pried out of bed no matter what city they wake up in, so instead of wringing my uncaffeinated hands and hoping to get us all on the same schedule, I just bring my own breakfast, coffee included. Whether in Paris, Rome, or a tent, you will find me hand-grinding my coffee beans, brewing a pour-over, and snacking on a pastry left over from the previous day while I peruse headlines. Avoiding the time and expense of a sit-down breakfast, and indulging in a bit of morning self-care, means we can focus on the adventures and meals we are actually going to remember. —Chris Morocco, food director of Bon Appétit and Epicurious

My parents and I meet in Aruba every winter for a family vacation that’s warm and relaxing when everyone else is cold and miserable. It’s a classic only child/parents trip where we spend most of our time eating lunch talking about where we’re going to eat dinner. We spend our days mostly just reading parallel to each other. I’ve never been an e-reader user, so I have to bring several physical books to read to get me through the week. The key is that my mom and I have some shared taste in books, so when we finish our own we can swap and save ourselves buying extra books (or lugging even heavier luggage through the airport). —Alison Leiby, comedian and writer

Finding creative ways to entertain wiggly one-year-olds with limited attention spans can be daunting, but it turns out all you need is a good window. From London to California, staying in rooms on low floors with windows overlooking lots of activity below has resulted in seemingly endless engagement—waving at commuters striding by or golfers strolling a few feet away? Bonus points if the room has curtains with electronic controls—that guarantees an extra hour of ooohs and aaahs, at least. —Sarah Khan, contributing editor

I have a thing for seeing “The World’s Biggest or Oldest,” and so here we were at Missouri’s Oldest Wooden Suspension Bridge. “Don’t tell your mom,” I said to my kids, because any bridge that was built from wood during the Civil War should be treated with caution—and, also, all good dad stories start that way. We started up the van, piled everyone in, and began the adventure. The wood cracked and echoed as we drove over the river. It sounded like a popping spinal cord. We swayed, and for a moment, I actually questioned myself. I should have just taken them to the park. Thankfully, I can now confidently say that Missouri’s oldest wooden suspension bridge can hold the weight of a mini-van. —Shannon Carpenter, humorist

New York is an expensive city and the best way to keep it cheap is to throw petty cash at it. Instead of buying tickets to an overpriced kid’s experience, I give my children a few twenty dollar bills and take them to Chinatown for a shopping spree. They love the atrocious arcade, and there are fake Labubus, squishy dumplings, a plastic “jade” green dragon that my son now keeps on his bedside, and a 'gold' necklace my daughter thinks is treasure. —Stephanie Danler, author

As a seasoned travel writer, I know that any hotel that’s gotten media coverage is often pushing $1,000/night. My trick for affordable family lodging is loading up Google maps, figuring out which area we’re going to stay in, and picking a cheerful place with good reviews in the $250 range. It’s almost never one you’ve heard of, but they’re always clean and cheerful, usually family-owned, and we always leave with big hugs and promises to keep in touch. That doesn’t happen at the Georges V. My kids (12 and 8) loved a converted windmill in Rome, a townhouse two blocks from the Rijksmuseum and—most recently—an orange grove near Siracusa where they spent so much time chasing the owner’s cats they even forgot Wi-Fi existed. A steal at any price! —Priyanka Mattoo, filmmaker and author

People tell you about a million things you “need” before your baby is born, but there's one that has come in the most handy, and I've had a true love/hate relationship with it since day one: A travel car seat. After a ton of research—some may say over-research—we bought a Maxi Cosi Romi. It's rated high for safety, but it's also easy to strap into a car and also an airplane. We always bring it on the airplane, and that's where the headache comes in. While the Maxi Cosi Romi is considered lightweight, it's also big and bulky, and the only way to get it through the airport and onto the plane is by wearing it in a special backpack. I'm pretty sure that I'm one of the most annoying people in any airport as I'm walking through it since I can't really see if I'm bumping into people in the TSA line or getting to the gate. The thing is that, after I considered the numbers, it's actually a really wise investment because most rental car places charge anywhere from $10 to $15 a day to rent a car seat, so we've already started saving money because of it. Now, that's about a hundred or more dollars we're not spending when we go out of town and bring the Maxi. It's wildly annoying and inconvenient, and I look like I'm wearing a jetpack as I'm walking through the airport, but it's worth it. —Jason Diamond, 'Figuring' family columnist at The Cut

I️ live in Brooklyn where people leave really good stuff out on the street. Once I️ found one of those giant extra storage containers you put on top of your car. I️ haven't found the time to figure out how to put it on top of the car; it's been taking up valuable space in my home for over a year. The other day, I️ found another one that looked even fancier. I️ dragged it home. One day, ideally before our car gets too old to work anymore, I️ will figure this out, then get rid of the extra one somehow (do I️ need to call 311?). After that, my husband, our two kids, our dog, our parakeets and I️ will pack up everything we could possibly need to rent a house somewhere for a week or two. I️ have my eye on Block Island or Martha's Vineyard. —Liana Finck, cartoonist and author

From bustling markets to neighborhood festivals, I've learned that some of the best family entertainment happens in the streets. In Oaxaca, my four-year-old was mesmerized by calendas, raucous community processions with brass bands, towering puppets called monos de calenda, and rag-covered revelers known as tiliches. Paradegoers tossed my kid candy, fireworks exploded overhead, and the party raged late into the night. We didn't pay a peso. Before booking pricey family activities, research local festivals, celebrations, and public performances. Sometimes the most exciting show in town is happening right on the sidewalk. —Ashlea Halpern, contributing editor

On our last family vacation to Rajasthan, a passionfruit lassi from our five-star hotel nearly ruined the trip. We’re talking emergency roadside bathroom stops and several days of Zofran. We’ve been taking our kids—Vijay, Vik, and Meera—to Rajasthan since they were babies; they’re teenagers now. And we’ve learned one unexpected lesson: the sickest we’ve ever been in India was after eating at our five-star hotel. More than once.

So we’ve adopted a new philosophy: pack the right medicine and embrace street food. In Jaipur, Vineet Sharma, founder of Jaipur Walks, led us on a street food tour around the bustling market, introducing us to piping-hot pyaaz kachori and creamy, saffron-rich kulfi. His advice? Choose clean stalls with high turnover, and favor food cooked fresh and served hot over raw offerings. At busy stalls, food is often prepared continuously and served straight from the heat—sometimes a more reassuring bet than a meal at a luxury hotel or high-end restaurant. And if your stomach’s going to revolt, at least make the meal memorable. —Christine Chitnis, author and photographer

This may not be the most popular piece of advice, and I doubt it'll be the most obvious: take your kids overseas during school time. Hear me out: the longer you can stretch your child's travels beyond the limited school holidays when ticket prices always soar, the better for your wallet. Of course, every parent needs to set their own boundary on what and how much school their kind can miss in the name of travel but I feel, especially at a young age, the benefits of the trip outweigh the additional days in the classroom. As I am writing this, I am preparing to leave tomorrow with my first grader for Italy for a week. We are cooking up plans to take our two kids on a long haul trip to the southern hemisphere that'll cut into a week of school. I have heard from more than one expert that Disney especially, should be done when your kids are young, and outside normal school holidays if you want to get great value. I don't do it often but I do balance the guilt with rules—we have to read every day, and do exercises from a homework packet to keep on top of the basics. One day, and soon, I know I won't want to do this much more. But for now, I'm taking every chance to do so. —Erin Florio, global features director
