8 underwater sculpture parks and museums worth the dive

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 8 underwater sculpture parks and museums, from Grenada and Mexico to Italy, Cyprus and Australia, put statues and art on the seafloor for visitors to find

The sculptures in this selection were made to be seen through seawater rather than across a gallery floor. Light, weather and tide change how each piece looks from one day to the next. Some of the works serve as memorials. Others take up themes such as migration and the bond between people and the sea. At least one was installed partly to steer divers away from a natural reef.

That aim belongs to the Museo Subacuático de Arte, or MUSA, off Cancún and Isla Mujeres in Mexico. Its first sculptures went into the water in 2009. One stated goal of the project was to draw divers away from natural reefs and toward the sculptures. The artwork, in other words, doubles as an alternative dive site.

The oldest entry here is far older than MUSA. In 1954, a bronze statue by Guido Galletti known as Christ of the Abyss was lowered into the Bay of San Fruttuoso in Liguria, Italy. A replica followed in 1965 on the Key Largo Dry Rocks reef off Florida. Molinere Underwater Sculpture Park, created in Grenada in 2006, is widely described as the first park of its kind. The newest site in this selection, the Museum of Underwater Sculpture Ayia Napa in Cyprus, opened in 2021.

The sculptor Jason deCaires Taylor created or co-created most of the sites covered here. His work ranges from large groups of life-size human figures to a single sculpture of a girl holding up the surface of the sea. Off Lanzarote in Spain's Canary Islands, one of his pieces refers to migrant sea crossings. In Australia, the Coral Greenhouse sits at John Brewer Reef on the Great Barrier Reef.

The eight entries span the Caribbean, the Bahamas, the Florida Keys, the Canary Islands, the Mediterranean and Australia. They form a selection, not a ranking or a complete list. A common question is whether seeing underwater statues requires scuba diving. The answer depends on depth and local conditions. Shallow pieces can sometimes be seen by snorkelers. Deeper ones generally call for scuba gear and training. Anyone planning a trip around underwater sculpture parks should confirm each site's requirements with local operators first.

Museo Subacuático de Arte, known as MUSA, put its first sculptures into the sea off Cancún and Isla Mujeres in 2009. One stated aim of the project was to draw divers away from the area's natural reefs.

The museum lies in Mexico's Caribbean waters. Its works are split between two areas: the sea off the resort city of Cancún and the sea off Isla Mujeres, a nearby island.

MUSA was not the project of a single person. Jason deCaires Taylor created it with Jaime González Cano and Roberto Díaz Abraham.

One of Taylor's works at the museum is The Silent Evolution. It is a large group of life-size human figures placed together on the seafloor. Seen from above, the figures form a crowd standing beneath the surface.

The sculptures are made from pH-neutral marine cement. That material lets coral grow on the work. As coral settles, the figures become surfaces for reef growth as well as works of art.

That design ties the art to the project's reef-protection aim. The sculptures give divers a separate place to go in the same waters as the natural reefs.

Jason deCaires Taylor created Molinere Underwater Sculpture Park in Molinere Bay, Grenada, in 2006. It is widely described as the first underwater sculpture park.

The Grenada park predates Taylor's project in Mexico. His first sculptures for Museo Subacuático de Arte, off Cancún and Isla Mujeres, went into the sea in 2009. That puts Molinere three years ahead.

The best-known work at Molinere is Vicissitudes. It shows a group of children holding hands in a ring. The figures form one connected circle on the seabed.

The sculptures at Molinere sit in shallow water. That separates the Grenada park from Museo Atlántico in Lanzarote. Taylor's figures there rest at about 14 meters below the surface.

Molinere also differs in form from Christ of the Abyss in Italy. That earlier work is a single bronze memorial by Guido Galletti. Molinere is a park made up of several sculptures.

The Grenada site came first in a run of seafloor projects by the same artist. Taylor made Ocean Atlas off Nassau, Bahamas, in 2014. Museo Atlántico followed in 2016. The Museum of Underwater Sculpture Ayia Napa in Cyprus opened in 2021.

Within this selection, Molinere is the earliest of Taylor's sites.

Museo Atlántico is an underwater museum off Playa Blanca, on the Spanish island of Lanzarote. It opened in 2016, and its sculptures rest on the seabed at a depth of about 14 meters, or roughly 46 feet.

The museum is a project by the sculptor Jason deCaires Taylor. Its works are human figures arranged in groups and placed in open water rather than in a gallery or tank.

Lanzarote belongs to the Canary Islands, a Spanish archipelago in the Atlantic Ocean off the northwest coast of Africa. Playa Blanca is a resort town on the island's southern coast. The museum's name refers to that Atlantic setting.

Rubicon is one of the site's named works. It shows a line of figures walking toward a wall. The title echoes the expression "crossing the Rubicon," which means passing a point of no return. The phrase comes from the river in northern Italy that Julius Caesar crossed with his army in 49 BC. That crossing set off a civil war in the Roman Republic.

The Raft of Lampedusa is a second named work. It depicts a boat carrying a group of figures. The piece refers to migrant sea crossings.

Its title plays on The Raft of the Medusa, a painting by the French artist Théodore Géricault. That work shows survivors of the 1816 wreck of the French navy frigate Méduse off the coast of West Africa. It hangs in the Louvre in Paris.

Lampedusa is a small Italian island in the Mediterranean, lying between Tunisia and Sicily. It has long served as a landing point for boats carrying migrants from North Africa toward Europe.

The Canary Islands are also an arrival point for migrant boats. Those vessels cross the Atlantic from the coast of West Africa, a route separate from the Mediterranean one that leads to Lampedusa.

Both named works take crossings as their subject. Rubicon draws on a crossing that could not be undone, and The Raft of Lampedusa draws on journeys made by sea.

Christ of the Abyss, known in Italian as Cristo degli Abissi, is a bronze statue by the sculptor Guido Galletti. It was lowered into the Bay of San Fruttuoso, on the Ligurian coast of northwest Italy, in 1954. It rests at a depth of about 17 meters.

The statue is a memorial. It honors Dario Gonnella, an Italian diver who died while diving. It was made to remember one person. It was not built to create habitat or to steer visitors away from natural reefs.

San Fruttuoso Bay lies on the Portofino promontory, between the towns of Camogli and Portofino. The cove is also home to the medieval Abbey of San Fruttuoso, which stands near the shoreline.

The statue depicts Christ with his arms raised toward the surface. Galletti cast the work in bronze, a metal long used for public monuments on land. Most of the underwater sculpture parks that followed decades later use cement figures arranged in groups. Christ of the Abyss stands alone, with no surrounding installation on the seabed.

Among the eight sites in this selection, it is the earliest installation. Molinere Underwater Sculpture Park in Grenada, widely described as the first underwater sculpture park, followed in 2006.

A replica of Italy's Christ of the Abyss statue stands in about 25 feet of water off Key Largo, Florida. It was installed in 1965 on the Key Largo Dry Rocks reef, inside John Pennekamp Coral Reef State Park.

The Florida figure is a copy of Cristo degli Abissi, a bronze statue by the Italian sculptor Guido Galletti. The original stands in the Bay of San Fruttuoso, on the Ligurian coast of Italy. It memorializes Dario Gonnella, an Italian diver who died while diving.

Egidi Cressi, an Italian diving-equipment maker, donated the Key Largo replica. The gift brought a copy of an Italian underwater memorial to a reef in the Florida Keys.

The two figures share a design but not a setting. The original was lowered into a bay. The replica stands on a coral reef, with Key Largo Dry Rocks as its base.

That reef lies within a state park in Key Largo. John Pennekamp Coral Reef State Park takes in the stretch of reef where the replica was installed.

The Museum of Underwater Art's Coral Greenhouse opened in 2020 at John Brewer Reef on the Great Barrier Reef. The museum's work also includes Ocean Siren, which stands above the water on The Strand in Townsville.

The museum takes its name from Townsville, a city on the coast of Queensland. Its pieces are not confined to a single site. Some are placed out on the reef, and at least one stands in the city itself.

The Coral Greenhouse is the museum's submerged work. It sits at John Brewer Reef, which is part of the Great Barrier Reef off the Queensland coast. The installation puts sculpture directly onto a reef system.

Ocean Siren takes a different form. It is not submerged. The figure rises above the waterline on The Strand, the seafront in Townsville.

The sculpture changes color. Those changes follow ocean-temperature data. Its appearance on a given day reflects readings taken from the sea.

That design links the shore piece to the reef. The Coral Greenhouse sits in the water itself. Ocean Siren displays information about that water to people on land.

The arrangement also means part of the museum can be seen without entering the sea. Anyone on The Strand can view Ocean Siren from the shore. Seeing the Coral Greenhouse requires a trip out to John Brewer Reef.

The Museum of Underwater Sculpture Ayia Napa, known as MUSAN, opened in 2021 in the sea off Ayia Napa in Cyprus. It is a project by the sculptor Jason deCaires Taylor.

MUSAN is the most recent opening among the eight underwater sculpture parks and museums in this selection.

The site takes Taylor's work to the eastern Mediterranean. His other installations in this group sit off Grenada, Mexico and the Bahamas. Others are off Lanzarote in Spain's Canary Islands and on the Great Barrier Reef off Townsville, Australia.

Like the Museo Subacuático de Arte off Cancún and Isla Mujeres, the Cyprus site carries the name of a museum. It does not present itself as a single statue or a sculpture garden.

MUSAN is one of two entries in this selection that sit in the Mediterranean. The other is Christ of the Abyss, a bronze statue by Guido Galletti in the Bay of San Fruttuoso off Liguria, Italy.

Ocean Atlas is a single large sculpture off Nassau, Bahamas, made by Jason deCaires Taylor in 2014. It shows a girl holding up the surface of the sea above her.

The figure stands about five meters tall. It weighs about 60 tonnes. That scale is far beyond the size of any real child. The work reads more as a monument than a portrait.

Ocean Atlas is one work, built around one figure in one pose. Many underwater sculpture sites spread a large number of figures across a stretch of seabed. This piece rests on a single image instead. With no crowd around it, the gesture of the one figure carries the whole meaning of the work.

The title comes from Greek mythology. In the myth, Atlas is a Titan who fought on the losing side of the war between the Titans and the Olympian gods. As punishment, Zeus condemns him to hold up the sky. Ancient artists often showed him bent beneath a sphere. One example is the Farnese Atlas, a Roman marble statue now in Naples.

Taylor's version swaps the sky for the ocean. A girl takes the place of the Titan. The surface of the water becomes the weight she carries.

Those substitutions change the story the image tells. In the myth, the burden is a sentence handed down by a god to a defeated warrior. In the sculpture, it falls on a child, and the load is the sea itself. The act of lifting remains, but the bearer and the burden are both new.
