The stoppage stranded thousands of passengers at airports across the country. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said old equipment was overloaded, causing “instability” in radar communications.

The nationwide ground stop that brought all air travel to a halt across the country and stranded thousands of passengers has been canceled, according to the Federal Aviation Administration, though delays from the outage are expected to persist in the coming days.

The order on Friday halted nearly all departures and stranded thousands of passengers at airports across the country. Hundreds of flights were diverted to other airports and more were canceled, even as some internationally bound flights managed to secure permission to take off. Many passengers were taken off flights or told to go home. Chaos spread at airports as travelers sought answers from airline and airport employees.

Sean Duffy, the transportation secretary, warned that it would take “some time” for air travel nationwide to recover from the ground stop. Speaking at a news conference on Friday night shortly after flight traffic had been restored, Mr. Duffy predicted that flight schedules would be fully back to normal by the time the Thanksgiving travel season begins.

“I believe that it’s going to take us some time to get back to capacity,” he said, adding: “We’re going to have some makeup to do.”

Mr. Duffy decried Congress, accusing lawmakers of dragging their feet when it came to fully funding upgrades he warned were vital to prevent similar failures from happening again.

“It’s going to continue to fail,” Mr. Duffy told reporters, adding: “How many times does this have to happen for Congress to give the money to fix it?”

Mr. Duffy said that the antiquated radar and telecoms systems that controllers rely on to transmit data and other information between facilities began to falter at 3:36 p.m. The aging system, which has undergone a number of upgrades, became overloaded, officials said.

Appearing alongside him, Markwayne Mullin, the secretary of homeland security, stressed that there was never any indication that the communications failures had been triggered by a cyberattack, but he added, “we are still looking, and we’re not going to rule it out.” He emphasized that lives were never in danger.

But the incident appeared to inject new fuel into Mr. Duffy’s now monthslong campaign to secure at least $30 billion in extra funding for air traffic control modernization.

Last year, Congress approved $12.5 billion to upgrade aging air traffic facilities and technology, funds Mr. Duffy has repeatedly referred to as a “down payment” when asking for tens of billions more. On Friday night, Mr. Duffy appeared to up the ante, telling reporters that he didn’t just want another $30 billion for the project, but another $50 billion. He argued that it should be delivered in emergency funding before the end of the year, after which control of Congress — and the budget — switches from Republicans to Democrats.

Some Democratic lawmakers have already raised questions about how efficiently and effectively the F.A.A. has spent the money they have been given thus far. But Mr. Duffy has long argued that his department cannot be expected to complete a meaningful overhaul of the nation’s air traffic control system without a massive influx of funding.

Friday’s nationwide ground stop was only the third that the F.A.A. has issued this century. It ranks as the second-longest, after the ground stop issued on Sept. 11, 2001, in the wake of the terrorist attacks on the World Trade Center in New York. There was a shorter one on Jan. 11, 2023, because of an outage in the F.A.A.’s system to alert pilots to safety issues.

More than 2 million people pass through airport security checkpoints on a typical day, according to Transportation Security Administration data. On Thursday, the agency screened nearly 2.5 million people. According to FlightRadar24, an aviation tracking site, there were more than 1,000 airborne aircraft bound for U.S. airports during the nationwide ground stop. According to the flight tracking app Flighty, there were more than 30,000 departures and arrivals scheduled for the remainder of the day.

It could take days for air travel to fully normalize after an event of this scale, said Vikrant Vaze, a Dartmouth professor specializing in commercial aviation logistics.

“It’s going to take a lot of gymnastics” to get every airline’s aircraft, passengers and crew members back in the correct positions, he said.

The Federal Aviation Administration issued a rare nationwide ground stop Friday afternoon, after outdated air traffic communications equipment became overloaded with data and began to fail, officials said.

The outage stranded thousands of passengers and caused widespread delays from which airlines and airports are still struggling to recover. It also highlighted persistent vulnerabilities in the aging U.S. air traffic infrastructure and a funding fight, as administration officials demanded lawmakers approve tens of billions of dollars more to accelerate systemwide upgrades.

Sean Duffy, the transportation secretary, told reporters at the White House Friday night that the radar and other air traffic communications started to falter at 3:36 p.m. when old routers became overloaded with data. While the failures were not universal, they were pervasive enough to prompt officials to issue the unusual nationwide moratorium on takeoffs, Mr. Duffy said.

Appearing alongside him at the White House, Markwayne Mullin, the homeland security secretary, stressed that there was no evidence that nefarious activity had triggered the technology failures.

“We’ve seen zero evidence that this was a cyberattack,” Mr. Mullin said, suggesting that new equipment and new technology the Mr. Duffy’s department had added to the old infrastructure was what had overwhelmed it.

Mr. Duffy insisted that the failure was caused by the antiquated, legacy communications equipment, not recent upgrades to the system.

“What we have is an incredibly old air traffic control system,” Mr. Duffy said, predicting that until significant upgrades are made, “it’s going to continue to fail.”

Mr. Duffy warned that it would take “some time” for air travel nationwide to recover from the ground stop, predicting flight schedules would be fully back to normal by the time the Thanksgiving travel season begins.

“I believe that it’s going to take us some time to get back to capacity,” he said, adding: “We’re going to have some makeup to do.”

Mr. Duffy said that the F.A.A. would prioritize larger commercial flights ahead of private and charter flights, to get previously grounded flights off the ground.

Mr. Duffy has long been pushing for a sweeping overhaul of the U.S. air traffic infrastructure, routinely complaining that controllers have been hampered by the country’s failure to upgrade outdated equipment.

“We have old, old switches, old routers — we use 1980s little copper lines,” he said to reporters Friday. Only last week, the F.A.A. announced that it had begun to phase out the use of floppy disks.

Last year, Congress approved $12.5 billion to fund upgrades to the country’s aging air traffic facilities and networks, some of which has been directed toward communications system upgrades. Mr. Duffy noted Friday that air traffic facilities that had been upgraded to digital systems did not experience the same overloading issues that prompted the F.A.A. to issue the ground stop order.

But he stressed that having an air traffic control system dependent on a “patchwork” of upgrades and fixes was untenable, calling on Congress to spend tens of billions more to allow for more upgrades.

For months, Mr. Duffy has been calling on the Republican-controlled Congress to give his department another $30 billion to accelerate efforts to upgrade air traffic control systems. On Friday night, he upped the ante, telling reporters he wanted $50 billion — and that he wanted it before the end of the year.

“This is an emergency, I want Congress to give us emergency funding,” he said, adding: “How many more times does this have to happen for Congress to give the money to fix it?”

But it is unclear whether Republicans will prioritize such a sum before the end of the year, after which control of Congress shifts to the Democrats, who are expected to scrutinize how the F.A.A. has utilized air traffic funding they have received so far. Republican lawmakers have filed bills in the House and Senate to authorize $30 billion in air traffic upgrades, but neither piece of legislation has cosponsors.

Later on Friday, Representative Hakeem Jeffries of New York, the House’s top-ranking Democrat, appeared to reject Mr. Duffy’s accusing tone and shifted the blame back to the Trump administration.

“Today’s unprecedented breakdown is the latest in a series of unacceptable air travel incidents under the Trump administration’s failed leadership,” he said in a statement, adding that “American travelers deserve timely answers from the Trump administration about how and why this happened and assurances that steps are being taken to ensure that our air travel system remains safe and functional.”

Nationwide, more than 8,200 flights — or over 31 percent of those scheduled in the United States for Friday — were canceled, according to Cirium, an aviation data firm. And the disruption was already spilling over into Saturday, usually a slower day for travel, with more than 5 percent of flights canceled before the day had even begun.

A communications outage on Friday led the Federal Aviation Administration to pause departing flights nationwide, but pilots were not entirely cut off from federal air traffic controllers.

In a Friday night news conference, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the outage was intermittent, so federal controllers were still able to reach pilots.

“It wasn’t a complete blackout in communication or radar,” he said. “So we were able to keep some of those communications going.”

But even in a complete outage, aviation authorities have multiple backups to maintain contact with pilots.

Pilots and air traffic controllers primarily communicate by voice radio, using primary, backup and emergency frequencies.

Controllers and airline dispatchers can also send text-based messages to pilots. And many commercial planes are equipped with satellite communications, which are especially useful for reaching people on the ground when the planes are flying in remote airspace like over the ocean.

If a control tower loses radio capabilities, it can also communicate with pilots with high-intensity colored lights, which pilots can acknowledge by rocking their wings or flashing lights in response.
