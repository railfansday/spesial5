Nature on the Board: Should Brands Opt In?

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

This story is part of ‘The Business Case for Nature’, a series exploring fashion’s impact on nature and vice versa. Read more here.

For 50 years, natural beauty company Faith In Nature has been guided by the slogan ‘nature is the boss’. It was plastered on the office walls and repeated ad nauseam by staff, but had no legal footing. Then, in 2021, creative directors Simeon Rose and Anne Hopkins put the provocation to a group of groundbreaking legal experts: how could they turn the slogan into something more?

Enter Nature on the Board (NOTB), a growing global movement where companies appoint a proxy for nature to their board of directors, recognizing and advocating for the rights of nature in decision-making processes, and flipping traditional corporate governance on its head. Some companies appoint a nature director — a human proxy for nature, who holds a formal board seat, written into the company’s articles of association, with the same financial and legal duties as other board members. Others appoint nature guardians, who perform a similar role in advocating for nature at the board level, but they don’t necessarily have voting rights.

A new programme by Kering and the Centre for Sustainable Fashion hopes to reimagine how companies make high-level decisions, but is the industry ready to embrace change?

NOTB was proposed by Faith In Nature, and fleshed out by legal non-profits Lawyers for Nature and the Earth Law Center (ELC), but it was inspired by Indigenous communities who have practiced nature-centric governance for millennia. “The current business system is operating exactly as it was designed to: businesses pool capital, build goods and services at scale, speed and low cost, and accrue benefits for shareholders,” explains Mary Johnstone-Louis, senior fellow in management at the University of Oxford’s Saïd Business School. “Putting nature on the board is radical because it introduces a stakeholder on which every business depends, but whose interests are not typically represented at the board level.”

While there was no precedent for putting nature on the board, there were other case studies to draw on, says Lawyers for Nature co-founder and managing director Brontie Ansell. “Until relatively recently, women were seen as objects of law, rather than subjects of law. Like nature, they were not allowed to hold property, vote or voice concerns. Now, we look back on that with horror,” she explains. “Likewise, we are very familiar with widening the stakeholder pool in boardrooms. Many now include HR managers, union representatives, and health and safety officers. Extending this to nature is critical to human survival.”

Alongside Faith In Nature, NOTB has since been adopted by dozens of companies, including British interiors brand House of Hackney, Norwegian apparel brand Northern Playground, and French IT services company Norsys. Each has put their own spin on the blueprint, contributing to a fast-evolving consensus on best practices, and throwing open the doors for further innovation. Vogue Business invited these early adopters to share the tensions, toggles and opportunities.

Businesses are just as diverse and idiosyncratic as nature, says Faith In Nature managing director Hannah Whittaker. When it comes to putting nature on the board, each business has to do it in its own way.

NOTB encourages companies to give nature voting rights, but it’s possible to test nature governance without this. Outdoor apparel brand Patagonia made waves in 2022 when it created a nature purpose trust model, making a collection of environmental non-profits the beneficiary of 98% of its non-voting stock, while retaining approval rights in the Patagonia Purpose Trust, controlled by the family of founder Yvon Chouinard.

Approaches may also differ by jurisdiction, notes ELC’s nature governance business development associate Anna Solgaard, who acts as the nature guardian for Norwegian clothing label Northern Playground. Finding ways to give nature more legal rights requires “creative lawyering”, adds Alexandra Pimor, director of the ELC’s Nature Governance Agency. “That’s the beauty of nature on the board: we did not have to change the law to do it, we just changed our relationship with the law to give it a different purpose [taking existing rights for board members and extending them to nature]. In a capitalist environment, the corporate world is probably the easiest place to embed the voice of nature, because companies are given so much freedom.”

At Norsys, nature is both a director and a shareholder with a 10% stake in the business. “This is a strong model because it means nature will always be represented on the board, it’s not just one nomination we forget about in three years,” says the company’s nature director Frantz Gault, author of Nature at Work and co-founder of Vivøices, a public interest organization promoting the representation of the living world in organizational governance. Gault helped design the nature governance at Norsys, complementing his own role with a council of advisors, who also decide how dividends from nature’s 10% stake are spent.

Lawyers for Nature, on the other hand, prefers for nature directors to remain as “conflict-free” as possible. In the frameworks it has designed, nature representatives are not heavily salaried by the company, they don’t have a bonus linked to profit, they don’t owe the company money or vice versa, and they don’t have — or at least, they commit to disclosing — any other conflicts of interest. “By minimizing those conflicts, the nature representative can speak in the purest form possible about what nature needs,” says Ansell.

At Northern Playground, the articles of association include a “safety valve” whereby the nature proxy can be relieved of their duties if the company is approaching bankruptcy, to reduce the risk of them being held personally liable.

Another contention point is whether the nature proxy should have the right to veto strategic decisions, as well as voting on them. “If you get to the point where you need a veto, you’ve already lost,” says Dr Juliet Rose, head of development at the British botanical garden and research hub The Eden Project, and nature director at Faith In Nature. “You’re trying to get everybody moving in the same direction, so nobody even suggests something that would require a veto.”

At Norsys, Gault has the power to veto, but has never activated it. To keep the “nuclear power” in check, he is required to confer with a council of 10 scientific experts before taking a position in board meetings. Still, knowing the veto exists can be persuasive enough. “Everyone knows I have the power to veto, so the other directors listen when I speak, and they work with me to find a consensus.” If a veto isn’t on the cards, companies can implement the right to disagree, and the right to express that disagreement in public, adds Gault, or the right to resign. “These are little legal technicalities, but they help make nature’s voice more meaningful.”

Whichever approach a company chooses, Vivøices recommends running a year-long pilot before codifying nature’s role or communicating the program to consumers. “You do a pilot, then map the impact and what happened, how it changed the way decisions were made, how it influenced the culture internally, and what it means to really integrate the position of nature,” says co-founder Niels de Fraguier.

When House of Hackney advertised a directorship “for mother nature and future generations” in 2023, it received over 500 applications — its most popular job listing to date. But finding the right person to represent something as nebulous as nature was no small feat. “We had applications from scientists, teachers, poets and everything in between,” says House of Hackney co-founder Frieda Gormley, author of In the Company of Nature.

The challenge is finding someone who can bridge economic and ecological realities, says the University of Oxford’s Johnstone-Louis. “They have to protect the company, and at the same time, they have to speak for nature. That means they have to toggle between immediacy and long-termism; between business as usual and what business could be,” she says. Regardless of their professional background, humility and diplomacy are the key competencies, adds de Fraguier.

Both Faith In Nature and House of Hackney appointed Lawyers for Nature’s Ansell as their inaugural nature director, establishing the legal basis for the role and ironing out any kinks. Her successors have wildly different skill sets: Rose has a background in plant science and ecology, while House of Hackney’s nature director Charmian Love specializes in governance, having co-founded B Lab UK, the organization behind the B Corp certification. Love says her goal is to create tools that make it easier for more diverse nature proxies to step into the role in future, while Rose says her success is defined by how well she empowers staff throughout the company, so whoever holds the nature directorship is not working alone, or resting solely on their own, inevitably limited, experience.

Ideally, nature-centric decision-making cascades down through the company, and the nature director works in tandem with a well-established nature lead. While the former stewards the long-term purpose of the business, the latter embeds it in day-to-day operations. Without a nature director in their corner, a nature lead can easily fall into the same trap as sustainability leads, tasked with overhauling the business but lacking the resource or buy-in to do so, instead operating in a silo and trying to shoehorn change into business as usual. And without a nature lead to operationalize nature-centric decision-making, a nature director risks becoming little more than “window dressing”, says Johnstone-Louis.

To avoid tokenizing nature, Faith In Nature has designed a nature dashboard, which translates its goals into tangible projects each team can track progress against. Projects fall into five governing principles: governance, operational impact, sourcing, community and regeneration. “We had to work through the confusion and connect each team with the outcomes we wanted for nature, each of which has budgets, resources and KPIs attached,” explains Whittaker. “Otherwise, the concept can be quite theoretical for staff.” These projects span from changing the adhesive on labels to make at-home recycling easier, to running the brand’s first major television ad in collaboration with Channel 4 and B Corp.

At House of Hackney, Love starts every board meeting with a nature degradation pulse-check and a poem about nature, based on climate policy leader Nigel Topping’s remarks that “we need to be guided by science and moved by poetry”. Beyond board meetings, Love has defined 10 topics that are material to nature, from supply chain and procurement to regulatory changes and financial investments. Decisions about these topics are automatically escalated to a monthly nature meeting. She has also introduced a spin on the traditional ‘going concern test’ boards use to assess how nature-related decisions will affect the company’s future viability and license to operate.

On top of this, Love has a private email address where staff can discreetly voice concerns or ideas, and the company regularly invites inspirational nature-related speakers into the office. The next phase is to trial a physical card game, she says, which will live in every meeting room, encouraging staff to question their daily decision-making through a nature-centric lens, inspired by similar games at tea brand Pukka Herbs. And at the end of the year, House of Hackney will publish an open-source toolkit for NOTB, informed by its Greenprint fellowship, where 10 SMEs — including Lush, Davines and Vivobarefoot — experimented with nature governance over 12 months.

NOTB is not just another sustainability box for companies to tick; it’s a paradigm shift that goes beyond sustainability, toward regeneration.

“Philosophically, these are two very different landscapes,” explains Solgaard. “Sustainability is an anthropocentric paradigm, but nature is ecocentric, which means that the more-than-human world is valued alongside humans. Sustainability works with separation and reductionism, which is why we have all these boxes to tick, and we struggle to view things holistically, or to see how everything is interconnected. Nature is everything.”

Putting nature on the board also means moving beyond the risk mitigation approach that has defined so many corporate nature strategies, and underscored the business case for nature, to date, adds Johnstone-Louis. The risk mitigation approach asks how companies can continue to extract resources like cotton and wool pulp from nature, and reduce supply chain disruption from nature-related disasters like water scarcity and flooding. The NOTB approach — especially for companies like House of Hackney, which have combined nature with future generations — asks how decisions made today will affect the ability of future systems to thrive, sometimes looking as far ahead as seven generations. “It’s a different way of seeing the world,” she adds.

Addressing nature directly is a leap into the unknown — not least because so few companies have mapped their nature risks and dependencies. But rather than face this with fear, Pimor encourages companies to see the opportunities. “We are facing the most incredible, unprecedented ecological challenge. Facing the unknown means becoming more open to creativity, innovation and imagination. That’s what the voice of nature brings to the table.”

Deep transformation tends to be slow and steady, concludes Ansell. “Slow isn’t ideal in the situation we find ourselves in, but it’s better than going backwards. The idea here is for nature to be a gentle guiding voice, moving the rudder one degree at a time until the ship has completely changed course. It’s exciting. There’s so much still up for grabs in terms of distilling the theory and making it work for each individual sector or situation.”
