Te Araroa: mum, please send me $32

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

After my very dark dome day, I went to bed feeling proud of myself but worried that I’d wake up feeling equally tired. Much to my delight, however, I slept well and woke up feeling pretty restored. It’s amazing what a shower, some food, and a sleep will do for your body.

Notes from the trail log at the dome cafe (no longer actually a cafe)

We had been discussing “trail legs” for a couple of days now, wondering if maybe we were finally getting them. I think on this day I got my first inkling of them, we walked as a group this morning (except Charlie who we knew would catch up as she’s the fastest out of all of us) and zoomed through the first ten kilometres. It was easy gravel road and the clouds hadn’t burned off yet. I felt good despite how hard I’d found the day before.

Sinèad, Annabel and I stopped for a snack break before the final hill portion of the day. The sun had started to burn off the clouds at this point, and it was starting to get hot. But we knew we only had about 15km into Puhoi (where we’d find food and maybe even a beer), and a campsite in 10km where we could replenish our water. So we pushed on.

This section of the day was a slog, not because the terrain was hard (it was just a gravel road) but because the sun was so hot. Saying we were sweaty would be an understatement, but there was nowhere to stop to cool off because the whole track was exposed. So we pushed on.

At some point Charlie did catch us and we all continued our climb up the gravel hill. This was the moment that I really decided that my merino hoodie was going to have to go. Luckily I have Fraser at home with access to my lighter-weight synthetic sun hoodies so I got him to send one to my friend in Auckland. Feels nice to not have to spend money unnecessarily.

Eventually, like finding an oasis in a desert, we spotted a little side track that led to a grassy, shaded area. I made a beeline for it and dropped all my stuff. It was approaching noon and I decided it was lunch time so took off my sun hoodie and pulled out my remaining trail pizza ingredients. This break was such a relief!

After drinking about half a litre of water and eating my two wraps (this time with added chilli flavoured corn nuts for crunch), I felt fortified enough to keep walking in the sun.

The rest of the afternoon passed in a sweaty blur and soon we found ourselves descending a strange path between two paddocks toward the Remiger road campsite at km 533. At this point I had pretty much run out of water so I powered ahead, focused on the water tank I knew was at the camp. It was a beautiful camp with a nice shelter, and after our 20 very hot kilometres it was a really great place for a break.

We had decided the night before that we’d try to make it into Puhoi and the call of the pub was strong enough to keep us from pitching tents at Remiger road. After a decent, shoe off, stretch out kind of break we were ready to conquer the final 4.5km of road walking.

With eternal hope in our hearts, we gave hitching a go, but people only sped up when they saw us. Thanks, Auckland.

Once we realized that hitching wasn’t going to happen, we began our road walk to Puhoi. It went surprisingly quickly and soon Charlie was dancing and waving her poles out front of the Puhoi tea rooms.

Annabel, Charlie, me and Sinèad at the tea rooms after checking in. Photo courtesy of Sinèad

The tea rooms were our home for the night, for $10 you get a very non-private shower (but there’s hot water so I’m not complaining), wifi, charging, and a tent site. The owner also does a special offer for TA hikers where he re-opens the cafe just for us and will make you anything off the menu or a burger. This seemed much nicer than walking a further 500m to the pub and was probably also cheaper, so we all agreed to stay in for the night.

We also met up with Moritz who had taken a zero at the tea rooms. He was full of energy and went to the shop to buy us snacks and used his flip fuel to top up our gas canisters. Before I left for Te Araroa I told Fraser I was sure I’d meet someone who would be able to do that, and once again: the trail provides.

I went to bed full and happy, and with the knowledge that because of tides, we’d have a very slow morning and then a chill 8km kayak down the Puhoi river the next day.

With our kayaks booked at 1pm, we had a slow morning. I had time to blog, charge all my electronics, dry my tent and order my blasphemous breakfast (see blog post about food for more). It was a luxurious morning and included coffee!!

At about 11:30am we headed toward the pub and general store where I did a small resupply and got some lunch and another coffee. I was not messing around with coffee anymore, not after how I felt on the dome day.

At 1pm, we made our way to the kayak place, got our briefing, and dropped off our packs. The awa (river) is the official TA trail, which is pretty cool! It requires an outgoing tide, so the moon and ocean and river decide what time your day starts. It felt sort of lovely to know that we didn’t have a choice of when we started. The awa has long served as a highway for waka (boats) and its name translates to “slow water”.

The awa is aptly named, and the kayak down it was so peaceful. I spent a lot of time just being carried by the current and paddling intermittently only to steer. Though the highway now runs over and beside the awa, it’s still a lovely space. As I paddled down, I watched the water turn from a murky brown river to clear, salinated ocean water.

A penguin floated past and I desperately tried to point it out for the people behind me. As I approached the end of the river, I spotted rays. It was magical.

Once off the kayaks, everyone agreed that it would be rude not to swim. So we got in the now ocean water. It was refreshing, and so warm compared to the water I’m used to swimming in in Wellington. The ground, however, was a whole strange experience. I spent most of the swim trying to keep my feet from touching the gooey mud on the bottom and Annabel and I screamed as our feet sank in.

Soon we were all back in our dry clothes, socks and dry-ish shoes. We said bye to Anica who was nursing an Achilles injury, which was sad.

There wasn’t much left in the day and we’d have to wait for a tidal section on the other side of a small hill. We set off anyway, and got our legs back into the habit of walking after our lazy morning and kayak.

On top of the hill between the end of the Puhoi and Waiwera

By the time we reached Waiwera (which translates to hot water because of the hot springs in the area) Charlie and I were not feeling keen to wait for the tides. My beach-blisters were hurting and making me feel like I was limping, and Charlie was just generally not feeling well. We decided to split up, and she and I took a bus into Ōrewa to check us all into the campground while the other two waited for the tides.

Charlie on the bus – she has a very similar shot of me

The Ōrewa holiday park was by far the worst place we’ve stayed. The “tent sites” were actually just hard-packed ground with a ton of tree roots. But the staff were really nice and I decided I’d ask at the office how much it would cost to upgrade to a cabin (it ended up being $1.25 extra each). I’m so glad I did!! There were some strange characters at the park and one guy told us that his laundry had been stolen the night before, so it felt nice to have a lock on the door.

Our little cabin was soon full of tents drying out, and dry bags jammed into every corner. We pondered whether we could fit Moritz in on the floor but luckily another hiker, Tim, had booked the cabin beside ours so he got a real bed.

I was happy to leave Ōrewa, and knew we were heading for a big supermarket and shopping mall. The walk this morning was easy and quick into Silverdale, where we all split up to run our respective errands. I spent way too much money buying blister plasters and restocking my supplements at chemist warehouse, but offset that by NOT buying a pastry at the coffee shop.

Caffeinated and ready to go, we packed up our new things and started the walk to Stillwater. This was all road and started relatively pleasantly with wide sidewalks and grassy shoulders. But eventually we got on the final stretch of road to Stillwater and it became the worst road we had encountered on the whole trail so far. Big trucks blasted past us and we scrambled to not fall into the steep ditches. When we finally came to a spot where someone could pick us up, Annabel, Sinèad and I decide to try to hitch.

Charlie, being ever the bravest of us, continued along the road and said the road did eventually get better.

A lovely couple picked us up and started telling us about a cabin we could stay in along the Ōkura walkway. This led to them calling their daughter who said that the cabin was SO FAR into the walk, like thirty minutes. We all laughed out loud at that and then she asked her mum to send her $32. Walking the bulk of that scary road was made worth it by that one phone call.

The couple dropped us off at the end of the road, and we ate our lunch at a little park before joining the Ōkura walkway. We got lucky with tides and were able to skirt the edges of the coast for this walk and very quickly found ourselves at Dacre Cottage. This was a gorgeous campsite with a shelter, toilets, water and some pretty nice, flat spaces to pitch tents. We were there early but the next kilometre or so involved a tidal crossing and it wasn’t low tide until 8pm. We would have to wait until the morning and cross at low tide.

Once again the TA fearmongering began. We had received videos from Sven who crossed a day or two before us showing everyone in their undies with the water up to their chests. I decided it would be fun even if it was meant to rain tomorrow. And besides, we all had indoor beds booked tomorrow night so it didn’t matter if all our stuff got soaked, right?
