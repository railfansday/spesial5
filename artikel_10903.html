Most accessibility problems on a website aren't hard to fix. Low-contrast text. An image with no alt text. A button that doesn't tell a screen reader what it does. None of these need a rebuild or a specialist.

Every year, WebAIM scans the home pages of the top million websites and checks them against basic accessibility standards. The 2026 report found that almost 96% of the pages scanned had at least one automatically detectable accessibility failure.

What stands out even more is what the errors actually are. The same problems have topped this report for seven years in a row. These aren't new, complex edge cases; they are the same simple, repeating mistakes.

In this guide, we'll look at what those top errors actually are, why they keep making it to production, and the simple workflow habits you can use to catch them before your users do. We'll also cover where tools like TestMu AI's Accessibility Testing Suite fit into that workflow.

It's easy to assume teams just don't care. But that's rarely the reason. Most developers know what alt text is for. Most designers know low contrast is a problem. The real issue is simpler; a few patterns explain most of it:

None of these come from not knowing accessibility rules. They come from how software is actually built: fast, in multiple moving pieces, with much of the page assembled from components and third-party code that nobody on the team wrote from scratch.

WebAIM's report found that a small number of accessibility issues account for most detectable WCAG failures across the top one million websites. The sections below explain what each issue is, why it matters, and how to fix it.

This is the single most common accessibility error, appearing on nearly 84% of homepages, according to WebAIM.

Poor contrast means the text color is too close to the background color, making it hard to read. For instance, light gray text (#999) on a white background (#FFF) yields a contrast ratio of 2.85:1, which is well below the standard requirement. For anyone with low vision or color blindness, that text becomes unreadable.

The Fix: WCAG asks for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text. Running a quick check with tools like the WebAIM Contrast Checker or a Lighthouse audit will give you the exact ratio before your page ships. Because the tools lay it out so clearly, this is easily one of the quickest errors on this list to catch and fix.

This is the second most common accessibility error, appearing on more than half of the homepages scanned by WebAIM.

Alt text is the written description a screen reader reads out loud in place of an image. Without it, a screen reader either skips the image entirely or announces the file name, which tells a user nothing about the image.

For example, if it's a product photo, a chart, or an icon that functions as a button, missing alt text means a screen reader user has no idea what they're looking at, what it does, or what happens when they click it.

The Fix: Describe what the image actually shows instead of just saying it's an image. If it's purely decorative and adds nothing a screen reader user needs, use an empty alt attribute (alt="") instead of leaving it off. That tells the screen reader to skip it cleanly.

Missing form labels appeared on 51% of home pages. This one is easy to miss because the form inputs often still look labeled. A form field looks perfectly labeled on screen when the label is simply placed next to the input box. A sighted user sees "Email" written next to a box and knows what to enter.

A screen reader, however, doesn't rely on visual placement. It relies on the HTML. If "Email" isn't actually connected to that input field in the markup, the field may be announced as simply "edit text" or "blank," leaving the screen reader user with no indication of what information it expects.

Nearly half of all homepages contain links with no accessible name. The most common examples are icon-only links, such as a search icon, shopping cart, or social media icon. Sighted users recognize these icons immediately, but a screen reader may announce them simply as "link," giving users no indication of what each one does.

The Fix: Give every icon-only link an accessible name using visible text, aria-label, or another appropriate labeling method. This preserves the visual design while allowing screen readers to announce the link's purpose.

About 31% of home pages have buttons with no accessible name, and the share has risen every year since 2021. The usual culprits are icon-only buttons: a hamburger menu, a close "X" on a modal, a play button, or a carousel arrow. A screen reader announces these as just buttons, so users can't tell whether they open a menu, close a dialog, or submit a form.

The Fix: Give every button an accessible name. Use visible text where you can. For icon-only buttons, add aria-label (for example, aria-label="Close dialog") or visually hidden text inside the button. If the icon is an image, provide alt text that describes the action, not the image.

Around 14% of homepages don't specify the page's language. Without it, screen readers have to guess how the text should be pronounced, which can result in words being read with the wrong pronunciation or accent.

Many users rely on a keyboard instead of a mouse to navigate a website. If a menu only opens on hover, a custom dropdown only responds to clicks, or a button can't receive keyboard focus, those controls become unusable for them.

The Fix: Every interactive element should be reachable with Tab and Shift+Tab and operable with the keys users expect: Enter for links and buttons, Space for buttons and checkboxes, arrow keys inside menus, tabs, and radio groups, and Escape to close dialogs and menus. You can test this by trying to navigate the entire page using only the keyboard.

Developers sometimes remove the browser's default focus outline to improve the visual design but don't replace it with another focus style. As a result, keyboard users can no longer tell which element is currently selected as they move through the page.

The Fix: Never remove a focus outline without providing a clear replacement. Every interactive element should have a visible, high-contrast focus indicator that tells keyboard users where they currently are on the page.

Videos without captions exclude people who are deaf or hard of hearing and make content harder to follow when audio isn't available or practical. Captions also help users understand speech in noisy environments or when the audio quality is poor.

The Fix: Provide accurate captions for every video. If the video contains important spoken information, include a transcript as well. Automatically generated captions are a good starting point, but they should always be reviewed for accuracy.

On mobile, small buttons and links are hard to hit accurately. Users may tap the wrong control, trigger an unintended action, or have to try multiple times before the touch registers. WCAG 2.2 Level AA sets a minimum target size of 24x24 CSS pixels, with 44x44 CSS pixels recommended (WCAG AAA and Apple's own guidance).

The Fix: Give every tappable element enough size and padding to be reliably tappable, even if the visible icon inside it remains small. Aim for 44x44 CSS pixels, and never go below 24x24.

Run a scanner across a page, and it catches a good chunk of what's wrong. Missing alt attributes, contrast ratios below the required threshold, a form field with no label in the code. Automated scanners catch these immediately.

According to most industry estimates, automated tools catch about 30 to 40 percent of accessibility issues. That's worth running. It also means most of what actually breaks a page isn't something a scanner can see at all.

Take alt text, for instance. A scanner can confirm an image has an alt attribute. It can't tell you if that attribute says anything useful. "Image description here" and an actual description of the photo both pass the same check because the tool only confirms the field isn't empty.

Take another example of a custom div button with role="button". A scanner sees the role and perceives it as a button. It has no way of knowing that nobody added the keyboard handling that makes the button actually work, because that's not something a static scan can test. You'd only catch it by unplugging your mouse and tabbing through the page yourself.

Reading order works the same way. A page can pass every automated check and still present its content in an order that makes no sense once you strip away the layout and just listen to it, top to bottom.

None of this means automated tools aren't worth using. It means they're a baseline, not a final check. Treating a clean scan as proof that a page works is exactly how these errors keep making it to production in the first place.

Most of the errors covered here aren't difficult to fix on their own. The harder part is catching them before they enter your codebase. The best time to find these problems is while you're designing, building, and reviewing a feature, not right before a release.

Automated testing should be part of that process. It catches common issues quickly, right as they're introduced, but it isn't a substitute for manual testing. A clean scan only tells you that no automatically detectable issues were found.

A passing scan still isn't the whole job, which is why the manual checks below matter.

Before shipping, spend a few minutes using the page yourself. Navigate using only the keyboard and verify that the focus indicator remains visible. If your site includes forms, menus, or dialogs, ensure they work without a mouse. For critical user journeys, testing with a screen reader will surface issues no automated tool can flag.

Finally, if the same issue keeps recurring, update your design system, component library, or review process to prevent it from happening again. Preventing a bug at the root is always easier than fixing it repeatedly.

Every error in this piece affects a real person trying to use your site. Low contrast text means someone with low vision can't read what you've written. A missing form label means someone using a screen reader can't fill out the form at all. These aren't rare situations; they happen constantly, on ordinary pages, to people who are simply trying to use your product.

That's what accessibility actually comes down to. It is not just a standard to meet, but the same usability you already care about, extended to people who navigate a page using assistive technologies.
