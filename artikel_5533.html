Opinion: The problem with ‘zero tolerance for bad behavior’ signs at hospitals

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

By Autumn Fiester, Aliza Narva, and Stacy C. Smith

Fiester is professor of medical ethics and health policy at the University of Pennsylvania’s Perelman School of Medicine and director of the Penn Program in Clinical Conflict Management. Narva is the director of ethics at the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania. Smith is a registered nurse, certified health care ethics consultant, and qualitative researcher.

Enter any hospital today and you’ll see signs warning patients and visitors that abusive language, disruptive behavior, or aggression will not be tolerated. Some threaten removal from the facility. Others warn that security or police may be called for infractions. Almost all of them declare “zero tolerance” for any of these behaviors.

The motivation behind these signs is understandable. Workplace violence against health care workers is a serious problem in U.S. hospitals, with research demonstrating an increase in frequency over the past five years.

In response to this widespread problem, both state governments and hospitals have made a concerted effort to demonstrate that they are taking this problem seriously. They have enacted a range of anti-violence measures that include reporting systems, de-escalation training programs, weapon-detection portals, increased security presence, and posted behavior signs.  Our own health system has implemented similar measures.

While these efforts are laudable in intent, not all of them are evidence-based with demonstrable efficacy in curtailing workplace violence. That includes the now-ubiquitous posted hospital behavior signs. Multiple studies that have tested these signs do not deter patients or families from committing acts of violence or reduce the frequency of physical threats.

If posted hospital behavior signs were merely ineffective at reducing workplace violence, they would be pointless, but not harmful.

But we believe that behavior signage is more problematic than it appears. The content of the signs sends clear messages to patients and families about their tenuous status and value in the institution. These signs telegraph to patients and their loved ones that they are on notice, warned that their care can be withheld and their behavior policed — even punished. In the urgency to reduce violence against health care workers, hospitals have failed to consider the message these signs communicate and whether that message aligns or conflicts with the core values of medicine: trust, care, compassion.

In our review of posted behavior signs, we found that they target only patient and visitor conduct. They rattle off a list of patient and family “do nots,” the hospital’s mechanisms of behavior enforcement, and the dire consequences of behavioral infractions. Even when the signs give a nominal nod to the institution’s commitment to reciprocal expectations for respectful communication between patients and the clinical staff, the list of “do nots” applies only to patients and families. They list no parallel behavioral standards for nurses or physicians. That makes behavior signs ignore the relational aspect of conflict. Research on lapses in clinical professionalism points to a more nuanced explanation for some of the challenging behaviors exhibited by families and patients. The Joint Commission has even named such clinical behavior as one of the causes of violence against health care workers.

Behavior signs also display from a type of institutional amnesia, forgetting why patients and families have come to the hospital in the first place: They are in pain, anxious, frightened, grieving. They may have or soon receive a life-altering diagnosis or a heartbreaking prognosis. They are vulnerable. Is this a group of people who should receive stern warnings and threats of removal as they enter the building?

When signs espouse “zero tolerance” for “noncompliant,” “disrespectful,” or “demanding” behavior, they signal to patients and their families that raising concerns about their care can be interpreted as misconduct. In a setting where patients and families already feel profound vulnerability, voicing a complaint about their clinical care can feel too risky.

That silence risks patient safety. Data show that patient and family feedback can reveal genuine safety concerns and quality issues that would fly under the radar if they remained unvoiced. Without patients and families feeling welcome to express concerns, hospitals can miss the exact information that would improve patient care and prevent patient harm.

Hospitals do not have to choose between protecting hospital staff and preserving patient/family trust in the institution.

Imagine signs that convey not threats, but shared expectations and a willingness to help when things go wrong:

“Our staff is here to help you during what may be one of the most difficult days of your life. We ask everyone, including staff, patients, and families, to treat one another with respect. If something is not going well, please let us know so we can help resolve it.”

Such messages still make clear that violence will not be tolerated. But they also clearly convey that health care is a partnership, that the experience of illness can be challenging, and that civility helps everyone. They express behavioral standards, while also indicating that the hospital’s response to challenging interactions is to help, not punish. Some hospital systems have already replaced their “zero tolerance” signs with civility signs that project care and concern.

Protecting health care workers from violence must continue to be an institutional priority, but that effort requires evidence-based prevention programs, including efforts to understand and address the root causes of conflict.

Messages about patient, family, and clinical staff behavior can be delivered in a way that acknowledges the concerns of hospital employees while also preserving patients’ and families’ sense of belonging and their ability to ask hard questions, voice concerns, and participate fully in their care.

The words we post on hospital walls must communicate more than rules. They must communicate medicine’s values.

Autumn Fiester, Ph.D., is professor of medical ethics and health policy at the University of Pennsylvania’s Perelman School of Medicine and director of the Penn Program in Clinical Conflict Management. Aliza Narva, J.D., R.N., is the director of ethics at the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania, where she leads the hospital’s ethics committee, ethics consultation service, ethics education initiatives, and nurse leader ethics fellowship. Stacy C. Smith, Ph.D., R.N., is a registered nurse, certified health care ethics consultant, and qualitative researcher whose work focuses on conflict and communication at the bedside.
