How migration shapes 9 U.S. states with older populations

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These nine states with older populations show how retirement migration, young-adult out-migration and aging in place shape the U.S. age profile, state by state

A state's median age is a record of who is born there, who arrives, who leaves and who stays put. When retirees move in and young adults move out, the midpoint of a population's age distribution climbs. Lower birth rates push it up as well. When families with children settle and older residents depart, it falls. Each state's figure is the sum of those shifts over decades.

The U.S. Census Bureau is the main source for this picture. It publishes median age by state, along with the share of residents 65 and older, through its population estimates and the American Community Survey. Median age is the age that splits a population into two equal halves. Half of residents are older, and half are younger. The share of residents 65 and older measures something different. It counts how much of a state's population has reached the traditional retirement age.

Those figures change with every release. A state's position can shift from one year to the next, and the order depends on which measure and which year a reader looks at. For that reason, this list does not rank anything. It is a selection of nine states with older populations, drawn from New England, the Mid-Atlantic, the South and the Mountain West.

Some names recur. Maine, Vermont and West Virginia have repeatedly appeared near the top of Census Bureau median-age rankings. Florida has the largest share of residents 65 and older among large states. The other states in this selection — Delaware, Pennsylvania, Montana, New Hampshire and Connecticut — round out a picture that spans small rural states and larger ones with major cities.

Two forces run through the slides that follow. The first is the migration of retirees into a state, which is central to Florida's age profile. The second is young adults leaving for other states, a pattern that shapes places such as West Virginia and Maine.

The age of a state's population affects how it plans. An older population changes the size of the local workforce. It shifts demand toward health care and long-term care. It also influences what a state collects in taxes and what it spends. Each slide below looks at one state's age profile and the factors behind it.

Maine has repeatedly appeared near the top of U.S. Census Bureau median-age rankings. Young adults leaving the state is one of the forces behind that older age profile.

The pattern has held across more than one Census Bureau release. Maine's exact median age and its position on the list belong to the reporting year of each release. No single figure describes the state permanently.

A high median age means a population leans toward middle and later life. In Maine, that tilt reflects decisions made over many years by residents of every age.

Young-adult out-migration is part of the explanation. When young adults move to other states, the residents who remain skew older. That shift raises the median age even when nobody else in the state moves.

Out-migration is one factor in Maine's age profile, not the whole story. The residents who stay and age in place also shape where the state lands in each release. So do the people who move in.

Florida has the largest share of residents aged 65 and older among large U.S. states. Its standing rests on that share of seniors rather than on median age. This sets it apart from the smaller states that tend to lead median-age lists.

A high share of older residents does not mean a state lacks younger people. Florida is also home to large numbers of children and working-age adults. Its retiree population stands out because of how much of the total it represents.

Size changes what that share means. In a small state, a high proportion of seniors can still add up to a modest number of people. In a state as populous as Florida, a similar proportion describes a large group in absolute terms.

Retirement migration is a defining feature of Florida's demographics. The state sits in the Sun Belt, the band of states across the southern U.S. Many people move there after leaving the workforce. They spend their later years somewhere other than the place where they built their careers.

That pattern differs from states where an older age profile builds up mainly because younger residents leave and longtime residents stay. In Florida, the older population grows partly through arrivals from other states.

The two routes leave different marks. An aging profile driven by departures reflects a smaller pool of younger residents. One driven by arrivals reflects a state that draws newcomers in later life.

Age 65 is also the point at which most Americans become eligible for Medicare. That makes the 65-and-older share a common reference point for health care planning at the state level.

Vermont, like Maine and West Virginia, has repeatedly appeared near the top of U.S. Census Bureau rankings of median age by state. That recurring position places it among the U.S. states with older populations.

Vermont's exact median age is not fixed, and neither is its precise place in those rankings. Both can move when the Census Bureau publishes new estimates. What recurs is Vermont's presence near the top across releases.

Vermont is a small, largely rural state in northern New England. It is one of the least populous states in the U.S. It borders the Canadian province of Quebec to the north and New Hampshire to the east. Massachusetts lies to the south, and New York lies to the west.

Vermont shares its northern New England setting with Maine. Maine is another state that recurs near the top of Census Bureau median-age rankings.

West Virginia has repeatedly ranked near the top of U.S. Census Bureau median-age rankings. Younger adults moving to other states are one factor behind that older age profile.

This pattern does not require older people to move in. When residents in their 20s and 30s relocate, they take their age bracket with them. The people who stay behind skew older as a group, even though no individual resident has aged any faster.

That sets West Virginia apart from a state whose older profile comes mainly from retirees arriving. In West Virginia's case, the older skew can build through departures alone.

The effect can compound over time. Children born to adults who have already left are counted in their new home states, not in West Virginia. That keeps the younger end of the state's age distribution smaller than it would otherwise be.

West Virginia's exact spot in the median-age rankings is not fixed. Its recurring place near the top is the consistent part of the record.

Delaware is one of the nine states in this selection of states with older populations. It represents the Mid-Atlantic coast in a group drawn mostly from New England and other regions.

Delaware is a small state on the Atlantic coast. Only Rhode Island covers less land. Delaware shares borders with Maryland, Pennsylvania and New Jersey.

Pennsylvania is part of this selection of states with older populations, and it differs from most of the others in one basic way: size. It is one of the most populous states in the Northeast. Its age profile covers millions of residents rather than a small, mostly rural population.

Those residents live in very different places. Philadelphia, the state's largest city, sits in the east. Pittsburgh, its second-largest city, sits in the west. Smaller cities, towns and rural counties fill the territory between them.

That spread shapes how a statewide figure should be read. A single median age or 65-and-older share for Pennsylvania folds big-city neighborhoods and rural counties into one number. The result describes the state as a whole, not any one community within it.

Montana is the Mountain West state in this selection of states with older populations. Its inclusion shows that an older age profile is not limited to New England, the Mid-Atlantic or Florida.

Montana is a large, mostly rural state. Its residents are spread thinly across long distances, with wide gaps between towns.

That geography separates Montana from the smaller, more compact states in this selection, such as Delaware and Connecticut. Montana's older residents live across open country rather than in a densely settled region.

New Hampshire is one of the nine states in this selection with older populations. It sits in northern New England, between two neighbors that have repeatedly appeared near the top of U.S. Census Bureau median-age rankings: Maine and Vermont.

Maine lies to the east and Vermont to the west. Massachusetts borders New Hampshire to the south. The Canadian province of Quebec lies to the north.

The state also has a short stretch of Atlantic coastline between Maine and Massachusetts. That gives it both an inland, mountainous north and a southern edge that faces the Boston area.

This slide does not give New Hampshire a median age, a share of residents 65 and older or a rank position. No dated Census Bureau figure for the state was available for this article.

Connecticut places this selection of states with older populations firmly in the Northeast. It is one of five states here from the U.S. Census Bureau's Northeast region, which is the largest regional group among the nine.

The bureau sorts the 50 states into four regions: the Northeast, the Midwest, the South and the West. It then splits each region into smaller divisions. The Northeast has two of them, New England and the Middle Atlantic.

Connecticut sits in New England, a division of six states. Maine, New Hampshire and Vermont are also in this selection, and all three share that division. Pennsylvania is the fifth Northeastern state here. It belongs to the Middle Atlantic division.

The other four states come from two regions. Delaware, Florida and West Virginia fall in the South. Montana falls in the West. The Midwest contributes no states to this selection.

The New England states on this list are not alike. Connecticut is more densely populated than Maine, New Hampshire or Vermont, which are largely rural. Its place alongside them shows that, within this selection, an older population in New England is not limited to rural states.

The regional grouping describes where Connecticut sits in the bureau's framework. It is a matter of geography, not a measurement. Membership in the Northeast does not by itself set a state's median age or the share of its residents who have reached retirement age.
