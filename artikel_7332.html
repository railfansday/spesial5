An AI agent does things its customer would not, on that customer’s behalf. Three businesses answered that problem three ways in October.

People are starting to send AI agents to do things for them online: Buy the groceries, book the flight, check the bank balance. The agent goes to the business’s website and acts in the customer’s place. Every business now has to decide what to do when one shows up, and in October three big ones answered three different ways.

I think Walmart got it right: It is about to give each agent a separate profile inside the customer’s account. An AI agent will do things the person maybe wouldn’t have, so the two are not the same. But it does those things on the person’s behalf, so they need to be linked. A separate profile lets the business see both: the agent and the customer who sent it.

Walmart says why it built Agent Profiles: “Today, when a customer uses an AI agent, it signs in using a customer’s account. This means that all purchases – whether made by the customer or the agent – appear together, without any differentiation.”

An agent might browse more pages than the customer would, maybe all of them. That’s the most basic difference. Beyond that, it could be literally anything.

So it’s not as simple as letting the agent sign in as the customer. Send a dog to shop for you at a local market, with a set amount of money. The dog only does it to bring the shopping back to you, and the whole thing only makes sense if the sellers know who sent the dog.

Walmart’s spending limit is the set amount of money, and the profile is the sellers knowing who sent it. A block stops the customer from using an agent at all. A shared login hides the agent behind the customer.

DoorDash says outside AI agents have placed “maybe several hundred orders in total”, in CEO Tony Xu’s words on the Q3 call, as TechCrunch reported. In the same quarter DoorDash handled 993 million orders, more than 10 million a day.

The rules for how an agent tells a business who it is are still a draft. The AI customer-service company Sierra and Meta are writing them as the Personal Agent Protocol: 40-plus companies are helping shape it, and its website named no business using it with customers when I checked on 1 November. On October 30, the draft added a recommendation that agents sign their requests with Web Bot Auth, a proposed way for an agent to prove which company runs it. According to the bot-protection company DataDome, as PPC Land reported, Meta’s Muse agent has been signing some of its requests that way since around 12 October, without an announcement.
