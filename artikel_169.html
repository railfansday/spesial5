UK will miss climate targets if it weakens EV sales rules, advisers warn

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Analysts say mooted move to let more petrol and diesel vehicles be sold would wreck plan to reach net zero by 2050

Weakening the UK’s electric car sales rules would probably cause the government to miss legally binding climate targets, according to advisers tracking Britain’s progress to net zero.

The analysis by the Climate Change Committee (CCC) suggests the policy would blow a hole in the plan for achieving net zero carbon emissions by 2050 – prompting concerns that Andy Burnham’s government will have to slash the UK’s carbon emissions reduction targets, known as the carbon budgets.

In a letter published on Wednesday, the CCC said the UK is “likely to miss” its climate targets if it confirms plans to allow more sales of petrol and diesel cars and vans in the next four years.

Those changes could result in an extra 8.4m tonnes of CO2 pollution a year from the UK by next decade.

The electric vehicle rules are the single biggest contributor to those climate goals, meaning the government will have to find other major carbon emission reductions, unless it amends the budgets to allow more pollution.

Carmakers must sell a bigger proportion of electric cars each year under the rules, known as the zero emission vehicle (ZEV) mandate, or face fines of £12,000 per vehicle.

However, the industry has lobbied intensively for months to cut the headline targets from 80% electric by 2030 to as low as 50% or 60%, arguing that the mandate was putting them under too much financial pressure.

Electric cars were 26.6% of UK new car sales during the first 10 months of 2026, well below the 33% headline target. However, carmakers are expected to comply with the mandate using a series of loopholes known as “flexibilities”.

The committee’s chair, Nigel Topping, wrote to Heidi Alexander, the transport secretary, to oppose any reduction of the targets.

Such cuts would, he said, “put the government’s commitment to tackling climate change at risk, miss an opportunity to reduce costs for a typical household by about £800 a year, increase the UK’s exposure to volatile oil markets by up to 15m barrels of oil a year in 2030, and damage long-term competitiveness as our main export markets rapidly transition to electric vehicles”.

Some in government have become alarmed in recent months about the impact the government’s various changes could have on the UK’s ability to meet its climate commitments.

The government has just cut funding for installing heat pumps, the second-biggest decarbonisation policy. Burnham has also alienated some Labour MPs by waving through gas extraction from the Jackdaw gasfield, and considering approval for the massive Rosebank oilfield.

MPs voted earlier this year to approve a new set of carbon reductions for the years 2038-42.

However, the Guardian has learned that one cabinet minister in the Keir Starmer government wanted the government to go back to parliament to scrap those planned reductions and enact a more conservative target instead.

The idea did not come to fruition though, with other cabinet ministers mostly opposed.

An industry source with knowledge of discussions in government said that serious consideration was still being given to changing the carbon budgets in the run-up to the UN’s Cop31 climate conference, subject to parliamentary approval.

The source said it was not clear if the proposal was still being discussed. A government source strongly denied that it was under consideration.

The changes to the mandate also mean the UK is unlikely to meet the carbon budget covering 2033 to 2037.

Several analysts said the government will struggle to find emissions reductions elsewhere, given the scale of pollution from petrol and diesel cars and vans. The policy could also face opposition from the governments of Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, each of which have their own carbon budgets.

Burnham on Thursday told Cop31 in Antalya, Turkey, that he was “proud to support this Cop’s focus on electrification” and urged people to choose electric cars and heat pumps.

Doug Parr, chief scientist at Greenpeace UK, said: “The Burnham government cannot talk of climate leadership on the international stage while doing a U-turn on ZEV targets at home.”

Topping added: “Weakening the zero-emission vehicle mandate would miss an opportunity to reduce the cost of living for people, undermine Britain’s competitiveness and leave us more exposed to volatile fossil fuel prices.

“People are buying electric vehicles in record numbers because they are cheaper to own than the alternative.

“The mandate gives those up and down the supply chain the certainty they need to invest, supports thousands of jobs, and keeps the UK on track to meet its climate commitments.

“British drivers deserve better than a backtrack on such critical policy.”

The car industry criticised the CCC’s analysis. Mike Hawes, chief executive of the Society for Motor Manufacturers and Traders, said: “A successful EV transition needs a strong market and a competitive industry.

“The CCC, however, ignores the damaging cost of the targets it defends: billions in unsustainable discounts and millions more in government incentives.

“Contrary to the CCC’s claims, the mandate is deterring local investment, as however much manufacturers export, without a strong local market there is no business case to build here.”

A government spokesperson said: “We remain committed to the phaseout of all new petrol and diesel cars by 2030 and to all new car and van sales being zero emission by 2035, as well as to our legally binding net zero targets.

“Our consultation on the mandate will not change this and will ensure we are taking a pragmatic and balanced approach to the ZEV transition.”
