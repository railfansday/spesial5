At Ignite 2026, the agent race moved to context, control and price

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Something shifted at this year's Microsoft Ignite, and it was not a model. On Tuesday, Microsoft used its Ignite stage, and partners timed their own releases to the same day, to announce enterprise agent products. The pattern was consistent: the competition has moved from who has the smartest model to who grounds agents in your data, who governs what they may do, and how you pay for the work they complete.

Start with context. Microsoft's headline platform announcement was Microsoft IQ, an "enterprise context management platform" that combines four context layers - Work IQ (documents, meetings and collaboration), Fabric IQ (governed business data), Foundry IQ (policies and institutional knowledge) and Web IQ - with more than 350 third-party connectors. The pitch, in Microsoft's own words, is that every organization has access to the same frontier models, so "what sets an organization apart is everything around the model." Reusable business plugins let teams curate governed context and skills once and reuse them across agents - including assistants in the ChatGPT and Claude marketplaces, not just Microsoft's Copilot. Customer spotlight AEMO, Australia's energy market operator, co-engineered an operational intelligence capability with Microsoft in under 12 weeks to help control-room operators filter thousands of signals while, Microsoft stresses, humans remain in control.

Then look at the partnerships. Microsoft and Salesforce, longtime CRM rivals, announced Copilotforce: Salesforce customer context, workflows and 38+ prebuilt sales skills inside Microsoft Copilot. The governance terms are the interesting part. Salesforce continues to control data access and business actions, Microsoft applies its compliance controls to communications and documents, and any writeback to the CRM requires explicit user confirmation. Accenture is design partner and pilot customer, deploying the integrations in its own sales organization, which has already scaled Copilot to more than 740,000 people. Availability is mixed, as launch-day lineups usually are: Salesforce in Copilot is a public preview, data federation from Microsoft OneLake into Salesforce Data 360 is generally available, and Sales Cloud in Teams is still in pilot.

BlackLine, whose platform more than 4,300 organizations use to run financial operations, is bringing OpenAI's frontier models to the office of the CFO, paired with a Finance Control Console so finance teams can see what agents do, set limits and reinforce human approval where it matters. "We're building agents and controls as one system," said Jeremy Ung, BlackLine's chief technology officer - a tidy summary of where enterprise requirements now sit.

Genesys pulled a different lever: price. Its standalone AI agents put an agentic virtual agent into production inside an existing customer-service environment without migrating to Genesys Cloud, and its Genesys Arena offers prebuilt Agent Kits covering more than 50 common use cases. The bigger signal is the pricing model: effort-based pricing, measured in what Genesys calls Experience Units, with each interaction classified as simple, moderate or complex so customers pay less for easy tasks. That is a quiet but real shift, from paying for AI as technology by the token to paying for work performed. The standalone agents are generally available now; the Agent Kits are due later this month.

Even Microsoft's model news reinforced the point: seven new MAI models introduced almost entirely through price-performance claims. Microsoft says MAI Code 2 reaches 98% of GPT-6.1 Sol's coding quality at about 40% of the price, averaged over four benchmarks it selected (DeepSWE 1.1, Terminal Bench 2.1, SWE-Atlas and FrontierCode v1.1), while conceding the model is explicitly not yet tuned for harder, long-horizon work. When a model vendor's headline is the ratio rather than the capability, the differentiation has moved elsewhere.

None of this proves the plumbing works at scale; these are launch-day claims from vendors with something to sell, and most integrations are previews or pilots. But the questions leaders can bring to vendors have sharpened. Who grounds the agent in your data, and under whose permissions? What can it do without asking, and what requires a human yes? Are you paying for tokens, or for finished work? This week, the loudest answers came from the context and control layer, not the model.
