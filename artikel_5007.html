Tanggal: 2026-11-18

German Lawmakers Back Customs Powers to Seize Crypto of 'Unclear Origin'

Germany's parliament has approved sweeping new powers for the country's customs agency, allowing it to seize assets, including crypto, whose lawful origin is in doubt, even when no criminal investigation is underway.

The Bundestag passed the Customs Financial Justice Act, or Zollfinanzgerechtigkeitsgesetz, on Thursday, November 12, the German government said. Under the law, customs can more easily seize assets where there are "justified doubts" about their lawful origin, an instrument the government describes as a "suspicious wealth order." Customs will also be able to better monitor digital cross-border payment routes and secure crypto assets.

"With this, we are sending a clear signal to criminals: we are getting tougher," German Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil said, according to the government.

The powers apply to "significant" assets, which typically means those worth more than €100,000, according to the government's bill. Because owners of such assets are usually able to explain where they came from, while the state struggles to prove an illegal origin, the government argued that it is reasonable to ask owners to disclose that information. "Almost every law-abiding citizen will be able to explain where these assets came from," the bill's explanatory notes say, as summarized by the Bundestag's news service.

Klingbeil has said customs could hold assets for up to 180 days without a conviction, during which owners would have to show they were obtained legally. "The Porsche and the Rolex are then gone for now," he said, according to the Berliner Zeitung. Any permanent confiscation would require a court ruling. Following an amendment by the governing coalition, that decision will be made by a panel of finance court judges rather than a single judge.

The law also reorganizes Germany's customs administration, strengthens its Financial Intelligence Unit and gives customs a legal basis to use technologies such as artificial intelligence. It forms part of an action plan against organized crime that the government adopted on February 25, 2026.

Germany has seized large amounts of crypto before, and has been quick to sell it. In 2024, authorities in the state of Saxony began selling the nearly 50,000 BTC they had seized from operators of the movie piracy website Movie2K, moving hundreds of millions of dollars' worth at a time as the sell-off continued. Blockchain analytics firm Arkham later said authorities had failed to seize a further 45,000 BTC linked to the site. The new law would give customs a way to seize crypto of unclear origin without first having to tie it to a specific crime.

German authorities have also gone after laundering infrastructure. Last year, they joined Swiss police in shutting down Cryptomixer, a mixing service that had laundered more than $1.4 billion in Bitcoin since 2016. Separately, the finance ministry has drafted a 25% tax on crypto gains from 2027.

The Bundestag's finance committee backed the bill on Wednesday with votes from the governing CDU/CSU and SPD, as well as the far-right AfD. The Left Party voted against, and the Greens abstained.

The Greens said Germany's money laundering problem is estimated at €100 billion, and that the government's expectation of only €308 million in additional revenue showed the gap between its ambitions and the law. The Left Party said the confiscation process was "built for the cash courier, not the banker."

The law still needs to pass the Bundesrat, the upper house of parliament that represents Germany's states.
