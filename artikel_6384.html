Engineers created a new tattoo ink that disappears and reappears when you shine different lights on it

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Engineers built tattoo inks that switch on under UV light and fade indoors, and the inventor has worn the color-changing tattoo for two years.

A tattoo is forever. That is the whole appeal and the whole problem. About a quarter of people with body art end up unhappy with a permanent mark.

Now there is an ink that stays colorless under the skin until ultraviolet light hits it. Then it turns blue, magenta or yellow in a few seconds, and fades again under ordinary room light.

Engineers at the University of Colorado Boulder built the inks from color-changing dyes sealed inside tiny plastic beads. They tattooed the inks into silicone and pig skin and showed that the three colors can be written, erased and rewritten with light alone.

One person has carried the inks in living skin for more than two years. He is the lead author.

The tattoo itself doesn’t go anywhere. Only the color does.

Carson Bruns of the ATLAS Institute and the Paul M. Rady Department of Mechanical Engineering at CU Boulder led the work, with Mollie Stockton, Emily Gardner and Hyejin Kwon.

The multicolor images were made only in skin models, and the inks have not been through safety testing for people.

In an interview with Earth.com, Bruns put the first caveat in plain words. “First, a PhotoTat is still a permanent tattoo. The pigment stays in the skin indefinitely, exactly like conventional tattoo ink.

What is rewritable is the color, not the tattoo,” he said. “Rewritable does not mean removable.”

Each ink is water carrying beads of PMMA, the clear plastic in plexiglass, bone cement and some dermal fillers.

The beads are a few hundred nanometers across, about the size of ordinary tattoo ink particles. Each one is loaded with a dye that flips between a colorless form and a colored one when light hits it.

The team chose PMMA for a second reason. It bends light almost the way skin does, so the beads scatter very little of it and are hard to see when the dye is switched off.

Three dyes give three primary colors. Mixed in different ratios, they produced a range of shades in a 16-patch test array.

To write a full-color picture, the team switched all three on at once, then erased each color selectively with the red, green and blue light of a laser projector.

“PhotoTat is like a switch. You turn the tattoo on with UV light, but you can turn it off again when you go to grandma’s house,” Bruns said.

Switching the tattoo on takes two to three seconds under a small 365-nanometer UV lamp. That dose of UV is roughly what six to 10 seconds of average sunlight delivers, the team calculated, far below what it takes to redden even the most sensitive skin.

How fast the color fades depends on how bright the room is. Under typical indoor lighting it lasts for hours, but it can persist for days. A bright white keychain lamp pressed against the skin erases it in seconds.

Bruns told Earth.com that mismatch is what makes the system usable. “That asymmetry is what lets skin behave like a display without any power source in the body,” he said.

Sunlight complicates things, because it carries enough UV to switch the ink partly on, even on an overcast day. The team found that SPF 30 sunscreen over the tattoo switched it off completely outdoors in every skin model they tested.

Bruns had a set of black fish-scale outlines tattooed on his shin and thigh. Between January 2024 and January 2025 he filled them with the color-changing inks, more than 30 small tattoos in all. The inks for that pilot came pre-mixed from HYPRSKN Inc., a company he co-founded.

When he assessed the tattoos, 1.4 to 2.2 years after they went in, all three colors still switched on under UV and off under white light.

In one scale he drew a smiley face with a UV pen and erased it. Then he wrote a heart through a printed mask, erased that, and wrote a musical note through a stencil.

Asked by Earth.com what surprised him, he named that durability. “I was surprised by how well the PhotoTats held up in my skin,” he said.

Color-changing compounds are known to wear out with repeated switching, he explained, and skin clears and shuffles pigment over time. So whether the inks would last for years in living tissue was an open question.

One person’s leg is not a study of people. Bruns has fair skin, and the authors wrote that a single-subject pilot can’t be generalized to anyone else.

In the lab, the blue dye wore out after 10 to 100 switching cycles and left behind a permanent purple tint that stayed visible in the off state. The authors call that burn-in a critical flaw, because an ink that is supposed to disappear can’t disappear.

Magenta and yellow held up far better over the same number of cycles.

Bruns said his own blue tattoos have so far shown negligible fading or purple discoloration after years of switching. This is different from the lab result that he raised himself.

Two other gaps are built into the chemistry. There is no white channel, so the system can’t make a patch lighter than the surrounding skin. And the blue reads as blue rather than a true cyan, which narrows the range of colors.

The color change also weakens as skin gets darker, for the same optical reason that colored tattoos show less contrast on brown skin, Bruns said. That limit comes from melanin, not from the ink.

Bruns is co-founder and president of MGC INK CORP, which sells a single-color version of this technology as Magic Ink. He is also co-founder and chief scientific officer of its parent company, HYPRSKN Inc.

Two co-authors hold financial interests in both companies, and Bruns is a co-inventor on a related patent assigned to the University of Colorado.

The single-color ink has been through a pilot clinical study of 11 people, published separately in the Journal of Nanobiotechnology.

No adverse events were reported, and the color switching still worked after at least a year. That study validates one formulation, not the three-color system, he said.

Beyond body art, the team sees medical uses. Many people getting radiation therapy are tattooed with small permanent dots so doctors can line up the beam, and most of them report being unhappy with the marks.

“They’re not beautiful, and they become a permanent reminder of a traumatic experience,” Bruns said.

“With PhotoTat, we could turn an involuntary medical tattoo into something that’s only visible to the doctor when you go in the clinic, and then it can be invisible the rest of the time.”

The three-color inks have not been through safety testing in people. Each color has to pass toxicity, sterility and purity testing on its own, Bruns said, and that work is still ahead.

The team also did not look at where the nanoparticles end up in tissue. Their statement that the beads stay in the dermis rests on what is already known about ordinary tattoo pigments, not on biopsies of their own samples. They list that check as a next step.

Clinical studies of the platform in more than one person are underway, the authors wrote. Finding a sturdier blue dye is the chemistry problem that has to be solved first.

Until then, the color-changing tattoo is a laboratory result, one man’s leg, and a single-color product with a different formula.

The full study was published in the journal Matter & Light.
