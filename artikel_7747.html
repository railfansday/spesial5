Imagine cranking up the thermostat on a freezing winter morning to get cozy, only to find that somehow your living room still feels chilly. Maybe there’s a draft coming from the windows, or one area never seems to warm up. Whatever the reason, you might be heating a home that’s letting the warmth escape—and paying through the nose for it.

According to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s ENERGY STAR program, you can save an average of 15% on heating and cooling costs by sealing air leaks and adding insulation where it’s needed. The tricky part? Figuring out exactly where the problems are.

That’s where a thermal imaging camera comes in handy. Instead of guessing which windows need resealing or where insulation might be missing, you can use one to spot temperature differences and take a closer look.

A thermal imaging camera detects infrared radiation emitted by objects and turns this into a visual representation of surface temperatures. Warmer (red, orange) and cooler (blue, purple) areas appear in contrasting colors, allowing you to see temperature differences that you can’t see with your own eyes.

This opens up some useful possibilities for homeowners. Imagine scanning around a window and spotting a cooler section along the frame. That doesn’t automatically mean there’s an air leak, but it’s a good reason to check the weatherstripping, caulking or surrounding insulation.

But remember: thermal imaging isn’t a crystal ball. There can be several reasons for surface temperature differences, and the camera won’t tell you exactly what’s wrong behind a wall. Still, it can help narrow down the places worth checking out before you start ripping off trim or making repairs.

There’s no tiresome setup process or annoying account registration. I took the camera out of its box, plugged it into my phone, downloaded the app and started viewing the thermal feed. I was super impressed by the camera’s sensitivity. As I walked around my house, I could actually see the residual warmth of my footprints on the carpet. Plugged-in electronics appeared as warm spots, while my air-conditioning vents stood out in deep blue against the warmer ceiling.

The app also automatically highlighted particularly hot and cold areas with temperature readouts, making it easy to identify points of interest without constantly examining the entire image. Every now and then, the hotter readouts were difficult to read because the red writing was so close to the red heat source, but a little shift in camera angle normally fixes it.
