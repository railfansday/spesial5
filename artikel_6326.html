When did the Anthropocene begin? Scientists make the case for a specific year

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Scientists argue the Anthropocene began in 1952, separating a sharp global transformation from thousands of years of human influence.

People were reshaping soils 2,000 years ago, and lead pollution appears in the geological record stretching back 3,500 years. But when did human activity begin transforming Earth on a global scale?

A group of 37 researchers argues that early human impacts and the planet’s recent transformation are different stories.

Using one name for both, they write, obscures how sharply conditions changed in the mid-20th century.

They propose reserving “Anthropocene” for a geological epoch beginning in 1952, while giving the longer history of human influence a separate name.

The argument appears as a perspective piece, built on published evidence rather than a new dataset. Its lead author is Jan Zalasiewicz of the University of Leicester, who co-chairs the Anthropocene Working Group.

Co-authors include Buhm Soon Park of KAIST in South Korea, Julia Adeney Thomas of the University of Notre Dame, and Jürgen Renn of Germany’s Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology.

“The Anthropocene is a common language for understanding how rapidly and extensively humans are changing the Earth,” Park said.

“By clarifying its meaning and criteria, we can compare changes in the global environment more accurately and discuss together how we should respond.”

The relatively stable climate of the Holocene, which began about 11,700 years ago, helped people develop farming and build cities.

The authors’ case is that this stability ended in the middle of the 20th century.

That period saw the postwar surge of industry, population, and consumption known as the Great Acceleration.

The surge left marks in mud and ice. Across sites around the world, the chemical and physical traces of human activity pile up in layers centered on 1952.

That is also the year fallout from the first hydrogen bomb test settled across the globe. Those layers are the evidence the proposal rests on.

Humanity has used more energy since the 1950s than in the whole of the Holocene before it, most of it from fossil fuels.

A formal proposal to add the Anthropocene to the geological time scale, starting in 1952, went to the International Commission on Stratigraphy in October 2023.

The commission rejected it in March 2024, amid disagreement over whether an epoch only seven decades old was too brief to count.

The rejection did not retire the word, and the authors sort its uses into two families.

The meaning they defend is a geological epoch beginning when the Earth system stopped behaving as it had during the Holocene.

The other, which they call the extended Anthropocene, gathers up every substantial human impact over tens of thousands of years, from the earliest reshaping of ecosystems to the steam engine.

Proposed starting points have ranged widely. Some put it at 1784, when James Watt improved the steam engine.

Others point to European colonization of the Americas in the 1600s, or to farmed soils thousands of years earlier.

The authors call the extended version a valid idea but a different one. It can’t be pinned to a layer of rock, so the “-cene” ending, which marks a geological epoch, doesn’t fit it.

They suggest a separate label, such as “anthroposphere,” to keep the two apart.

The authors’ table of changes since the 1950s reads like an inventory of a different planet. Global freshwater use rose more than 300%, and the water held behind dams rose 1,500%.

Those dams are one reason rivers now deliver half as much sediment to the sea as they did.

The changes also reshaped marine and terrestrial life.

Predatory marine fish have declined about 90% since 1950 as the global catch quadrupled, and coral reefs, kelp forests, and mangroves have each lost about half their extent.

Humans and their livestock now make up about 98% of the mass of mammals on land, a shift other researchers have described as pushing life toward a mass extinction.

The authors lean on the planetary boundaries framework, which sets safe limits for nine Earth processes against pre-1950s Holocene baselines.

Seven of the nine have been crossed. That framework already treats the 1950s as the dividing line, they wrote, and a formal epoch would make that comparison standard.

Whether the commission will vote again is open, and the authors call formal recognition an ongoing aim.

The evidence keeps growing as new sites are analyzed, they wrote, from ocean chemistry to the plastics and persistent pollutants piling up in modern sediments.

They also acknowledged that a formal name on the time scale would not, by itself, make other fields adopt it.

In the scenario the authors describe, atmospheric carbon dioxide would still approach 500 parts per million even if the world reaches net zero carbon dioxide emissions by 2050.

Most polar sea ice and coral reefs would be lost, and sea level rise would keep accelerating.

Their research priorities are tracking heat, acidity, and plastics through the oceans, and pinning down the planetary boundaries and the tipping points behind them.

For now, 1952 remains a proposed boundary, and the Anthropocene remains a term with more than one meaning.

The perspective was published in the journal Nature Reviews Earth & Environment.
