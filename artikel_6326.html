Scientists tracked millions of phones to uncover patterns in carbon emissions

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Scientists used anonymized cell phone mobility data to estimate carbon emissions, testing the approach across four countries.

When COVID-19 lockdowns froze the planet in early 2020, carbon emissions plummeted almost overnight.

The problem? The instruments tracking those emissions couldn’t keep up.

Conventional inventories lag reality by months. Satellite inversions come with their own trade-offs between resolution and coverage.

A new study explores another approach: using people’s movements to help estimate carbon emissions.

The research was led by Mengning Wang and Xin Lu from China’s National University of Defense Technology.

Collaborators included Xiaoling Zhang from the University of Hong Kong and scholars from various international institutions.

The researchers turned anonymized cell phone signals from 318 million Chinese mobile users into what amounts to a near-real-time carbon meter.

They tested the framework across four countries.

Commuting, deliveries, business trips, and holiday travel often generate carbon emissions.

The researchers reasoned that if so many carbon-producing processes involve physically moving people and goods, then the movement itself might serve as a fast measuring stick.

They took anonymized signaling data from China Unicom and converted it into daily intercity mobility networks.

Each city became a node and each flow of people between cities became an edge.

From these networks they extracted structural features – how central a city is, how tightly clustered its connections are, how many links it maintains – and combined those with population and geographic data.

Then they trained machine learning models against the GRACED dataset, which tracks daily CO2 emissions across seven sectors: power, industry, residential consumption, ground transport, domestic aviation, international aviation, and shipping.

The window researchers studied was January and February 2020. It’s hard to imagine a harder test.

Spring Festival travel sent 68.74 million daily intercity trips surging through the network. Then lockdowns hit.

Mobility crashed by 71.3%. Multi-source carbon emissions dropped from an average of roughly 356.5 million kilograms of carbon per day to a minimum of 209.6 million – a 41.2% collapse. The whole thing happened in weeks.

And yet the correlation between daily national mobility and CO2 emissions held firm at r = 0.89. At the city level, it was a more modest but still significant r = 0.49.

The best-performing model – LightGBM, a tree-based ensemble – achieved R² values of 1.00 for multi-source emissions, 0.99 for domestic aviation, and 0.98 for ground transportation across 366 cities and 60 days.

Those are extraordinary numbers for something as messy as urban carbon output.

The real surprise came when the framework crossed borders.

Using open-source mobility datasets, the team applied the same approach to Italy, the continental United States, and Mexico.

The model reached R² values of 0.91, 0.95, and 0.99 respectively against the GRACED inventory.

Tested against a completely independent dataset – ODIAC, which derives emissions from nighttime light imagery and power-plant data at 1 km resolution – it still scored between 0.90 and 0.99.

Four countries, two independent benchmarks, consistent accuracy.

Something unexpected emerged from the mobility data. The Louvain community detection algorithm carved China’s 366 cities into 11 functional clusters.

These are groupings defined not by provincial borders but by how people actually move between them. Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei formed one cluster, pumping out 62.75 million kilograms of carbon daily.

The Yangtze River Delta formed another, at 49.71 million.

These clusters aren’t just descriptive. More central, more connected clusters emit more carbon.

But here’s the policy hook. China’s existing climate governance tends to target a handful of major emitting cities while overlooking smaller ones that sit in structurally critical positions within the network.

The mobility-defined clusters offer something different: a template for coordinated regional decarbonization that matches how economies actually function rather than how administrative maps happen to be drawn.

The authors aren’t overselling this. They call mobility a “partial but observable proxy” – a complement to inventories, not a replacement.

The Chinese analysis covers a single extraordinary two-month window. The data captures intercity movement, not what happens within a city.

Mobility alone may not fully capture emissions changes driven by seasonal energy demand, holiday schedules, or weekly cycles.

There are subtler problems too. Cellular signaling data inherits all the biases of telecom coverage – regional disparities, operator market share, sampling artifacts.

And the fundamental link between mobility and emissions could shift as remote work spreads, transport electrifies, and travel patterns reshape.

A correlation forged during the most dramatic behavioral disruption in modern history might not hold the same way during a quiet Tuesday in 2030.

Still, the core appeal is hard to argue with. Standard emissions inventories are accurate but glacially slow.

With timely access to mobility data, the framework could provide frequently updated emissions estimates.

If it scales, climate policymakers would gain a behavior-informed reading of the present rather than a months-old accounting of the past.

The team calls for fusing their mobility approach with remote sensing, energy structure data, and meteorological variables.

The ambition is to make carbon monitoring something closer to weather forecasting: imperfect, always updating, but fast enough to actually be useful.

The study is published in the journal The Innovation.
