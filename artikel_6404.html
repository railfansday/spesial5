Howler monkey droppings hold clues about the fragmented forests they live in

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

In southern Mexico, the mix of gut bacteria in howler monkeys varied with forest patch size, the trees growing there, and how well the patches were connected.

Howler monkeys can survive in small patches of forest.

A study in southern Mexico found that the mix of bacteria in their guts varied with the size of those patches, the trees growing there, and how well the patches were connected.

Researchers found these patterns in droppings from adult male mantled howler monkeys.

Repeating that sampling could help conservationists track changes over time without capturing the animals.

The study was led by Tabor Whitney while she was a doctoral student at Northwestern University. She worked with primate researchers at Universidad Veracruzana in Mexico.

The work was carried out in the Los Tuxtlas Biosphere Reserve in the state of Veracruz. About 60% of the original forest cover in the reserve has been lost. The remaining forest is broken into patches among pastures, farm fields, and towns.

The 10 study patches ranged from about 5 acres to nearly 500 acres (2 to 199 hectares). Farmland or pasture bordered every one of them.

Whitney and field assistants followed adult male howlers in October and November, during the 2022 rainy season. When a male defecated, the fresh droppings were collected from the ground with sterile sticks.

The team tested the droppings for three things. The first was the bacteria in them. The second was the fatty acids that those bacteria produce. The third was the breakdown products of stress hormones.

The trees in every patch were also measured. Across patches, average trunk width ranged from about 10 to 16 inches (25 to 42 centimeters).

Biologists call the community of microbes in an animal’s gut its microbiome.

“An awesome takeaway of the study is the knowledge the gut microbiome is the most sensitive to ecological change,” Whitney said.

Gut bacteria were analyzed in 41 samples from 32 males. Monkeys from different patches carried different mixes of bacteria.

These differences were linked to all four measures of a patch’s size and layout. Its area and its distance from other forest were among those measures. All seven measures of the trees growing in the patch were linked to the differences too.

Plant fiber is digested by some of the bacteria involved. One such group is Prevotella, and monkeys in larger patches carried more of it.

A different group of bacteria was most common where trees were small. The same was true where few rows of trees linked a patch to other forest.

Only one forest measure predicted the diversity of bacteria carried by a monkey. That measure was trunk thickness. Less variety was found in monkeys from patches with thicker-trunked trees.

The authors suggested that monkeys in mature forest may eat a narrower diet. Fewer kinds of microbes may be taken in as a result.

The team also tested distances to the nearest houses and roads. Neither distance predicted any of the three health measures in the main analysis.

Stress hormone levels were measured in 36 males. They didn’t differ clearly from patch to patch. One pattern, however, was the reverse of what the team had predicted.

Males in larger patches tended to have higher hormone levels, not lower ones.

Higher levels were also found in patches with more fig trees. Figs are a central part of the howler diet.

The authors explained the result in social terms. Several howler groups are supported by larger patches. More than one group may be attracted to the same place by fig trees.

Hormone levels could be raised by competition during encounters with rival groups, not by food shortage. Ordinary demands such as reproduction and fighting infection are also known to raise these hormones.

A monkey with a high level is therefore not necessarily in trouble.

Plant fiber is fermented by gut bacteria, and small fatty acids are released in the process.

Those acids are used by the animal for energy. These acids also help maintain the gut lining and the immune system.

No difference between patches was found in the amounts of these acids. Instead, the team’s attention was drawn to the balance between propionate and butyrate, two of the acids.

A surplus of propionate over butyrate has been proposed as a sign of an unbalanced gut. That pattern has been documented both in people and in wild primates that are short of food.

More propionate than butyrate was present in the droppings from all 10 patches.

In four of the 10 forest patches, the ratio of propionate to butyrate was about 2.6 or higher.

Human studies have linked that threshold to gut disorders. Whether it signals a health problem in howler monkeys remains unknown.

The authors wrote that a reduction in butyrate-producing bacteria may be indicated by the pattern. The reduction may extend across the whole landscape.

The researchers also raised the possibility of long-term effects on the monkeys’ gut lining and immune function.

Howlers in southern Mexico suffered a mass die-off in 2024. Up to 31% of some local populations died from the combined effects of extreme heat, drought, and habitat degradation.

Baseline health records are needed if early warning signs of decline are to be detected, the authors wrote.

Two conservation priorities were recommended by the authors. Patches of mature, varied forest should be protected and enlarged. Rows of trees and wooded strips along rivers should keep those patches connected.

The researchers also suggested that a wider variety of trees be planted, including fruiting species. Howlers would then be able to move through the canopy in search of food and mates.

Permission from local landowners was needed before the team could reach the monkeys.

“Landowners we’ve spoken with are really open to these conversations,” Whitney said.

“They’ve said they love seeing the monkeys, and if they can help them travel on the trees, they’re very interested in it.”

She said the same sampling could be repeated every five or 10 years. Changes could then be compared over time, and the effect of conservation work could be checked.

Every sample came from an adult male. All of the samples were collected in a single rainy season.

Female and young howlers have different energy and hormone demands. The results therefore can’t be assumed to apply to them.

The gut bacteria samples were also limited and unevenly distributed across patches. Some patches supplied one sample, while others supplied as many as 12.

The team identified which bacteria were present. What those bacteria were doing inside the monkeys was not measured.

The study found links between forest features and gut bacteria.

Further research could test whether changes to the forest lead to changes in the monkeys’ gut bacteria.

The authors also noted that forests across Los Tuxtlas may be too uniformly degraded.

That could explain why the fatty acids varied so little. Comparisons with other landscapes are needed, they wrote.

Future research could pair repeated sampling with long-term records of the monkeys’ behavior. Such work could test whether these signals change as the forest does.

The team is now preparing a study comparing the gut bacteria of male and female howlers.

The full study was published in the journal Animal Conservation.
