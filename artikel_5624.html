Less Intensive Follow-Up of Lung Nodules May Result in Larger Cancers

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

— Study suggests a return to more intensive recommendations for follow-up of small nodules

A less intensive approach to monitoring newly detected small lung nodules failed to meet noninferiority criteria compared with a more intensive strategy, and may result in larger cancers at diagnosis, a cluster randomized pragmatic trial showed.

Among the patients with lung cancer, 36.3% of those in the less intensive group had cancers larger than 20 mm at diagnosis compared with 27.7% in the more intensive group, reported Michael K. Gould, MD, of the Kaiser Permanente Bernard J. Tyson School of Medicine in Pasadena, California, and colleagues.

After adjustment, size progression to larger than 20 mm was 7 percentage points (95% CI -2.0 to 15.9) more common in the less intensive group than the more intensive group, which exceeded the prespecified noninferiority margin of 5 percentage points, but was not statistically significant.

"Although neither group was clearly superior in the main analysis, we cautiously advise guideline developers to consider returning to the prior, more intensive recommendations for follow-up of small nodules, particularly in younger persons," the authors wrote in the Annals of Internal Medicine.

Indeed, a subgroup analysis showed that tumor progression was significantly more likely among younger patients in the less intensive group.

"We weren't able to validate the less intensive approach reflected in the current guidelines -- we weren't able to demonstrate noninferiority," Gould told MedPage Today.

In addition, "progression in size and stage are more common than one would think, perhaps suggesting that the whole idea of a CT follow-up strategy be reconsidered," he said. "When we see one-third of patients having progression, we need to do better."

Given the frequency of size progression in both groups, "newer approaches to follow-up for patients with small pulmonary nodules, including volumetric measurement, novel biomarkers, and radiomic tools, should be rigorously evaluated," Gould and team wrote.

The "Watch the Spot" trial enrolled 34,686 participants at 14 healthcare systems between March 2017 and July 2019. Mean age was 64.5, and 50.8% were women. All had a newly identified pulmonary nodule measuring 15 mm or less in diameter on a diagnostic or screening chest CT scan, defined as the index scan.

The protocols for less and more intensive CT follow-up were modeled on current and prior recommendations from the Fleischner Society and the Lung CT Screening Reporting and Data System (Lung-RADS).

Lung cancer was diagnosed within 27 months of nodule identification in 1.5% of those in the less intensive group and 1.4% of those in the more intensive group.

The median nodule size on the index CT scan was 9 mm, and 70% of all cancers were detected incidentally. More than 25% of lung cancers were diagnosed in participants with nodules measuring 6 mm or less in diameter at the time of detection on the index CT scan.

Median tumor size at diagnosis was 16 mm in the less intensive group compared with 15 mm in the more intensive group, and stage III or IV cancer was somewhat more common in the less intensive group (31.4%) than in the more intensive group (28.6%).

Adherence to protocol-based follow-up was "poor" in both study groups, the authors observed -- 42% in the less intensive group and 45% in the more intensive group. After adjustment among these patients, diagnosis with progression to a tumor size larger than 20 mm was more common in the less intensive group by 14.9 percentage points.

"Adherence is important," Gould observed. "While we're waiting for newer approaches -- whether it's volumetric measurement or novel biomarkers -- the poor adherence we observed in our study represents a very important opportunity for quality improvement."
