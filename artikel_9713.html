Welcome to HackerNoon’s Meet the Writer Interview series, where we learn a bit more about the contributors that have written some of our favorite stories.

My first job, in 2015, was finding out what people really think of things: market research, then reputation analysis for a national lottery brand. Everyone has a view of a lottery. Nobody is neutral. Then I moved to Istanbul and co-founded an e-commerce business selling Turkish goods directly to customers. That is where I learned what I now tell founders: you can be the voice of your company, but the product needs its own accounts, its own email and its own search presence from day one.

Later I worked on a large language model project. From that side you learn two things quickly. An answer is never as fixed as it looks: while a model is still being worked on, the same question can come back noticeably different from one round to the next. And geography matters, just as it always did in search. The same question asked from two countries can return two different pictures of the same company.

That path explains where I am now. I'm a partner at BARS Agency. We build and promote digital products, work on reputation and on how people and companies are presented in AI search. Geometrika started there as a tool we built for ourselves and our clients, to see how AI assistants describe a brand and which sources they lean on, so that strategy could start from data instead of guesses. Exhibo, a global art gallery and culture platform where I'm co-founder and editor-in-chief, asks the same question about culture: which galleries, museums and places become visible online and which quietly drop out.

On paper, these look like different careers. It's one question asked in different rooms: how does something get seen and why do people believe what they see?

I studied art history and I'm studying psychology now, which covers both halves of that question. I lived in Istanbul for several years. These days I live between Doha, Dubai and Egypt.

My latest Top Story asked why a two-hour transfer in the Alps can cost more than the flight that got you there. It belongs to a series I keep coming back to: hidden economics, the prices everyone pays and nobody explains. The newest piece in it is about film rebates in the Middle East. The detail I can't stop thinking about is that a desert now comes with a percentage attached. Every line producer knows the rates by heart. Almost no government in the region publishes what it has actually paid out.

The method doesn't change from piece to piece. Start with a number that should be public. Find out whether it is. If it is, read every row of it.

On HackerNoon it's mostly search, AI and the psychology around them. Somewhere along the way the psychology pieces and the search pieces turned into the same subject. People tend to accept a coherent, confident explanation without checking how it was put together. Memory does it to us: a manager sincerely remembers backing the idea that won. AI answers do it for us: one fluent paragraph, assembled from sources of very different quality, reads as settled fact.

Elsewhere it's culture. At Exhibo I write about art, architecture and the stories attached to objects and places. One pattern keeps coming back: fiction borrows a real place and forgets to give it back. Tatooine is in Tunisia. Agrabah is stitched together from real cities.

The subjects wander. The question underneath doesn't: what is actually going on beneath something everyone takes at face value?

I start with a table. Whatever I want to know, row by row. Half of it usually fills in an afternoon. The piece lives in the cells that refuse to fill.

Then I go to the primary documents, which in this region often means a French PDF, a ministry decree or a press release that is generous with every number except the one you need. When I find a dataset I add it back up against its own totals before I trust a single figure from it.

The research eats the writing. I can spend a week finding out that a number almost never exists in public. That week is invisible in the final piece. Finding out that something isn't there is a finding too. It just takes longer to write down.

The other challenge is restraint. When you've read seven years of a ledger, you want to show the reader every row. The reader wants three.

I want companies to be able to see how AI systems describe them and where that picture comes from, the way they once learned to read their own search results. Most still check it by asking ChatGPT about themselves once and hoping for the best. And what actually moves those answers is rarely what anyone expects. Start a podcast and the assistants often pick it up. I once shared a few of my own photos of desert monasteries. Within weeks the AI version of me had become a cultural heritage contributor and was drifting towards "philanthropist". Nobody has ever complained about that particular error.

I want the same for culture. A serious gallery in Doha or a small museum in Cairo shouldn't disappear from what people find online simply because the information about it was never made easy to find.

Trips planned around exactly one thing I want to see. Lately it's usually a ruin with a desert around it, though it can just as easily be a coastline or a forest. Everything else is allowed to happen on the way, as long as there's European coffee somewhere on the route.

And when the list runs out, I go out into the desert just to look at nothing for an hour. It is the only place where my phone and I agree to ignore each other.

Languages. Spanish, Arabic and, less gracefully, Turkish. Spanish feels familiar, at least in its logic. Arabic and Turkish don't. Many Arabic words are built from three-consonant roots, so one root gives you a writer, a book and an office. In Turkish a whole sentence can be a single word thirty letters long. Both break my brain a little.

Translators and AI do fine with a menu. Face to face, they still get lost. So I'm doing it the long way.

And art, which stopped being a hobby the day it became a job. I will still talk Monet and Manet with anyone who confuses them.

First, an experiment I've wanted to run for a while: asking every major AI assistant who I am, then tracing the wrong answers back to where they came from, when they came from anywhere at all. A machine's idea of you is assembled from places you have forgotten you ever wrote.

Then more hidden economics: who pays for a free museum. The British Museum opened free in 1759, on a collection a public lottery had helped pay for. In 2026 the Louvre and the Grand Egyptian Museum both raised their prices. Someone always pays. The question is who. And whether they know it.

Where else would a story about airport transfers in the Alps sit next to one about falling in love with chatbots? I've stopped asking which of them is the tech story.

If you ever go looking for a number and it isn't there, write that down. The absence is usually the story.
