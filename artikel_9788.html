Imagine someone asks you, “Hey, do you want to jump from the edge of space?”

Your first response would probably be, “What the hell is wrong with you?”

But when that exact question was asked to Felix Baumgartner, he basically went, “Yeah, sure. Why not?”

Now, you might be wondering who on Earth was crazy enough to ask that question in the first place.

Ladies and gentlemen, this is the story of the Red Bull Stratos mission: a man crazy enough to jump from the edge of space, a company crazy enough to fund it, and a PR gamble that somehow paid off spectacularly.

Now switch the M and the E, and you get Max Emilian Verstappen—okay, that’s not how names work. You get Max Verstappen. But let’s not get distracted.

Earned Media Value (EMV) is the estimated dollar value of publicity a brand receives without paying for it directly.

“The estimated dollar equivalent of unpaid public exposure.”

Which is technically correct, but sounds like a corporate robot wrote it.

So here’s the version my brain understands:

You do something. People can’t stop talking about it. You didn’t pay them to talk about it. That’s EMV.

Influencers posting because they genuinely found something cool? EMV.

People sending the campaign to their friends? EMV.

Yeah... people are definitely going to talk about that.

A simplified formula looks something like this:

EMV = (Total Impressions × Industry CPM) ÷ 1,000

Basically, it estimates how much money the brand would have had to spend on advertising to achieve the same amount of attention.

Some companies also factor in engagement, audience quality, creator influence, and even audience sentiment. After all, ten million people saying, “This campaign is brilliant,” and ten million people saying, “This campaign is terrible,” are two very different outcomes.

Because it lets them estimate the value of all the buzz they generated without paying directly for every conversation.

Imagine your campaign gets millions of people talking. If buying that same amount of attention through traditional advertising would’ve cost millions of dollars, that’s exactly what EMV is trying to estimate.

It isn’t money sitting in Red Bull’s bank account.

It’s simply a way of estimating the advertising value of the attention they earned.

Compared to EMV, ROI is wonderfully straightforward.

ROI simply measures how much profit (or loss) you made compared to how much you invested.

ROI = (Net Profit ÷ Cost of Investment) × 100

Suppose you invest $1,000 into something.

That means your investment generated a 50% return.

A positive ROI means your investment made money.

(Or, as I like to call it, congratulations—you’ve invented a very expensive hobby.)

So what’s the difference between EMV and ROI?

EMV asks: “How valuable was all the attention we earned?”

ROI asks: “Did we actually make money compared to what we spent?”

The other measures financial performance.

We’ll need both of these in a minute because Red Bull Stratos wasn’t just an engineering miracle.

And that's exactly why Red Bull Stratos is still taught as one of the greatest marketing campaigns ever executed.

Somewhere inside Red Bull, on what was probably a completely normal Tuesday, someone had a thought.

A completely normal thought, according to Red Bull standards. Which is already slightly concerning, because Red Bull’s definition of “normal” has historically been questionable.

“Everyone is doing television commercials, right?”

Then came the question that changed everything:

At the time, YouTube livestreaming was still a relatively new idea. Instead of buying a traditional advertising slot, Red Bull could create something people would actively choose to watch.

What could possibly be big enough to capture the world’s attention?

“What if a man jumped from the stratosphere?”

Now, most companies would probably stop here and call it a concerning brainstorming session.

Red Bull looked at the idea and apparently said:

And just like that, Red Bull Stratos was born.

With an estimated investment of around $50 million, Red Bull wasn’t simply funding a stunt. They were funding a scientific mission, an engineering challenge, and one of the boldest marketing gambles in history.

Fifty million dollars is the kind of money that makes normal people rethink buying a coffee.

Red Bull looked at that amount and thought:

“Perfect. Let’s use it to make a human being fall from the sky.”

Unlike certain Formula 1 teams that shall remain unnamed (Ferrari, I’m looking at you), Red Bull actually had a plan.

An Austrian skydiver with years of experience performing extreme jumps, Felix was not exactly someone who looked at a dangerous idea and immediately walked away.

When Red Bull approached him with the idea of jumping from the stratosphere, his reaction was essentially:

Which is either the bravest sentence ever spoken or the sentence responsible for several safety engineers losing sleep.

But this wasn’t just a man standing on a platform and falling.

Behind that jump were scientists, engineers, safety experts, pressure suits, a specially designed capsule, and a massive helium balloon carrying Felix nearly 39 kilometers above Earth.

Basically, hundreds of extremely intelligent people worked together to make sure one person could safely do something that sounded like a rejected cartoon plot.

On October 14, 2012, Felix Baumgartner stepped out of his capsule approximately 39 kilometers above Earth.

During his free fall, he reached a maximum speed of around 1,357 km/h, making him the first person to break the sound barrier during a free fall.

The entire event was livestreamed, from the balloon launch to the moment Felix stepped into the unknown.

This was not a commercial people had to sit through before watching something else.

This was the thing people wanted to watch.

The jump itself lasted around 10 minutes, with approximately 4 minutes and 20 seconds spent in free fall before his parachute opened.

What looked like a man simply falling from the sky was actually years of preparation, engineering, testing, and scientific collaboration.

Red Bull had spent years preparing for a moment that would last only a few minutes.

But those few minutes had the potential to become one of the greatest marketing successes ever created.

Because this was not a pre-recorded commercial where a company could quietly delete the footage and pretend nothing happened.

And the internet, famously known for being calm and reasonable during unexpected events, was waiting.

If something went wrong, millions of people would see it happen in real time.

Red Bull wasn’t just risking $50 million.

They had spent years preparing for a moment that would last only a few minutes.

And then Felix stepped out of the capsule.

At no point during the livestream did Felix stop mid-free fall, pull out a can of Red Bull, take a sip, and say,

“Look at me! I’m literally flying through the stratosphere!”

Because he was wearing a Red Bull-branded pressure suit.

The massive helium balloon carrying him nearly 39 kilometres into the sky?

They didn’t interrupt people’s attention.

Nobody tuned in because they wanted to watch an advertisement.

“Oh my God. Someone is actually going to jump from the edge of space.”

Humans have a fascinating habit of stopping to watch extraordinary things unfold.

When something unbelievable happens, people naturally gather around it.

Instead of asking millions of people to spend thirty seconds watching their advertisement...

...they created something millions of people willingly spent hours watching.

And the brand just happened to own that moment.

Because Red Bull has never really sold caffeine.

They’ve sold the idea that normal is boring.

People don’t buy Red Bull because it’s simply an energy drink.

They buy into everything the brand represents.

Whether that’s Formula 1, cliff diving, mountain biking, air races, or a man casually deciding gravity is optional.

Then the internet did what the internet does best.

Friends sending each other the livestream.

Every conversation became free advertising.

Exactly what we talked about earlier with Earned Media Value.

Nearly eight million people watched simultaneously on YouTube.

Hundreds of millions watched clips afterwards.

The campaign generated an estimated $6 billion in earned media value.

Red Bull didn’t interrupt millions of people to tell them they were exciting.

They did something so exciting that millions of people interrupted their own lives to watch.
