Understanding the genetic basis of gene expression can shed light on the regulatory mechanisms underlying complex traits and diseases1,2. Single-cell-resolved measures of RNA levels and single-cell expression quantitative trait loci have revealed genetic regulation that drives cell states and types across diverse tissues3. Here we describe phase 1 of TenK10K, which leverages single-cell RNA sequencing data from more than 5 million cells across 28 immune cell types, along with matched whole-genome sequencing data from 1,925 individuals. This provides power to detect associations for rare and low-frequency genetic variants that have largely been uncharacterized in their impact on cell-specific gene expression. We map the effects of both common and rare variants in a cell-type-specific manner using a recent method that increases power by modelling single cells directly rather than aggregated ‘pseudobulk’ counts. We identify putative common regulatory variants for 83% of all 21,404 genes tested and cumulative rare-variant signals for 47% of those genes. We explore how genetic effects vary across cell types and states, develop a framework to determine the degree of single-cell expression quantitative trait loci cell-type specificity and show that about half of the effects are observed only in one or a few cell types. By integrating our results with functional annotations and disease information, we further characterize the likely molecular modes of action for many disease-variant associations. Finally, we explore the effects that genetic variants have on gene expression across continuous cell states, and effects that vary cell-state abundance directly.

Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have revealed hundreds of thousands of association signals between genetic variants and human diseases and traits4, but the modes of action by which most of these variants affect phenotypes remain to be characterized, especially non-coding variants. The characterization of the impact of genetic variants on gene expression—known as expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) mapping—can help linking variants to the putative genes they regulate1,5,6,7,8,9, thus identifying the genes and pathways involved in disease pathogenesis, which is a critical step in identifying opportunities for therapeutic intervention10.

However, important limitations remain. First, eQTL mapping has traditionally been limited to common genetic variation (minor allele frequency (MAF)) greater than 1–5%) due to relatively small sample sizes and the widespread use of genotyping arrays, which do not accurately genotype most rare variants and complex structural variants11, compared with whole-genome sequencing (WGS) approaches. Second, eQTLs are extremely context specific, with many studies demonstrating that genetic variants frequently affect gene expression levels in ways detectable only in specific tissues3, sorted cell types6,10,12,13 or on stimulation14,15. Single-cell eQTL mapping has further demonstrated that the effects of many genetic variants on gene expression can only be detected within specific cell types and even transient cellular states3,16,17,18,19,20,21,22,23.

Here we introduce phase 1 of the TenK10K (up to 10,000 cells for 10,000 individuals) study, encompassing 5.4 million cells from 1,925 individuals of European ancestry selected from a broader cohort of 2,103 individuals. In accompanying papers, we leverage the same dataset to investigate the effect of tandem repeat variation on gene expression24 and the putative causal links between gene expression measurements and human diseases and traits25, and an overlapping set of samples to explore the impact of genetic variation on chromatin accessibility26.

Here we use an association method we recently developed, SAIGE-QTL27, to map common-variant and rare-variant single-cell eQTLs across immune cell types. We identify more than 150,000 common-variant effects and more than 30,000 rare-variant effects on gene expression, including signals for nearly 5,000 genes (roughly 25%) with no previously reported eQTLs in a large blood eQTL study5. We propose a framework for quantifying the extent of cell-type specificity or sharedness of eQTLs and show that about half of the effects are found in only one or a few cell types, and almost none are ubiquitous across all. We perform statistical colocalization between our eQTLs and 60 disease phenotypes and blood traits, identifying more than 60,000 colocalization events for more than 5,000 genes, providing potential molecular mechanisms in almost 900 genes associated with human phenotypic variation. We use a biologically driven deep learning method, scDeepID, to identify continuous cell states related to cell function and map more than 15,000 eQTLs that vary dynamically along those states. Finally, we identify 164 independent genetic variants that regulate the abundance of cell states.

Each cell was assigned to their donor of origin by means of demultiplexing and to an immune cell type using a combined approach of reference-based classification and hierarchical clustering as recommended by the single-cell eQTLGen Consortium28 (Methods). Each cell was assigned to one of 28 different cell types, spanning the key expected immune lineages (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 2).

In addition, we generated WGS data for all 1,925 individuals, which was processed and quality-controlled using established pipelines (Methods). We called 66,658,242 sites across single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions and deletions (indels), of which 53,784,018 (80.6%) were rare (MAF < 1%), roughly 4.5 times as many as from SNP array data (Methods and Supplementary Fig. 4).

To assess the effects of common genetic variation on gene expression changes, we mapped cis eQTLs, testing for the effects of common (MAF > 1%) variant sites within ±100 kilobases (kb) of the gene body on single-cell expression profiles (testing all sufficiently expressed genes; Methods). We used SAIGE-QTL27 to map eQTL effects using single-cell profiles modelled as Poisson counts, rather than relying on ‘pseudobulk’ aggregation. We mapped eQTLs separately in each of the 28 cell types and confirmed the type I error was controlled using permutations (Fig. 2a). At a false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 5%, we identified 154,932 eQTLs in at least one cell type from 17,674 unique eGenes (83% of all genes tested; Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 5). The number of eQTLs identified varied substantially across cell types, largely due to the number of cells available for each cell type and the resulting number of individuals tested affecting statistical power, as previously shown in refs. 16,27 (Fig. 2b, Extended Data Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2).

We performed fine mapping using SuSiE29 (Methods), showing an extra 24,925 (a 16% increase) non-primary (secondary, tertiary and so on)30,31 eQTL signals for 7,131 eGenes (40.4%). We used 95% credible sets to quantify the number of independent signals for each eGene, and explored differences between primary effects (variants from the credible set containing the top variant for each eGene) and non-primary effects (all other credible sets). We find that primary effects contain larger credible sets, and genes in the higher-proportion cell types have substantially more non-primary effects (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 6). As shown by others31, non-primary signals were found further from the transcription start site (TSS) and more likely in enhancer as opposed to promoter regions (Fig. 2c,d).

We compared the eGenes we identified with those from bulk blood eQTL studies. First, we considered the eQTLGen consortium study5: the largest eQTL study so far in whole blood. We identified a comparable number of eGenes compared to eQTLGen (17,674 and 16,987, respectively), despite the 15-fold smaller sample size (1,925 compared with more than 30,000 individuals; Extended Data Fig. 2a). More than one-quarter of our eGenes were not identified in eQTLGen (n = 4,572, 25.9%); those genes were slightly more lowly expressed, and their expression was more cell-type-specific (Extended Data Fig. 2b,c) compared with eGenes found by both studies; TenK10K-unique eGenes were also more likely to be eGenes in fewer cell types overall, in rare cell types (n < 60,000) compared with more abundant ones or in one cell type only (‘cell-type-specific’, Extended Data Fig. 2d–f), demonstrating the importance of mapping eQTLs at cellular resolution. Conversely, 23% of eQTLGen eGenes were not detected in our study: these were extremely lowly expressed across all 28 cell types (average expression 4% as opposed to roughly 30% for shared eGenes; Extended Data Fig. 2b).

An alternative approach to detect cell-type-specific eQTLs is bulk eQTL mapping on sorted cell types, which has proved to be well-powered even with modest sample sizes. We compared our eQTLs to four such studies (BLUEPRINT6 and CEDAR13, and refs. 10 and 12), matching cell types like for like. Overall, we demonstrate similar to much-increased power to identify cell-type-specific eQTLs, although we note the much larger sample size used in this study. Our study provides assessment of more granular cell types (n = 28) than many previous studies using sorted immune cell types (for example, 13 in ref. 10; Extended Data Fig. 3).

Compared with the OneK1K study16, the previously largest single-cell expression quantitative trait loci (sc-eQTL) map in blood, we identified nearly three times as many eGenes (17,674 compared with 6,469). This reflects several factors, including the increased power from both larger sample size (about twice the individuals: 1,925 compared with 982), number of cells per individual (2,825 compared with 1,291), the consequent more granular cell-type resolution (28 compared with 14 cell types) and the increased power due to modelling single-cell expression profiles rather than pseudobulk. The eGenes identified in our study, rather than OneK1K, were less highly expressed and more likely to be expressed in fewer cell types (in our data; Supplementary Fig. 7).

Finally, to quantify the improvement from cell-type resolution within our data, we compared the eQTLs identified separately in each cell type with a new set of eQTLs obtained from pseudobulk expression values aggregated across all 28 cell types (Methods). At FDR < 5%, this pseudobulk analysis identified 11,359 eGenes, similar to the number found in our most abundant cell type, the CD4+ central memory T (CD4TCM) cells, and nearly 30% fewer than when we consider all eGenes identified in at least one cell type (Extended Data Fig. 4).

We correlated our eQTL effect sizes with promoter-activity scores from PromoterAI32 for each gene’s lead variant across cell types, finding good agreement (median correlation 0.66; Supplementary Fig. 8 and Methods).

Most eQTL studies so far8,9,16,17 have focused on common genetic variants (MAF ≥ 1–5%). Studies investigating the effects of rare variants (MAF < 1%) on tissue-level gene expression7,33 have conducted ‘outlier analyses’, in which rare variants are sought specifically in individuals with extreme expression levels for a given gene rather than performing systematic population-level assessments.

Using SNPs and indels from WGS and SAIGE-QTL, we mapped the effects of rare variation (MAF < 1%) within a cis window (±100 kb) around each gene’s body, using gene-set-based tests for increased power (Methods). For each cell type, we tested for the effects of local rare variants on all sufficiently expressed genes (Methods) using two tests designed to capture different regulatory mechanisms: the burden test, and the sequence kernel association test (SKAT)34.

Overall, we observe 34,376 rare-variant signals across all cell types for 10,005 unique rare eGenes (47% of all genes tested) after FDR correction on the combined P values from the burden and SKAT tests (FDR < 5%; Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 9 and Methods). Type I error was well-controlled for the rare-variant tests (Fig. 2a), and the number of cells per cell type was highly correlated with the number of eGenes identified (correlation 0.98, Extended Data Fig. 1).

Most eGenes were identified by SKAT alone (29,260, 85%), followed by both SKAT and burden (4,975, 14.5%) and burden alone (141, 0.5%). This is consistent with SKAT being more appropriate when variants in the set have mixed direction of effects (that is, some increasing, some decreasing gene expression), which we expect in a regulatory context (Fig. 3a).

Rare eGenes were more ubiquitously expressed across all cell types, whereas common eGenes were expressed in a more cell-type-specific manner (Supplementary Fig. 10a), although this may be partly due to power (Supplementary Fig. 11). Single-variant test results show the expected relationship between allele frequency and effect sizes, with rarer variants showing larger effects on gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Both common and rare eGenes were enriched for putative promoter activity as estimated by PromoterAI32, with stronger-effect variants being enriched at rare allele frequencies (Supplementary Fig. 10c).

To further characterize our rare-variant signals, we overlapped them with functional annotations from public datasets to characterize genomic regions as putative enhancers, promoters, transcription factor binding sites, DNase sites and others, as well as splice-altering and promoter regions based on SpliceAI35 and PromoterAI32 scores, respectively. Finally, we included cell-type-matched open-chromatin regions using single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (scATAC-seq) for a subset of our samples26 (Methods, Extended Data Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Table 3).

We performed a second set of gene-level tests focusing only on variants in cell-type-matched open-chromatin regions or in regulatory regions (Methods). Despite reducing the number of variants tested by more than half, this analysis yielded more eGenes across cell types (1.2 times as many eGenes on average, Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 12). This was especially true when weighting variants based on their distance from the TSS of the gene27, probably reflecting the proximity of regulatory regions to the gene. By contrast, when up-weighting rarer variants using the beta(1,25) distribution, the all-cis variant test performed slightly better, possibly because very rare expression-affecting variants are more likely to be coding and thus excluded from our regulatory-focused annotations (Supplementary Fig. 13).

As an example, we identified significant downregulation of SH2D2A in CD4TCM cells (Cauchy combined P value = 1.48 × 10−2), driven by several rare variants in functional regions not previously reported, probably due to their low frequency (MAF < 0.005), highlighting the value of WGS (Fig. 3d). The top variant (1:156816831:A:AC, rs561132719) is a 5′ untranslated region indel overlapping an open-chromatin peak in T and natural killer (NK) cells from scATAC-seq data generated for a subset of our cohort (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 14 and Methods). The signal is stronger on conditioning on the top common variant for the same gene (conditional P = 2.6 × 10−4). SH2D2A encodes the T cell-specific adaptor protein that induces cytokine production in CD4+ T cells specifically and was linked to autoimmune conditions including hypothyroidism36.

As another example, PRDM4 expression was downregulated by many rare variants across T cell subpopulations, most significantly in CD4+ naive T cells (CD4Naive; P = 1.2 × 10−17). One variant (12:107695720:A:G, rs34017446) was previously reported (in whole blood, with consistent direction of effect5); we also report two new rare(r) variants here. We propose that one of these (12:107761146:T:G, rs71454708; MAF = 0.0099), in high linkage disequilibrium (LD) with 12:107695720:A:G, is probably causal, as it overlaps a CCCTC-binding factor binding site and likely promoter region (Methods), and was predicted to cause expression downregulation (PromoterAI score, −0.77; Extended Data Fig. 6a). PRDM4 encodes a transcription factor that mediates cell proliferation and has been linked to several cancers37.

In some cases, we identified rare variants that probably affect pre-mRNA splicing (n = 126 unique eGenes, with at least one rare variant with SpliceAI score of 0.5 or more, P ≤ 10−6). For 98 of the 126 genes (78%) the effect of the splicing variant on gene expression was detected in only one cell type. However, we observe that roughly 50% of all splicing events come from at least one of the five most abundant cell types (CD4TCM cells, CD4Naive cells, NK cells, CD8+ effector memory T (CD8TEM) cells and CD14Mono cells) suggesting that this is probably an effect of statistical power: we propose that given a better representation of less abundant cell types, we would observe splicing mechanisms to be mostly shared across cell types. We found an association between 15:79311418:G:A (rs141194056) and TMED3 across several cell types (most significantly in NK cells, P = 1.6 × 10−24). This variant (NM_007364.4:c.168+1G>A) is predicted by SpliceAI and AlphaGenome38 to abolish the canonical donor splice site in intron 1, with use of resulting in cryptic donor usage leading to premature termination and nonsense-mediated decay, consistent with the observed reduced expression of TMED3 (both in our data and in AlphaGenome-predicted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) tracks, Fig. 3e).

Furthermore, the synonymous variant 19:51367458:C:T (rs143501994), was associated with increased CLDND2 expression, with the strongest effects in NK and CD8TEM cells (P = 8 × 10−77 and P = 3 × 10−75 respectively). This variant lies at the penultimate base of exon 3 (NM_152353.3:c.429G>A, p.(Ala143=)). AlphaGenome predicts that it promotes exon 3–4 splicing, reducing intron retention and consequent non-stop decay leading to increased stable CLDND2 transcript abundance, consistent with the single-cell eQTL association direction (Extended Data Fig. 6b).

An important question with single-cell eQTL analysis is the degree to which loci show genetic effects in specific cell types; in other words, how ‘cell-type specific’ are they? For several reasons, this is complex to address directly: genes are variably expressed across cell types, leading to true eQTLs being missed for lowly expressed genes; differences in cell numbers lead to variable power to detect eQTLs across cell types making like-for-like comparison between cell types difficult and LD between loci means we cannot always infer whether alternative top eQTL variants for the same gene in different cells are tagging the same or different causal loci. Existing methods for simultaneously assessing cell-type specificity (such as mash39) are restricted to genes with expression across all conditions, thereby representing only a subset of all expressed genes (Supplementary Fig. 15).

Here we defined five possible scenarios for a given eQTL identified at FDR < 5% in one cell type (cell type A) in pairwise comparison with another cell type (cell type B; Fig. 4a). In scenario 1, the corresponding eGene of the eQTL is not sufficiently expressed (less than 1% cells) in cell type B (specific eGene). In scenario 2, the eGene is sufficiently expressed but no eQTL is identified in cell type B (specific eQTL). Scenario 3 represents a case in which two eQTL variants in low LD (r2 < 0.5) for the same eGene are identified in cell types A and B (distinct eQTL). Conversely, when the same eQTL or an eQTL in LD (r2 ≥ 0.5) for the same eGene are found in cell types A and B, it represents scenario 4 (shared eQTL, concordant effect) when the direction of allelic effect per extra minor allele on gene expression is concordant, or scenario 5 (shared eQTL, discordant effect) when the direction is discordant. To account for power differences when cell type B is rarer, we performed pairwise random-effect meta-analysis and reclassified eQTLs with potential association in cell type B (meta-analysis P < 10−5 × P value in cell type A) as scenario 4 or 5 depending on effect direction concordance (Methods).

We assigned a scenario to each pairwise comparison for all 154,932 common-variant eQTLs (Supplementary Table 4), then aggregated scenarios into a single score per eGene by taking the maximum scenario value across 27 contrasts (as illustrated in Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 16 and Methods).

In the overall eGene set aggregation, 419 of 17,674 (2.4%) eGenes had a maximum score of 1, representing specific expression in only one cell type. For example, EDN3 has an eQTL at 20:59249905:T:C (rs259962) and is expressed only in CD8-positive central memory T cells (CD8TCM; Fig. 4a,b). A higher percentage of eGenes (15%) were expressed in at most seven cell types (one-quarter of all cell types), and just more than half (9,723 out of 17,674, 55%) of all eGenes were sufficiently expressed in all 28 cell types (Supplementary Fig. 17). Among eGenes that are sufficiently expressed in two or more cell types, 1,733 (9.8% of all eGenes) had a maximum score of 2, indicating the presence of an eQTL in only 1 of 28 cell types. For example, rs3917929 (1:36478697) is an eQTL for CSF3R only in classical monocytes (CD14Mono; Fig. 4a,b). About half (8,506) of eGenes were significant in at most one-quarter of all cell types (7/28), with only 35 eGenes (0.2%) found to have a significant eQTL in all 28 cell types (Supplementary Fig. 18).

A maximum overall score of 3, representing the presence of at least two distinct eQTLs (LD r2 < 0.5) for the same eGene in several cell types, was observed for 2,530 (roughly 14%) eGenes. For example, CETP is upregulated by different variants in classical (16:57020104:G:T, rs2052880) and non-classical (16:56971389:C:T, rs1532625) monocytes (Fig. 4a,b). The remaining eGenes showed evidence of a shared eQTL (same variant or two different variants with LD r2 ≥ 0.5) in two or more cell types, in which 10,547 (roughly 60%) eGenes showed a concordant direction of allelic effect on gene expression and 2,445 (roughly 14%) showed discordance. For example, ISG15 was downregulated by 1:1013490:C:G (rs4615788) in both NK and CD8TEM cells, whereas FCGRT was upregulated by 19:49513222:T:G (rs111981233) in NK cells but downregulated in non-classical monocytes (CD16Mono; Fig. 4a,b). The proportion of eGenes across different cell-type specificity scenarios did not change notably when excluding the rarer cell types (Supplementary Fig. 19).

Similar to previous observations22, cell-type-specific eQTLs (scenarios 1 and 2) were generally found further from the gene, and more likely in enhancer regions, whereas shared eQTLs (scenarios 4 and 5) were more often in promoter regions (Fig. 4c). Distinct eQTLs (scenario 3) resembled cell-type-specific eQTLs more than shared ones according to these features. As expected, eQTL sharedness was grouped by cell lineage and broad cell-type categories (Extended Data Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 20). Sharedness within both lymphoid and myeloid lineages and main cell types was higher than outside the lineage and main cell type, both including and excluding rare cell types (number of cells fewer than 6,000; which are underpowered and skew the distributions; Supplementary Fig. 21).

An equivalent analysis on rare variants identified 2,560 cell-type-specific effects (26%), either cell-type-specific expression (n = 75, roughly 1%) or true cell-type-specific regulation (n = 2,485, 25%) and 7,445 shared (or probably shared; Methods) effects (74%; Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. 22, Methodsand Supplementary Table 5). Although rare-variant analysis is gene-based whereas common-variant analysis is variant-based, meaning results are not directly comparable, we observe a similar proportion (roughly 26%) of eGenes with cell-type-specific regulation in both analyses (scenarios 1–3 for common; scenarios 1 and 2 for rare).

We used colocalization analysis to identify the relationship between our cell-type-specific eQTLs and genetic risk loci for 16 diseases: asthma, autoimmune, cancer, infectious and neuroinflammatory conditions, and 44 blood counts and traits from the UK BioBank (UKBB)40 (Methods). We restricted the analysis to common eGenes (FDR < 5%) and genomic regions with at least one genome-wide significant GWAS variant (P < 5 × 10−8).

We found strong colocalization, indicating that the trait and cell-type-specific gene expression are driven by the same variant (posterior probability of a shared causal variant (PP H4) ≥ 0.8), for 5,287 genes in 60,458 gene–cell type–trait combinations (Fig. 5): 5,491 events (1,469 unique genes) for disease traits, 27,585 (3,572 genes) for blood serum traits and 27,382 (3,331 genes) for full blood counts (Supplementary Table 6). Different cell types contributed to different traits, for example B cells most contributed to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), CD4+ T cells to rheumatoid arthritis and dendritic cells to Alzheimer’s disease (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 23).

These colocalization events were remarkably cell-type specific: of 16,993 unique gene–trait pairs, 7,521 (44%) were uniquely found in one cell type, and 13,073 (77%) in at most four, compared with only 12% when considering all eGenes (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 24). Cell-type-specific eGenes (scenarios 1 and 2) had a substantially higher proportion of colocalization events than shared eGenes (scenarios 4 and 5; Supplementary Fig. 25). Out of 5,288 colocalizing genes, 893 (17%) were not previously reported as eGenes in eQTLGen and of those, 49% were specific to a single cell type (and 68% to at most 2 out of 28), highlighting the importance of cell-type-specific maps of genetic regulation for identifying new molecular mechanisms of disease variants, and identify the likely relevant cell types. The number of colocalizations of sc-eQTLs across traits was 5.2-fold larger than the equivalent analysis using our all-cell-type pseudobulk eQTLs (Extended Data Fig. 8).

We identified a new colocalization between OSM expression and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) specific to NK cells (PPH4 = 0.99). OSM encodes Oncostatin M, a cytokine contributing to many inflammatory diseases41, with a recently demonstrated role in NK cell-mediated IBD risk42. The top eQTL (22:30196498:C:G (rs713875)) leads to OSM overexpression in NK cells in individuals with the IBD risk allele, consistent with previously observed elevated OSM in patients with IBD43. Notably, OSM is expressed broadly (20 out of 28 cell types), and has eQTLs in 11 out of 28 cell types, yet only the NK-specific variants (downstream of the gene) colocalize with IBD (PPH4 for all other cell types less than 0.06): the OSM eQTL variants in other cell types are located several kilobase pairs away, upstream of the gene and not in LD (r2 = 0.0005 between top NK cell and top CD4TCM cell variant (22:30300755:C:A (rs9619096)): a scenario 3 eGene example in our cell-type specificity framework). Effect sizes were concordant between eQTL and GWAS signal, confirming that increased expression of OSM in NK cells corresponds to increased IBD risk (Fig. 5e–h).

We also identified a colocalization between Alzheimer’s disease and changes in IGHG4 expression specific to memory B cells (PPH4 = 0.99; lead eQTL variant: 14:105721893:C:T (rs1134590)). The IGHG4 gene encodes the immunoglobulin heavy constant gamma 4 (IGHG4); it is expressed in a subset of Alzheimer’s disease-linked memory B cells: a possible mechanism is that depleted memory B cells may initiate amyloid-β cascade-related neuropathological events leading to Alzheimer’s disease44. In our data, IGHG4 is expressed in Bintermediate, Bmemory and plasmablast cells—consistent with the observation that class-switch recombination from IgM or IgD to IgG, IgA or IgE only happens after B cell activation45—and the colocalization with Alzheimer’s disease stronger in Bmemory compared with Bintermediate (PPH4 = 0.69). Higher IGHG4 expression corresponds to higher Alzheimer’s disease risk (Supplementary Fig. 26).

TNFSF15 eQTLs colocalized with IBD in dendritic cells, specifically plasmacytoid dendritic cells (PPH4 = 0.99; lead eQTL variant: 9:114796423:C:T (rs6478108)), cDC2 (PPH4 = 0.98) and ASDC (PPH4 = 0.95). TNFSF15 encodes the tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-like cytokine 1A (TL1A), a key regulator of inflammation and the only known ligand of the death-domain receptor 3 (encoded by TNFRSF25). A cis-eQTL for TNFRSF25 (rs2986751; identified by GTEx and recapitulated by us) was also recently implicated in IBD risk46. TL1A inhibition is an active area of drug development, with the anti-TL1A monoclonal antibody tulisokibart showing efficacy in phase II ulcerative colitis trials47. In our data, TNFSF15 is uniquely expressed in dendritic cell types, with lower TNFSF15 expression associated with higher IBD risk, consistent with previous reports in monocytes48 (Supplementary Fig. 27).

Other examples of new colocalization events identified were between the expression of the PYCARD gene in CD8TEM (lead variant: 16:31258718:A:ACACAC (rs58732039)) and both SLE (PPH4 = 0.91) and IgAN (PPH4 = 0.9); and a colocalization between a non-classical monocyte (CD16Mono)-specific eQTL (lead variant: 5:149822705:C:A (rs1078324)) for PPARGC1B and SLE (PPH4 = 0.94; Supplementary Fig. 28).

We aimed to identify eQTL effects modulated by continuous cellular states beyond discrete cell types. A key challenge for such analyses is the definition of cell states themselves: existing approaches typically either use unsupervised identification of axes of variability (for example, using principal components or related methods17,49), which are difficult to interpret, or test one or a few well-defined variables, such as disease state50 or sex and age51, missing new contexts yet to be characterized. Here we used an in-house deep learning method, scDeepID, a multi-task transformer capable of cell-type annotation, cell function identification and cell simulation, to define biologically informed yet general continuous cell states, and then mapped eQTLs modulated by those states (Methods).

We took NK cells as an example, given their recently characterized subcell type heterogeneity52. Using scDeepID label transfer from their integrated NK cell single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data52, we identified three subtypes within our macro-NK cells population: NKadaptive (most involved in cell activation), NKmature (most cytotoxic) and NKintermediate (showing signatures of both NKmature and NKCD56bright subtypes), with good concordance to the original publication based on key markers expression (Supplementary Fig. 28). Together with the NKCD56bright and NKProliferating cells, this gives us five NK cell subtypes (Extended Data Fig. 9a). We then trained scDeepID with gene ontology biological process functions to identify transient and functionally meaningful states within and across subtypes. The terms that were identified as key in NK cells were: cell killing, leukocyte cell–cell adhesion, myeloid leukocyte differentiation, regulation of cytoskeleton organization and canonical NF-κB signal transduction (Extended Data Fig. 9b and Methods).

To identify genetic regulation of gene expression mediated by these cell states, we mapped dynamic eQTLs using a linear mixed model with an interaction term between genotype and the above cell function scores (Methods). We confirmed controlled type I error using permutations (Supplementary Fig. 30). Considering all NK cells (n = 850,833) and all gene–SNP pairs with eQTL effects (FDR < 5%) in any NK subtype (16,759 unique gene–SNP pairs; Methods), we identified 10,923 cell-state NK eQTLs (for 6,360 unique gene–SNP pairs, 38% Supplementary Fig. 31), across the five cell functions, with cell killing, leukocyte cell–cell adhesion and myeloid leukocyte differentiation having the most dynamic eQTLs (Extended Data Fig. 9c).

For example, an eQTL for S100B (21:46540580:T:C, rs9306156) is modulated by the positive regulation of the canonical NF-κB signal transduction function, with higher function correlating with increased expression for C allele carriers, but not T/T homozygotes (Extended Data Fig. 9d). This variant was previously identified as an NK cell eQTL for S100B in OneK1K (ref. 16), is associated with lymphocyte counts changes53 and lies in an NK cell-specific enhancer54. S100B signals through the RAGE receptor to influence inflammation, suggesting that C allele carriers at this locus might mount stronger inflammatory responses by means of enhanced NF-κB/S100B feed-forward signalling through a cell-type-specific enhancer mechanism.

Another example is a dynamic eQTL for MYOM2, associated with regulation of cytoskeleton organization (8:2125316:G:A, rs62478456). MYOM2 expression is highest in low-function cells and declines as function increases in A/A carriers, whereas it remains low and stable in G/G individuals (Extended Data Fig. 9d). MYOM2 encodes Myomesin-2, a structural protein expressed in NK cells, which integrates with the cytoskeleton to support the mechanical rearrangements necessary for NK cell activation, immune synapse formation, and target cell killing55. The cell-state-specific dynamic suggests that in A carriers, higher MYOM2 levels in low-function cells may prime NK cells for rapid cytoskeletal remodelling on activation.

A further example is an eQTL for RPS26 (12:56042145:C:G, rs1131017), which is modulated in strength by the cell killing function, with the gene regulatory effect of allele G increasing RPS26 expression levels being strongest in more cytotoxic cells (Supplementary Fig. 32).

We sought to identify genetic variants that alter cell-state composition directly by applying genotype–neighbourhood associations (GeNA56) to test for cell-state abundance QTLs (csaQTLs). We mapped csaQTLs in eight main cell-type categories (B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, other T cells, NK cells, monocytes, dendritic cells and haematopoietic progenitor and stem cells (HSPCs), Supplementary Fig. 33). We confirmed the type I error was controlled using permutations after filtering to common genetic variants at MAF > 5% (as we found some inflation for variants between 1% and 5% MAF; Supplementary Figs. 34 and 35 and Methods).

In total, we identified 164 independent csaQTLs passing genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8), with the highest numbers discovered in monocytes, dendritic cells and NK cells (Fig. 6a). The discordance between csaQTL discovery power and cell-type abundance may reflect differential genetic and environmental contribution to cell-state abundance across immune subpopulations; NK, dendritic and monocyte populations have been previously described as showing high heritability for cell-state abundance traits compared with other PBMC subsets57. We replicated four out of five total csaQTLs previously identified in the OneK1K dataset56 (Extended Data Fig. 10): three csaQTL variants were significant in both analyses, and one locus significant in the OneK1K analysis was suggestive in our analysis (P = 7.1 × 10−8), in moderate LD with a lead variant (r2 = 0.46) showing a matching association with depletion of NKCD56bright cells (Supplementary Table 7).

We discovered new csaQTLs associated with abundance shifts in both discrete cell types, and continuous cell states. One example is the monocyte csaQTL 12:69350234:C:A (P = 1.2 × 10−17; Fig. 6b–e), previously associated with monocyte counts from ref. 58, and which we confirmed to share a causal signal through colocalization (PPH4 > 0.99; Methods). This variant is a missense change in LYZ (T88N), overlaps an sc-eQTL for LYZ in both CD14+ and CD16+ monocytes, and also colocalizes with an LYZ eQTL in heat-shock protein cells (Supplementary Fig. 36a). LYZ encodes lysozyme, an antimicrobial effector secreted by phagocytes, with myeloid-specific expression (Supplementary Fig. 36b). Gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on the top genes correlated with the associated cell state revealed enrichment for inflammatory response (Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P = 9.03 × 10−8) and TNF signalling by means of NF-κB (Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P = 7.87 × 10−8), suggesting that this csaQTL modulates the abundance of a pro-inflammatory monocyte or macrophage state (Supplementary Fig. 37).

We also identified a monocyte csaQTL 12:9953308:T:TG (P = 7.0 × 10−14, Extended Data Fig. 11a,b) that intersected a CLEC12A eQTL in CD16 monocytes Extended Data Fig. 11c). CLEC12A encodes a C-type lectin that inhibits monocyte and granulocyte activation59 and may regulate inflammation in response to cell death60. The associated cell state similarly showed enrichment for inflammatory response, TNF signalling by means of NF-κB, and IL2 and IL6 signalling (Supplementary Fig. 37).

In this paper and accompanying papers, we characterize the effects of genetic variation on gene expression and chromatin accessibility at cellular resolution in human immune cells from nearly 2,000 individuals. In this flagship paper, we mapped common and rare-variant effects across 28 immune cell types, identifying common-variant eQTLs for 83% of the genes tested and rare-variant signals for nearly 50%. For our most abundant cell types, the relationship between cell number and common eGene discovery plateaus at roughly 500,000 cells, suggesting this is the number required to begin saturating common-variant effects within a cell type. For rare variants, the relationships remain linear, indicating that further increases in sample size and more sophisticated variant selection will continue to yield further discoveries (Extended Data Fig. 1).

We identify nearly three times more eGenes than OneK1K16, the previously largest single-cell eQTL study, owing to several factors: larger sample size, more cells per individual, more granular resolution of cell types and improved single-cell-specific association methods. When comparing to bulk eQTLs from sorted immune cell types (for example, BLUEPRINT6), these studies seem to be similarly powered despite the smaller sample size for well-defined cell-type populations (for example, CD14+ monocytes) but much less so for more heterogeneous cell types (such as CD4+ T cells, subdivided into six subpopulations in our data); moreover, such studies are limited to known cell types with robust cell surface markers and, even then, are labour intensive. Finally, we identified almost exactly the same number of unique eGenes as the eQTLGen program5, the largest eQTL map using whole-blood ‘bulk’ transcriptomics from more than 30,000 people, despite a 15-fold lower number of individuals. We could not detect about 25% of eQTLGen eGenes: these were very lowly expressed in all of our cell types; by contrast, roughly 25% of TenK10K eGenes were not in eQTLGen: these were more cell-type specific, emphasizing the value of single-cell approaches for identifying cell-type-specific molecular variation that is difficult or impossible to observe in bulk transcriptome methods.

We propose a framework to quantify eQTL cell-type specificity that, unlike existing approaches, is not limited to genes tested across all cell types. About 50% of eQTLs were specific to one or a subset of cell types, and very few were ubiquitous, even after accounting for power differences. Cell-type-specific eGenes were substantially more likely to colocalize with disease traits (eightfold increase between scenarios 2 and 4, Supplementary Fig. 25), and the single-cell approach yielded on average 2.6 times as many colocalization events as the all-cell-type pseudobulk, suggesting that eQTL maps in increasingly granular and harder to access cell types and states may be key to bridging the gap between eQTL and GWAS variants61. This framework has limitations: the threshold used to define cell-type specificity is data driven (Supplementary Fig. 38) and conservative, but no independent dataset of comparable or larger sample size was available to evaluate the winner’s curse bias, and the heuristic LD r2 = 0.5 cut-off for defining shared versus distinct signals may not be appropriate for all applications.

An important advancement over existing single-cell eQTL studies was our ability to map rare-variant effects using WGS and gene-level tests. SKAT was substantially better powered than burden, probably reflecting mixed-effect directions of cis-regulatory variants. Restricting to functionally annotated improved detection despite reducing the variant set by half, although more cell-type-specific annotations and larger sample sizes will be crucial for further progress. Although we have scATAC-seq from matched cell types26, we are at present underpowered for a straightforward analysis restricted to cell-type-specific open-chromatin regions alone.

Using a biologically driven deep learning method (scDeepID), we defined cellular states corresponding to specific functions in NK cells and identified more than 15,000 NK cell-state eQTLs. Such approaches are promising for identifying dynamic eQTLs across biologically relevant contexts, which are probably widespread11; however, scalability of existing complex interaction models remains a limitation. In addition to exploring the impact of genetic variation on gene expression within defined cell states, we mapped the effects of genetic variants on the composition of cell states within each main cell type, which have been associated with disease risk58,62. Using a recently proposed method56, we identify 164 csaQTLs across eight main cell types, with most detected in monocytes, dendritic cells and NK cells, consistent with higher estimated heritability in these cell populations56. Limited overlap with cell count GWAS40,58 probably reflects both statistical power and biological differences: most csaQTLs affect granular cell states rather than the broad categories measured in existing GWAS.

In accompanying TenK10K papers, we investigate the impact of tandem repeat variation on gene expression24, the putative causal relationships between molecular QTLs and human phenotypic associations25, and the impact of genetic variation on cell-type-specific chromatin accessibility26. Together, these publications demonstrate the power of population-scale cellular genomics3 and the importance of sequencing-based approaches, ideally WGS, for accessing low-frequency variants and complex structural variation. Population cellular genomic datasets complement high-throughput experimental perturbation approaches, and further expansions in sample size, ancestry diversity, disease state and tissue types will be critical for building a comprehensive understanding of the molecular origins of human disease.

Blood samples were collected from 2,339 consenting individuals from two Australian biomedical research cohorts: the Tasmanian Ophthalmic Biobank (TOB) (ethics protocol 2020/ETH02479) and the BioHEART study (ethics protocol 2019/ETH08376) cohort.

The TOB cohort consisted of 983 individuals (562 female, 421 male) recruited between January and February 2018 by the Royal Hobart Hospital and Menzies Institute of Medical Research in Tasmania, Australia. The selection criteria for inclusion in the cohort were as follows: (1) age 18 years and over, (2) no signs of ocular disease and (3) self-reported British, Scottish or Irish ancestry.

The BioHEART63 cohort included 1,356 individuals (561 female and 795 male) recruited between 29 March 2016 and 2 May 2023, presenting to study sites for clinically indicated cardiovascular imaging or acutely with ST elevation myocardial infarction. The selection criteria for inclusion in the cohort were as follows: (1) age 18 years and over and (2) presenting with cardiac disorders (or for the investigation of suspected cardiac disorders) including: acute myocardial infarction, suspected coronary artery disease, patients undergoing cardiothoracic or vascular surgery, or plastic surgery involving resection of vascularized tissue.

After donor, variant and cell quality control, including selection to individuals of European ancestry only, 1,925 individuals remained: 950 from TOB and 975 from BioHEART (Supplementary Fig. 1).

This study complied with all relevant ethical regulations and was overseen by the Human Research Ethics Committees at the Royal Children’s Hospital (Melbourne, Australia), Northern Sydney Local Health District (Sydney, Australia), St. Vincent’s Hospital (Sydney, Australia) and University of Tasmania (Hobart, Australia). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

For the TOB16 cohort, blood samples were collected from each participant and stored in 1× 8-ml heparin cell preparation tubes and 1× 9-ml EDTA tubes. For the BioHEART cohort, aliquots were stored at −80 °C.

DNA extraction from BioHEART buffy coat samples was performed by the Australian Genome Research Facility using QIAamp96 DNA QIAcube HT kit. DNA extraction from TOB samples was performed at the Menzies Institute of Medical Research (University of Tasmania) using the QIAmp Blood Mini Kit. For a subset of TOB samples that failed initial quality control checks during sequencing, DNA was extracted from PBMCs a second time using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit and eluted in an AE buffer by the Garvan Molecular Genetics laboratory service. DNA concentration was measured for all samples using fluorometric quantitation with the Quantifluor from Promega before sequencing.

WGS data were generated for all individuals from the TOB cohort (n = 983) and BioHEART (n = 1,356).

Sequencing library preparation was performed using the KAPA PCR-free v.2.1 kit (Roche) for the TOB samples, and the DNA PCR-free kit (Illumina) for the BioHEART samples. Short-read WGS was performed using Illumina 2 × 150 bp chemistry on the NovaSeq 6000 instrument to achieve mean 30× coverage. Library preparation and sequencing were performed by the Garvan Sequencing Platform.

Alignment and variant calling were performed in line with GATK-DRAGEN Best Practices. Reads were aligned from FASTQ (BioHEART) or CRAMs (TOB) against the GRCh38 reference genome (masked version, as available at https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us/articles/360035890811-Resource-bundle) using a fork of DRAGMAP v.1.3.1 (https://github.com/populationgenomics/DRAGMAP). When aligning from CRAMs, bazam (https://github.com/ssadedin/bazam) was used to first convert the reads back to FASTQ format. Calling of SNPs and indels was then performed using GATK4 HaplotypeCaller in DRAGEN mode. The resulting gVCF files were combined using Hail’s VDS Combiner and stored in the variant dataset (VDS) format.

Hard sample filtering was applied to remove individuals with: high rates of contamination (FREEMIX ≥ 5%); an increased percentage of chimeras (PCT_CHIMERAS ≥ 6.3%); median insert size less than 250 bp and sex aneuploidy or discordance between reported and observed sex. The dataset was also inspected for outlier samples based on the number of singletons, heterozygous /homozygous variant ratio, transition/transversion ratio and insertion/deletion ratio, taking care not to exclude ancestry outliers on the basis of these metrics.

A set of high-quality sites, defined as an LD-pruned subset of autosomal, biallelic, allele frequency greater than 0.01, call rate greater than 99% and InbreedingCoeff greater than −0.25, were extracted from 1,000 Genomes and the Human Genome Diversity Project. Samples with call rate less than 0.85 across these sites were also excluded.

Following hard sample filtering, related individuals were identified using Hail’s implementation of PC-Relate and confirmed using somalier relate v.0.2.15 (https://github.com/brentp/somalier). For family sets consisting of second-degree or closer relatives, as defined by a kinship coefficient greater than 0.088, the sample with the highest quality scRNA-seq data was retained, as determined by the number of cells and cell-type composition. When equal quality single-cell data were available, the sample with the highest mean genome sequencing depth on chromosome 20 was retained.

Ancestry inference was then performed on the high-quality, unrelated set of samples, by combining this set with background samples from 1,000 Genomes and the Human Genome Diversity Project, then computing principal components using gnomAD’s run_pca_with_relateds method and the same set of high-quality sites defined for call rate estimation above. Ancestry labels were assigned on the basis of genetic similarity, as identified through principal component analysis, to those of known ancestry in the background datasets. For association testing, only samples assigned to the European cluster were retained (Supplementary Fig. 39).

Allele-specific Variant Quality Score Recalibration or AS-VQSR (GATK v.4.2.6.1) was run using all quality control-pass, unrelated samples (regardless of ancestry), using the default training resources and priors. For SNPs this is hapmap (prior, 15), omni (prior, 12), 1000 G (prior, 10) and dbsnp (prior, 7). For indels this is mills (prior, 12), axiomPoly (prior, 10) and dbsnp (prior, 2). The following features were included for all SNPs and indels: AS_ReadPosRankSum, AS_MQRankSum, AS_QD, AS_FS, AS_SOR. AS_MQ was used for SNPs only. All sites failing on the site-level filter calculated by AS-VQSR were removed.

Within the European-only association callset, we then removed any variants that had no high-quality genotype calls (defined as genoype quality (GQ) ≥ 20, depth of coverage (DP) ≥ 10 and allele balance greater than 0.2 for heterozygotes) or with an excess of heterozygotes at that site compared with Hardy–Weinberg expectations (InbreedingCoeff < −0.3). A final filter to remove sites with missingness greater than 15% was then applied before association testing.

scRNA-seq data were collected from PBMCs from samples from individuals from the TOB (OneK1K16; n = 1,036) and BioHEART63 (n = 1,012) cohorts. Supplementary Fig. 40 illustrates the experimental workflow and key steps.

TOB samples were previously pooled during preparation of the OneK1K project, with pools made up of 1,214 individuals16. To capture more cells per individual (aiming for up to 5,000 cells per individual), two pools (n = 24–28) at a time were further pooled (weighted by cell viability) and loaded into three sequencing lanes (aiming for 40,000 cells each, so 120,000 in total). In addition, ‘maxi pools’ of four combined pools (96–112 individuals) were sequenced in two separate lanes to compensate for possible low cell numbers.

By contrast, BioHEART samples were pooled (weighted by cell viability) for this project; each pool comprised 8 individuals and was sequenced in a separate sequencing lane. We aim to include 40,000 cells (roughly 5,000 cells per individual) per pool. In some cases, pools with low cell viability were sequenced again to rescue cell numbers and get closer to the target.

Cells were captured using the single-cell 3′ gene expression high-throughput (3′ GEX HT) kit v.3.1 from 10X on a Chromium X machine. For the TOB cohort, cells were recaptured starting with the original biomaterial (OneK1K individuals) that we had biobanked and using the new 10X chemistry with the new high-throughput kit. The long-term cryopreservation of these samples led to a smaller proportion of monocytes in the TOB cohort compared with BioHEART (Supplementary Note 1). Library preparation and short-read sequencing were performed using Illumina’s NovaSeq 6000 machine.

scRNA-seq reads were aligned to a genome reference (GRCh38, Gencode release 44, Ensembl 110) using STAR64, as implemented within 10X’s CellRanger software (v.7.2.0) that also estimates the number of cells captured (Supplementary Fig. 41). CellBender65 (v.0.3.0) estimated numbers of cells differed substantially from those estimated by CellRanger, leading us to discard these results (Supplementary Note 2).

To assign cells to their donor of origin, as well as to remove doublets (droplets containing more than one cell), we used Demuxafy66 (v.2.1.0), a tool to runs a number of demultiplexing and double detection software and outputs a consensus call for each individual cell. Using Demuxafy, we ran vireo67 to demultiplex cells to their donor of origin within each sequencing library, using genotypes extracted from either SNP array data (available as part of the OneK1K data; for TOB) or using common (MAF > 5%) biallelic SNPs from WGS (for BioHEART). A handful of individuals did not have available genotype data, but vireo allows us to include the expected number of individuals in each pool with or without genotypes, which makes the donor assignment and doublet detection more accurate. For doublet detection, we used Demuxafy to run scds68 and ScDblFinder69 and used majority voting between all three tools (vireo, scds and scDblFinder) to determine the final set of doublets for each sequencing library (Supplementary Fig. 42).

To assign individual cells to specific immune cell types we follow the recommendations of the single-cell eQTLGen consortium28. We adopt a combined approach that uses scPred70, hierarchical progressive learning, scHPL71 and Azimuth as the reference dataset72 as detailed in https://powellgenomicslab.github.io/WG2-pipeline-classification-docs/index.html. To run this step, we converted our single-cell Scanpy object into Seurat73 objects. We also used CellTypist74,75 (v.1.6.2, model used ‘Immune_All_High’) to confirm cell typing of individual cells, with good concordance (Supplementary Fig. 43). As the former approach provided more granularity (28 cell types versus 18 identified by CellTypist), we proceeded with the 28 cell types for the remainder of the article.

Next, we performed quality control with Scanpy (v.1.8.2)76. For each library, we removed cells with more than 20% mitochondrial reads, fewer than 800 reads detected and fewer than 1,000 or more than 10,000 genes detected (Supplementary Fig. 44). We also removed individuals with abnormal cell-type composition and individuals with fewer than 100 cells.

Overall, after cell and sample quality control (based on scRNA-seq and WGS), a total of 5,438,679 cells from 1,925 individuals were left for consequent analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1).

To test for the effects of sets of both common and rare-variant associations with single-cell gene expression, we used the recently proposed SAIGE-QTL method27. In brief, SAIGE-QTL implements a Poisson mixed model, thus effectively modelling single-cell counts and repeat cells for the same individual. Furthermore, several computational and statistical speed-ups are implemented to improve speed, scalability and storage usage. Finally, implementation of both single-variant tests (in which effects of the genotype at a single locus are assessed), as well as set-based tests (in which signals from several variants within a region are assessed together in their combined effect), is available, as briefly described below.

We mapped eQTLs separately in each of the 28 cell types. We confirmed that our tests were calibrated by shuffling the individual IDs and reperforming the test for our most abundant cell type, CD4TCM cells, the results of which show no evidence of false positives for both common and rare-variant tests (Fig. 2a).

For every gene and cell type, we fit a single null model using SAIGE-QTL, the same for both subsequent tests (common and rare-variant tests alike). Briefly, we fit a Poisson model using the raw counts from the single-cell expression data as the outcome variable (phenotype), and the following as covariates: sex and age of the individuals, seven genotype principal components, five cell-type-specific expression principal components, cohort status (TOB or BioHEART), percentage of counts from mitochondrial genes and total counts. We provide all extra parameters and flags as part of the available code in GitHub. We only test genes that are expressed in at least 1% of cells in each cell type. For a subset of gene–cell-type combinations the model failed to converge (0.27%) and these were excluded from the analysis (Supplementary Note 3).

We test for the effects of common genetic variants on single-cell expression using the single-variant test as implemented in SAIGE-QTL. We consider all SNPs and indels with MAF ≥ 1% and missingness less than 15% in our sample. We test for cis (variants within a ±100-kb window around the gene body) effects only. We apply a two-step multiple testing correction as recommended in the SAIGE-QTL paper27, using the Aggregated Cauchy Association Test77 at gene level and Storey’s q value method (qvalue package in R) across genes. At FDR < 5%, we identify a total of 154,932 eQTLs in at least 1 of the 28 cell types, from 17,674 unique genes (83% of all 21,404 genes tested).

The number of eQTLs identified varied substantially across cell types, ranging from 11,491 eQTLs in CD4TCM cells (85% of all genes tested in this cell type) to 449 eQTLs in innate lymphoid cells (3%) as shown in Fig. 2b. This was probably explained by differences in the statistical power across cell types, largely due to the number of cells available for each cell type (correlation between log10 number of cells and number of eGenes detected, 0.98; Extended Data Fig. 1).

The same analysis was run again for all cell types (common variants only) using a ±1-Mb cis window around the gene instead. These results are not used for any of the analyses described in the main paper (except Supplementary Fig. 45), but we made the summary statistics available, see ‘Data availability’ for details.

We test for the effects of rare variants on single-cell expression using the set-based test as implemented in SAIGE-QTL. We consider all SNPs and indels with MAF < 1% and missingness less than 15%. We test for the combined effects of all variants within a cis region around the gene (±100 kb around the gene body). As described in the SAIGE-QTL publication, we consider a burden test and the SKAT test and use the Cauchy method to combine P values at the gene level. Similar to the approach used for the common-variant results above, we use the q value method and report significant results at FDR < 5%.

Overall, we observe 34,376 rare-variant signals across all cell types, for 10,005 unique genes after FDR correction using Storey’s q value on the Cauchy combined P values from the two tests (the burden test and SKAT). The percentage of genes with a rare-variant combined signal out of all gene tested ranged from 0.09% (innate lymphoid cells) to 39% (CD4TCM cells; Fig. 2b). Once again, the tests were well calibrated when shuffling individual IDs (Fig. 2a) and the number of cells for each cell type was highly correlated with the number of eGenes identified (Extended Data Fig. 1). We discarded results from the Aggregated Cauchy Association Test-V test because we found them to be not calibrated in this setting (Supplementary Note 4).

An equivalent analysis was performed after restricting the association to only (cis rare) variants that were functionally annotated (details below).

Single-cell ATAC-seq data were obtained from a subset of the TOB cohort that includes 30,829 nuclei from 8 individuals26. Sequencing reads were aligned to the Human Genome Reference GRCh38-1.2.0 (gencode v.44) using Cell Ranger ATAC (v.2.1.0) and the chromatin peaks were called using the built-in MACS2 (ref. 78). Cell-type labels were annotated based on a CITE-seq reference72 with a combined Multiome bridging dataset26, consistent with the 28 PBMC cell types annotated for scRNA-seq data. After quality control, 17,099 nuclei were retained to annotate the chromatin peaks in each cell type for the eQTL signals.

To annotate all rare variants before association testing (in the run using only variants in likely-to-be-functional regions), we used the TenK10K scATAC-seq data to annotate variants found to be in open-chromatin regions for the relevant cell type. We first obtained the open-chromatin peak list for each of 26 cell types (2 removed due to super rarity; innate lymphoid cells; n = 33 and CD8Proliferating; n = 4) from ref. 26. For each cell type, we re-called peaks from the raw fragment files using the MACS3 tool (v.3.0.3) integrated into SnapATAC2 (v.2.8.0). The number of peaks identified (q < 0.05) per cell type ranged from 43,014 in CD4Proliferating to 269,371 in CD16Mono, with a median of 181,702. Then we set up a searching function to check whether the rare variant is located within any peaks in the corresponding cell type.

We used publicly accessible resources to annotate our variants, including ENCODE79,80,81 (v.3) ciscandidate regulatory elements (cCREs), University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) genome annotation database82, Activity-by-Contact (ABC)83,84 scores, FANTOM585 enhancer annotations, functional assay and conservation or constraint results from a deep literature search, chromHMM86 predictions and enhancers from the VISTA Enhancer Database87 with positive signal. Any annotations not from genome build GRCh38 were converted using the UCSC LiftOver tool. Any LiftOver segments mapping to non-canonical chromosomes and/or fragmented less than 100 bp in GRCh38 were dropped. All overlapping intervals within categories were merged using bedtools merge88. Detailed annotation information and references are summarized in Supplementary Table 3.

PromoterAI is a convolutional neural network that uses roughly 20 kb of sequence context around a promoter variant to predict its effect on gene expression. It is pretrained on histone modifications, DNA accessibility, transcription factor occupancy and strand-specific CAGE value around TSS at base-pair resolution and fine-tuned with a curated set of rare non-coding promoter variants associated with unusually high or low expression in GTEx v.8. PromoterAI scores range from −1 to 1, with negative values indicating a predicted under-expression effect and positive values indicating a predicted overexpression effect. More details on PromoterAI can be found in the publication32 and common variant lead eQTLs are annotated with their PromoterAI score (when applicable) in Supplementary Table 4.

SpliceAI35 was run on all coding tested variants (common and rare) and the scores are available in Zenodo89. AlphaGenome38 and Pangolin90 were also used to analyse the possible mis-splicing outcomes of two variants.

We used an aggregated ‘functional and open’ flag to label any variant sitting in a region considered to be in any of a promoter, enhancer or actively transcribed region, in a transcription factor binding site (Supplementary Table 3), predicted to be splicing-altering or found to reside in an open-chromatin region in the relevant cell type based on our own scATAC-seq (details above).

We ran SuSiE29 (R package ‘susieR’, v.0.14.2) for fine mapping. Specifically, we used the ‘susie_rss’ function using L = 10, Z scores from the SAIGE-QTL summary statistics, effective N (number of individuals tested, which varied across cell types, Fig. 2b) and correlation matrices we calculated for all-cis variants for each gene (noting that SAIGE-QTL using a Poisson model violates SuSiE’s linear assumption, Supplementary Note 5). We considered each credible set as an ‘independent signal’, and we defined as a ‘primary’ signal the credible set containing the variant with the smallest P value, which is the same as the main eQTL for each eGene reported throughout the rest of the article. For genes with more than one independent signal, all other credible sets were defined as ‘non-primary’. The credible set size in Fig. 2c refers to the number of variants contained in each credible set. For the comparison between primary and non-primary signals (Supplementary Fig. 6) all variants with PIP > 0.9 in either the primary credible set or any of the non-primary credible sets for every gene were considered in terms of both their distance from the TSS and their functional annotations.

We aggregated expression values by taking the mean value at the level of gene and individual for each cell type. We then used each cell-type pseudobulk as a biological replicate and ran SAIGE-QTL (Poisson) using all the same parameters as for the main results presented in the article. In the comparisons to the single-cell cell-type-specific analyses (Extended Data Figs. 4 and 8b), only genes tested in the pseudobulk analysis as well were retained (n = 21,192).

To quantify cell-type specificity, for each of the 154,932 identified eQTLs, we first performed pairwise comparisons of association estimates in the primary cell type and in each of the other 27 cell types. We classified these pairwise comparisons into five scenarios based on eQTL association, eGene expression and direction of allelic effect on gene expression in the two cell types: (1) eGene is expressed only in the primary cell type, (2) eGene is expressed in both cell types, but eQTL identified only in the primary cell type, (3) independent eQTL variants (LD r2 < 0.5) for the same eGene are identified in each cell type, (4) same eQTL variants or different variants in LD r2 ≥ 0.5) for the same eGene are identified in each cell type with concordant direction of effect and (5) same eQTL variants or different variants in LD r2 ≥ 0.5) for the same eGene are identified in each cell type with discordant direction of effect. These scenarios are summarized in Supplementary Table 8.

Further, we used a pairwise meta-analysis approach for all eQTLs that are initially classified as in scenario 2 to take into account potential type 2 error due to insufficient statistical power for association analysis in rarer cell types. This was done for each significant eQTL (FDR < 5%), by comparing its effect size in the primary cell type to its effect sizes in each of the other cell types. Meta-analysis was performed using a random-effects model with the metafor package in R91 (v.4.0.0). We compared the resulting meta-analysis P value to the primary Pn value, denoting an empirically chosen threshold of 105-fold reduction (Supplementary Fig. 38) as evidence of subdiscovery eQTL signal in cell type 2, in which case the eQTL in question would be reclassified as scenario 4 or 5 depending on concordance of allelic effect direction.

Next, we calculated an aggregate score for each of the 17,674 common-variant eGenes by taking the maximum value of the scenarios above across all pairs of cell types for each eGene (results presented in Fig. 4a, bar plot on the right). Supplementary Table 1 describes interpretation of the scenario or aggregated values in each of the comparisons.

Given that correlation of effect estimates are proportional to LD r2 between variants, we noted that variants in lower LD are more likely to show a discordant allelic effect direction and therefore may result in overestimation of aggregated count for scenario 5. We repeated this approach using the gene-level rare-variant association results. For each gene in a given cell type identified at FDR < 5% based on the Cauchy-aggregation P value (from burden test and the SKAT test), we compared the estimates for the same gene against those derived from each of the other 27 cell types. Similar to the common-variant results above, each pairwise comparison was initially classified into one of the following scenarios: (1) a gene was expressed in the primary cell type only, (2) a gene was expressed in both cell types, but the rare-variant association signal was identified only in cell type 1 and (3) the rare-variant association signal was identified in both cell types. To account for potential misclassification of scenario 2 genes in which a rare-variant association signal was absent due to lack of statistical power in the association test in the secondary cell type, we meta-analysed effect estimates from burden test results of both cell types using a random-effects model. Genes with a meta-analysis P value 105-fold smaller than the original meta-analysis were reclassified as probably shared eGenes. Unlike the common-variant results, we did not perform a comparison of direction of effect sizes given that the discovery was done using unsigned P values from Cauchy aggregation of burden and SKAT tests.

We use the COLOC92 package to perform statistical colocalization between our eQTLs and a number of traits and diseases using GWAS summary stats from publicly available sources.

Specifically, we downloaded GWAS summary statistics for the following 16 disease traits: Alzheimer’s disease93, asthma94, breast cancer95, COVID-1996, Crohn’s disease97, IgA nephropathy98, IBD97, lung cancer99, malignant lymphoma36, multiple sclerosis100, Parkinson’s disease101, prostate cancer102, rheumatoid arthritis103, SLE104, type 1 diabetes105 and ulcerative colitis97.

In addition, we downloaded UKBB GWAS summary statistics for 44 blood cell count and serum traits40 (SNPs and indels only) (from https://gymreklab.com/science/2023/09/08/Margoliash-et-al-paper.html).

Colocalization was performed using the coloc.abf function from COLOC (v.5.2.3)92. Colocalization was performed using SNP GWAS summary statistics and common-variant eQTLs in our dataset with eQTL type set to ‘quant’ and providing N and MAF rather than sdY as single-cell expression was not normalized and modelled as a Poisson distribution by SAIGE-QTL.

We computed cell function scores as cell states using scDeepID, a multi-task transformer, in which a biologically informed masked-encoder was used to generate cell function embeddings along the supplied database, followed by combining a token [CLS] for representing each cell’s identity. A set of transformer blocks were then used to learn the high-level data representation of the data, followed by two task heads for cell-type identification and cell simulation. The attention-based latent space was extracted from the model by attention rollout106, and cell function scores were calculated by attribution methods for deep learning models. scDeepID can be flexibly used with different supplied databases such as Reactome, gene ontology and more.

To define the cell states in the main NK cells of the TenK10K cohort, we first trained our scDeepID model on a publicly available dataset that contains different NK subtypes52, then performed label transfer by the trained model on our data. Specifically, we used Scanpy to automatically select roughly 600 highly variable genes (min_mean=0.0125, max_mean=3, min_disp=0.5) in the reference dataset, then only kept the shared genes between the reference and TenK10K cohort to train the model (that is, 560 genes). In particular, we further defined NKintermediate, NKadaptive and NKmature cells in the NK cell type. Then we trained a separate scDeepID model with the three fine-grained cell types as well as NKCD56bright and NKProliferating cells (with 2,315 highly variable genes, selected by Scanpy with min_mean=0.0125, max_mean=3, min_disp=0.5). We used the human gene ontology biological process database as a guide to train the scDeepID models above. We then extracted the attention-based latent space and normalized each cell function score with a zero mean and unit variance.

We used the cell states defined above to test for genotype × cell-state interactions. We focused on all eQTLs mapped in any of the NK cells subpopulations (NK, NKProliferating and NKCD56bright cells n = 16,759 unique variant–gene pairs) and used lme4 (ref. 107) to test the significance of the interaction term cell state × genotype for each eQTL and each of the five cell states, while correcting for age, sex, cohort (BioHEART or TOB), total counts as fixed effect covariates and including an individual term as a random effect.

where sc. exprs is the single-cell expression profile of the target eGene, ‘cell state’ is one of the cell-state functions estimated using scDeepID, genotype is the genotype vector at the variant tested, encoded as the number of minor alleles for each individual (0, 1, 2; expanded such that each cells from a given individual has the genotype of its individual of origin), ‘individual ’ is a random-effect term to account for the fact that each cell is not an independent observation and \(\varepsilon \) is a Gaussian error.

We performed csaQTL associations using GeNA56 v.1.0.0. We generated single cell objects for each main cell type group and applied the following processing to each object. Following a standard Scanpy pipeline, we normalized read counts per 10,000, log-transformed normalized counts and selected roughly 3,000 highly variable genes per main cell type (min_mean=0.0125, max_mean=3, min_disp=0.5). The scanpy.pp.regress_out function was used to obtain residualized counts, removing technical variation associated with total counts (library size) and percentage of mitochondrial reads. Expression for each gene was scaled to unit variance across cells. We then created MultiAnnData objects containing the processed counts and donor covariate information. We applied principal component analysis with Scanpy using default parameters. Harmony108 (harmonypy v.0.0.10) was run with default parameters, using sequencing library pools as batch assignment to correct principal components for technical batch effects between sequencing libraries. The harmonized principal components were used to construct a nearest neighbour graph and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) using Scanpy default parameters.

Next, we generated a neighbourhood abundance matrix (NAM) and its principal components with CNA109 (v.0.1.6) using default parameters. We then applied GeNA, controlling for age, sex, cohort and the top seven genotype principal components. We tested associations for all common variants (MAF ≥ 0.01 and percentage missing genotypes less than 15%). To calibrate the model, we also ran GeNA on permuted genotypes generated by random label swapping (one permutation performed per cell type). On the basis of this permutation testing, we observed poor calibration for variants below 0.05 MAF and removed them from the analysis. We considered variants passing the genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10−8) as statistically significant. Lead SNPs were defined using the clumping method as described in the GeNA publication56. In brief, at each locus the most significant SNP was considered the lead SNP, and all SNPs with LD > 0.8 to the lead SNP or within a 1-Mb window were considered LD-SNPs. We considered the number of unique lead SNPs for csaQTL loci as the total independent csaQTLs discovered. To visualize cell counts adjusted for covariates, we calculated the fraction of cells within each main subtype belonging to each minor subtype. Using these cell fractions, we fit a linear model with the lm function in R, including age, sex, cohort and the first seven genotype principal components as covariates, and visualized the residuals from the fitted model.

To characterize the cell-state phenotypes detected by GeNA, we used methods previously described in ref. 56 briefly, we calculated the Pearson correlation between GeNA neighbourhood-level phenotypes and expression of highly variable genes. Highly variable genes were then ranked by Pearson’s r for each csaQTL and used as input to GSEA. The fgsea (v.1.32.2) R package110 was used to run GSEA for all Molecular Signatures Database hallmark gene sets. Gene sets with Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. To visualize the expression of enriched gene sets, module scores were calculated with the Scanpy score_genes function and overlayed on to UMAP plots.

To obtain GeNA summary statistics compatible with colocalization analysis, we calculated fixed-phenotype summary statistics for each significant lead csaQTL using methods described in ref. 56. Colocalization was run with the coloc.abf function from the COLOC R package92, using default parameters. We provided COLOC with these fixed-phenotype summary statistics, as well as GWAS from two blood cell count and serum trait GWAS studies. We used the UKBB GWAS summary statistics for 44 blood cell count and serum traits described above, supplemented with an extra 26 blood trait GWAS performed in ref. 58. Colocalizations with PPH4 > 0.8 were considered significant.
