The Buried Tamales at the Heart of the Yucatán’s Day of the Dead

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

On a fall afternoon in Halachó, a small town in the Mexican state of Yucatán, Wilson Alonzo stood before a smoldering mound of dirt and began digging. Slowly clearing away branches, leaves, and soil, the chef unearthed a large disc enclosed in banana leaves. He peeled the wrapping back and examined the golden edges of the round tamale inside. He nodded. It was ready for the altar.

Every October and November, Yucatecans like Alonzo gather at the edges of underground ovens and cook mukbil pollo (“buried chicken”), a sacred tamale made only for the Yucatán Peninsula’s Day of the Dead. Known as Janal Pixán (“Food for the Souls” in Yucatec Maya) or Los Finados, the holiday welcomes returning spirits: children on October 31, adults on November 1, and all spirits on November 2, followed by a farewell feast eight—or in some cases, 30—days later. Above anything else that might grace an ofrenda, which could include anything from hot chocolate to relleno negro to beer, mukbil pollo is the star that occupies every altar’s center.

“It is the absolute highlight of the offering,” Alonzo said. One bite confirms why: The crispy, caramelized crust of lard-enriched masa gives way to a smoky, savory mouthful of chicken bathed in a brothy crimson sauce of achiote, tomato, sweet and spicy peppers, epazote, onions, and garlic.

But Alonzo—whose restaurant, Ya’axche, specializes in traditional Yucatecan dishes—said it’s not mukbil pollo’s flavor that nourishes spirits; instead of eating it, they consume the tamale through its aroma. And a crucial part of that aroma, he added, comes from the píib. Dating back to the ancient Maya, this underground cooking technique uses wood, stones, and leaves to impart a slightly herbaceous, smoky succulence to dishes like mukbil pollo—also known as pibipollo or pib—and cochinita pibil.“Cooking it in a gas oven simply isn’t the same,” Alonzo said. “You lose the unique aromas and flavors imparted by the earth itself.”Not everyone sees the píib as essential. In cities, where outdoor space is limited, most tamales are baked indoors. When Steffan Igor Ayora-Díaz, a retired anthropologist, was growing up in the city of Valladolid, his mother made mukbil pollo at home, then brought it to a local bakery to cook in an industrial oven. “Nobody said, ‘Oh, it has to be in a píib,’ ” he explained.

Ayora-Díaz now lives in the Yucatán state capital of Mérida, where he said beliefs surrounding Janal Pixán lean more secular. Not only do most eschew the píib, but he and many of his friends no longer build ofrendas, instead celebrating the holiday by gathering with family over a meal. Mukbil pollo is still important, but it plays a role more analogous to turkey on Thanksgiving. “We’re still going to eat it because that’s the food that you eat on these days, but you’re not really thinking of dead people when you’re eating it,” he said.

While purists decry these nontraditional approaches to the holiday, Ayora-Díaz points out that mukbil pollo is a blend of ancient and modern. “It’s a ‘traditional’ dish, but I’m putting ‘traditional’ in quotes because it’s relatively new,” he said. The Maya have been cooking in píibs and eating tamales for millennia, but ingredients like garlic, onions, and lard arrived with the Spanish conquistadors in the 1500s. And, according to Ayora-Díaz, mukbil pollo only started appearing in written records in the 1900s.Janal Pixán, too, is a hybrid of sorts, blending Christian rituals for All Saints’ Day and All Souls’ Day with Maya beliefs surrounding death and ancestor veneration. In Xocén, a small village of largely Yucatec Maya residents about 20 minutes outside Valladolid, honoring the dead can mean not only praying for their souls at Catholic mass but welcoming them into one’s home as guests and offering them the same hospitality as the living.

“We are dealing with spirits who require respect and specific observances, and who actually come to visit us,” Miguel Tun, a Yucatec Maya tour guide, said. “Even the yards look clean during this time. You walk past someone’s house and see that everything is spotless because visitors are coming.”Tun lives in a modern home next to his parents’ palapa in Xocén. The Tuns, like many Yucatec Maya, make their tamales only for the biix, or eighth day of Janal Pixán, when souls are believed to return to purgatory. In Xocén, the local version of mukbil pollo is perfectly suited for this journey. Called chachak waaj (“red tamale” in Yucatec Maya), it’s like a small hand pie.

“The idea is that the souls can easily carry the food with them,” Tun said. “It’s about seeing them off. It’s like when people travel—say, from abroad—to visit, and we give them a gift.”

This gift isn’t just the tamale itself but the time spent creating it. I visited the Tuns to learn how to make chachak waaj from scratch. We began in the morning. After shredding chicken, slow-cooking sauce, mixing manteca (rendered pork lard) into masa, assembling each tamale, and wrapping them in banana leaves, we needed headlamps to navigate the píib in the darkness.

“If you are going to make an offering as part of a spiritual practice, it has to be something into which you have poured your effort, you have sacrificed your time,” he said. “That holds significant meaning within the Maya worldview.”

The hours spent making chachak waaj also provide an opportunity to reminisce about the dead. “It’s a moment where the whole family is together, remembering things while cooking, making preparations, or kneading the dough,” Tun said. “Sometimes the conversation is about what’s happening in the community, but there are moments where someone makes a comment about something the deceased person used to do or say.”

At 9:30 p.m., Tun and his father returned from the backyard píib carrying several trays of tamales. It was time for the final step. The Tuns, several cousins, and an aunt gathered on plastic chairs and tree stumps and started unwrapping. They inspected each crust, making sure it was sufficiently crispy (the ones that weren’t would head back to the píib). As they worked, scattering charred leaves at their feet, they talked about an upcoming dance recital for one of their cousins.Later Tun told me this is, for him, the most important part of Janal Pixán. “Even though a loved one is gone, you demonstrate strength, you show that the family remains united,” he said. “You want that person, when they arrive, to see everyone together, to see that bond. That’s what matters most. I believe that’s the true essence of the Day of the Dead.”
