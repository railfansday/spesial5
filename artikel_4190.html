Account bans are a dangerous thing, especially for titles with a price tag. There’s a reason most games make you agree to terms of use before you get started playing online, clarifying bannable offenses and ensuring you know the groundwork you’re playing under. Without them, and without proper explanations for bans, companies can get into more than just PR trouble.

Modern Warfare 4's launch party is still going, with a free play period for DMZ ready to rake in new players.

Back in early August, a Battlefield 6 player named Rychadv complained on Reddit that his profile has been randomly, unexplainably banned after a period of inactivity, with no support levied from EA after attempts at appeal. Today, he revealed that he took EA to court over the case and won, regaining access to his account effective immediately.

While most don’t carefully read the terms and conditions many online games ask of you, the agreement does serve as somewhat legally binding, acting as a way for the studio running the game to establish when and if they can pull your plug. Cheating, improper communications use, or any step out of line with the terms laid out can be used as a justification to temporarily, or permanently, ban you.

The odd part that swung this case fully in Rychadv’s favor was the lack of an actual motive. For no discernible reason, after an extended break from the game, DICE had simply banned his account, with no means of reacquiring it or fighting the ban. Especially on a full-priced title like Battlefield 6, this type of hiccup is strictly unacceptable, potentially costing Rychadv a whole new copy if he didn’t drag DICE to court.

Bans need to be carefully considered by studios before they’re implemented; otherwise, they risk both stepping on toes and unjustly kicking out solid chunks of their populace. The threat of bans, more so than the bans themselves, is often enough to keep the problem children in line. We’ve all had a mouthy, rude player on the team, the more invested in the game they are, the less likely they are to get heinous.

Without them, and without proper explanations for bans, companies can get into more than just PR trouble.

This widely depends on both the player culture and ban frequency of the game itself, and is far more common in free-to-play titles where accounts can quickly rise and fall. For full-priced experiences, developers need to be a bit more careful with the ban hammer, and they especially need to make sure any perceived bannable offenses actually happened.

The gradual move away from physical media for the majority of the gaming industry makes the threat of random bans a bit more serious, especially with cases like Rychadv’s still out in the open. It’s not just multiplayer titles that are always online, and who's to say some studio couldn’t kick you out for what you do in a single-player game in the future, even when it's only affecting your copy?

This is definitely stepping into the speculative, but it's important to remember that digital titles aren’t really yours; you simply buy a license from the studio to play. Without the promise of physical media, there’s a certain formlessness to the games we buy, like they could slip away at any moment.

With no promise of actual ownership, situations like Rychadv’s could happen more and more, putting the onus of action onto the players to hold studios over the fire a bit. When you agree to the terms of service, make sure to read up on it. While you’re playing by the studio’s rules, they need to respect them as well, especially for titles with a price tag.

Improper use of bans could lead to more legal disputes like Rychadv’s, as players become more willing to hold studios to their word.
