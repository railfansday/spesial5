Billionaire Fanatics CEO opened his first business at 12, and nearly lost it all by 16: ‘I went from death… to making $10-15 million a year as a kid’

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Fanatics has become a one-stop shop for sports fans looking to rep their favorite teams and athletes. But the company has grown into a powerhouse in its own right, striking partnerships with nearly every major U.S. sports league, including the NFL, NBA, and MLB, and generating roughly $14 billion in annual revenue.

For cofounder and CEO Michael Rubin, however, the road to building that empire nearly ended before he turned 20.

Rubin caught the entrepreneurial bug at just 12 years old, when most kids are more concerned with middle school homework than starting a business. By 13, he was selling ski equipment out of his parents’ basement in Pennsylvania, and a year later, he had opened his own storefront just down the road from his home. He was making money quickly—and even went in with his friend in buying a Porchse before he could even legally drive.

But in around 1988, there was an unusually warm winter, and so his customers weren’t coming.

“It didn’t snow that year,” Rubin recalled in a recent conversation with podcaster David Senra. “End of the year, I had $200,000 of bills, $80,000 of inventory, and I was sitting right in front of bankruptcy.”

To get himself out of the hole, he negotiated his debts down to $35,000, went door to door borrowing money from neighbors, and used the proceeds from a new venture in the closeout business to repay his loan within three weeks. Five years later, at 21, Rubin was generating $150 million in sales buying and selling closeout merchandise.

“I went from death in its eyes, bankrupt, thought I was complete failure, giving up on entrepreneurship, to five years later, doing $150 million, making $10 to $15 million a year as a kid,” Rubin said. “And so to me, each disaster, if you learn from it, leads to your next success.”

Rubin spent just one semester at Villanova University before dropping out to focus on business. He continued building his sports merchandise operation, KPR Sports, which eventually became part of Global Sports Inc., later renamed GSI Commerce.

As the internet took off, Rubin shifted the business toward e-commerce, helping major retailers and sports teams sell merchandise online.

But the 2008 financial crisis nearly wiped him out again. Rubin had borrowed heavily against his GSI stock, which lost roughly 90% of its value during the bear market. He began calling a friend who managed a hedge fund, where Rubin had invested $5 million, repeatedly asking for his money back. After roughly 100 calls, he finally got it—with just hours to spare before the bank could seize his shares.

The business eventually rebounded and was bought by eBay in 2011 for $2.4 billion. As part of the deal, Rubin bought back the company’s sports e-commerce business, laying the foundation for today’s Fanatics.

The company has since grown into a giant valued at $31 billion in 2022, while Rubin’s personal fortune has grown to an estimated $11.6 billion, according to Bloomberg.

Still, Rubin said he wouldn’t trade the ups and downs that got him to his level of success today.

“I liked the adrenaline of fighting for my life,” Rubin said.

“If it’s the same shit every day, it’s boring,” he added. “Business is a rollercoaster. Business is an adventure. If you’re a real entrepreneur, you like that. You thrive on that.”

Fortune reached out to Fanatics for further comment.

Rubin isn’t the only successful business leader to have nearly lost it all at a young age.

In the early days of Ray Dalio building out his now-multibillion-dollar hedge fund business, he was broke living in New York City—and had to ask his father to borrow $4,000 to help cover bills.

The experience gave him humility, but also made him develop a deep respect for those who do fail—and embrace the lessons learned.

“Anyone you see succeeding is only succeeding at the things you’re paying attention to—I guarantee they are also failing at lots of other things,” Dalio wrote.

“The people I respect most are those who fail well. I respect them even more than those who succeed. That is because failing is a painful experience while succeeding is a joyous one, so it requires much more character to fail, change, and then succeed than to just succeed.”

Mark Cuban has echoed that failure is an inevitable part of building a successful career. On his path to a $9.8 billion net worth, he made countless early missteps—from trying to sell powdered milk to getting fired from jobs and making bad investments. It’s the ability to not let them define any entire career is what matters.

“No one is going to know or care about your failures, and neither should you,”  he said. “All you have to do is learn from them.”
