How Shame Can Drive Crime

Updated November 9, 2026 | Reviewed by Ekua Hagan

Shame is often said to be the most powerful emotion. We have all felt the sharp pain of shame: the fear that we are not good enough. “Shame strikes deepest into the heart of man…it is an inner torment, a sickness of the soul…” (Sedgwick & Frank, as cited in George, 2025).

Shaming can—and does—occur every day in parent-child relationships as a means of communicating that a behavior is undesirable or unacceptable, helping children learn social norms. Its inhibitory effect can be protective. Although such moments may be difficult, this kind of corrective shaming is most often followed by a restoration of connection with the parent and reassurance that bad behavior does not define the child as bad or unacceptable.

Early shame expressions have been observed in infants as young as 7 months, and self-awareness emerges in the second year of life (George, 2025), during the phase identified by Erik Erikson as “Autonomy vs. Shame and Doubt.” The central tasks during toddlerhood are learning to do things on one’s own and developing skills, self-control, and self-confidence. When caregivers are overly restrictive, harsh, or critical, the shame that arises can instead lead to self-doubt and low self-esteem.

Serious problems arise when withdrawal of love accompanies shaming, and the “bad” behavior is not differentiated from the person of the child, leading to a more global sense of being flawed or unlovable as a human being. Unbearable feelings of shame can follow. A young child whose parent turns away or conveys contempt or indifference must find a way to manage without the parent’s help. There is no repair to provide relief, reconnection, and the ability to move forward.

Shame is biologically stressful. The flood of painful emotion inhibits speech and thought, and the person feels acutely self-conscious, small, and exposed (Herman, 2018). Children erect defenses to protect themselves from the pain of devaluation of the self. They may become enraged, turning the discomfort of self-blame into blaming others. They may become perfectionistic, hoping that being perfect will protect them from criticism and rejection. They may withdraw from other people, finding safety in isolation.

Child abuse, such as violence or the threat of violence, control of bodily functions, or degradation, subjects children to shame states that are overwhelming. They experience “an intense and destructive sense of self-disgust, verging on self-hatred” when they are treated as a physical object by those who would be expected to love and care for them (Fonagy et al., 2003, as cited in Herman, 2018). These powerful feelings of self-hate do not evaporate and are easily set off by future events.

We know that childhood maltreatment is associated with risk for committing future violence among juveniles and adults. Hypothesized links between violence and childhood abuse include learning violent behavior from parents, effects of trauma on brain development and functioning, including dysregulation of the stress response system and deficits in self-regulation (Miley, Fox, Muniz et al., 2020; Burke, Ellis, Peltier, et al., 2023). There is little in the literature that examines the contribution of the shame that arises from experiences of child abuse, but I believe it is an invisible contributor more often than we think.

Significant levels of shame can lead to withdrawal or to greater externalizing and aggressive tendencies (Maggi, Zaccaria & Breda et al., 2022). People who suffer pervasive feelings of devaluation and inadequacy fear exposure of the devalued self and mask it with acts of aggression. I saw this in juvenile offenders I interviewed for my book, Before Their Crimes: What We’re Misunderstanding about Childhood Trauma, Youth Crime, and the Path to Healing.

One boy, Simon (not his real name), routinely brutally beaten by his father, was infuriated when some younger members of his gang refused to do his bidding during a confrontation with another gang. “I felt I needed to prove myself, not only to myself, but to the younger guys. I said f*ck it, and I pulled the trigger.” In a moment, his life and that of another boy ended tragically.

Carl (another pseudonym) was physically abused by his mother throughout childhood. The family had modest means, and as a teenager, Carl was intensely ashamed as he compared himself to other kids. He felt compelled to begin stealing to acquire clothes and objects that assuaged a profound sense of inferiority and inadequacy.

A second way that shame is sometimes a factor in aggression and violence is that it impairs the development of the capacity for empathy (Maggi, Zaccaria & Breda et al., 2022). Empathy develops in and through relationships in which we feel seen, heard, and loved. Maltreated children have instead been harshly treated in a context where empathic response is absent.

Most children are unable to manage traumatic experiences or overwhelming shame on their own. Unmanageable feelings must be kept at bay and people at a distance to protect the vulnerable self. If you can’t allow people close to you, there is no chance to develop relationships that might be reparative. When you cannot permit your own feelings, the feelings of others are not real to you. You don’t see your victims as human beings like yourself, but as threats or enemies that must be defeated or destroyed.

When abused children turn to crime, the traumatic nature of maltreatment plays a central role in disrupting normal development. We should not forget that the shame of feeling unlovable and devalued that accompanies abuse compounds the damage and finds its way into the dire strategies meant to protect the shattered sense of self.
