stone plinths and secret blue rooms shape bureau de change’s paris jewelry boutique

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

bureau de change transforms an 18th-century paris building into a nikos koulis boutique with sculptural limestone and hidden octagonal rooms.

London-based architecture studio Bureau de Change has designed a new boutique for Greek jeweler Nikos Koulis in Paris, where sculptural limestone displays occupy the ground floor and a pair of cross-shaped cabinets conceal octagonal rooms upstairs. The retail project occupies an 18th-century building on rue Boissy d’Anglas and marks the studio’s second collaboration with the jewelry house.

Following their earlier collaboration in Athens, the team at Bureau de Change approached the Paris boutique through the history of its building and the craftsmanship associated with traditional Parisian ateliers. The two-story interior brings together contemporary display furniture and original architectural details, with materials chosen for their texture and finish.

On the ground floor, natural light falls across herringbone timber floors and powdery plaster walls. Monolithic stone plinths organize the jewelry displays, while slim metal frames around the windows introduce a sharper geometry against the softer surfaces.

The metalwork also draws from a familiar detail found throughout Paris. Its decorative edges reference the ornamental profiles of traditional zinc bar counters, a small architectural gesture that connects the boutique to the city’s older interiors.

The custom display plinths take their shape from an earlier Bureau de Change design for Koulis’ Athens flagship. For the Paris store, the architects superimposed the form of a metal display-case base onto a block of limestone and extracted it, leaving a negative impression in the stone.

Each block retains evidence of this process. The lower sections have a rougher, textured finish, while the upper surfaces are polished. Glass vitrines sit above the limestone, with faceted metal junctions that echo the geometric precision of the jewelry itself.

The plinths are also modular and can be moved or combined to accommodate different collections and events. This flexibility extends to Koulis’ growing body of work, which now includes limited-edition objects and furniture alongside fine jewelry.

The design team introduces pink marble through a series of smaller architectural interventions. Chiseled pieces form the entrance door handles, their irregular surfaces set against the smooth stainless steel casings around the windows.

The same marble reappears along the staircase as a custom floating handrail. Its sculpted form follows the ascent to the upper floor, where the boutique shifts from an open retail space to a more private salon.

From the street, the storefront frames selective views of the interior. The restrained palette of stone and plaster continues inside, with metal details appearing throughout the window surrounds and display furniture.

Upstairs, the boutique takes on the atmosphere of a private apartment. Eclectic furniture and artworks from Koulis’ personal collection fill the salon, which accommodates individual jewelry appointments as well as dinners and events. Pieces in vibrant red interrupt the muted palette and recall the use of color in the Athens store.

The most unusual elements are two freestanding, full-height cabinets that divide the sitting and dining areas. Each has a cross-shaped plan, but when opened, the structures transform into octagonal spaces. One contains eight triangular jewelry vitrines arranged around a deep-blue interior that visitors can physically enter.

The second cabinet becomes a bar, with triangular shelves and drawers accessible from either side. Closed, the two structures read as large pieces of furniture within the salon. Opened, they reveal an entirely different arrangement, with the jewelry displays and entertaining spaces tucked inside their geometric forms.
