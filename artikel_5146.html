Six senators say Treasury's proposed safe harbor could favor federally licensed stablecoin issuers.

The GENIUS Act generally allows qualifying state supervision for issuers with $10B or less in outstanding payment stablecoins.

Lawmakers want temporary protection for state issuers while certification reviews remain pending.

The GENIUS Act safe harbor debate is exposing a potential imbalance in America’s stablecoin rules. Six US senators warn that proposed Treasury regulations could give federally licensed issuers an advantage while state-supervised companies wait for regulatory approval.

In a November 17 letter to Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, the lawmakers argued that federal applicants could receive temporary protection while state licensing frameworks remain under review. That’s more than a technical paperwork dispute. It could influence where stablecoin companies choose to operate.

Six senators told Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent that the department's draft GENIUS Act rule protects some pending federal stablecoin applicants while leaving state-licensed issuers exposed. Without a fix, they wrote, issuers may chase a federal charter instead, "the opposite…

The dispute centers on a safe harbor provision in Treasury’s proposed implementation rules. Certain companies with pending federal licensing applications could receive temporary relief from compliance restrictions if their federal regulator grants a waiver.

State-supervised issuers don’t appear to receive equivalent protection under the proposal, according to the senators.

That creates an awkward choice. A company seeking continuity might pursue federal oversight through the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), even if state supervision was its preferred route.

The lawmakers argue this would undermine Congress’s intention to preserve both pathways.

The GENIUS Act generally allows issuers with $10 billion or less in outstanding payment stablecoins to operate under qualifying state frameworks. Those frameworks must meet standards substantially similar to federal requirements.

Issuers above that threshold generally face additional federal oversight, subject to statutory exceptions and waivers.

For growing stablecoin businesses, the calculation isn’t simple. They must weigh whether their state regulator can secure certification in time and whether future expansion could push them into federal supervision anyway.

A federal license may look more predictable. That’s precisely the competitive imbalance the senators want Treasury to address.

The letter, signed by Cynthia Lummis, Kirsten Gillibrand, Angela Alsobrooks, Kevin Cramer, Pete Ricketts and Mark Warner, asks Treasury to extend safe harbor protection to issuers in states expected to qualify before January 18, 2028.

The request comes as the implementation deadline approaches. The OCC finalized its GENIUS Act rule on October 21, covering reserves, redemptions, risk management and supervision. It is scheduled to take effect January 18, 2027.

Treasury’s separate rulemaking remains central to the dispute. No announcement confirms that it has accepted the senators’ request.

The GENIUS Act safe harbor question now comes down to whether state stablecoin issuers receive comparable transition protection or face pressure to choose federal licensing.
