Before You Plan Thanksgiving Dinner, Plan Family Dynamics

By this point in November, many of us have started planning Thanksgiving. We’re discussing who’s coming, who’s bringing what, and whether people prefer the canned or fresh cranberry sauce. We may also be planning conversations that haven’t happened.

Will someone ask about our relationship and whether we’re married yet? Will our parenting become a topic of discussion? Will we spend another holiday doing the work while everyone else watches football?

I have written about the friction that can accompany family gatherings. As Thanksgiving approaches again, I keep wondering would it would look like to prepare for the family dynamics as thoughtfully as we prepare for the meal.

Think about the moment you’re most concerned about. Perhaps it’s a particular question, a familiar disagreement, or the expectation that you’ll organize everything. Now consider what you’re predicting. Are you anticipating a situation that happens regularly? Or are you imagining several possible arguments and preparing a response to each one? Past experiences give us useful information, but mentally replaying an argument doesn’t necessarily give us a useful plan.

Try making the concern specific: “I’m worried someone will comment on my career decision.” Then identify a response you would feel comfortable using: “I’m comfortable with my decision, and I’m not looking for advice about it today.” You may still feel uncomfortable and dread having the conversation but having a response ready simply gives you somewhere to begin.

We often approach holidays with goals that depend on other people, like everyone will get along, no one will make an insensitive comment, or everyone will appreciate the effort. Those hopes are understandable, but they’re also difficult to guarantee.

Consider a few decisions you can make yourself. How long will you stay? Which preparations can you reasonably take on? What will you do if a conversation becomes uncomfortable? You might decide to visit for dessert rather than spend the entire day. You might ask someone else to bring the side dishes. You might arrange transportation so you can leave when you’re ready.

These decisions deserve space alongside the grocery list.

If a familiar pattern bothers you, consider whether there’s a request you can make before the gathering. For example, if you usually manage the meal and cleanup, “I need more help this year” is a starting point. A more specific request gives others a clearer way to participate: “Can you handle the dishes after dinner, including putting the leftovers away?”

The same applies to traditions. If the usual schedule no longer works for your household, raise that issue while plans are being made. Others may be disappointed. You can acknowledge that disappointment while still explaining what you can offer.

In an uncomfortable moment, finding the right words can be difficult. Choose language that sounds like something you would actually say. For an intrusive question: “I don’t have anything I want to share about that.” For unsolicited advice: “I’ll ask if I need suggestions.” For a conversation you want to leave: “I’m going to step away for a few minutes.”

A prepared phrase won’t determine how another person responds. It can help you communicate your own decision more clearly.

Preparation can also include thinking about what you’re looking forward to: Is there someone you’d like to spend time with? A recipe you want to learn? A game, walk, or family story you’d enjoy sharing? Make a little room for that experience rather than assuming it will happen once everything else is finished.

Once you’re home after the gathering, give yourself a fair assessment of the day. One awkward exchange doesn’t have to define the entire gathering. You can appreciate the parts you enjoyed and think honestly about what you’d change next time.

Thanksgiving takes preparation. Along with deciding what belongs on the table, we can decide what we have the time, energy, and willingness to bring to it.
