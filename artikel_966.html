The Women's World Cup is coming home... but 2035 cannot be treated as a fix for the game's problems, writes TARA ANSON-WALSH

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Published: 14:36 EST, 23 November 2026 | Updated: 17:04 EST, 23 November 2026

The United Kingdom will host the 2035 Women's World Cup, the first football World Cup to be staged on these shores since 1966, it was announced to much fanfare on Monday.

Yet while the countdown can now officially begin, the biggest challenges facing the women's game cannot be kicked eight-and-a-half years down the road.

Over in Zurich, FIFA president Gianni Infantino undertook his usual long-winded and self-serving monologue which precedes these ceremonies, in which he heralded the 'incredible growth' of the Women's World Cup, describing a time when they 'didn't exist'.

'We all remember the last Women's World Cup,' the embattled 56-year-old Italian said, calling it 'a huge success'.

The irony, of course, is that the last Women's World Cup in Australia and New Zealand is remembered by many for all the wrong reasons, with newspaper back pages peppered with images of Spain captain Jenni Hermoso's kiss with the former president of Spain's FA, Luis Rubiales, for days after the final.

Rubiales was later convicted of sexual assault for the incident, which triggered widespread protests across the country and overshadowed Spain's terrific 1-0 triumph over England in Sydney.

There is an added layer of irony in that Infantino had, two days before the 2023 final, angered many women worldwide with yet another controversial speech in which he told them: 'You have the power to change', but that they had to 'convince us men what we have to do'.

Alongside this patronising statement, Infantino also insisted that equal pay at the World Cup 'would not solve anything'.

Three years later, Infantino is unfortunately still at the helm, while UEFA's attempts to unseat him following his controversial bid to sell off the World Cup now look to have failed spectacularly, with the football despot increasingly likely to secure a fourth term.

What the social and political landscape will look like by the time this women's footballing bonanza arrives on these shores in 2035 remains unclear. What is clear, however, is that the game's continued progress will require more than the record attendances and soaring commercial revenues the FA are predicting.

It will require the figures who have too often held it back to finally step aside, and that process should begin with the likes of Infantino being shown the door.

It will require those at the top of English football to put their heads together and work out how to stem falling attendances in the league. The average attendance across the first eight WSL matchweeks of 2024-25 was 8,173. Last season it was 7,472. This season, it is tracking at around 5,677.

And third, it will require somewhere other than Wembley to stage the final.

Wembley was, of course, the scene of Chloe Kelly's moment of madness, yet by the time the 2035 Women's World Cup comes around it will also have hosted the men's finals of Euro 2024 and 2028.

The FA paid off its debt on the national stadium in 2024, 17 years after its reopening, and is no longer obligated to host major events there to service repayments.

The rules have been left open for another venue to emerge to host the final, such as Man United's proposed new 100,000 stadium

While under current FIFA regulations, Wembley is the only one of the proposed 22 stadiums that meets the requirements to host a final, the rules have been left open enough for another venue to emerge should a suitable stadium be completed before 2035.

Step up Sir Jim Ratcliffe, although please refer back to point one in this regard – the Manchester United co-owner who famously asked former captain Katie Zelem 'what do you do here?' while touring the club's women's facilities.

United's proposed new 100,000-seater stadium was included in the 2035 bid and could yet come under consideration from FIFA.

While there are still many hurdles to clear before then, hosting a tournament final in Manchester would undoubtedly be huge for the women's game, not least given the optics of a bid built around being 'all together', described as 'a rallying cry to unite people through football'.

London should not be the epicentre of the tournament, not if the intention is to spread the women's game across the United Kingdom rather than concentrate it in the capital, where Arsenal's attendances continue to dwarf those of every other WSL club.

In other words, the women's game in this country cannot be put on hold until 2035.

It cannot afford to wait eight-and-a-half years for this World Cup to come and rescue it. Time to get cracking.
