These formulas are basically a cheat code to supple, hydrated, healthy skin.

If you shop as much as ELLE’s beauty-obsessed editors, you’re already pretty familiar with a few common skin care ingredients. Those with acne-prone skin know the benefits of salicylic acid, retinol, and mandelic acid all too well. Oily skin types typically reach for non-comedogenic formulas, while dry skin drinks up hyaluronic acid. Still, if there’s one ingredient all skin types can use in a routine, it’s ceramides. New to that world? Let us help you get more familiar.

“Ceramides are fats, or lipids, that your skin makes on its own,” explains Dr. Anne Han. While you might not have heard of them, think of ceramides as your skin’s security guard. “They make up about half of the lipids in the stratum corneum, the outermost layer of skin. I like the ‘brick and mortar’ picture: skin cells are the bricks, and ceramides, along with cholesterol and fatty acids, are the mortar that holds them together. That mortar keeps water in and keeps irritants, allergens, and microbes out,” she adds. When your skin becomes particularly dry and crackly, this could be a sign that it needs a ceramide boost.

“When ceramide levels drop, the barrier gets ‘leaky.’ Water evaporates more easily, and skin turns dry, tight, flaky, itchy, and more reactive. That happens with eczema, as we age, in cold, dry weather, and with overwashing or harsh actives. Ceramides in a moisturizer help top up what the skin is missing,” Dr. Han shares. Not all skin care products offer instant gratification, but adding a ceramide moisturizer to your lineup can boost moisture levels “sometimes within a day of the first use, with more benefit after a few weeks of steady use,” Dr. Han suggests, adding that “studies show ceramide moisturizers raise skin hydration and cut water loss” and “work best alongside cholesterol and fatty acids, the other two parts of the skin’s natural lipid mix, not on their own.”

All in all, every skin type can benefit from a ceramide moisturizer for an instant boost of moisture and overall improvement in skin over time. There’s power in numbers, so when shopping for an effective ceramide moisturizer, Dr. Han advises looking for one that combines similar ingredients. “Look for ceramides together with cholesterol and fatty acids, which mimic the skin’s own lipid mix. Ceramides used alone are less effective at barrier repair,” she says. Plus, look for formulas with “skin-identical” ceramides, which look like “ceramide NP, AP, or EOP (older names include ceramide 1, 3, and 6-II)” on labels.

Furthermore, you’ll want a moisturizer packed with “humectants, like glycerin or hyaluronic acid [which] pull water in” or “occlusives, like petrolatum or dimethicone to seal [moisture] in; niacinamide can help skin make more ofits own ceramides,” Dr. Han adds. Other important markers to look for include “fragrance-free” to avoid irritating sensitive or eczema-prone skin; for acne-prone skin, “non-comedogenic” products are great because they don’t clog pores.

“They do different jobs, so it isn’t really either/or. Ceramides are barrier ingredients and help the skin hold water and resist irritation, and that is what makes something a moisturizer,” Dr. Han explains. Meanwhile, peptides—skin care’s buzziest ingredient—are touted for their anti-aging properties. “They are meant to signal the skin to make more collagen or to soften the look of fine lines. If your main concern is dryness, sensitivity, redness, or eczema-prone skin, start with ceramides. The science behind them is more direct, since you are replacing a lipid your barrier actually lacks. If your barrier is in good shape and you want something for fine lines, a peptide product is a reasonable, gentle add-on,” she adds.
