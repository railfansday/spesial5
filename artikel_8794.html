Architecture as Evidence: Reading the Built Environment Through Forensic Investigation

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Architecture is commonly examined through its visible form, materials and spatial organisation. The physical fabric of a building can also retain evidence of events and alterations that are not apparent from its present appearance. Cracks, repairs and structural changes may indicate when and how a building was modified, while photographs and material fragments can provide further evidence about the circumstances surrounding those changes. Forensic investigation examines these traces, using the built environment and its surrounding records to establish how particular events, alterations or failures occurred.

One prominent example is Forensic Architecture, an interdisciplinary research agency based at Goldsmiths, University of London. Working across architecture, research, filmmaking, science and legal investigation, the agency examines contested events and human rights violations. Its investigations bring together architectural models, satellite imagery, photographs, video footage, physical evidence and witness testimony. Architectural methods are used here to examine existing spaces and reconstruct events that have taken place within them.

Existing buildings often contain evidence of successive alterations and repairs. Changes in masonry, structural additions and layers of later finishes can record how a building has been modified over time. Differences in brickwork, inserted structural members or paint layers beneath later finishes can help establish the sequence of these changes. Compared with historical drawings and photographs, such details can indicate when different parts of a building were constructed, replaced or altered.

The investigation of the Beirut Port Explosion on 4 August 2020 illustrates how separate visual records can be assembled into a spatial reconstruction. Working with the Egyptian publication Mada Masr, Forensic Architecture examined photographs and videos recorded from different parts of Beirut to reconstruct the sequence of events at Warehouse 12.

Investigators geolocated individual videos and plotted each camera's field of view against a three-dimensional model of Beirut. Footage without metadata was synchronised by comparing changes in the shape and movement of the smoke plumes. The analysis identified four separate plumes emerging from different parts of the warehouse over approximately fourteen minutes. Photographs from January 2020 and a video recorded in December 2019 were also used to reconstruct the warehouse interior. This model helped investigators estimate the area occupied by the 2,750 bags of ammonium nitrate.

Combining recordings requires investigators to establish their authenticity and provenance. Developed by the United Nations Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights and the University of California - Berkeley Human Rights Center, the Berkeley Protocol on Digital Open Source Investigations sets out standards for the identification, collection, preservation and analysis of digital information. It addresses the credibility and authenticity of sources, the context in which material is collected and the safety of those who provide it.

Forensic Architecture used three-dimensional modelling to examine the progression of the Grenfell Tower fire in London. The investigation collected hundreds of videos recorded by residents and members of the public, then positioned the footage on a wireframe model of the tower using projection mapping. Aligning each recording with its camera location and viewpoint allowed investigators to compare the spread of the fire across different parts of the building.

The publication of a forensic reconstruction also involves decisions about how evidence is interpreted and presented. A photograph can be authentic yet misleading when separated from its context. This places individual buildings within the wider patterns of occupation, activity and change that shape the historic city.

Forensic methods are often associated with investigations of conflict, disasters and human rights violations. Some of the underlying methods of examining physical evidence also have relevance to architectural research and conservation, although the aims of these practices differ. Forensic reconstruction seeks to establish what happened in a particular place and sequence of events. Architectural investigation for conservation examines how a building was constructed, altered and maintained over time. It draws on the physical fabric, construction details and historical documentation to establish the building's development and condition.
