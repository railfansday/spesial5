An ancient Roman settlement missing from all history books was found in a Welsh village right before new houses were built

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A survey taken before the planned construction of 300 homes at Mancot in Wales led archaeologists to a 2,000-year-old Roman settlement.

In December 2025, archaeologists from Heneb were working on a housing site beside Gladstone Way in Mancot, a village in Flintshire, northeast Wales. Castle Green Homes planned to build 300 houses on the land.

The ground on that plot held a Roman settlement that was completely unknown, never mentioned in historical records.

The site is about 2,000 years old and produced an unusually large collection of Roman metalwork. Heneb published photographs captioned as a Roman brooch casting mold from the dig, and the finds are still being analyzed.

The development is called Gladstone Grange, and Flintshire Council had allocated the land for housing. The developer assessed the site for archaeological value before the project began and hired Heneb, the Trust for Welsh Archaeology, as part of that process.

A geophysical survey, which maps buried features without digging, picked up structures and signs of historic activity under the ground. Heneb then monitored the site before and during the early stages of the work.

The trust says it completed a full investigation of the remains before the site was developed. It announced the find on November 9, 2026, and said the fieldwork has ended.

Excavators uncovered a Romano-British industrial settlement, the term archaeologists use for communities in Britain under Roman rule. It sat inside a network of plot boundaries and drainage systems.

A site plan released by Heneb marks groups of buildings lettered A to H. A large drainage ditch formed the eastern boundary for most of them.

The settlement grew in phases. Timber houses came first, and Heneb says some of them may date to the end of the Iron Age.

By the third century AD, elongated, semi-rectangular buildings had replaced those houses. One photograph from the dig shows the outline of a third-century building in the cleared soil.

Heneb thinks the remains continue west under the part of the village now called Big Mancot. The announcement does not say how far they run.

Heneb lists a wide variety of brooches from Mancot. People in Roman Britain pinned their clothes with them and also wore them as decoration.

The trust published photographs of the objects. One shows a disc brooch set with enamel. Heneb says the decoration appeared only after extensive laboratory conservation.

Another photograph shows an enamelled brooch of the Wirral type. A third shows a large brooch of a kind often found on Anglesey, the Welsh island where detectorists recovered fifteen Iron Age gold coins.

The dig also produced a lead spindle whorl, a weight for spinning thread. A denarius of the emperor Commodus turned up too, and Heneb’s caption dates it to AD 192.

Three more photographs carry the caption “Casting mould of a Roman Brooch.” Heneb does not say whether they show one mold or several.

Heneb says “substantial evidence” suggests the inhabitants made these brooches themselves. The trust thinks they probably worked within “a wider industrial supply network” that served local people and Chester.

Mancot overlooks Chester, site of the Roman legionary fortress of Deva Victrix. Romans built it in the AD 70s and occupied it for more than 300 years. The Twentieth Legion was based there for most of that time, according to Cheshire West and Chester Council.

Very little Roman activity had been recorded in this part of Flintshire. Heneb says the land around Chester has often been described as a “largely undeveloped hinterland,” which is why the trust considers Mancot significant.

Other industrial sites had already turned up elsewhere in north Wales. At Pentre Ffwrndan, near Flint, Roman workers smelted lead ore from Halkyn Mountain from about the mid-60s AD. The former Clwyd-Powys Archaeological Trust, now part of Heneb, says it is likely the lead was shipped to Chester.

Volunteers and archaeologists working there in 2017 and 2018 excavated a Roman road aligned toward a probable quayside on the estuary.

At Prestatyn, bronze smiths, iron smiths and enamellers worked in adjacent timber workshops from about AD 90 or 100 until about AD 160, the national record of Welsh monuments says. A trust leaflet adds that excavators there found slag, bronze scrap and parts of casting molds.

Near Wrexham, a detectorist’s lead ingot led researchers to the first confirmed Roman villa in North Wales. Another detectorist in the region found up to 15,000 Roman coins.

Heneb places Mancot within this emerging pattern and argues that Roman Wales was “far more complex and interconnected than previously understood.”

Heneb’s announcement gives no total for the brooches, the coins or the buildings. It also does not mention furnaces, hearths or metal waste at Mancot, the kinds of remains reported at Prestatyn.

The link to Chester remains an inference, and Heneb words it as a probability. The trust has named no buyer, and the announcement cites no chemical test that ties the metal to the fortress.

The objects are undergoing analysis and conservation. They will then go to Flintshire Museum Services, which plans a public display, though Heneb has given no date.

Heneb has finished digging at Gladstone Grange, where 300 homes are being built. The brooches, the casting finds and the Commodus coin stay with conservators for now.
