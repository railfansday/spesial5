A couple walking their dog spotted a rusty can and found 1,427 buried gold coins

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

A California couple discovered 1,427 gold coins buried in eight rusty cans on their property in 2013, uncovering a treasure worth over $10 million.

In 2013, a Northern California couple known only as John and Mary were walking their dog on their property when they noticed a rusty can poking out of the ground. It was the first of eight cans that together held 1,427 gold coins.

The coins were struck between 1847 and 1894 and had a face value of $27,980. Kagin’s, Inc., the coin firm that marketed the find, estimated the coin hoard‘s value at more than $10 million.

“We found the coins buried on a little area of our property that we call the Saddle Ridge,” Mary said in an interview with the couple. The treasure became known as the Saddle Ridge Hoard.

“I saw an old can sticking out of the ground on a trail that we had walked almost every day for many, many years,” John recalled.

“John used a stick to dig up the first can,” Mary explained.

Carrying the can toward the house, the couple stopped for a breather because it was so heavy. John joked that it must be full of lead paint.

“Right after making the comment about it possibly being paint, the lid cracked off and exposed a rib of a single gold coin,” he recounted.

“Of course, it was a very surreal moment,” John noted of his first reaction.

Back at the trail, about a foot (30 centimeters) to the left of the first can, the couple broke into a second one with a small hand shovel.

In all, the coins lay in eight decaying metal canisters, all less than a foot (30 centimeters) beneath the surface.

The cans were numbered and had been placed in a curious alignment with the North Star, the American Numismatic Association reported.

“Years ago, on our first hike, we noticed an old tree growing into the hill,” John recalled in the association’s account. An unusual angular rock also sat up the hill from where the coins were buried.

“Starting at the rock, if you walk 10 paces towards the North Star, you wind up smack in the middle of the coins!” Mary pointed out.

For safekeeping at first, John hid the coins at home. “I dug a hole under the wood pile and got a slab of green board to cover it,” he remembered.

Most of the gold coins were $20 Liberty double eagles struck at the San Francisco Mint from 1855 to 1894, according to the Professional Coin Grading Service (PCGS), which certified each coin.

Kagin’s counted almost 1,400 $20 gold pieces, 50 $10 gold pieces and four $5 gold pieces in the hoard.

The $20 coin dates to the California Gold Rush. An 1849 example was the first $20 coin struck by the U.S. Mint during that rush, according to the Smithsonian Institution.

David Hall, co-founder of PCGS, called the hoard “a literal time capsule” of coins that circulated in the late 19th century. In 2014, more than a dozen of its coins ranked as the finest known, or tied for finest known, in the PCGS Population Report.

One of them, an 1866-S “No Motto” double eagle, was valued at around $1 million.

David McCarthy, senior numismatist at Kagin’s, evaluated the coins after the couple contacted him. “Many pieces were finer than anything known in major collections or museums,” he observed.

Based on the coins’ dates and condition, along with the decay of the cans, Kagin’s concluded that the hoard was buried over a significant period of time in the late 19th century.

“Unlike other hoards and treasures, this one includes a great variety of dates, many of which are in pristine condition,” noted Donald Kagin, president of Kagin’s.

Who did the burying remains unknown. One idea links the coins to a robbery of the San Francisco Mint in 1901, but the wide range of coin conditions has put that theory to rest.

Other suggestions include a secret society or a landowner hiding gold around the time of Franklin D. Roosevelt’s gold confiscation order. Neither idea has been confirmed.

About 90 percent of the coins were to be sold through Amazon’s collectibles store, in what Kagin’s called the first major numismatic treasure sold there. Others were sold through Kagin’s own platform.

Before the sale, a sample of the coins went on display at the American Numismatic Association’s National Money Show in Atlanta, from February 27 to March 1, 2014.

With the proceeds, John and Mary paid off personal debt and donated to local charities.

“We love our lives as they are – I hope we can help our family members and our community and give back some,” Mary told the interviewer.

Before Saddle Ridge, the largest reported buried gold find in the U.S. had a face value of $4,500. Construction workers found it in Jackson, Tennessee, in 1985, and it later sold for around $1 million.

The information was obtained from the American Numismatic Association.
