10 Things I’m Dreading About Thru-Hiking the Appalachian Trail

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

I recently wrote about the 10 things I’m most excited about when it comes to thru-hiking the Appalachian Trail.

However, as any Appalachian Trail thru-hiker can tell you, the AT is a trail that throws a lot at you. As excited as I am to experience such an iconic trail culture, I am dreading some of the more uncomfortable aspects of the journey. So, before I willingly sign up for five months of relentless discomfort, here’s what I am dreading most about thru-hiking the Appalachian Trail.

Coming from the dry air of the West, the sheer humidity of the East Coast is a fear of mine. I know what it’s like to sweat, but I’m used to everything drying out overnight. There is a special kind of misery in waking up damp, packing away a wet tent, and immediately sweating through my clothes before I even start the first climb.

Drying gear out feels nearly impossible when the air itself feels wet, and I know I need to accept that I will seldom feel truly dry on this trail. That’s easy to write about from my comfortable couch; yet in practice, I fear that the persistent dampness will chip away at my mental fortitude more than I can currently anticipate.

Rain gear can only do so much. Photo via Katie Jackson

While the shade and lightning protection are comforting, I find the prospect of the AT’s endless Green Tunnel daunting. On other trails I’ve hiked, even the tree-covered sections would frequently open onto a high ridge or exposed pass that rewarded me with fantastic views. The AT’s canopy shelters hikers from their hard-earned views more than I’m used to, and I once again fear the mental toll of not seeing the vistas I love.

I know the answer is to learn to love the minute changes of the Green Tunnel, but that’s a big ask when I’m accustomed to walking through some of the grandest, most objectively stunning views in America. Will I be able to find a deeper motivation for a thru-hike other than seeing pretty places? Your guess is as good as mine.

The trees are beautiful, but after a while I want to see some MOUNTAINS! Photo via Katie Jackson

Ticks freak me out in a way I struggle to articulate. As someone with a deep fear of needles, something about looking down and seeing a bug with its head buried inside your skin makes the back of my neck itch. Even the concept of tick checks causes some anxiety; the possibility of finding a tick on me is as stressful as the creature itself.

To make matters worse, this isn’t a fear I can just tamp down and avoid. The consequences of leaving a tick attached can be severe. Lyme disease is on the rise in ticks found out East, along with a whole host of other tick-borne illnesses. The idea of having to perform a full-body tick check every single night adds a persistent, low-grade anxiety to camp life that I’ve never experienced on a trail before.

I’ll also throw snakes on this list. I’m not stoked by the number of venomous snakes out there. Photo via Katie Jackson

Western trails can be steep, technical, exposed, and rocky, but the long-distance trails are often graded with switchbacks and are designed for foot traffic. The AT, by contrast, frequently takes the most direct route up and down the mountains’ fall lines, and much of the trail is naturally composed of tree roots, mud pits, and slick rock.

My pace while hiking the Long Trail was slower than any long trail I’ve done before; to keep myself from falling on the challenging terrain, I sometimes had to slow down to nearly a crawl. This unpredictable terrain stresses my joints and muscles in a way that a clear singletrack doesn’t. I’ll be going into this trail with a persistent back injury, so I’m worried that five months of awkward steps will be a bit too much for my body to handle.

Not only is it very humid, but the trail itself doesn’t make it easy to stay dry. Photo via Katie Jackson

Even in the best of circumstances, I don’t sleep well on trails (or in normal life). One thing I do love about sleeping on thru-hikes is the total silence that comes with the backcountry. However, on the AT, I don’t expect to spend much time at all in the deep backcountry.

While the noise of nearby roads or towns isn’t the end of the world, I truly dread sharing shelters with the worst wildlife of all: snorers.

I know that hiker etiquette suggests choosing a different spot to sleep if you know yourself to be a snorer, but I’m also not naive enough to expect all hikers to follow that social contract. People suggest earplugs, but I’ve never found earplugs to actually block noise for me. Maybe I have oddly shaped ear canals, maybe I’m hypersensitive to the sound, or maybe I just haven’t found the right pair quite yet.

Either way, I worry that I will end up not able to utilize the convenience of the shelters because staying in one with a snorer will lead to a sleepless night.

Shelters seem so comfortable and convenient, but I know I need to prioritize being able to actually fall asleep. Photo via Katie Jackson

Hiking southbound means you are racing the calendar from day one, and as the weeks tick by, watching the daylight shrink is a constant stress. Out West, even when days get shorter in the fall, you usually have wide-open skies that let you stretch your hiking hours into the late dusk. Under the heavy canopy of the AT, once the sun starts dropping, the Green Tunnel goes pitch black in a hurry.

I’m not opposed to night hiking, but I don’t have a ton of experience with it. In order to meet the pace I’m hoping to keep on trail, I know I’ll have to get a lot more accustomed to it. That will either turn into a pleasant surprise as I learn I really love hiking after dark, or it’ll be a constant frustration.

The woods after dark can get a little spooky. Photo via Katie Jackson

While I love a trail town, the AT in particular seems like a trail that could suck me into a town and make it difficult to leave. Towns, I’ve found, are the single biggest cause of my spending on thru-hikes, so I want to try my best to avoid financial traps when possible. Hostels are unfortunately too comfortable and I love a meal from a restaurant too much to resist when I’m near one; and, on the AT, you’re always near one.

I don’t personally think that spending time in towns will affect my momentum or motivation, but I have a very specific amount of money saved for this hike and I fear for my budget. On the CDT, I spent less than $5,000 on my hike, largely by hitting the trail already in shape and minimizing my time in towns. On the AT, I worry that neither of these things will be possible for me.

I can’t help it! I love a side quest! Photo via Katie Jackson

I have never felt grosser than I did at the Northern Terminus of the CDT. Like many, I struggle to eat while hiking, especially if I’m trying to force down food I’m not thrilled about. Combined with the food desert that blankets much of that trail, I found myself eating pure junk for five months straight. By the end of the trail, my diet of Honey Buns and sticks of butter left me exhausted, poorly recovered, and craving a vegetable.

This is more of a general worry about thru-hiking than an AT-specific fear. This time around, I really want to force myself to eat better, but I worry I’ll run into the same problem with a lack of appetite. I’m not sure I can mentally or physically handle five more months of Little Debbie’s products, but I also know my body will need every calorie I can get my hands on.

I know I’m a generally stressed out person. Photo via Katie Jackson

While part of my affinity for cowboy camping stems from my laziness about setting up my tent, I truly love opening my eyes at night and seeing the wide expanse of stars in the sky with the Milky Way slashing across.

For so many reasons (bugs and rain), I know that frequent cowboy camping on the AT is generally a little less realistic. Even if I do choose to sleep out under the stars, the odds that I actually get a clear view of the sky through the trees feel low.

Even in the daytime, I’m massively attached to the wide-open sky sprawl of the West. I love seeing the weather roll in hours in advance. I love seeing glimpses of the trail miles away. Trading those sweeping views for something that feels a bit more sheltered and claustrophobic already makes me a little bit sad.

I love an open, expansive view. Photo via Katie Jackson

Opting to start southbound means beginning my hike in the relatively unforgiving Maine terrain. There is no gentle “ease in” period to build trail legs, such as what I had in New Mexico on the CDT. I’ll be thrown directly into some of the steepest and most technical terrain on the entire trail.

In my experience on the CDT, I felt physically the strongest near the middle but actually felt weaker and weaker as I hiked further into Montana and the hike started to wear on my body. Therefore, I’m trying to remind myself that Maine may be almost equally as challenging for me if I were to go NOBO; if my brain and body are exhausted from the culmination of the hike, I still won’t be in great shape to take on the climbs, even if I feel like I should be.

Still, knowing that the first week on trail will likely be a masterclass in physical suffering is a terrifying way to start a long journey.

I’ve done enough hiking in the White Mountains to know it won’t really get any easier after Maine. Photo via Katie Jackson

Pre-hike nerves are expected before a trail, or at least that’s what I keep telling myself.

I’ve loved every long trail I’ve had the privilege of hiking before, even the ones I didn’t expect to deeply love, so I’m excited to face these fears, figure out which ones don’t actually bother me at all, and work through the remainder while also discovering all the surprises about the trail that I love.
