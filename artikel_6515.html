The Chandra X-Ray Observatory Unmasks a Supernova Impostor

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

When is an apparent supernova not really a supernova at all? That's a tough question to answer, especially since no supernovae have been observed in the Milky Way in the telescope era. The ones that astronomers see are mostly many millions of light years away in distant galaxies.

SN 1987A was the closest modern supernova, but it exploded in the Large Magellanic Cloud, which is still more than 160,000 light years away. And that was before the Hubble launched, and studying its aftermath was difficult. Now we have automated supernova surveys that scan the sky for exploding stars. One such survey detected what seemed to be a supernovae in 2016, in a distant galaxy.

Sometimes, a star exhibits variations in brightness before it explodes as supernovae, and that can create confusion around what's actually happening. That describes the object detected in 2016.

That object was a massive star in a distant galaxy that's showing behaviour associated with a typical supernova explosion. Supernovae explosions are the final act of massive stars that reach the end of their lives, and their prodigious energy output can light up the sky. That's what this star does, sort of.

But unlike a supernova with a single explosion, this star has a volatile outburst every 113 days, something that no supernova can repeat. In an effort to understand what's behind these repeated outbursts, astronomers turned to one of NASA's workhorse space telescopes, the Chandra X-ray Observatory. Chandra's exceptional angular resolution sets it apart from other x-ray observatories, and it can pick out faint sources in detail at great distances.

The volatile star, named AT 2016blu, has about 33 solar masses and is in the spiral galaxy NGC 4559, around 30 million light years away. AT stands for astronomical transient, and blu is a sequential suffix that tells us the star was the 1,685th transient reported in the year 2016. AT 2016blu is also called a luminous blue variable (LBV), and was first discovered in 2012 by an automated supernova search.

LBVs are rare, and as noted above, exhibit powerful variations in both their brightness and their spectra. Their variability is usually considered to be unpredictable, but in this case, it's periodic.

New research in The Astrophysical Journal has an explanation for AT 2016blu. It's titled "AT 2016blu: Accretion-powered Outbursts in a Luminous Blue Variable and Compact Object Binary," and the lead author is Mojgan Aghakhanloo, a Virginia Institute of Theoretical Astronomy Origins Fellow at the University of Virginia in Charlottesville.

"We present the first X-ray detection of the supernova (SN) impostor AT 2016blu in NGC 4559," the authors write.

Astronomers are calling AT 2016blu a 'supernova impostor' because LBVs are often detected in supernova searches. "Supernova (SN) impostors are transient events associated with massive stars that are often initially discovered in SN surveys," the authors explain. "However, because their outbursts are less energetic and less luminous than true SNe, and because the star is expected to survive, they are subsequently classified as SN impostors."

XMM-Newton, an ESA x-ray observatory, also observed AT 2016blu four times while looking at NGC 4559 X7. So there's a collection of happenstance observations of the region.

Chandra's x-ray data from its 2026 outburst shows that AT 2016blu is 100 times brighter in x-rays than in its data from 2001 to 2002. This is best explained by a binary companion. "These outbursts are most likely triggered by periastron encounters in an eccentric binary system in which the primary is an LBV," the authors write. The secondary is almost certainly a neutron star or a black hole. As they orbit each other, the LBV ejects material that finds its way to the secondary. The material orbits the secondary at very high speeds in an accretion ring, and that heats it up until it emits x-rays.

Based on the x-ray luminosity, the researchers estimate that about 2.7% of Earth's mass is transferred during each outburst.

As is often the case, the researchers had to rule out a potential background source, and for that they relied on Chandra, too. "The alternative to a genuine counterpart to AT 2016blu would be a background quasar with a coincident position," the researchers explain. "This quasar, furthermore, would have brightened at a time coincident with our observations, and thus the AT 2016blu outburst."

Its discovery could shed light on other, similar transient phenomena. Many stars show changes in brightness before they explode as supernovae, and maybe some of them are similar to AT 2016bu. "The discovery of an LBVâcompact-object binary undergoing accretion-powered outbursts also connects AT 2016blu to a growing class of models in which massive starâcompact object binaries explain a variety of pre-SN transient phenomena," the authors explain.

The next step, as is often the case in observations of new types of astrophysical objects, is to find more of them.

"Expanding the sample of such systems is therefore essential to better understand the evolutionary pathways and physical mechanisms connecting SN impostors, LBVs, and interacting SNe," the researchers conclude.
