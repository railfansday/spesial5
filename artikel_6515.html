How Costume Designer Trish Summerville Reimagined ’70s Hollywood Style for ‘The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth’

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Quentin Tarantino’s Once Upon a Time in Hollywood is rich in striking, darkly memorable imagery: Margot Robbie shimmying in a sunshine-yellow two-piece at the Playboy Mansion; the sunbaked menace of Brad Pitt’s visit to the Manson ranch; the audaciously bloody denouement, all pit bulls and flamethrowers, playing out high in the Hollywood Hills, with the glittering carpet of nighttime Los Angeles extending endlessly beyond.

But for some—this writer included—the film’s most indelible legacy is its costumes. And, more specifically, those of Pitt’s laid-back (and possibly wife-murdering) stuntman Cliff Booth. Double-denim layers, gold-rimmed aviators, scuffed moccasins—and, of course, those Hawaiian shirts—the character’s late-’60s California cool burnt up the screen and almost certainly played a part in the Americana wave that plenty of menswear fanatics are still riding.

So, when David Fincher stepped into the director’s chair for The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth—the Netflix film picks up the story of Pitt’s character nearly a decade later—the task of evolving those indelible looks fell to one of the director’s most trusted creative collaborators: costume designer Trish Summerville. Indeed, the trust ran so deep (the pair had previously worked together on The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo, Gone Girl, and Mank) that Summerville didn’t even know what the project was when she said yes. “I’m always in for whatever he’s doing,” she tells Vogue. “I don’t know anyone else I would do that with.”

Brad Pitt as Cliff Booth, Elizabeth Debicki as Karen Crosby, Scott Caan as Charlie Peters, and Carla Gugino as Toni Becka.

Across the eight-year gap between the two films, a lot has changed in Hollywood—not least in its style. By the late ’70s, it’s the heady era of Studio 54 excess, punk-rock rebellion, and Halston halter necks. Still, rather than leaning into disco-fever, polyester-everything clichés, Summerville looked to the real-world period artifacts she could get her hands on locally, scouring the vintage mail-order catalogs and archival racks at Los Angeles’s legendary Western Costume, among other places. Under Fincher’s famously exacting eye, every garment had to serve the story without ever feeling like, well, a costume—and it was a challenge that required building nearly every piece from the ground up. (Though with a little inspiration from some select vintage garments along the way.)

Arguably most seductive of all are the costumes worn by Elizabeth Debicki: in Cliff Booth, the six-foot-three Australian actor plays Karen Crosby, an enigmatic, glamorous Hollywood studio executive whose crisp wardrobe practically gleams within the seedy underbelly of 1970s Los Angeles conjured by the film’s production designer, Donald Graham Burt. Dressed exclusively in varying shades of white—a strict mandate from Fincher that required extensive color-testing with cinematographer Eric Messerschmidt—Debicki’s looks deliberately stand out, but never feel disconnected from the world around her.

“Unless it’s something in the script that really wants the clothing and the costumes to jump off the screen, I never want it to distract,” Summerville says, a sentiment that could apply to the lived-in textures of her costuming work on Cliff Booth as a whole. “It should just help you watch this story.”

Below, Summerville talks figuring out exactly the right cut of 1970s denim for Pitt, looking to Lauren Hutton and Cher as muses for Debicki, and why the decade’s unstudied sex appeal continues to shape fashion today.

Vogue: Obviously, you’ve worked with David a number of times before—but how did this project end up in your lap?

Trish Summerville: It came up through Ceán Chaffin, who’s Dave’s producer. We met at a coffee shop, which was very formal for us, and she didn’t really say what it was—she just said that they had a project coming up, a film, and they’d like me to do it, but she couldn’t tell me what it was because it was quite secretive at that time.

I had to say I was in without knowing what it was. Because of my history with Dave and really liking working with him a lot, I’m always in for whatever he’s doing. I always hope I’m available when he has a project; I hope I’m asked to do it. The only thing I knew was it was a film and it would be in LA, and that was all I knew until I agreed. And then I got an explanation of what it was, and a text from Dave afterwards saying, “Yay, I’m glad you’re in.”

I presume you had seen the first film. Did you revisit it before starting work?

Yeah, I definitely had seen the first film, and after they did call me, I did rewatch it so I could get refamiliarized with the characters, but then I got the script, and realized there’s not a lot of overlap in the characters—it’s pretty much just Brad, so it’s a whole new story about him.

When we last saw Cliff Booth, it was 1969, and this film is set 1977. How did you first start thinking about the ways in which his look—and the look of the city generally—was going to feel distinct from the first film?

I did a lot of research. We have a couple of rental houses here that have pretty extensive research materials, and Western Costume is always a great place to start. They have a search library of periodicals, photographs from sets, behind-the-scenes stuff, and every kind of catalog you could imagine. So it was really important for me to look at everything from films in Hollywood that were happening, behind the scenes, the actors, and then also looking at everyday catalogs like JCPenney or Sears catalogs, looking at Vogue magazine, Ebony magazine—just so I could have a range. It needed to cover what’s depicted on screen as the movie within the movie, then also, how people dressed every day.

Even though there wasn’t fast fashion like there is now, in that amount of time you did see a shift in silhouettes across both men’s and women’s clothing—shapes of jeans, shapes of jackets, the jewelry. But with Brad’s character, it was really about keeping the integrity of who his character is, and the ease—Cliff Booth is really just a fucking cool guy. It was about shifting him a little bit: changing the silhouette of his jeans, giving him Roper suede boots instead of moccasins, not doing as many layers this time, having his shirts buttoned a little bit lower. He’s really, really comfortable with who he is.

Oh, and we definitely wanted to start the film off with the Hawaiian shirt from the first film, so the audience has something to immediately recognize and feel a kinship with.

Tarantino is known for having quite detailed descriptions in his scripts about costumes. Was that the case here?

Yeah, in some instances there were. Some of the descriptions of Cliff, it would say he’s wearing Billy Jack boots, or that he puts on puka shells for when he tends bar. But it wasn’t excessive. We stuck pretty much to anything that was a key story point, or that seemed really integral to the character—he’d maybe describe that he’s wearing sunglasses here, or a leather jacket. With Hot Rod, it would describe the style of clothing he would wear, but nothing as specific as a color or anything like that.

How much of the costumes were sourced, recreated, or made from scratch?

Pretty much most of it was made, at least for our principals. When it’s a period film, and you have stunts, and a really long shoot—we shot, I think it was 105 or 107 days—you really have to have at least doubles of things. It’s too scary. We kind of have a saying in the costume world: “If you have one, you have none.” For Elizabeth, we made her costumes, especially because Dave was very specific that he just wanted her always in a tone of white, and white is very scary in general.

Brad Pitt as Cliff Booth and Elizabeth Debicki as Karen Crosby.

And with Brad’s character, even the shirts that we found for him—like the Hawaiian shirt and the tiki shirts and the liquor bottle shirt—we bought rayon fabric and printed all those ourselves. For the liquor bottle shirt, one of my shoppers found a vintage one and sent me a picture, and I was like, “Oh my God, that shirt is amazing.” Then I found another one, and then one of the guys in our department said, “Wait, I think I have this shirt at home—I’ve had it for 20 or 30 years.” So we found three different versions of that shirt from the mid-to-late ’40s to the ’70s. I selected all the bottles I liked from the different shirts and changed the colors a bit, and the illustrator I work with drew up a pattern, and then we had a printer here in Los Angeles print up the fabric. We even built jeans for him—we found a Levi’s shape that was great on him, and then we built multiples of them. His barong—the shirt he wears at the end—we ended up making 68 of that particular shirt. It’s all embroidered, all natural fibers, because of how the shirt is used for him and his stunt guy.

Brad knows a lot about fashion—he has his own cashmere line, for one. Was dressing him quite a collaborative process?

It is. He does have opinions, and he’ll bring in inspiration pieces or things that he likes. Then it’s just trying a bunch of shapes, and retrying things once we build them. He really liked the idea of having T-shirts that leaned into an automotive inspiration for Cliff, which makes sense, and we chose to use that at the speedway in the figure-eight races. Working with the Evel Knievel logo, because Evel Knievel was so big at that time, and being a stuntman, he would really have an affinity for that. And then Brad was like, “Oh, I want a red, white, and blue helmet.” So we went to the props guy and said, “We’re going to have this Evel Knievel vibe for him for the T-shirts—could you have a helmet painted for him?”

Brad’s great about little details, like keeping on these native beaded bracelets that he wears always, which wears them in and gives them a lot of life. And he took some of the jeans and his bracelets home at the end, which is nice.

Tell me about working with Elizabeth. Was there anyone specific from Hollywood history or otherwise that you were looking to when figuring out her wardrobe?

I was really inspired a lot by Lauren Hutton for Crosby, because you can’t deny that if you saw Crosby, she could be a fashion model. Even though her character is rooted in reality, she’s very glamorous. Lauren Hutton had this really classic look to her, but very sexy, and with this edge—she would wear very drapey tops and really fitted high-waisted pants with silk blouses. There was also a little Cher vibe too, with the drapey halters. And Elizabeth was a lot of fun to dress. She’s really great about exploring character—with her, it was more talking about who Crosby is and what her day-to-day is like. I always try to work very closely with the production designer and the director of photography. Debicki’s going to be wearing white: how white can I be? Can it be optic white? Does it have to be a toned-down white? For some of her costumes, we had to do two different versions for when she was outside versus inside, because the tones read so differently.

Like Brad, Elizabeth looks amazing in those high-waisted trousers and flares.

She really does. It was so fun. In that figure-eight racing scene when she’s walking through between the bleachers up that little alleyway, it was quite nice to see other people in the cinema take a gasp—because she stands out so much there in this all-white look, so elegant and beautiful. And right before that, when she’s walking down the street at the premiere—so elegant and chic and regal.

Were there any other moments that, when you saw the film on the big screen, you were especially satisfied with?

It’s hard to choose, because I really loved the Manila stills that you see as flash-cut stills—that was several days of shooting, having this whole Filipino vibe, and these brothels, and a Filipino rock band to dress with big platforms and crazy makeup. I also love the scene when Hot Rod is leaving the movie set, and he’s in the velour one-piece Bordeaux jumper with the saddlebags, walking out and talking to Scott Caan. And seeing the tennis scenes—for us, the tennis section was such a big thing because we had to make so many multiples, again all in white, with body-makeup tanner getting on the clothing. And the Verdusco brothers! They would always come together to do their fittings—they never showed up solo—and after we took individual photos, they would always do a back-to-back pose or a karate pose.

As someone who immersed themself so deeply in ’70s fashion for this—an era that’s been cropping up all over the runways recently—what do you think it is about that decade’s aesthetic that remains so aspirational today?

I think it just was cool. People were very sexy without trying to be sexy. You didn’t have to have all your skin exposed—it was something about the silhouette and the drape of the fabrics and the ease. Silk blouses that are just a little bit open, thin gold chains, and the nice drape of the pants. People were so much more carefree then; music was big and fun, and you were out and dancing. I keep joking, “Oh my God, can we please just have jeans with no stretch in them?” Because it’s such a better fit, and the weight of that denim was really nice. Plus you had disco, punk rock, rock and roll: it lent itself to individuality.

Did any of that rub off on your own wardrobe after wrapping the film?

Yeah, actually! For the premiere, I wore the jeans worn by Lauren Glazier, who plays Raquel, Brad’s bartending partner. At the speedway, she’s wearing a pair of ’70s Levi’s with a good boot-cut flare to them, and I borrowed those and wore them to the premiere with a Tom Ford dinner jacket. I definitely had to wear six-and-a-half-inch heels to accommodate the length, because she’s quite tall and I’m not!
