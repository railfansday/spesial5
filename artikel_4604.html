Is Cheating in Marriage Ever Justified?

Posted November 23, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

“Yes, cheating can damage a marriage,” a couples therapist I interviewed a few years ago told me, “yet it can also make the marriage stronger.”

This approach is playing with fire. Most of us generally would not recommend such an activity, as we know that while fire can warm your home, it can also burn your house down. Instead, we would more likely suggest learning how to use fire responsibly—for the warming and cooking, not the burning.

We could also compare cheating on your spouse with taking care of your health. Living through cancer can become the catalyst for making better decisions about how to live your life. Yet I wouldn't recommend smoking two packs a day to attempt to induce this appreciation—as it can also lead to another outcome.

It’s the same with relationships. Betrayal can become the catalyst for candid discussions about what hasn’t been working, open conversations about intimacy, and a renewed appreciation of what you have. Alternatively, it can lead to the loss of the relationship—the scorched-house equivalent of playing with fire.

After infidelity, both people become aware that the other person was willing to take this risk. All the post-cheating epiphanies do not erase this awareness, and it feels extremely painful and difficult to assimilate for the person who was betrayed.

Why is this? Cheating is the ultimate form of narcissistic validation. It’s a trade: your short-term validation for your partner’s long-term suffering.

And suffer they will. “I will regret it for the rest of my life,” shares one woman in a recent survey. “My marriage was already falling apart, but I will never forget the look of hurt on my now ex-husband's face when he found out. He forgave me many years ago, but I still struggle to forgive myself some twenty-plus years later.”

To understand the problem with betraying your intimate partner, consider the words of Afghan-American novelist Khaled Hosseini. “There is only one sin, only one, and that is theft. Every other sin is a variation of theft,” shares Hosseini in The Kite Runner. “When you kill a man, you steal a life. You steal his wife’s right of a husband, you rob his children of a father. When you lie you steal someone’s right to truth. When you cheat, you steal the right to fairness.”

To make matters worse, when you betray another human being, it’s not just the right to fairness you are taking from them. You also take their right to believe in love, in acceptance, in belonging.

In other words, you take everything that most matters to them. “There is no more wretched act than stealing,” Hosseini concludes.

“At the end of the day, it wasn’t the cheating that hurt the most,” confides another woman, “it was the betrayal. Someone I trusted so much didn't trust me enough to simply say, ‘Hey, I'm unhappy, and I want out.’”

Despite the harm we do to others, in our fragile, volatile search for validation, many of us are capable of seeing past how infidelity will affect the other person. We often don’t see the forest when standing right in front of a tree. We don’t think about the trust that will be eroded, and almost always never fully recaptured, if we cheat.

Why? Trust in a relationship is like air. You don’t think about air unless you don’t have it. Then it becomes the only thing you think about.

When you convince another human being to build their life around you and then take that safety and security from them through betrayal, you dispel the closest approximation to sacredness you will ever experience in your life.

It is at this point that you learn that when you destroy what is sacred, you destroy the sacred within you.

Cheating, then, is not only an ethically flawed form of validation; it also injures ourselves. We need to find better ways to express our relationship dissatisfaction.

If your partner is unable to listen to you or adapt with you for a protracted period, separation may be a recourse. It’s very simple: If you can’t continue your commitment, tell them rather than going down the road of pretend. They deserve as much from you.

Once you’ve come clean, you can move in another direction in your intimate life—and seek the Holy Grail of external validation you’ve been craving—knowing you were honest and did what you could to preserve the sacred within your relationship.
