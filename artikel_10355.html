11 career decisions high performers regret making too late

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Some career decisions become costly only after too much time has passed. The following insights, from experts in the field, reveal which moves high performers often wish they had made sooner, from protecting their health to asking for help and building stronger networks. These lessons offer clear steps for making better choices before regret sets in.

Delegate Authority While Capacity Remains

The single decision high performers almost always make too late is firing themselves from day-to-day fulfillment, because their ego is still addicted to being the hero inside the company.

When I founded my first Scandinavian fine jewelry brand in Oslo at 22, I built our early momentum by personally touching every single bottleneck: approving every creative cut, negotiating every creator seeding deal, and rewriting campaign copy at 2:00 a.m. Because revenue was climbing and we were landing cultural placements like Khaby Lame and Vogue Scandinavia, I mistook exhaustion for leverage.

The turning point came when I realized that by being the best operator in the room, I had capped the company’s valuation at the limit of my personal nervous system. High performers wait until they hit burnout to build standard operating procedures and delegate authority, whereas you actually have to hand off the keys while you still have 40% left in the tank.

Once I forced myself to step out of daily order orchestration and document our visual and distribution protocols so the business ran without my fingerprints on every transaction, two things happened: Our enterprise value became transferable (allowing us to close a clean private acquisition exit in Q4 2024), and my own bandwidth opened up to direct film productions. If I could go back, I would have documented and delegated my favorite tasks 12 months earlier, not just the chores I hated.

Mommi Live, founder, managing partner & creative director, Mommi Limited

Archive Evidence Beyond External Platforms

Keeping their track record inside someone else’s platform.

High performers usually build their reputation where the work happens: an employer’s system, a marketplace profile, a client’s internal tools. It feels like the record will always be there. Then the job ends, the platform changes its rules, or the old proof simply stops being shown, and years of results are hard to point to.

I learned this with my own business. I started selling video editing on Fiverr in 2020, while I was studying computer science. That grew into Mark Studios, which has completed more than 8,000 orders and earned 4,500+ reviews, with a 4.9 rating and Top Rated status. But Fiverr only shows roughly the last two years of reviews, so the visible proof of the early years keeps shrinking even as the total grows. The work was real. The evidence just lived somewhere I didn’t control.

What I’d do differently, and what I tell anyone doing great work inside a company or a platform: Keep your own record from day one. Write a short case study after every project that matters, with the problem, what you did, and a measurable result. For a channel whose thumbnails and titles we’ve handled since August 2025, VidIQ showed views up 30.1% week over week when we captured the stats in May. That number now sits in a case study on my own website instead of only in a client conversation. Ask for testimonials you’re allowed to reuse, and save the numbers while they’re fresh.

Your reputation is an asset. Make sure you hold a copy of it.

Mark Santos, founder & creative director, Mark Studios LLC

Ask for Help Before Depletion

The decision I see high performers regret most isn’t burning out. It’s waiting until they’re depleted to ask for help, because by then the choice is already gone. Their body makes it for them.

I’m a licensed counselor supervisor and president of a South Carolina practice with more than 60 therapists across 10 offices, plus online therapy. I watch this play out in my own field constantly. The strongest clinicians I supervise are usually the last ones to bring a hard case to supervision. Asking feels like admitting they can’t handle it, and they’ve built their whole identity around being the person others lean on. So they carry it quietly. And it doesn’t show up right away. It shows up months later as irritability, or dread on Sunday nights, or a sudden wish to quit work they used to love.

What I tell them is pretty straightforward. Build the support in while things are still going well. Put a standing check-in on the calendar with a supervisor, a mentor, or a therapist before there’s anything urgent to report. When I started my practice with my husband, David, in 2019, I learned fast that the same logic applies to running a company. Support that you set up early costs you maybe an hour a week. Support that you set up late costs you a year of recovery. The best time to ask for help is when you don’t feel like you need it yet.

Kenza Haddock, president, Oceanic Counseling Group

Broadcast Accomplishments to Gain Advocacy

High performers are great at getting things done, often to the detriment of their career progression. If you’re so busy taking care of things, then communicating out what you’re accomplishing is often the last thing on your list. That’s either because you don’t want to brag, or you assume others know what you’re working on. Not learning to communicate up and out is often a regret. 

I’ve worked with a senior leader whose manager was let go. They established relationships with their new manager and also with other senior leaders in the organization. They learned to communicate regularly as a part of their role to inform the what and the why of their accomplishments through one reorg after another. When yet another manager was let go, they were promoted into the role due to the awareness and extended support they’d built. 

I’m now working with a younger employee with a new manager on how to own their weekly reporting, proactively communicating to the team what their weekly accomplishments are and how these fit into the greater goals. People can only advocate for you if they can say what you actually do. High performers often learn this alternate side of their role far too late.

Dana Zellers, executive & leadership coach, team facilitator, Dana Zellers

Own Your Professional Future

I’ve talked with more than a thousand executives in the past four years and the biggest regrets that I hear from high performers is relying on their employers to take care of them instead of taking ownership for their career decisions.

I have had more clients than I can count who worked for Fortune 100 employers for 20+ years. They believed that they would retire with that company, but then the business changed, they lost their job, and they were left trying to figure out where to go next.

No matter where you are in your career, you need to remember that companies are usually going to look out for themselves first. You need to plan your career accordingly. “Job hugging” has been the newest trend, where workers hang on to jobs that they don’t necessarily love. Sure, there is risk in leaving your job, but there is also risk at staying in your current job, especially if layoffs are on the horizon.

Staying open to potential opportunities, both actively through networking and passively through an optimized LinkedIn profile, is key to building a career that lasts.

Colleen Paulson, executive career consultant, Ageless Careers

Align Ambition With Desired Work

One decision high performers often make too late is to actively manage their careers rather than assuming that success in their current jobs will take care of that for them. Excelling at your job and managing your career are related, but they’re not the same thing. I’ve seen this play out in two ways.

First, high performers become exceptionally good at identifying what it takes to succeed within their organizations. They earn promotions, navigate politics, and build expertise and relationships. But they don’t always stop to ask whether what they’re building is what they actually want.

Over the last few years, I’ve worked with leaders who realized that the roles they’d spent years working toward had taken them further away from the work they loved, whether that’s working directly with clients or coaching their teams. We often think about the title and impact, but not the altitude we’ll have to lead at. A promotion often means moving away from doing the work yourself to creating the conditions for others to do it, through strategy, resource allocation, and management. That’s a very different job.

Second, high performers forget to build career optionality before they need it. They invest heavily in building skills, relationships, and credibility within their organizations. That’s important, but incomplete. Internal recognition matters, but so does building an external professional network. Think of it as a savings account you’re contributing to over time.

Too often, I’ve coached leaders who gave everything to their organizations, sometimes staying for more than a decade, only to find themselves facing a layoff, burnout, or a desire to pivot—and realizing they had limited external networks and little understanding of how their skills would translate elsewhere.

A few simple ways to avoid these traps:

• Before pursuing a promotion, talk to people already in the role. Understand what their day-to-day looks like, what they’ve had to give up, and what success actually requires.

• While you’re happily employed, invest in relationships outside your organization and learn about other industries. Volunteer opportunities and nonprofit board service can be excellent ways to do this while also giving back.

• The goal is not to constantly be on a job hunt. It’s to make sure you’re building a career that reflects what you want, with the flexibility to make different choices along the way.

Lisa Friscia, president and founder, Franca Consulting

Pursue Ventures Through Small Bets

One decision high performers often regret making too late is waiting to act on an entrepreneurial idea because it feels safer to stay on secure footing. With more responsibility, high performers can get attached to income or get sucked into “golden handcuffs.” They’re less likely to leave for a more enjoyable job or take a risk at starting something up. I have seen this with seasoned corporate leaders who had a viable concept for years, but once they were settled with a mortgage, family responsibilities, and a stable role, the leap started to feel like too much to lose. 

The regret often sharpens when they later watch someone else succeed with a similar idea, because possibility suddenly looks like proof that they could have moved earlier. What could have been done differently is keeping the risk muscle active through small, regular bets while still employed, such as testing the idea in low-stakes ways and building comfort with uncertainty. That approach reduces the gap between the idea and bringing it to market, so the decision is not one big, intimidating jump. 

Also, it’s important to have accountability partners who ask us hard questions or challenge the status quo related to our experience, comfort, and career. How much money is enough? When is the right time? I hear high performers regret missed opportunities with a radical role departure or a job abroad or starting something new, going back to school when they were younger. We feel guilted or bullied into the norm by our families, peers, neighbors and society to do the “right” and “responsible” thing, then later wish we had taken the road less traveled or taken a risk.

Janice Perkins, executive coach, Janice Perkins

Restore Health Through Daily Habits

The one decision high performers often regret is not focusing on their health and well-being as a function of improving their daily performance. 

All too often, high performers have had success all of their lives. They create habits in their 20s when their energy levels are high and their body can bounce back relatively quickly. As they start to advance their career in their 30s, 40s, and 50s, these high performers continue with these same habits, focusing only on work. Working late, missing sleep, skipping meals, and perhaps drinking alcohol (even in moderate amounts) drain more and more energy, physical and mental, from their daily output. 

To these habits, they add coffee to wake up and sugar intake to combat the energy crashes. This leads to poor sleep, and the cycle continues every day. 

It is possible to reverse these bad habits with small intentional changes in one’s daily lifestyle. A couple of simple examples are arising at the same time every day, including weekends, and getting morning daylight within the first 60-120 minutes of waking up.

Steve Fiore, executive performance advisor, Rooted in Curiosity

Serve Your Network Regularly

As a career coach, I usually connect with high performers when they are in the midst of, or planning for, a transition. The #1 regret I hear is that leaders feel they lost touch with their network while building their careers.

They understandably hesitate to make an ask when they haven’t connected in three, five, or even 10+ years. It can feel cringey and awkward reaching out when so much time has passed.

But now more than ever, your network is your career net worth. So I hope more high achievers keep that in mind.

The easiest way to keep in touch with your network? Be a helper.

Haley McNeel Drake, résumé writer & career coach, The Career Revisory

Exit Places That Diminish Your Worth

One important decision I’ve seen time and time again among high performers is not leaving an organization sooner when their value went unrecognized. Being a high performer doesn’t automatically translate to timely promotions or special accolades for great work. 

In the pursuit of success, a high performer can work to increase their visibility and yet those efforts don’t lead to a promotion. Not because they’re doing anything wrong or focused on things that won’t get them noticed, but because of office politics or favoritism. What people often overlook and hate to admit is that you simply may be in the wrong environment where no matter what you do, your value will be unrecognized. 

For high performers, staying too long in an environment after making efforts to be “promotion ready” will eventually catch up, once they end up in an environment where they are valued, appreciated, and recognized for their work.

Owning your competence, knowing your value, and being honest with yourself is part of high performance. Not getting a promotion the first time you ask for one isn’t necessarily a sign that you need to start looking or you’re being undervalued. However, the challenge arises when the same cycle repeats, and you’re getting empty promises instead of a kept word. 

Pay attention to nuance, yes: changes in the broader organization, natural attrition, etc. Timing is everything. But also, give yourself a timeline. If the opportunities you want to pursue don’t become available within that timeline, and those in charge can’t give you a blueprint to help you get where you want to be, you then make a different decision. And that could be planning your exit.

Lisa De Nicola, leadership & executive coach; founder, HER Lead; Lisa De Nicola

Broaden Expertise Outside Familiar Functions

One decision high performers often make too late is stepping outside the area where they’ve already proven themselves.

When you’re good at something, you tend to get more of it. You get promoted, take on bigger responsibilities, and become the person everyone relies on. That’s great for your career, but it can also keep you in a fairly narrow lane.

Earlier in my career at HD Supply, I spent time working directly with branch managers, and one thing I did was lead financial literacy sessions. These were for people making decisions every day about pricing, inventory, customers, and how to run their businesses.

I learned a tremendous amount from those conversations. They helped me understand the operating decisions behind the financial results, and that made me a much better finance leader.

Looking back, I would have sought out more of that exposure earlier rather than waiting for opportunities to come through my formal responsibilities.

My advice to high performers is to take on something outside your comfort zone before you feel completely ready. Spend time with customers, get involved in operations, or volunteer for a project that forces you to learn another part of the business.

Being really good at your current job gets you noticed. Understanding how the whole business works prepares you for the next one.

Thomas DeFabrizio, CFO, Vertage
