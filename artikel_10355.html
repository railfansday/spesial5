Grand Theft Auto VI, in many ways, tries to simulate real life. And life in 2026 would not be complete without a smartphone. GTA VI understands this and makes the phone not just a central part of the experience, but an object that exists within the world. You may try to escape into a fantasy of robbing banks and fighting drug lords, but you can’t get away from texts from your mom or nagging fitness app notifications.

Seeing a smartphone in a game isn’t a new phenomenon. The device has become a natural stand-in for the main menu in many games, giving players an interface that feels intuitive, because a phone is something they use every day. But the phone in GTA VI is different for a few reasons. One is that it behaves much like any other object in the game, whether it’s a gun or a bottle of beer. The action doesn’t pause when you use your phone. Instead, the game switches to a first-person viewpoint, where you can still walk around — or drive, if you’re feeling ambitious — while reading texts or flipping through a fictional version of Instagram.

Rob Nelson, the co-studio head at developer Rockstar North and producer on GTA VI, says that it would’ve felt “archaic” to have a static phone that worked like those in other games, or the one in GTA V, which appeared as a pop-up at the bottom of the screen across from the minimap. “We felt that it was important to make it physical so you have to look at it and put your attention on it, including while driving,” he explains. “It’s about finding the line between realism and player convenience. But the act of being lost in your phone felt like something worth doing, even though it could mean slightly more inconvenience. In this game you are committing to using it.” (Of course, texting and driving, like much of what you do in GTA VI, is both very dangerous and very illegal.)

You have to interact with the phone in the game constantly. Often the story plays out through phone calls and text messages, and you have a surprisingly large array of apps to use that serve a real purpose in the game. You manage your money in a banking app, use a rideshare app when you need a lift, track your workouts in a fitness app, scroll through a social network complete with comments and the ability to like posts, and sideload an AI tool that scans nearby cars so you can see how easy they’ll be to steal. You can even change the wallpaper and ringtone. (GTA VI has two main characters that you swap between, and each has their own phone, though they’re functionally identical.)

What’s most impressive is how all of these apps interact with each other. For instance, after pulling off a major heist, a friend might text you a news story about it, asking “was this you!?” You can then click the link, open up the news app, and read an actual article about what went down. These kinds of interactions can be functional, like when another character texts you a location, which you can then add to your map so the GPS can guide you there. Other times, they add texture to the world or depth to the characters. GTA VI is a love story about two criminals named Jason and Lucia, and you play as both. At one point, while controlling Lucia, I got a text asking for a selfie. I opened up the camera, shot a pic of Lucia drinking a latte, and sent it over. Jason replied immediately — and their relationship got a boost.

Phones are so fully integrated into the game that they can even interrupt the characters’ sleep. At the end of each day, you can click on a bed to rest for the night. But neither Jason nor Lucia will instantly fall asleep when you do — instead, they’ll sit down and pull out their phone, and you have the option to doomscroll or catch up on texts instead of going to sleep right away. Nelson says that this was implemented because the developers wanted to include the ability for the characters to sit on the bed at night, much like a real couple in a shared living space might do. “We needed something to do aside from just sitting there staring at nothing,” he says.

If nothing else, GTA VI’s phone shows how alike we all are: Even bank robbers being hunted by the police can’t seem to stop scrolling. “Phones are a constant part of our lives,” Nelson says of the evolution, “even more than they were in 2013.”
