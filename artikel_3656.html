Exclusive: See Camila Morrone in Netflix’s Sexually Charged ‘The Age of Innocence’

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

When Edith Wharton was in her mid-40s, she had a sexual awakening. For decades, the celebrated novelist had been stuck in a dull, loveless marriage to Edward Robbins Wharton, a fellow patrician 12 years older than her. Then she met debonair journalist William Morton Fullerton and fell—hard.

“I have drunk of the wine of life at last,” she wrote on May 21, 1908, in the document Wharton scholars call her “love diary.” “I have known the thing best worth knowing, I have been warmed through & through, never to grow quite cold again til the end.” Years later, she’d finally divorce Teddy and move abroad, where she’d channel these transformative experiences into her most celebrated novel: The Age of Innocence, published in 1920.

“I was at a place where I said I want to do anything except period, because I’ve done so much period,” says showrunner Emma Frost, whose résumé includes a trio of lavish historical series: The White Queen, The White Princess, and The Spanish Princess. But when Netflix asked if she might be interested in adapting The Age of Innocence, she reconsidered. Frost had read the novel and seen Martin Scorsese’s lush 1993 adaptation, starring Daniel Day-Lewis, Winona Ryder, and Michelle Pfeiffer. “I remembered it was the very first love story I ever engaged with that felt truly adult,” she says.

Then she learned that Wharton had actually written two alternate endings to her book—“and both the alternative endings have explicit sexual content.” It was a surprising development considering the published version of the novel, which is full of tortured yearning—there’s an achingly erotic moment concentrated on a character silently lifting a velvet ribbon to his lips—but contains no actual sex.

Her discovery led Frost to fall down a Whartonian rabbit hole. She learned everything about Wharton’s life-changing affair, devouring the 300 love letters the author had sent to Fullerton. “Once you actually delve into the Wharton archive, there’s all sorts of unpublished works that are explicitly about this experience of being a very sexual woman,” Frost says.

“The yearning, and the tension, and the longing, and the looks, and all of that delicious stuff is so important. I’ve been really obsessive in trying to capture that, and be very faithful to it.” But the sexy stuff in Wharton’s archive is what inspired Frost to make an Age of Innocence that’s not so innocent after all.

Ben Radcliffe as Newland Archer, Kristine Froseth as May Welland, and Camila Morrone as Ellen Olenska

As in the novel, her series—premiering on Netflix February 18—follows Gilded Age aristocrat Newland Archer, a gentleman lawyer preparing for a staid but satisfying life with his betrothed, well-bred ingenue May Welland. But his carefully planned future is thrown into disarray when May’s cousin, the independent-minded Countess Ellen Olenska, arrives in New York under a cloud of scandal, and with a highly unusual desire for this era: She wants to divorce her horrible husband.

Unlike the novel, Frost’s version also has nudity and graphic content: masturbation, voyeurism, a narratively important threesome. It’s not your grandmother’s Edith Wharton. But Frost would argue it’s very true to the spirit of its author.

And there’s more to her Age of Innocence than sex, of course. Wharton’s novel is told from Newland’s limited perspective; we only know what he knows, and see what he sees. Frost, though, wanted to make her version a true three-hander. “I think when you stay just with the male point of view, May becomes the antagonist, and Ellen becomes the thing to be won or saved or rescued. And that wasn’t something I wanted to do,” she says. “I wanted to have these two women really existing as flesh-and-blood women who we can care about.”

To accomplish this, she’d need a dynamic cast featuring The Witcher alum Ben Radcliffe as Newland, Kristine Froseth—who also stars in the Wharton-based Apple series The Buccaneers—as May, and Emmy nominee Camila Morrone as Ellen. Newland, says Frost, “doesn’t fit in with this society. He’s straining at the conventions. Ellen represents that other world, greater freedom”—the very freedom Wharton herself found when she ditched both New York City and her husband.

Radcliffe seemed to understand his character on a profound level. “There was a stillness that nobody else brought, and you could just see everything happening behind his eyes,” Frost says. Morrone, best known for playing a May Welland-esque character on Prime Video’s Daisy Jones and the Six, was similarly well-suited for hers: “She’s the most fearless actor I have ever met.”

Kristine Froseth as May Welland and Camila Morrone as Ellen Olenska

And then there’s May, who might be the trickiest member of the trio for modern audiences to understand. Unlike her fiancé and her cousin, she likes occupying a prescribed role in buttoned-up 1870s society—and she’ll do what she must to protect her perch, without malice or cruelty. Froseth found a way to convey the character’s depth and dimensionality.

“I always felt quite indignant on May’s behalf. All she ever did was fall in love with a man who courts her, say yes to a man who proposes to her, do everything right—and then along comes her cousin, and suddenly her choices are be shamed, be dumped, or fight for him,” says Frost. “Kristine just brings this light into the room. You love her, and you want her to be happy, and not to be disappointed. And that was the essential quality for me with May.”

There’s no real antagonist in Frost’s Age of Innocence. “I think it felt complex and morally right to keep saying, ‘There’s three people here that have feelings. What is the right thing in this situation?’” she says.

Kristine Froseth as May Welland and Ben Radcliffe as Newland Archer

Frost also wanted to flesh out the world of Wharton’s novel by adding mentions and subplots about real-world events its characters may have been aware of—like the Comstock Act of 1873, an anti-obscenity law that has never been repealed—and by introducing new settings like a boxing gym, where Newland goes to work out his aggression. In the series, that’s also where he befriends journalist Ned Winsett, a character from Wharton’s book reimagined here as a Black New Yorker played by Kel Matsena. “For me, it felt like a very organic thing to have non-white characters” in the series, says Frost. “They are the new ideas. They are the exciting conversations. They are the music that’s coming in.” Casting Argentine-American Morrone as the Countess Olenska, “making Ellen part of that influx, [also] felt right.”

As in any good period piece, the show’s costumes—designed by Daniel Orlandi, whom Frost calls a “bona fide genius”—and production design, overseen by Jean-François Campeau, are vital storytelling engines as well. “We always talked about these ridiculous little Victorians, trying so hard to believe they could live from the neck upwards—somehow divorce themselves from their animal nature and their bodies. The clothes and the sets were all part of that,” says Frost. “Weather and animals are really big motifs in the show. It’s about contrasting these people with the real, natural world and the weather that kind of goes, ‘Ha, ha, ha, you think you can somehow exist away from nature?’”

Camila Morrone as Ellen Olenska and Ben Radcliffe as Newland Archer

Though Frost and her team paid careful attention to detail, she cautions that sticklers may find things to quibble about if they examine the costumes carefully: “Our bustles are slightly smaller than they should be in the period.” And that’s without even considering the sexual aspect of the series. “Oh, there will be some pearl clutching,” Frost laughs. “Totally, I’m sure.”

But an expert in period drama like Frost knows when embracing historical accuracy actually matters, and when to let it slide. Wharton did too. While writing The Age of Innocence, Frost says, the author discovered that famed Swedish soprano Christina Nilsson did not actually sing Faust in New York in the year she'd set her novel—even though a performance of Faust starring Nilsson was the catalyst for her book’s action. Wharton wrestled with whether she should include the scene anyway. “And then she wrote to her sister-in-law and said, ‘I don’t care. Atmosphere is more important than historical accuracy.’”

“So I’m like, ‘Okay, Edith. If it’s good enough for you, it is good enough for me.’”
