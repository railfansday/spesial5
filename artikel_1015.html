By harnessing potent antibodies that can vanquish diverse viral strains, scientists are closing in on one of the most elusive goals in biomedical research: a vaccine for HIV. This year, several research teams described vaccination strategies that triggered the production of these broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs) in monkeys, and precursors to bnAbs in people, suggesting that an effective vaccine is possible. But devastating cuts to US funding for HIV research have left some efforts in limbo.

The world is closer than it has ever been to an effective HIV vaccine. It also has a drug capable of preventing infections: the long-acting injectable drug lenacapavir, which could avert an estimated one-third of new HIV infections in Africa if given to just 5% of adults. Now is the time for funders to ramp up investment, not cut it back, argues a Nature editorial.

Reference: UNAIDS & Gates Foundation report

Pregnancy causes drastic changes to the brain, but we know little about them because of past worries about the safety of scanning pregnant people. Now, researchers are working to fill that void by collecting scans from first- and second-time mothers and analysing thousands of archived brain scans acquired during routine clinical care. The work, presented last week at the US Society for Neuroscience annual meeting, could help to explain maternal mental-health conditions such as postpartum depression.

‘General purpose’ artificial-intelligence tools such as OpenAI’s GPT and Anthropic’s Claude are impressing researchers with their emerging biology skills, including protein design and structure prediction. In some tests, these models have even outperformed those designed specifically for biology tasks and trained on vast troves of protein-sequence data. Some scientists predict that generalist AI tools could eventually catch up with, or surpass, blockbuster models such as AlphaFold. Others suggest these new abilities could be the result of regurgitating training data, rather than advanced learning.

As I write, the United States army is preparing to detonate around 4.4 tonnes of unstable lead azide, a chemical used to make munitions, at an ammunition plant in Iowa. “It's essentially a lead bomb,” says occupational and environmental health researcher Patrick O’Shaughnessy. “Now it's a matter of how much gets ejected out of the bunker.” Academic researchers have banded together to gather independent public data about the plume of toxic lead dust that is expected to spread over the area.

Figure 1 | Position verification. a, To check the location of a party called the prover, two verifiers each send a random bit (a challenge), which are timed to reach the location simultaneously. The prover combines the bits and returns the answer. Because no signal travels faster than light, a prompt reply shows that the prover is at the location. b, However, if two adversaries, one near each verifier, copy a challenge and pass it to each other, they can answer in time even though nobody is at the claimed location. c, In Kavuri and colleagues’ scheme, one verifier generates two entangled photons, keeping one and sending the other to the prover. Both verifiers then transmit classical challenges. The prover uses the challenges to determine how to measure its photon and returns the result, while the first verifier measures the other photon. If the prover’s result arrives in time and the two measurements pass a statistical check (a Bell test), the transmitted photon must have been measured at the claimed location.

“Banks, power grids and satellite-navigation systems all depend on knowing where a request or a signal comes from, yet claims about location are notoriously easy to fake,” notes physicist Davide Bacco. Now researchers have outlined an experiment in which the position of a distant party is certified using quantum entanglement.

In a, two verifiers send random bits (challenges) timed to reach the claimed location simultaneously. The prover combines them and replies. Because signals cannot travel faster than light, a prompt response verifies the prover’s location. But two adversaries (b) can defeat this test by exchanging the challenges and responding in time. In (c), a verifier also sends one of two entangled photons, which are inexorably linked in a way that can be proven using a statistical check (a Bell test). If the prover’s result arrives inside the time window imposed by the speed of light and the photons pass the Bell test, the prover must be inside the target region. (Nature | 9 min read)

Chemist George Whitesides made landmark contributions to many fields, including patterning surfaces with molecules — a technique that enabled ‘lab on a chip’ diagnostic devices — advances in nanotechnology and more. He prided himself on switching research interests often, telling students, “If somebody else is working on something, don’t work on it.” In traditional chemistry departments, “there’s often a lot of pressure to stay in your lane, and he made it okay not to”, says polymer scientist Joseph DeSimone. “In fact, he made it exciting not to.” Whitesides has died, aged 87.

Read Whitesides’s guide to writing a scientific paper in a special issue of Advanced Materials that celebrated his 65th birthday.

Some mental disorders seem to take different forms in different places, writes mental-illness researcher Alexander Pereira. “Even depression – arguably the most common psychiatric condition in the world – seems to look strikingly different depending on when and where it appears,” he writes — with bodily symptoms, such as fatigue, prevalent in China, and psychological symptoms, such as low self-esteem, more common in the West. Pereira argues that our ability to adapt to our social environment might significantly shape the internal mechanisms of mental health.

Changing location — even just walking through a doorway — can signal to our brains that work is over and it’s time to relax, says cognitive scientist Joan Ongchoco. (BBC Future | 11 min read)
