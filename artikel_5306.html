Raleigh’s housing reforms helped ease rents, Pew study finds

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Pew reports Raleigh’s post COVID zoning reforms helped add 16,884 homes from 2021 to 2024. The city’s supply growth coincided with slower rent gains and a 15% inflation adjusted rent decline from 2022 to 2025.  AI Summary

For years, homebuilding leaders have argued that the most effective way to lower housing costs is to build more, and that cities that adopt pro-housing reforms will be rewarded with greater supply and more attainable housing. New research from The Pew Charitable Trusts shows what happened when Raleigh, N.C., followed that playbook.

Following the pandemic, Raleigh overhauled its zoning and land use laws to make building easier, Pew found. The result was a building boom, particularly for the townhomes and apartments the city’s earlier rules constrained. This uptick in homebuilding made renting more affordable in North Carolina’s capital city, especially for middle-income households and younger residents.

The report highlights how adding new housing supply at scale can improve housing affordability, echoing similar impacts that followed concerted rezoning efforts in Minneapolis-St. Paul, Minn., and Austin, Texas. The same dynamic is playing out across the Sun Belt, where many of the nation’s fastest-growing metros also have some of the loosest zoning rules, and where rents and home prices have dropped more dramatically than anywhere else in the country.

Raleigh didn’t rely on a single fix to make building housing easier. Its reforms combined a rewrite of its form-based code, an end to parking minimums, denser building near transit and a housing bond to support affordable housing.

“Of all of the drivers of housing costs – population growth, an influx of high-income earners, high land costs and high construction costs – we have the most agency and direct control over restrictive zoning regulations,” Patrick Young, Raleigh’s planning and development director, said in a statement.

Raleigh’s 2021 zoning reform, which expanded the city’s already-established form-based code, opened the door to more “missing middle” housing. It did so by replacing units-per-acre caps with physical standards such as lot size, yards, and building height.

The changes legalized duplexes, triplexes and quadplexes in more residential districts and expanded where townhomes can be built. Under the new form-based code, further refined in 2022, builders can now construct small apartment buildings and tiny houses with up to two dwelling units on compact infill lots.

As a result, builders can now construct missing middle housing in more of Raleigh’s neighborhoods, a shift that Pew links to better affordability for middle-income and younger residents.

Raleigh eliminated its citywide parking minimums in 2022. That allowed developers to fit more housing units on each property and, in some cases, spared them the expense of building costly parking structures, which can often make a project too costly to pursue.

The city also spurred housing growth by allowing taller, denser buildings near major transit corridors. Transit Overlay District zones allowed for an extra story along Bus Rapid Transit corridors, extending half a mile along some local bus routes.

Additionally, a density bonus allows developers to exceed height limits by up to 50% if at least 20% of the extra units are designated as affordable, below-market units.

The City of Raleigh approved an $80 million housing bond in 2020 that is projected to create at least 700 affordable homes, fund homebuyer assistance and help homeowners with home repairs.

About $14 million has already gone to four developments totaling more than 240 units, while another $24 million in gap financing will back over 470 additional units, more than 90% of which will be affordable to households earning 60% of area median income or less.

Between 2021 and 2024, Raleigh added 16,884 housing units, growing its housing stock by 7.7%, even as the city’s population grew by 5.8% over the same period. Raleigh’s housing growth also outpaced the state (5.7%) and national (3.2%) rates during that period.

Of the 16,884 units added, roughly 2,000, or 12%, were affordable to households earning 80% or less of area median income (AMI), with Raleigh’s housing bond funds helping support their development. Density bonuses contributed about 200 of those affordable units, accounting for 10% of the total.

Additionally, Raleigh’s code changes intentionally shifted a large share of production away from detached single-family homes toward townhomes, duplexes and tiny homes. Since 2021, the city has issued more than twice as many townhouse permits as in 2015 to 2020, and has permitted more than 200 duplex units and over 200 accessory dwelling units (ADUs).

As a result, average monthly rents rose at only half the national pace and fell in real, inflation-adjusted terms. Pew’s analysis estimates inflation-adjusted rents declined 15% from 2022 to 2025, and, as of 2025, the income needed to afford the city’s median rent dropped from 79% of AMI to 65%.

Raleigh’s experience mirrors a wider trend among fast-growing Sun Belt markets, where less restrictive zoning and fewer barriers to housing development have supported greater housing production and helped moderate housing costs. The South accounted for more than half of all U.S. housing starts between 2021 and 2025, while Sun Belt markets continue to lead the nation in rent and home price declines.

According to Yardi Matrix’s October 2026 multifamily report, rents fell year over year in 12 major US markets. The Sun Belt accounted for ten of those, led by Austin, Houston, Nashville, Tampa and Phoenix. The for-sale market shows a similar pattern, with home prices dropping disproportionately in the Sun Belt while climbing in the Northeast and Midwest.
