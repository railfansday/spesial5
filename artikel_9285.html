What Poetry Can Teach Us About Wonder: An Interview With Maya C. Popa

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

The most trusted resource for mindfulness and meditation.

An interview with poet Maya C. Popa on attention, uncertainty, and the art of remaining curious.

We often associate wonder with extraordinary experiences, moments of beauty or awe that stop us in our tracks. But what if wonder is also a way of paying attention, one that invites us to question what we think we know and remain open to what we cannot immediately understand?

Poetry offers a particular way of exploring this possibility. It asks us to slow down, look more closely, and make room for ambiguity, uncertainty, and experiences that resist easy explanation. In doing so, poetry may have something to teach us about presence, curiosity, and our capacity to find meaning in the world around us.

Poet Maya C. Popa has spent years exploring wonder through her doctoral research and her creative work. Her collection Wound Is the Origin of Wonder examines wonder beyond beauty and astonishment, while her newest collection, If You Love That Lady, explores the ways attention and experience can challenge our assumptions. In this conversation, Popa reflects on the relationship between poetry and mindfulness, the value of questioning our perceptions, and how cultivating wonder might deepen our connection to the world around us.

As a fellow graduate of NYU’s Creative Writing Program, I’ve had the opportunity to work with Maya over the past several years and follow the evolution of her writing. The author of three poetry collections and three chapbooks, and founder of the Conscious Writers Collective, she has devoted much of her creative and scholarly life to exploring how wonder shapes our  experience of the world.

Angela Stubbs: You’ve spent years exploring wonder both as a scholar and as a poet. Having lived with the subject for so long, how has your understanding of it changed??

Maya C. Popa: I’ve become more sensitive to how “wonder” is used conversationally, often as a substitute for admiration, surprise, or delight. It’s understandable, of course, because wonder can share qualities with the sublime or awe-inspiring. But my PhD focused specifically on the idea that wonder leads us to ask questions, which is where I think its true power lies.

My intention was to study wonder not merely through an aesthetic or critical lens, but as a fundamental human aptitude.

If we look at wonder’s etymology, it’s clear it’s not a tidy, straightforward, or uncomplicated experience. It derives from the Old English wundor, which some scholars have suggested may be related to German wunde or wound. The noun form means a surpassing or breach of the mind’s faculties, while the verb form means to demonstrate a state of admiration or astonishment, or to search for understanding or meaning. “The verb wonder,” writes scholar Daniel Fusch, “indicates an emotional response to a marvelous incident; the noun wonder indicates both the name for that response and the marvelous incident that provoked it…That is, at the sight of a wonder, we wonder; such are the beautiful complications of the English language.”

My intention was to study wonder not merely through an aesthetic or critical lens, but as a fundamental human aptitude that we could cultivate that ultimately serves as a vehicle for curiosity and empathy. Of course, I also believe it’s vitally important to live in a state of appreciation and gratitude, but wonder’s stakes and demands are psychically fascinating and deserve to be considered closely by teasing out these semantic distinctions. This allows us to fully own the power of wonder, not just treat it as another modern buzzword.

Angela Stubbs: You’ve spoken before about wonder as involving both knowing and not knowing. In the opening poem of If You Love That Lady, “At the Entrance of a Love Poem, I Hesitate,” there’s a sense that arriving at an answer can actually end the inquiry. What interests you about keeping a question alive? Is there something poetry allows us to experience by resisting the impulse to resolve or explain too quickly?

Maya C. Popa: Poems unearth and discover as they go; they are, as our wonderful Nobel Laureate Anne Carson suggests, “an action of the mind captured on a page…a movement of yourself through a thought, through an activity of thinking.” Wonder catalyzes the poetic impulse. I do think it provokes a generative restlessness that naturally seeks its shape in language.

I also believe that wonder can be passed from writer to reader.

I also believe that wonder can be passed from writer to reader. As Peter de Bolla suggests in his study of aesthetic experience, literary works of art are produced through the activity of reading. It makes sense—as we read, we’re responding in our heads as we metabolize it. Reading is inherently interactive, especially for writers.

So, to answer the question more directly, I absolutely believe in the value of keeping the question alive, and I think part of our sense of aliveness comes from that feeling of ongoing inquiry into life. Rilke advised the young poet to “Be patient toward all that is unsolved in your heart and try to love the questions themselves.” We can witness the results of our need for immediate resolution, certainty, and shortcuts everywhere in our culture. It’s at the heart of some of our greatest concerns (artificial intelligence, GLP-1s, etc.). There are an increasing number of ways around critical thinking. Sitting with, even taking as much enjoyment in the question as the solution, feels like an essential part of thinking and writing to me.

In the case of the opening poem, I wanted to begin with a self-interrogation that acknowledged the recurrence of love poems throughout history, and the ways that the beloved always seems to be situated at a distance, inviting the poet to write towards them to close the gap. Then there’s the notion, also helpfully introduced by Anne Carson, that desire requires a gap. So, this collection reflects a great deal on these questions.

I always think of Lucille Clifton saying that poems come out of wonder, not out of knowing.

Angela Stubbs: Throughout this collection, perceptions are revised: a mouse becomes a wren, a hawk moth is mistaken for a hummingbird. Do you see wonder as a willingness to discover that our first perceptions, or the stories we tell ourselves, are incomplete? Does this relate, in some way, to the writing process, which requires a similar receptivity? How do you know when to direct a poem and when to follow what it is becoming?

Maya C. Popa: I always think of Lucille Clifton saying that poems come out of wonder, not out of knowing. I value that kind of mental flexibility and capacity to revise one’s thinking or perception instead of stagnating or atrophying around a belief. That openness and capacity to evaluate and reevaluate as needed is invaluable to poets, who do this regularly throughout the process of revising. I certainly wanted to pay attention to this capacity when I noticed it in myself and deliver it on the page as precisely as I could. As a poet, I often feel I’m acting both as the lightning and the bottle that captures it at the same time.

Practically, I have to listen very closely for the deeper and more interesting questions beneath the surface of my writing as I go. What drove me to the page may not be what interests me by the end of many drafts. All of this requires me to set my ego to the side and be ruthlessly honest when something is not working, either because it isn’t rigorous or precise enough in its thinking, or it hasn’t yet found its music.

It’s rare that I’ll have a poem come together over only a handful of drafts; it’s more likely that I’m working on several poems over the course of a year or two, and many I will never share.

This requires me not to know for a lot of the time, and then, as I discover and distill what I’m doing, to make that discovery legible first to myself, and then hopefully to the reader. This takes revision in layers over many months or years. That is about the time it takes for me to really understand my own sound-sense on the page. It’s rare that I’ll have a poem come together over only a handful of drafts; it’s more likely that I’m working on several poems over the course of a year or two, and many I will never share.

Angela Stubbs: In “February Clear,” you turn from looking at the natural world toward the possibility that looking itself can change us. What is the difference, for you, between merely seeing something and giving it the kind of attention that might actually alter the person who is looking?

Maya C. Popa: I always think of the adage that we see things not as they are, but as we are. I also think that we often show up with our psyches in 12 different places at once. How often are we thinking of the thing we have to do next, the irritating conversations that we just had, what other people are doing on the internet, etc? It’s hard to stay in our own lane, and in our own moment.

[A]ttention and importance are also relayed and understood through language. If ours is significantly limited, how can we advocate for the natural world?

In that poem, I was trying to capture the experience of looking and really seeing, and acknowledging how often that isn’t the case. As someone who did not grow up with an agrarian vernacular, I also acknowledge that while my ancestors might have looked at green spaces and immediately conjured a palette of words for what they noticed, I can only name a handful of any landscape’s features. And those are mostly the obvious things: the hills, the grass, the rocks. I can’t name the kind of tree or type of grass. And this matters, because attention and importance are also relayed and understood through language. If ours is significantly limited, how can we advocate for the natural world? My friend, the extraordinary naturalist Robert Macfarlane, says that “we will not save what we do not love, and we rarely love what we cannot name.”

Angela Stubbs: We often talk about “cultivating wonder,” particularly in mindfulness and contemplative spaces, as though wonder were something we could deliberately produce. After studying and writing about it for so many years, do you think wonder itself can be cultivated, or can we only cultivate the attention, openness, and receptivity that allow us to recognize it when it appears?

If we treat wonder as being purely positive, as being in constant admiration, we will so often fall prey to our moods, which will make it hard or make it feel forced.

Maya C. Popa: I think we absolutely can and should cultivate a sense of wonder. But if we come back to its purpose as being partially to drive us to ask questions, not just to admire, I think we’re closer to the thing that actually counts, which is not taking things for granted. If we treat wonder as being purely positive, as being in constant admiration, we will so often fall prey to our moods, which will make it hard or make it feel forced.

Cultivating wonder initially involves paying attention a bit more deliberately than we usually would, before it becomes a kind of natural orientation. It’s slightly different from a gratitude practice, although it also gets us out of our sense of routine and shows us what is quite unlikely, the “marvelous singularity” of our human experience.

Rachel Carson said that “the more clearly we can focus our attention on the wonders and realities of the universe about us, the less taste we shall have for the destruction of our race,” and I stand by her thinking that wonder and humility are what safeguard us from egregious destruction. If we can wonder at the unlikeliness and distinctiveness of a human life, then we’re less likely to turn to violence. The more we develop our capacity to wonder, the greater our art, certainly, and the greater the hope that we will see our interconnectedness and protect and value each other.

Angela Stubbs is the founder of Pitch Perfect Media, a PR company dedicated to amplifying the voices of women and visionaries shaping culture and change. She holds dual MFAs in Creative Writing—one in Fiction from the Jack Kerouac School of Disembodied Poetics at Naropa University, and one in Poetry from the Creative Writing Program at NYU Paris—where her studies in contemplative education and poetics deepened her meditation practice. Angela leads heart-centered meditation offerings in Los Angeles and is currently at work on a poetry manuscript exploring memory, surrender, and the art of letting go.
