8 nonfiction books that delve into the darkest parts of sports

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

I took up running in 2010. I’d given up smoking and the weight I was steadily gaining was regularly making me cry. My partner at the time had been a serious competitive runner and believed I’d be able to just knock out an eight-minute mile.

In reality, it took me thirteen years and two nervous breakdowns before I finally ran a mile in 7:57, not long after I ran off (not literally) with a vicar. Somewhere in the middle of all that, I’d managed to reach number six in the UK for over-forties women’s tennis. It has gradually dawned on me that sporting success isn’t the healthy, glossy, happybodymaxx endeavour that shiny people on Instagram would have us all believe.

Here are eight books that each reveal a particularly dark but also compelling vision of hot sporting hell.

I read Open when I was on the verge of my own tennis-related nervous breakdown (alas not involving crystal meth like Agassi’s), during a heatwave in Nottingham, in a hotel room full of little flies that I’d become convinced were going to bite me and cause such a severe allergic reaction that I would die. The prologue is one of the best pieces of writing I’ve ever encountered: the extraordinary violence of a five-set tennis match, ending with the wounded competitors lying side by side, holding hands.

Then there’s Agassi’s father’s $10,000 bet (later reduced to $500) that his nine-year-old son could beat Jim Brown, the famous American Football player. The fact that the book was ghostwritten (by the wonderful J. R. Moehringer) gives it an additional layer of weirdness and gothic edge for me.

This is basically an OCD-fest (although officially Nadal does not have this condition) about Rafa being bullied to sporting success by his Uncle Toni. There’s a chilling story about Toni refusing to let seven-year-old Rafa have a replacement bottle of water to teach him a lesson about losing the first one (you don’t need a psychotherapist to make the link with the water bottle shenanigans on court), and another about telling him he must never make himself the center of attention, even when he was the best tennis player in the world.

Even as an adult, Nadal describes extraordinary anxieties, including the belief that if he makes a small mistake, his mother might die. The contrast between the superhuman control Nadal displays on the tennis court and the terrifying lack of control he feels everywhere else is what makes this such a disturbing and compelling memoir.

Serena Williams (with Daniel Paisner), My Life: Queen of the Court

There’s a brilliant scene in the incredible Teddy Perkins episode of Atlanta where Teddy, played by Donald Glover, does a roll-call of famous fathers who have abused their children towards greatness. He mentions Joe Jackson, Tiger Woods’s father and Richard Williams.

Like Andre Agassi’s father, Richard Williams was seemingly determined to create a tennis champion, trying the sport with his older daughters before it finally stuck with his youngest two, Venus and Serena. He would take them to the local tennis courts in Compton with shopping trolleys full of tennis balls. Initially, Venus was the more successful and Serena was determined to beat her own sister so she did not simply become a footnote in Venus’s story.

Swimming Studies was actually the book that set me off on my disastrous tennis adventure. It was a single line in it, something about how Leanne Shapton, recovered from her dark sporting childhood, was still placing in “age-group” events. It was those two words, age-group, that made me think perhaps I could resurrect my childhood tennis dreams.

When I read Swimming Studies, I was myself trying to swim seriously, but ended up with breaststroker’s knee—which is odd, as Shapton suffered exactly the same thing. I felt a little like the book was haunting me, and that I was literally absorbing its ailments quasi-gothically.

Brad Gilbert (with Steve Jamison), Winning Ugly

This was basically my bible during the year I played competitive tennis. Actually, that’s wrong. The Inner Game of Tennis was my bible. This was my grimoire. This may be one of the most fun dark books ever written.

Basically, Brad Gilbert tells you how you can win at tennis (and maybe even life too)—even if you are shit. You do things like turn up early and have a hit on the court with a local coach (impossible when I tried it), then shower, change and arrive late for the match and suggest starting with no warm up. Gilbert has lots of handy hints for ways to deliberately psych out your opponents with chat specifically designed to derail them.

I really loved Finn’s previous book, Running with the Kenyans, which begins with a stirring account of him unexpectedly winning a 5K fun run. After that he goes to Kenya, meets some Big Characters who introduce themselves with their marathon PBs, and becomes himself a more excellent runner. It’s uplifting and inspiring.

But this book about Japanese running methods is far darker. Finn begins this one battling injury and exhaustion and then encounters the weird, workaholic training methods of the Japanese, including running with exaggeratedly short strides and accelerated cadence. There was something bleakly joyless about this book compared with the first one, but I still hoovered it up.

Reading Born to Run isn’t like reading a normal book—it’s an era of your life. My heart skips and I get goosebumps just thinking about it. The ecstatic descriptions of people for whom running is both a way of life and a form of transport, and the theories about primal living, squatting and running barefoot, would go on to underpin my philosophy of sport and life for years to come (and still do).

After reading this book I became a primal forefoot runner. My friend Vybarr Cregan-Reid, author of Footnotes: How Running Makes Us Human, went totally barefoot, despite various broken-glass incidents in British parks. I will never forget the wonderfully compelling romance of Jenn Shelton and Billy “Bonehead” Barnett, and how they would eat pizza and drink Mountain Dew in the middle of their ultras, like running was something even Lana Del Rey could do.

Oh, how I loved this dark and crazy book that made running seem like part religion and part disco-party where everyone successfully hides their pain.

W. Timothy Gallwey, The Inner Game of Tennis

The Inner Game of Tennis changed my entire life and philosophy, and there’s nothing darker than that. I found myself using it as a way of understanding EVERYTHING. The concept is that you have two selves. Self 1 and Self 2. Self 2 will quietly do things if you just leave it alone, but Self 1 is constantly judging. During my disastrous tennis adventure, I could never really get rid of Self 1 and its constant voicings of my coming glory, imminent failure, place in the rankings, what I’d tell my coach, etc.

One of the tricks Gallwey recommends is reading the writing on the tennis ball as it comes towards you. I’ve encountered this kind of method in other places too, like reciting your own phone number backwards during a panic attack.

Another dark sports book I still live by is Steve Peters’s The Chimp Paradox, which has some similar ideas about quieting the crazed ego-animal within. Ultimately, if you can let Self 2 take control, you can enter the mythic space of the Zone or Flow, which is, according to Daniel Kahneman’s incredible book Thinking, Fast and Slow, the most optimal place a human can reach.

Scarlett Thomas was born in London. She is the author of highly acclaimed contemporary cult novels including The End of Mr. Y, which was longlisted for the Orange Broadband Prize for Fiction; Oligarchy; The Seed Collectors; PopCo; The Sleepwalkers; The Runner; and the Worldquake series of middle grade books. Her novels have sold over half a million copies worldwide and been translated into twenty-six languages. Scarlett is professor of creative writing and contemporary fiction at the University of Kent.
