Subcompact SUVs May Seem Appealing, but Compact Cars Are Often a Better Buy

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Unless you’re dead set on the tall seating position that an SUV provides, we think these small hatchbacks are far more satisfying than their crossover siblings.

Sure, SUVs are popular, but that doesn’t mean they’re always the best choice for your needs. The smaller an SUV gets, the fewer advantages it offers when compared with an equivalent sedan or hatchback. Here, we’re looking at the subcompact SUV segment and stacking up these small crossovers against compact cars from the same brand.

Subcompact SUVs are the smallest of their species. As the name suggests, they’re smaller than compact SUVs, and their tidier footprints often mean compromises in space, fuel economy, and driving satisfaction.

Automakers feel the need to offer additional ground clearance and all-wheel drive in these SUV models to justify their categorization. But these models typically share the same underpinnings and engines as their compact-car kin.

This means these SUV models are often heavier, less enjoyable to drive, and less fuel-efficient than their non-SUV siblings. Both types of vehicles also typically offer similar amounts of cargo and passenger space. Pricing, meanwhile, tends to favor the compact hatchbacks.

If these two types of vehicles are so similar, then why the different classifications? Blame size creep.

Cars and SUVs typically get larger with each passing generation. Let’s roll the clock back 25 years. While most modern compact cars are bigger than their counterparts from a quarter century ago, they still tend to cast smaller shadows than mid-size cars from the turn of the millennium.

On the other hand, the subcompact SUVs of today are typically larger than the compact SUVs of 25 years ago. Due to this, calling a modern SUV “subcompact” better describes its size relative to the overall SUV landscape as opposed to the larger passenger-vehicle space.

Odds are that if you’re shopping for one of these small-sized SUVs, you’re going to be just as well—if not better—served by these vehicles’ often less expensive and more fun-to-drive compact hatchback stablemates.

The HR-V is Honda's smallest SUV, but it’s still plenty spacious inside. The main downside of this model is that it’s available with only one powertrain: a naturally aspirated 2.0-liter inline-four that produces just 158 horsepower.

This means the HR-V accelerates at a leisurely pace. In our testing, this little Honda SUV needed 9.4 seconds to hit 60 mph.

The HR-V earns an EPA-estimated combined rating of 28 mpg in front-drive form. Adding all-wheel drive drops that figure to 27 mpg.

The Honda Civic hatchback has the same base engine as the CR-V, but it also offers a more powerful and more fuel-efficient hybrid variant that has quicker acceleration and much better fuel economy—up to 49 mpg combined, according to the EPA. The Civic doesn’t offer all-wheel drive as the HR-V does, but we’d advocate for a good set of winter tires instead.

In our testing, the Civic hatchback’s cargo area was only marginally smaller than the HR-V’s. It held six carry-on suitcases behind the rear seat, which is only one fewer case than the HR-V can accommodate.

More importantly, the Civic is far more fun to drive than the HR-V.

The Subaru Crosstrek is the spiritual successor to the old Impreza Outback Sport, which was essentially an Impreza hatchback with added body cladding. Compared to the old Outback Sport, the Crosstrek is a bit more distinct than the Impreza it’s based on. Even so, the Crosstrek is still effectively a lifted Impreza hatch with additional ground clearance and more rugged looks. As such, we don’t think the Crosstrek is worth the extra cost over the run-of-the-mill Impreza hatchback that Subaru still offers.

Subaru makes an even better case for the Impreza hatch for 2027 courtesy of a new base model that starts at less than $26,000. As we previously detailed, the Impreza beats the Crosstrek in nearly every metric in our testing: It’s quicker, more fuel-efficient, and offers as much space inside. The only reason we can think of to choose the Crosstrek is for its available gas-electric hybrid powertrain or its extra ground clearance.

A redesign for 2027 brings eye-catching new looks to the Kia Seltos subcompact SUV. A 147-hp four-cylinder comes standard, while a 190-hp turbo four is optional. For its second generation, the Seltos also offers an available gas-electric hybrid powertrain—an option the Kia K4 compact hatch lacks.

Sure, the Kia K4 hatchback doesn’t offer hybrid power. However, what this handsome hatch lacks in powertrain options, it makes up for in other ways.

Simply put, we prefer the way the K4 drives to the Seltos. Credit the compact hatchback’s lower center of gravity, which improves handling and behind-the-wheel engagement.

The K4 is also more miserly than the equivalent gas-only Seltos. When fitted with the available 190-hp turbocharged 1.6-liter four-cylinder, the K4 hatch is also notably quicker than its equally powerful Seltos kin. In our testing, a so-equipped K4 hatch accelerated to 60 mph in 7.0 seconds, while a turbocharged Seltos needed 8.2 seconds to hit the same mark.

We haven’t yet measured how many carry-on suitcases fit in the cargo bay of the new Seltos. Compared to the previous-generation Seltos, though, the K4 hatch fell one carry-on suitcase short of its subcompact SUV sibling’s sum.

The Mazda CX-30 is among our favorite subcompact crossover SUVs. It drives great, features a high-quality interior, and provides sufficient oomph with the standard 186-hp 2.5-liter four-cylinder engine. Opt for the 250-hp turbocharged 2.5-liter, and the all-wheel-drive CX-30 accelerates with even more vigor. But what if Mazda also sold a vehicle that paired all the positive traits of the CX-30 with a lower starting price, even better driving dynamics, and an available manual gearbox? Well, good news: It does.

The Mazda 3 hatchback may do without the higher seating position of the CX-30, but it makes up for it with livelier dynamics courtesy of its lower center of gravity and lighter curb weight. At a manufacturer-estimated 3111 pounds, the front-drive 3 hatch’s mass undercuts that of the all-wheel-drive CX-30 by 284 pounds.

While we think the available six-speed manual gearbox of the front-drive 3 makes it superior to its all-wheel-drive kin, we know not everyone will agree with us. No worries, though, because Mazda also offers the 3 with all-wheel drive. Opting for it adds 172 pounds to the curb weight of 3 hatchbacks with the standard 186-hp four. However, AWD is standard on turbocharged models.

In our testing, both the 3 hatchback and CX-30 were able to hold up to 16 carry-on suitcases with the rear seats folded. With the rear seats in place, the 3 hatch’s cargo area held five carry-on suitcases—just one fewer than the CX-30.

Acura’s subcompact SUV is about as interesting and exciting as watching C-SPAN. Short of its available all-wheel-drive system and slightly bigger interior compartment, there are few other reasons to pick the ADX over its compact hatchback sibling, the Integra.

With a starting price of $34,795, the Acura Integra undercuts the ADX by a significant $1755. It’s also significantly more enjoyable to drive and a good deal quicker.

Though both the Integra and ADX come standard with a turbocharged 1.5-liter four-cylinder engine that mates to a continuously variable automatic transmission (CVT), the Integra benefits from an extra 10 horsepower and 13 pound-feet of torque relative to the unit in the ADX. With 200 horsepower and 192 pound-feet of torque, a CVT-equipped Integra accelerated to 60 mph in a reasonable 7.1 seconds. An all-wheel-drive ADX, meanwhile, required a languid 8.7 ticks to hit 60 mph.

Admittedly, the fastback shape of the Integra cuts into its cargo-carrying capabilities. We fit 18 carry-on suitcases in its cargo bay with the rear seats folded and five with the seatbacks up. The squarer shape of the ADX afforded totals of 22 and seven, respectively.
