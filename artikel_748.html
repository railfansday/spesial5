Phoebe Bridgers’ Skeletal Transformation on ‘SNL’ Wasn’t Just a Startling Special Effect — It Emotionally Elevated One of the Best Pop Songs Ever Written About Death

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Senior Music Writer and Chief Music Critic

On her first appearance as a solo artist on “Saturday Night Live” since 2021. Phoebe Bridgers didn’t break any guitars this time. But maybe some hearts.

Or perhaps the impact was felt more as a chill down the spine. However it was received, Bridgers’ performance of “Still Standing” turned out to be a spooky stunner, pairing her already personal and emotional lyrics with the coup de grace no one saw coming: her face’s sudden transformation into a skull.

If this were any halfway-standard “SNL” musical number, the visually shocking special might have seemed like a belated Halloween stunt. But “Still Standing” is an extraordinary piece of work, focused, as any serious fan well knows, on her father’s death. So at a key moment near the end of the solo acosutic performance, the abrupt transition gave an extra jolt of life, so to speak, to one of the best modern pop songs ever written about death and how we process it.

On the most mundane (but still fascinating level), there’s the question of how they pulled it off. It was a practical effect, and not a digital one. Bridgers was wearing UV-sensitive skull makeup under her other makeup for her entire time on “SNL,” including her preceding number. The changeover almost felt like a jump cut, but it was black lights making the hidden skull come alive.

It was spooky, but spooky with a point: Without trying to put too definite a meaning on it, it was as if Bridgers was having to go over to the other side, herself, to figure out how to grapple with a semi-estranged family member’s passing and legacy, since trying to get that right on this side of the veil is a pretty effed-up mess.

Besides “Still Standing” having been a widely picked-over standout track on her recent “Lost Weekend” album, it’s been at the forefront of many fans’ minds because of the central place it has on the “Lost” tour that just swung through U.S. arenas. On a nightly basis, the most intimate moment has come when Bridgers would sit on the B-stage and confide a few thoughts about her dad’s death — unshared to social media, since phones were locked up — before singing this most confessional of confessional numbers. If they gave Grammys for Most Beautifully Shocking Number That Is Barely Whispered, “Still Standing” would be the shoo-in.

The song deals casually and yet joltingly with a past history of family abuse, physical and psychological, as well as just indifference, and how a child perceives it all. Mixed emotions come at nearly every turn in the lyric: “You tried to make me afraid / We were both shining.” In the final stanza, she emphasizes the finality of someone disappearing off the earth forever, as she does elsewhere on the album, refusing to sentimentalize any aspect of it: “Sometimes I want you back / But not today… / I can’t imagine you dead / But you are every day / Thank you for everything.” The details are specific, but probably more people than not will be able to relate to the emotion at some point in their lives, of how much to grieve someone who caused a lot of grief in his lifetime. That’s all heavy stuff… and heavier when the final plaintive notes have a skeleton singing close up to you on your TV screen.

But it was also only natural that, if she was going to sing the saddest song from her album, she would counter it with the happiest: “Bobby.” That contrast is endemic to the entire album, which is fueled just as much the ebullience of a growing relationship (with Bo Burnham unmistakably understood to be the Bobby in question) as by the darkness that comes with losing one of the crucial figures in your life. Albums don’t get much more yin-yang than “Lost Weekend,” and with two slots to fill on “SNL” — and a lively band in tow to help fill the one for “Bobby” — Bridgers got to reestablish her breadth in the briefest and best way possible.

The “SNL” version of “Bobby” was pretty close in spirit to the recorded version, but with one crucial difference: more yelling. For a moment or two, she got so close to a scream, it seemed like she might break into “I Know the End,” the song where she and the audience traditionally really let it all out. But this was happy howling. “This could be the end of wanting,” she sang in the refrain, and then just “This could be the end,” over and over, this time as a promise instead of a threat. In contrast to the other song choice, it was great to so vividly see and hear a Bridgers who’s in the delightful throes of just knowing the beginning.
