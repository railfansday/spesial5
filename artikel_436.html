COP31 draft deal calls for 'immediate' shift away from fossil fuels

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

The preliminary agreement at UN climate talks in Turkey calls for a rapid reduction of planet-warming emissions and at least $120 billion to help countries adapt to climate change.

COP31 co-host Australia has kicked off the second week of climate talks in Antalya with a draft political deal calling for an "immediate transition away from fossil fuels" and a "tripling" of finance for climate adaptation measures in developing countries to at least $120 billion (€109 billion).

The goal of other co-host Turkey to increase electrification of the world's economies to 35% by 2035 also features in the text. The draft emphasizes clean energy but notes that "electrification can provide co-benefits regardless of the electricity mix" — an approach that has proved divisive at the talks, with many insisting that electrification be strictly tied to renewable energy.

Language around a faster phaseout of oil, coal and gas is important because fossil-fuel producing nations have repeatedly pushed back against stronger wording in previous COP decisions.

"The new negotiating text contains all the necessary building blocks for a successful outcome to the conference: from the goal of climate neutrality for all industrialized countries by 2030 to a work program that can keep the 1.5-degree limit in place," said Greenpeace Germany head Martin Kaiser on Monday.

The draft expressed "deep concern" that the 1.5 Celsius (2.7 Fahrenheit) temperature threshold agreed at the 2015 COP in Paris to avoid runaway climate change was now out of reach. It stressed the need for "accelerating action to minimize both the magnitude and duration of any temperature overshoot and to return global warming to below 1.5C as soon as possible."

Recognizing a need to address rising carbon emissions, the draft highlighted efforts to "urgently and rapidly" scale up controversial technologies to capture and store CO2 to bring down atmospheric carbon levels.

"For the first time, all the parties here are grappling with the fact that the world will inevitably exceed 1.5 degrees. Negotiators must deliver a response," said Ani Dasgupta, president of the World Resources Institute, adding that this meant going beyond slashing emissions.

"This requires scaling carbon removal through both nature-based solutions like restoring forests and wetlands, or emerging technologies that directly capture carbon from air," he told reporters.

Kaiser, however, criticized the lack of binding commitments in the draft's resolutions. He added that Europe should work with China and the most climate-vulnerable countries to keep oil-producing nations, including Russia and countries in the Middle East, from "undermining the conference's decisions and turning them into a free pass for the oil and gas industry."

Other activists have dismissed carbon capture as a "false solution" being promoted by corporations.

Cosima Cassel, an expert at climate think tank E3G, was cautiously pleased with the draft's "ambitious language" but said it appeared the broad deal would now leave negotiators to "fight for trade-offs."

"The test will be whether the final decision keeps the core elements once that process plays out: ambition on overshoot, defense of the science, clean electrification and forward progress on adaptation finance," she said in a statement.

Disagreements over financing largely relate to who should bear the costs of international climate action, whether financial support should be provided through grants or loans, and how funding for developing countries can be increased.

Representatives of developing nations and small island states, regions bearing the brunt of the climate crisis, have protested climate inaction and a lack of financial support in recent days.

"Climate finance isn't an extra at these talks: it's the condition for everything else," said Rukiya Khamis, program manager for East Africa with environmental group 350.org. "It's alarming how financial pledges by wealthy, polluting countries are dropping like a stone — COP31 must stop this with a strong outcome on adaptation finance."

"Now we must fight to keep this [adaption finance] in the final decision and ensure these promises become real, grant-based money," said Harjeet Singh of the Fill The Fund campaign, which calls on rich countries and corporations to deliver the funding needed for climate-related loss and damage, such as homes destroyed by floods and harvests ruined by drought.

"Communities are suffering devastating losses and damages today precisely because we failed to deliver adaptation at the right time," added Singh.

At 18 pages and 144 items, the draft will be the focus of lengthy discussions in the coming days as delegates try to come to an agreement by Friday. Some observers went so far as to say the document's scope was too wide to be taken seriously.

"The draft final declaration reads like a Christmas wish list," said Niklas Höhne, a climate policy expert at the NewClimate Institute in Germany. "There are even several points on which we will obviously not be able to reach a consensus, including for example the proposal that developed countries achieve net-zero emissions by 2030."

But Chris Bowen, chief of negotiations and Australia's minister for climate change and energy, appeared optimistic.

"The general tenor and tone [of the talks] has been very constructive," he told reporters Monday. "Parties have come to me with strong views about various things, as you'd expect […] [but] they've also come in the spirit of cooperation, the spirit of respect and a spirit where parties have said to me that they want to help get an outcome."

David Waskow, director of WRI's International Climate Initiative, said the draft text showed a level of "seriousness" on important issues like finance, electrification, oceans, overshoot scenarios and emissions of greenhouse gases including methane, which has a warming impact more than 80 times stronger than CO2.

"The question is whether the level of ambition on those issues is carried through into the final outcome here," said Waskow. "And that's what we need to be watching closely over the coming days."

Additional reporting by Tim Schauenberg in Antalya.
