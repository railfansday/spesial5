When Construction Professionals Retire, Their Knowledge Shouldn't Retire With Them

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Ronnie Sheth is the founder and CEO of SENEN GROUP.

A CEO with three decades in the field once told me he can tell whether a project will succeed within its first few weeks. He looks at two things: the project manager and the client. A head of engineering at another firm told me he can look at a design and know what will work in the field. ​

I’ve seen what happens when people like them retire. What they know leaves with them. ​

Many experienced construction professionals are now close to retirement age; according to a 2025 report by The Center for Construction Research and Training, in 2024, “1 in 5 construction workers were 55 years or older.” Additionally, the Associated Builders and Contractors explained that “In 2027, the industry will need to bring in 456,000 new workers to meet demand as construction spending growth is poised to resume for the first time in years.”​

When that CEO knows a construction project is in trouble, he isn’t guessing. He is matching what he sees against past projects, such as a project manager stretched too thin or a client who keeps changing scope.

Many of those signals already sit within schedules, cost reports, change orders and daily logs. The issue is that they’re scattered across various systems, so finding them can take hours each week. ​

​AI can help construction teams by learning the patterns a firm’s best people carry in their heads and putting those patterns in front of the entire team across projects. While AI models can’t fully replicate the intuition, context and judgment that come with years of hands-on experience, they can recognize and surface some of these patterns. Experts should still make the hard calls, but AI can enable the rest of the team to see what the experts see. ​

The Associated General Contractors of America and Sage surveyed 951 firms for their 2026 outlook. Out of the respondents, 61% indicated that “their firms use AI or plan to increase investments in it,” compared to 44% a year earlier. The survey also asked how respondents use AI. The majority (45%) said they “deploy AI for office and administrative functions.” Other use cases included estimating (23%) and design or preconstruction (20%). Additionally, 16% said they use AI to recruit and train employees, as well as for other HR tasks.

AI can help with work that occurs in the office. But in my view, AI can play a bigger role in the field. Specifically, AI can benefit projects in various ways, but three stand out to me for construction companies.

​In 2024, construction accounted for 1,034 worker deaths.

AI can help safety directors keep workers safe. Specifically, computer vision can spot certain visible hazards and unsafe behaviors in real time, and predictive models can identify upcoming work that may carry higher incident risk so supervisors can intervene earlier.

​Experienced buyers know which suppliers tend to run late and when to order materials earlier. Translating that knowledge into a system can be hard.

AI-enabled procurement optimization can leverage machine learning to forecast material and equipment needs from a firm’s schedule, inventory and supplier history. ​Agentic AI workflows can then check stock, prices and lead times and draft purchase orders for a person to approve.

Using their gut instincts, construction CEOs can often tell which project is slipping and which machines are sitting idle.

Using computer vision, schedule and equipment intelligence can compare site imagery against a plan. Predictive models can flag delay risk while there’s still time to recover. AI-powered equipment monitoring can also help firms identify machinery that’s sitting idle or being underutilized.

First, I recommend that leaders start with the business problem they’re facing, not the technology. They should name the KPI they want to move, such as ​incident rate or days of delay.

Then, leaders should bring in stakeholders who will have to use AI, such as superintendents, project managers, safety leads and IT, from day one, and let them shape the work.

From there, leaders should have the right experts pick the technical path. Data and AI specialists should check whether a firm’s data is ready and decide what to buy versus build.

After that, leaders and their teams should integrate AI into existing workflows. They should make sure the insights AI generates inform day-to-day decisions and actions.

Finally, leaders should invest in their people. If workers worry AI will negatively impact or take away their jobs, silence from leadership can make those concerns worse. ​Leaders should tell their teams early on how AI will be used and what will change in daily tasks. They should train their teams on projects and share early wins. Leaders should give every AI project a small squad, such as a business owner accountable for the KPI, field experts who test the tool, engineers who build it and a change lead who drives adoption. In my experience, AI projects with defined owners move much faster.

​AI usage comes with risks. Construction leaders shouldn’t treat AI as a catch-all solution. Based on my observations, three implementation challenges can increase those risks.

​The first challenge is building on weak data. ​If schedules, cost codes and incident logs are incomplete or don’t match, the model learns the wrong lessons.

Then there’s skipping governance. Leaders should decide who can use which AI tools and which data points they can feed into them. ​I recommend leaders adopt this policy: no proprietary project plans in public AI tools.

Finally, there’s taking people out of the loop. An AI model may suggest a schedule based on past projects, while a project manager with 20 years on similar jobs knows this site needs more time. Leaders should task people with relevant expertise to make final calls.

Many construction firms have at least a few people whose years of experience give them an intuitive understanding of how projects work. I believe the construction firms that pull ahead will teach their systems to see what those people see before they leave.​

If you’re a construction leader, ask yourself: ​If your best project executive retired tomorrow, which of their calls could your company still make?​​
