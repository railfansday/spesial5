RNA-derived 2′,3′-cyclophosphate-terminated nucleotides act as immune signalling molecules in prokaryotes and eukaryotes1,2,3, but how plants perceive them remains unknown. Sucking insects inject digestive enzymes including RNases into the host plant to acquire nutrients. Here we demonstrate that NIB1, a glycine-rich RNA-binding protein in rice, senses 2′,3′-cyclic nucleotide monophosphates (2′,3′-cNMPs; 2′,3′-cAMP and 2′,3′-cGMP) and adenylyl-(3′→5′)-guanosine 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate (ApG>p) produced by a salivary T2 ribonuclease of green stink bug during feeding. Binding of the RNase products promotes formation of nuclear condensates containing NIB1, which recruit the core immune regulator Enhanced Disease Susceptibility 1 (EDS1) and activate defence, potentially contributing to the immune signalling of Toll/interleukin-1 receptor (TIR) proteins that also produce the nuclease products. Exogenous application of 2′,3′-cAMP, 2′,3′-cGMP or ApG>p elicits NIB1 and EDS1-mediated immune responses. Our findings unveil a conserved pathway in plants, in which glycine-rich RNA-binding proteins perceive the nuclease-generated second messengers to launch defence and point to a potential link between plant TIR nuclease activity and EDS1 signalling.

Insect pests cause losses to crop production and damage vegetation4,5. During feeding, insects release oral secretions containing proteins and other molecules that can activate or suppress host plant defence6,7,8. Digestive enzymes hydrolyse cellular components to generate nutrients for herbivores. These breakdown products also act as host-derived damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that activate plant pattern-recognition receptors9,10. Identifying host-derived signalling molecules and their defence activation mechanisms is essential for developing new plant-protection strategies.

T2-family ribonuclease (RNaseT2) proteins, which preferentially cleave single-strand RNAs between purine and uridine residues, are ancient enzymes that are widely distributed across organisms and functional in diverse biological processes2,11. Plant S-RNases, which mediate self-incompatibility, belong to this protein family12. Phytopathogens also secrete nucleases into host tissues13,14. Human lysosomal RNaseT2 generates purine-2′,3′-cyclophosphate-terminated oligoribonucleotides that are sensed by Toll-like receptor 8 to activate innate immunity2. Toll/interleukin-1 receptor (TIR) domains of plant intracellular nucleotide-binding and leucine-rich repeat (NLR) immune receptors possess enzymatic activities towards oxidized nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) and nucleic acids15,16,17,18,19. Plant TIR domains generate second messengers such as 2′-ADPR and pRib-AMP, which promote the formation of EDS1–Phytoalexin Deficient 4 (PAD4)–Activated Disease Resistance 1 (ADR1) complex and trigger disease resistance15,20,21. Activated plant TIR also catalyses the production of ADPr-ATP and di-ADPR, leading to the complex formation of EDS1-Senescence-Associated Gene 101 (SAG101)–N Required Gene 1 (NRG1), inducing plant cell death16,22,23. Plant TIR domains also synthesize 2′,3′-cAMP and 2′,3′-cGMP (2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP) through nuclease activity. Arabidopsis nudix hydrolase 7 (AtNUDT7) hydrolyses 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP and attenuates TIR-mediated cell death when overexpressed in Nicotiana benthamiana3. However, the contribution of 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP to EDS1-dependent immunity remains elusive.

Plant glycine-rich RNA-binding proteins (GRPs) encompassing one RNA recognition motif (RRM) at the N terminus and a glycine-rich region (GRR) at the C terminus regulate plant responses to abiotic and biotic stresses24,25. Arabidopsis GRP7 (AtGRP7) has a positive role in immunity, whereas the Pseudomonas syringae effector HopU1 suppresses AtGRP7-mediaed immunity by catalysing ADP-ribosylation of an arginine residue (R49) in the RRM of AtGRP726,27,28. These data indicate that the RRM of GRPs is central to immune function, although the underlying molecular mechanism remains unidentified. Here we report that the rice GRP NIB1 interacts with invading insect T2 RNases (such as NVR1) to promote ribonuclease activity and the production of 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP and ApG>p. Binding to the cyclic nucleotides via RMM stimulates NIB1 phase separation to assemble the plant defence machinery in the nucleus.

Nezara viridula (the green stink bug) is a polyphagous hemipteran pest feeding on a wide range of plants, including economically important food, fruit and vegetable crops and Nicotiana species. Populations of N. viridula have recently increased in rice fields. As a piercing-sucking mesophyll feeder (Fig. 1a), N. viridula uses needle-like stylets to puncture developing seeds or young leaves, extract nutrients and inject effectors29. To identify the secretory enzymes, we profiled proteins in its oral secretions using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) and identified 22 putative digestive enzymes, of which 15 were likely to be secretory based on signal peptide prediction (SignalP 3.0 server) and de-redundancy (Blastp) (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Transient expression of these proteins in N. benthamiana leaves identified one candidate enzyme that induced severe cell death and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production within 3 days post-agroinfiltration (dpi) (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c). This protein shows high sequence similarity to ribonuclease and we named it N. viridula ribonuclease 1 (NVR1).

NVR1 was predicted to have an N-terminal secretory signal peptide and no transmembrane region. Analysis using the protein family database (Pfam) revealed a ribonuclease (RNase) domain (Fig. 1b) that belongs to the RNaseT2 family11. NVR1 was detected both in N. viridula salivary glands and in infested rice plants, suggesting that it is secreted into plant tissues during feeding (Extended Data Fig. 1d). NVR1 exhibited a nucleocytoplasmic distribution pattern (Extended Data Fig. 1e).

H64 and H116 are conserved among T2 RNases from mammals, herbivorous insects, phytopathogenic fungi and plants (Fig. 1c and Extended Data Fig. 1f). NVR1 cleaved Cy5-labelled Arabidopsis tRNASer (83 nt) in vitro, whereas NVR1(H116A) and NVR1(H64A) mutants showed mild and strong reductions in substrate degradation, respectively, and the double mutant NVR1(H-doub) was almost completely inactivated (Fig. 1d), supporting H64 and H116 as key catalytic residues11. High-resolution mass spectrometry was subsequently used to identify products of the NVR1-catalysed reaction, which showed accumulation of 2′,3′-cAMP and 2′,3′-cGMP, as well as a dinucleotide-2′,3′-cyclophosphate with a mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of 675.1072 in positive mode (Fig. 1e,f). Given that preferentially cleavage of single-stranded RNA between purines and uridines by RNaseT2 produces guanosine or adenosine 2′,3′-cyclophosphate termini2, we designed two oligoribonucleotides, (UAGU)5 and (UGAU)5, as substrates to determine the structure of the product. NVR1 exhibited activity towards (UAGU)5—but not towards (UGAU)5—generating a peak at 5.1 min in the extracted ion chromatogram mode that was identical to the dinucleotide-2′,3′-cyclophosphate peak at m/z 675.1072, suggesting that NVR1 produced 2′,3′-cGMP-terminated AMP (ApG>p). The ApG>p synthetic standard, eluting at 5.1 min with a m/z of 675.1072 in positive mode, aligns with the NVR1 product (Fig. 1f) and matches the tandem mass (MS2) spectrum (Extended Data Fig. 1g). The accumulation of 2′,3′- cAMP/cGMP and ApG>P was rapidly induced in rice leaves within 2 h after insect feeding (Fig. 1g).

The 2′,3′-cNMP and ApG>p synthase activity of NVR1 was dependent on the two catalytic histidines H64 and H116 (Fig. 1h). High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)–mass spectrometry and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI) analysis showed that N. benthamiana leaves expressing empty vector or catalytic inactive NVR1 mutants accumulated much less 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP and ApG>p than leaves expressing wild-type NVR1 (Fig. 1i and Extended Data Fig. 1h). Leaves expressing NVR1 mutants were impaired in inducing plant cell death (Fig. 1j and Extended Data Fig. 1i). AtNUDT7 hydrolysed 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP and ApG>p (Fig. 1k). Co-expression with AtNUDT7 attenuated cell death activity of NVR1 in N. benthamiana at 3 dpi, with necrosis completed at 6 dpi (Fig. 1l and Extended Data Fig. 1j,k). These results indicate that the NVR1 enzymatic activity was causative to plant cell death.

To investigate plant responses to pest RNase, we generated rice NVR1-GFP transgenic plants that overexpress NVR1–GFP (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis revealed that genes associated with biotic stimulus, jasmonic acid and defence were significantly enriched in NVR1-expressing plants (Extended Data Fig. 2c). MYC2, NPR1, COI1 and ERF1 (Fig. 1m and Extended Data Fig. 2d) were upregulated and levels of jasmonic acid and salicylic acid levels increased (Extended Data Fig. 2e). The Cnaphalocrocis medinalis ribonuclease 1 (CMR1), an NVR1 orthologue expressed by Cnaphalocrocis medinalis—a lepidopteran pest of rice—was detected in oral secretions and also produced 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP (Extended Data Fig. 2f,g), congruent with the rice leaf-feeding data (Fig. 1g). Given this functional conservation, we evaluated the resistance of NVR1 transgenic lines to C. medinalis. The larvae gained less weight on NVR1-expressing plants than on controls (Extended Data Fig. 2h). Adult females laid fewer eggs on NVR1 transgenic lines, and larvae preferred feeding on control plants (Extended Data Fig. 2i,j).

NVR1-expressing plants were stunted and produced fewer tillers, phenotypes associated with the growth cost of constitutive defence activation, whereas rice plants expressing catalytically inactive NVR1(H-doub) did not upregulate defence genes and grew normally (Fig. 1n and Extended Data Fig. 2k,l). These results demonstrate that heterologous expression of NVR1 in rice elicited defence responses and enhanced plant resistance to insect feeding, and that NVR1 catalytic activity is essential for immune activation.

To uncover the molecular mechanisms underpinning the NVR1-induced plant defence response, we screened plant proteins that could bind to NVR1 by immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry (IP–MS). NVR1-interacting RNA-binding protein 1 (NIB1 (Os03t0670700-04), also known as OsGRP330,31) showed a strong interaction signal (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). NIB1 is a GRP composed of an N-terminal RRM and a C-terminal GRR and shares 80% N-terminal sequence identity with AtGRP726,27 (Extended Data Fig. 3c). Microscale thermophoresis (MST) indicated that NIB1 interacted directly with NVR1 in vitro (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 3d), and co-immunoprecipitation confirmed the interaction in planta (Fig. 2b).

Split luciferase complementation (SLC) showed that the NIB1 RRM, but not the GRR, interacted with NVR1 (Extended Data Fig. 3e), matching the MST data (Fig. 2a). AlphaFold2 predicted the RRM residue R22 as a contact site (Extended Data Fig. 3f), and the NIB1(R22A) mutant abolished the interaction in planta (Extended Data Fig. 3g). NVR1 interacted strongly with NIB1, independently of its catalytic activity (Extended Data Fig. 3h–j).

Given that NVR1-like secretory T2 ribonucleases are widespread in insects and phytopathogenic fungi (Extended Data Fig. 1f), and GRPs are conserved across land plants (Extended Data Fig. 3k), we investigated whether the RNaseT2–GRP interaction is a common phenomenon. We tested secreted RNases T2 from the insect pests C. medinalis (CMR1) and Helicoverpa armigera (HAR1) and the fungal pathogen Magnaporthe oryzae (MOR1). SLC assays demonstrated that the recognition of T2-family RNases by plant GRPs is universal (Extended Data Fig. 3l,m).

Given that GRPs bind RNA fragments27 and NVR1 requires its RNase activity to elicit defence, we investigated the potential binding of NIB1 to the RNaseT2 products. NIB1–GST was incubated with NVR1 products, denatured to extract the bound molecules, and analysed by high-resolution mass spectrometry. Notably, high-intensity signals of 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP and ApG>p were observed (Fig. 2c). Structural modelling of NIB1 with ligands identified two adjoining residues in RRM domain loop, R49 and G50 that were responsible for 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP recognition (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 4a). R49 is a known RNA-binding site27, whereas G50 contacts both 2′,3′-cAMP and 2′,3′-cGMP in our model. G14 and W17 serve as additional recognition sites for ApG>p (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 4a). In MST assays with 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP and ApG>p, NHS-labelled NIB1 exhibited high binding affinity. Substitution of either residue with alanine (NIB1(R49A) or NIB1(G50A)) markedly reduced the binding affinity, and the NIB1(R49A/G50A/G14A/W17A) (hereafter NIB1(4A)) mutation fully abolished the ApG>p binding (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 3d). G14, W17, R49 and G50 are highly conserved in a group of GRPs across land plants (Extended Data Fig. 3n), implying a critical role of these residues in ligand recognition. The NIB1(R49A) mutation impaired NIB1 binding to both RNA and 2′,3-cyclic nucleotides, whereas the NIB1(G50A) mutant maintained binding affinity towards FLAGELLIN SENSITIVE2 (FLS2) 3′ untranslated region (UTR) while showing strongly impaired binding to 2′,3-cyclic nucleotides (Extended Data Fig. 4b). Thus, NIB1 employs shared yet distinct recognition mechanisms for 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP and RNA.

In MST assays using 2′,3′-cAMP as a positive control, NIB1 did not bind 3′,5′-cAMP, 8-Br-3′,5′-cAMP, AMP or 2′,3′-cGAMP32 (Extended Data Fig. 4c–g). NIB1 exhibited 4-fold and 34-fold weaker binding affinities for 3′,5′-cGMP and GMP, respectively, compared with 2′,3′-cGMP (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 4h,i).

NIB1 contains a C-terminal intrinsically disordered region (IDR) (Extended Data Fig. 5a) that mediates phase separation33. PONDR predicted a high average disorder score similar to that of AtGRP7 (Extended Data Fig. 5b). In vitro, NIB1–YFP formed droplets that showed fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) and fused to form larger droplets (Extended Data Fig. 5c–e). NIB1–YFP signals also recovered after photobleaching in N. benthamiana leaves (Extended Data Fig. 5f).

To determine whether herbivory induces NIB1 condensation in planta, we generated two pNIB1::NIB1-YFP/ZH11 rice lines (Extended Data Fig. 5g). Following 6 h of feeding by third-instar C. medinalis larvae, distinct NIB1 nuclear condensates were clearly visualized (Fig. 2f), indicating that this phase separation is a physiologically relevant response to herbivory. Similar to the observations in vitro and in N. benthamiana nuclei, NIB1–YFP condensates in pNIB1::NIB1-YFP/ZH11 nuclei recovered fluorescence after photobleaching (Extended Data Fig. 5h).

We further analysed the interplay between NVR1 catalytic activity and NIB1 condensation. Wild-type NIB1–YFP, but not the C-terminal IDR-truncated NIB1(ΔIDR)–YFP, absorbed NVR1–mCherry into the droplets (Extended Data Fig. 5i–k). The droplets of purified NIB1–YFP protein with nucleic acid contamination, but not that of nucleic acid-free NIB1–YFP, were enlarged in the presence of NVR1–mCherry, whereas the catalytically inactive NVR1(H-doub)–mCherry did not exert such effect (Extended Data Fig. 5i–k). NIB1–YFP exhibited nucleoplasmic localization, and NVR1 significantly enhanced its nuclear accumulation, with about 70% of the examined nuclei containing distinct condensates. N-terminal YFP-tagged NIB1 showed a similar localization pattern (Extended Data Fig. 5l–n). Immunoblotting further confirmed that NVR1 promoted nuclear accumulation of NIB1 (Extended Data Fig. 5o). Nuclear condensates were not observed for the NIB1–NVR1(H-doub) or NIB1(ΔIDR)–NVR1 (Fig. 2g), revealing that the RNase activity of NVR1 is crucial for promoting NIB1 phase separation in the nucleus. In subsequent analyses, we focused on nuclear NIB1 condensates.

Multivalent interactions drive phase separation and locally concentrate molecules, as in dsDNA-dependent cGAS activation and NAD+/ATP-dependent plant TIR activation32,34. We investigated whether 2′,3′-cNMP and ApG>p would induce NIB1 phase separation. The NIB1(R49A)–YFP and NIB1(G50A)–YFP droplets were smaller than NIB1–YFP droplets with or without NVR1–mCherry (Extended Data Fig. 6a). When co-expressed with NVR1 in N. benthamiana, NIB1(R49A), NIB1(G50A) and NIB1(4A) did not generate sparkling nuclear condensates (Extended Data Fig. 6b), implying that NIB1 phase separation involves binding of 2′,3′-cNMP and ApG>p. Congruently, 2′,3′-cNMP and ApG>p (2 nM each) promoted NIB1 phase separation in vitro (Fig. 2h and Extended Data Fig. 6c,d), and treatment with 8-Br-2′,3′-cNMP or ApG>p increased nuclear condensates in N. benthamiana cells (Fig. 2i,j and Extended Data Fig. 6e). Notably, exogenous application of 50 µM 8-Br-2′,3′-cNMP and ApG>p for 6 h induced distinct nuclear condensates in the pNIB1::NIB1-YFP/ZH11 rice plant (Fig. 2k and Extended Data Fig. 6f), indicating that 2′,3′-cNMP and ApG>p act as agonists of NIB1 in promoting phase separation.

We further monitored NIB1–YFP condensation in N. benthamiana leaves upon treatment with nucleotide analogues. Consistent with findings from MST (Extended Data Fig. 4c–g), 3′,5′-cAMP, 8-Br-3′,5′-cAMP, AMP and 2′3′-cGAMP did not trigger condensate formation, whereas 3′,5′-cGMP and GMP induced far fewer condensates than 2′,3′-cAMP (Extended Data Fig. 6g,h). Co-incubation with NVR1, rice plant RNA (OsRNA) and 2′,3′-cAMP resulted in larger NIB1–YFP droplets in vitro, which required the NVR1 catalytic activity, NIB1 binding sites and 2′,3′-cAMP to achieve optimal effect (Extended Data Fig. 6i). In vitro reconstitutions revealed that NIB1–YFP formed droplets, dependent on 2′,3′-cAMP, OsRNA and salt, as well as protein and macromolecular crowding agent concentrations (Extended Data Fig. 6j–m).

To investigate the biochemical consequences of NIB1 liquid–liquid phase separation, we examined its effect on NVR1 enzymatic activity. NVR1 was more active in the presence of NIB1, and the substrate degradation increased with higher concentrations of NIB1 (Fig. 3a). When the C-terminal IDR-containing fragment was deleted (NIB1(ΔIDR)), the influence of NIB1 on NVR1 activity was reduced or negligible (Fig. 3b), suggesting that the IDR steers NIB1 to boost the NVR1 enzymatic activity. Consistently, the NVR1–NIB1 complex synthesized a higher amount of 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP and ApG>p than NVR1 alone, which also required the IDR in NIB1 for increased activity (Fig. 3c). Similarly, when co-expressed in N. benthamiana leaves, full-length NIB1 significantly enhanced NVR1 in generating 2′,3′-cNMP compared with NIB1(ΔIDR) (Extended Data Fig. 6n). We subsequently evaluated the effect of NIB1 on NVR1-induced cell death in N. benthamiana and found that removing the IDR from NIB1 largely abrogated its potentiation of NVR1-induced plant cell death (Extended Data Fig. 6o,p). Additionally, the NIB1(R49A) and NIB1(G50A) mutants also showed compromised acceleration of NVR1 catalysis (Fig. 3d) and the NVR1-induced plant cell death (Extended Data Fig. 6q,r). In sum, catalytically active NVR1 stimulates NIB1 phase separation, which in turn enhances NVR1 activity in degrading RNA and synthesizing 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP and ApG>p, thereby inducing plant cell death through a positive feedback loop. In this mutual potentiation interaction, NIB1 uses both the IDR and the RNA-binding sites in the RRM to amplify and transduce NVR1-generated signalling molecules.

NIB1 is highly expressed in multiple rice tissues (Extended Data Fig. 7a). To investigate the NIB1 function in plant defence, we used CRISPR–Cas9 to generate NIB1-knockout (NIB1-KO) and NIB1-overexpression (NIB1-OE) rice lines (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 7b–d). NIB1-OE and NVR1-OE plants showed similar transcriptional changes, with 553 (59%) of the upregulated genes overlapping (Fig. 4b), including genes associated with response to biotic stimulus, jasmonic acid and defence (Fig. 4c). MYC2, NPR1, COI1 and ERF1 were downregulated in NIB1-KO plants but markedly upregulated in NIB1-OE plants (Extended Data Fig. 7e), consistent with the RNA-seq data and the levels of defence hormones salicylic acid and jasmonic acid (Fig. 4d). C. medinalis larvae gained more weight after feeding on the NIB1-KO lines than with the wild-type plants; by contrast, their growth was retarded on the NIB1-OE plants (Fig. 4e). Females and larvae preferred NIB1-KO over wild-type and NIB1-OE plants in oviposition and feeding-choice assays (Extended Data Fig. 7f,g). Insect feeding strongly induced MYC2 in wild-type rice, but the induction was much weaker in the NIB1-KO mutant (Fig. 4f). NIB1-OE plants had reduced tillering, partly resembling the NVR1-OE phenotype (Extended Data Fig. 2l), whereas NIB1-KO plants grew well and produced more tillers (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 7h), probably reflecting their differences in immune intensity. These results suggest that NIB1 positively regulates plant defence responses.

We next investigated whether the NVR1-induced plant defence responses were contingent on the presence of NIB1. Treatment of rice plants with purified NVR1 induced ROS production, which was attenuated in NIB1-KO lines (Fig. 4g). Consistently, NVR1-induced cell death was mitigated in NbGRP7-KO leaves compared with wild-type N. benthamiana (Fig. 4h and Extended Data Fig. 7i,j). Thus, the RNA-binding protein NIB1 mediates NVR1-dependent induction of plant defence response.

To further determine whether NIB1 senses 2′,3′-cNMP and ApG>p to mediate NVR1-induced immune activation, we generated overexpression complementation lines in NIB1-KO rice plants, including wild-type NIB1, NIB1R49A and NIB1G50A. The two recognition-deficient complementation lines were unable to rescue the immune responses in NIB1-KO mutants, as indicated by defence hormone accumulation (Fig. 4i), insect resistance (Fig. 4j and Extended Data Fig. 8a,b) and the expression of MYC2, COI1 and NPR1 (Extended Data Fig. 8c). Expression of NIB1R49A and NIB1G50A in wild-type rice plants did not lead to a clear upregulation of these defence genes (Extended Data Fig. 8d,e). Together, these results show that NIB1 activates plant defence against insect feeding and that sensing of 2′,3′-cNMP and ApG>p is crucial for this immune function of NIB1.

MST assays with NIB1(ΔIDR) exhibited similar binding affinity to 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP and ApG>P as wild-type NIB1 (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 8f). However, overexpression of NIB1(ΔIDR)–YFP in NIB1-KO rice plants did not rescue the compromised insect resistance (Fig. 4k). Moreover, treatment with 50 µM 8-Br-2′,3′-cAMP induced condensate formation in pNIB1::NIB1-YFP/NIB1-KO plants but not in 35S::NIB1ΔIDR-YFP/NIB1-KO plants (Extended Data Fig. 8g,h). Thus, both ligand recognition via RRM and subsequent phase separation via IDR are essential for the NIB1-dependent defence response.

To connect the NVR1–NIB1 module to the plant defence network, we performed IP–MS on leaves co-expressing NIB1–YFP and NVR1. EDS1, CDPK and MAPK6 were identified as NIB1 interactors (Extended Data Fig. 9a). Comparison with the NVR1 interactome revealed that the shared components were enriched in herbivore-response pathways (Extended Data Fig. 9b).

EDS1 is a central immune signalling node35,36,37. In SLC assays, NIB1 interacted with rice EDS1 (Extended Data Fig. 9c). EDS1–YFP co-immunoprecipitated with NIB1–Flag in N. benthamiana (Fig. 5a), and His–NIB1 pulled down EDS1–Flag from N. benthamiana leaves (Extended Data Fig. 9d). Catalytically active NVR1, but not the inactive NVR1(H-doub) mutant, stimulated NIB1 recruitment of EDS1 into sparkly nuclear condensates (Fig. 5c and Extended Data Fig. 9f). The rice CR-eds1 mutant generated by CRISPR–Cas9 accumulated lower levels of jasmonic acid and salicylic acid (Fig. 5d) and expressed lower levels of COI1, NPR1 and ERF1 (Extended Data Fig. 9h–j). The induction of MYC2 expression in response to insect feeding was minimal in the rice CR-eds1 mutant (Fig. 5e). The immune activation phenotype of NIB1 overexpression was highly attenuated in the rice eds1-KO mutant and NIB1-triggered induction of defence genes PR1, MYC2, COI1, NPR1 and ERF1 was diminished in CR-eds1 rice plants (Fig. 5g and Extended Data Fig. 9m,n). Treatment with 8-Br-2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP and ApG>p induced the expression of PR1 and MYC2 in rice leaves, but this induction was compromised in NIB1-KO and CR-eds1 plants (Fig. 5h). Similarly, 8-Br-2′,3′-cAMP treatment upregulated NbPR1 and NbMYC2 expression in wild-type N. benthamiana but not in NbGRP7-KO or N. benthamiana epss (eds1a, pad4, sag101a, sag101b) mutants (Fig. 5i). The upregulated genes were predominantly enriched in defence pathways (Extended Data Fig. 9q). These data demonstrate that EDS1 and NIB1 are important signalling components for the immune function of 2′,3′-cAMP.

Herbivore-induced endogenous 2′,3′-cAMP concentration reached around 21 μM in rice (Fig. 1g), comparable to the 50 μM concentration used in our assays. Treating N. benthamiana leaves with 21 μM 8-Br-2′,3′-cAMP for 6 h upregulated NbPR1 and NbMYC2 to similar levels as the 50 µM 2′,3′-cAMP treatment. Thus, the herbivore-induced endogenous accumulation of 2′,3′-cAMP is physiologically sufficient to elicit defence responses (Extended Data Fig. 9r). Exogenous application of 50 μM 8-Br-2′,3′-cAMP via spray significantly enhanced plant resistance against herbivory under both laboratory and paddy field conditions (Fig. 5j,k and Extended Data Fig. 9s).

Given that plant TIR proteins function as 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP synthases3, we tested whether the TIR-induced immunity also involves NIB1 or the orthologous GRP by expressing the TIR domain of Arabidopsis RPS4 (RPS4TIR) in NbGRP7-KO lines. Arabidopsis RPS4TIR-induced cell death in NbGRP7-KO leaves showed slight attenuation at 36 h post-infiltration (hpi) but became obvious and complete by 56 hpi (Extended Data Fig. 10a–c). The cell death triggered by the rice TIR-only protein OsTIR was also slightly attenuated in the NbGRP7/NbGRP8-KO mutants when observed at 40 hpi (Extended Data Fig. 10d–g), with obvious cell death and completion at 68 hpi (Extended Data Fig. 10h). These results suggested a general role of GRP in sensing the 2′,3′-cyclophosphate-terminated mono- and dinucleotides. In sum, our results show that GRPs sense the nuclease products of 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP and ApG>p, contributing to the activation of the EDS1-dependent signalling pathway (Fig. 5l).

Plants express many cell-surface and intracellular immune receptors to perceive alien molecules and activate defence38,39. Here we found that the rice GRP NIB1 interacts with invading RNaseT2 and senses 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP and ApG>p to induce plant defence. The presence of NVR1 orthologues in oral secretions of the piercing-sucking pest Apolygus lucorum (ALR1; data not shown) and chewing insect C. medinalis suggests that T2-family ribonuclease effectors are conserved across divergent feeding types and may be a broadly deployed strategy in plant–insect interactions. Given the involvement of 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP in abiotic stresses40,41,42,43, it is worth further investigating the role of GRPs in leveraging biotic and abiotic stresses. Whether and how plants discriminate between different sources of second messengers are also important questions to address.

Condensates or membraneless compartments are engaged in a variety of plant developmental and stress response processes44,45,46,47. NIB1, as a sensor, perceives 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP and ApG>p and forms nuclear condensates that recruit the core immune regulator EDS135,36,37 and activate downstream signalling pathways, highlighting protein phase separation as a mechanism in response to second messengers. Our study focuses on the immune function of nuclear NIB1, whereas the biological roles of its cytoplasmic condensates deserve future investigation. Exogenous spraying of these RNA degradation-derived signalling molecules triggered plant defence responses, highlighting their application potential as immune activators. Our study opens a door for exploring the engagement of RNase products in plant defence, adaptation and development.

Plant TIR-catalysed and NAD+-derived products induces formation of EDS1–SAG101–NRG1 and EDS1–PAD4–ADR1 heterotrimers, as well as oligomerization of ADR1 and NRG1 as calcium channels at the plasma membrane, to confer immunity. The link between TIR nuclease-derived 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP and EDS1 remains unclear, and the nuclear function of EDS1 in TIR-mediated immunity is unknown. Our unpublished data show that 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP does not contribute to the EDS1–SAG101–NRG1 or EDS1–PAD4–ADR1 heterotrimer formation, consistent with the proposed role of 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP in amplifying rather than initiating TIR signalling3. In bacteria, 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP have been shown to modulate activities of transcription factors48. 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP-induced NIB1 condensate in the nucleus recruits EDS1 to trigger defence, potentially via transcriptional modulation, providing a possible explanation for the biological relevance of plant TIR nuclease activity. Plant TIR-only proteins but not TIR–NLR receptors possess nuclease activity—thus, TIR nuclease activity and 2′,3′-cAMP/cGMP are likely to be involved in TIR-only-mediated basal immunity but not TIR–NLR-mediated effector-triggered immunity3,49, consistent with the weak contribution of GRPs towards TIR-induced cell death phenotypes in N. benthamiana. The T2 RNase NVR1 produces ApG>p, and the nuclease product spectrum of plant TIR-only proteins awaits further studies. Future studies could investigate whether other GRPs are also involved in perceiving such nuclease products and their interactions. Detailed analyses of NIB1 condensate components will add valuable knowledge to understanding this nuclease-based defence pathway.

Phytophagous insects, especially mesophyll feeders, generate various small molecules from biopolymers by injecting digestive enzymes into plant tissues, in addition to mechanical injury. Detection of DAMPs is essential for plants to activate defence programmes. Although the wide-scale cultivation of transgenic crops expressing Bacillus thuringiensis insecticidal proteins has effectively controlled the lepidopteran chewing insects in the field, heteropteran mesophyll feeders such as the mirid bug A. lucorum (Miridae) and the green stink bug N. viridula (Pentatomidae), are less sensitive to these proteins and are emerging as a threat to agriculture50. Our findings show that plant GRP is a sensor of the invading RNase products and orchestrates immune responses to activate an EDS1-dependent signalling pathway, providing a potential target for advanced plant protection technologies.

The third-instar C. medinalis larvae were placed on leaves of the 12-day-old rice seedlings of the indicated lines. Larval weight was recorded after the feeding for three days. Oviposition selection assay was conducted in nylon mesh cages (90 × 70 × 90 cm). Three one-month-old, uniform rice plants per genotype were grouped and arranged triangularly to prevent positional bias. 15 pairs of newly emerged moths were mated for 48 h. Subsequently, males were removed, and the 15 mated females were released into the cage centre with a 15% honey solution. After 24 h of oviposition at 26 °C (12 h:12 h light:dark photoperiod), the total number of eggs laid on the three plants of each genotype was summed. The assay was repeated with three independent cages. Oviposition preference was quantified by the oviposition index: OI = (T − C)/(T + C), where T and C are the number of eggs in the mutants and wild type, respectively. The deviation of the oviposition index against 0 was tested using a one-sample Student’s t-test.

For larval feeding preference, leaf segments (3 cm) from 3 plant lines were arranged equidistantly in a circle within a Petri dish. Ten third-instar larvae (pre-starved for 2 h) were introduced to the centre. After 1 h of selection in darkness, the number of larvae settled on each plant was recorded. The selection rate was calculated as Ntarget/Ntotal selection × 100%, where Ntarget and Ntotal selection represent the number of larvae on a specific plant and the total number of larvae making a definitive choice, respectively.

For field test, wild-type ZH11 seeds were sown in soil, and 35-day-old plants were transplanted into six 1 m × 1 m field plots. Each plot was surrounded by two rows of rice plants serving as buffer rows, and all six plots were maintained within mesh enclosure. Four weeks after transplantation, the plots were arranged in an alternating pattern and sprayed with either water or 50 μM 8-Br-2′,3′-cAMP. Following treatment, 20 mated female C. medinalis moths were released at the centre of the enclosure. Once rolled-leaf shelters became visible, the number of shelters was counted. Insects and plant positions were randomized in laboratory assays, field treatments followed an alternating design, and outcomes were assessed blind to genotype or treatment.
