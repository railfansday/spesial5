Roku Pro Series LX OLED TV Review: One Small Step for OLED, One Big Step for Roku TVs

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Roku’s first OLED TV lowers the bar for bringing a new, high-performance OLED screen home.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. Why Trust Us?

It seems that the rumors of the end of OLED supremacy in the TV marketplace were greatly exaggerated. At the start of 2026, flashy new display technologies like RGB LED emerged to challenge that status quo, theoretically offering a brighter alternative to OLED panels, with greater color coverage. But at the end of the year, OLED TVs, with their ability to control each of a TV’s millions of tiny pixels point-by-point, remain the best option for overall picture performance. In fact, despite the fact that only two companies, LG and Samsung, currently make OLED panels, the number of TV brands that actually use those panels is expanding: Roku, the latest brand to jump into the space, released its first two OLED TVs this fall.

As you’d expect from Roku, both the baseline Roku Pro Series and the brighter and slightly fancier Roku Pro Series LX are value-oriented models, with list prices that undercut brands like Samsung and LG. But like many of our favorite picks at Popular Mechanics, they are designed to balance premium performance and great usability, with a simple setup and handy quality-of-life features that competitors lack, like a built-in remote finder.

After spending several days with the flagship Pro LX, I’m happy to report that Roku’s first foray into OLED TVs is already a strong contender for anyone curious about the benefits of upgrading to an OLED screen. The Pro LX’s picture quality doesn’t quite reach the heights of legacy OLED sets like LG’s C6 let alone flagships like the Samsung’s S95H. But it holds its own, and arguably outdoes them both when it comes to actually living with your TV. If you’re after a best-of-both-worlds OLED approach, the Roku Pro LX is an enticing new option at a very nice price.

Everything about setting up the Pro LX is built around simplicity. Like last year’s LED-powered Roku Pro, the LX eschews multi-piece pedestal stands and tiny bags of screws for feet that attach with screw-on knobs—no tools required. Dual stand positions include higher clearance for a soundbar, though the feet’s width of just over 42 inches on the 65-inch model I tested doesn’t leave much space up front on most consoles. The design doesn’t look quite as stylish as its LG and Samsung rivals, but the build feels stout, and the ultra-thin panel of just over an eighth of an inch up top adds a premium vibe.

The TV turns on automatically when plugged in, and immediately walks you through pairing the voice remote, which charges via USB-C so you never have to worry about batteries. It’s not quite as handy as Samsung’s solar-powered remotes, but its design is intuitive, including options like backlit keys and hands-free “Hey, Roku” voice control. If you’re not into an always-ready mic, you can mute it with a hard switch on the remote’s left edge.

Even the LX’s packaging is designed with forethought, including a plastic screen cover that peels off with a feather-light tug. But before you remove it, you’ll see a yellow arrow that points to my favorite feature by far: A remote finder button. A quick press emits a pleasant chirp from the remote buried in your couch cushions or, if you’ve got a toddler like me, hidden in any number of places out of reach from grabby little hands. I have no idea why this isn’t a standard option across brands, but kudos to Roku. It’s the simple things.

At the TV’s left flank, you’ll find a reasonably well-stocked input hub, including dual HDMI 2.1 inputs for gaming in VRR (Variable Refresh Rate) at up to 144 Hz, an HDMI 2.0 port, and a separate HDMI eARC port for connecting a soundbar or audio system. That’s important because the Pro LX’s sound is particularly thin and weak, all but requiring an audio upgrade. Other inputs in the stack include Ethernet to back up WiFi 6 support, USB-C and USB-A inputs, optical input, and a cable/antenna tuner. It would have been nice to get a full set of four HDMI 2.1 inputs, which you’ll find in similar LG and Samsung TVs, but I appreciate that Roku offers two ways for serious gamers to connect apart from the HDMI eARC port.

Notable features include ALLM (Auto Low Latency Mode) for fast-paced gaming and support for all major HDR formats, including the top two advanced options, Dolby Vision and HDR10+. In contrast, LG TVs stop at Dolby Vision, while Samsung only offers HDR10+. It’s always nice to have options, and the addition of Dolby Vision, the most common format for streaming, gives Roku a leg-up over Samsung OLEDs.

As you’d expect from an OLED, the Pro LX delivers fantastic contrast between the lightest and darkest images from the moment you turn it on, starting with perfect black levels from its obsidian screen. A polarizing layer does a fine job diffusing reflections for moderately bright rooms, about on par with LG’s C6. It doesn’t offer the powerful ambient light control of matte screens like Samsung’s S90H, but I generally prefer the richer perceived black levels of glossy screens, and the Pro LX is happy to indulge with impressive depth in darker moments.

Roku claims that the Pro LX is around twice as bright as the standard Roku Pro OLED, and after tinkering with the settings a bit, you’ll get plenty of pep to contrast those rich black levels. Using the TV’s most accurate HDR Dark picture mode, I measured around 1200 nits of peak brightness in a 10 percent window with a Portrait Displays C6 HDR5000 colorimeter, a G1 pattern generator, and Calman software.

That’s around 150 nits less than I got with the C6, but the difference is negligible in real-world watching: both TVs look similarly bright in moments like the sun glancing off a windshield or a glowing candle in a dark room. Roku’s model provides more than enough power for impactful HDR punch and vibrant colors, especially since most streaming content tops out at 1,000 nits. Interestingly, you’ll need to change the TV’s separate Picture Brightness setting to the highest “Brighter” mode to unlock that peak brightness. Unfortunately, the picture doesn’t do as good a job following the intent of directors and mastering engineers here as in the dimmer “normal” mode. That said, while slightly overbright, the TV still looks quite good overall in “brighter” mode. It’s just more of a tradeoff than you’ll see with the C6 at peak brightness.

Likewise, the colors aren’t quite as accurate as the C6. You can see the difference in certain specific shades, like the deep oranges in Mad Max: Fury Road, which sometimes looked a little too, well, orangey. I also noticed some color banding, or image artifacts, in especially challenging test scenes, but it rarely showed up in regular films and TV shows.

For most of my viewing, colors looked rich and natural. The vivid coral adventures in Netflix’s Our Planet provide a dazzling rainbow of shades that gave me chills, and watching Spider-Man: Brand New Day was a cinematic experience, with an almost marbled touch to details. There’s enough vibrancy to get you lost in little moments like the flashing gold of Ego’s palace in Guardians of the Galaxy Volume II, or the glowing beads of light reflecting off the Mandalorian’s helmet in his spaceship.

Speaking of space, the TV’s pinpoint light control makes deep-space scenes look incredibly realistic and engaging, especially compared with backlit models like last year’s mini-LED Roku Pro. Shadow details are also impressive, even in challenging scenes from murky shows like The Chilling Adventures of Sabrina, pairing with the TV’s flashy highlights for a lush experience. And while viewing the screen off-angle adds a slight green tinge, as it does with a lot of LG OLEDs, it’s worlds better than the pale colors and accentuated blooming you’ll see in the vast majority of LED TVs. Such is the advantage of OLED.

Of course, the biggest advantage of a Roku-branded OLED is marrying OLED’s technical prowess with a more accessible smart TV operating system. While Sony’s Google TV OLEDs are a solid alternative, Roku OS is notably simpler, more reliable, and in some ways more joyful to use than Samsung’s Tizen, LG’s webOS, and (especially) Panasonic’s Fire TV.

The fact that Roku OS just works exactly the way it’s supposed to is frankly half the battle. I’ve had fewer glitches with Roku OS in several years of TV reviewing than any other system, going back to TCL’s early Roku TVs. On top of that, I like the little extras Roku still offers like space themes, Star Trek-branded wallpaper, and silly movie references in the ever-expanding Roku City screen saver, like Cameron’s dad’s ill-fated Ferrari from Ferris Bueller’s Day Off.

Like all smart TVs I test, Roku OS has gotten less intuitive and more insidious in recent years, from plays to sell your data like ACR (Automatic Content Recognition) to pushier ads, such as a Home Depot-sponsored holiday wallpaper that suddenly appeared as I was writing this review. Luckily, you can turn that and most other annoying features off (though Roku immediately put the ad elsewhere). I’m also not as pleased with the latest Home screen layout, which pushes features like Continue Watching and the full list of apps below (you guessed it) more ads.

The upshot is that it takes more work to reorganize the layout for the best experience, but the good news is you can customize most options, and Roku OS is still the best built-in streaming system I’ve tested.

Roku’s Pro Series LX isn’t the best-performing OLED in its class, with both the Samsung S90H and LG C6 providing a cleaner, more accurate picture, but it is comparable and cheaper than either one at full price. (And will likely remain so for the rest of the year as holiday sales kick in.) Moreover, its slick blend of usability and performance could make it the top choice for OLED-curious folks who value a TV’s livability as highly as its picture quality. If you want to make the jump to OLED, and your budget is already stretched, Roku’s your new go-to.
