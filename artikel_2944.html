The Future Is Brighter Than You Think: Why Technology Could Make Humanity Better Off

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Lindsey Witmer Collins is Founder and CEO of WLCM.AI, an Inc. 5000 company building AI solutions for enterprises, and Scribbly Books.

​I know how that sounds, and I know it makes me seem uninformed or naive, but I’m really just trying to make an important choice thoughtfully. It’s a choice we all get to make: How do I orient myself to the world I’m in and the one I’m building? And, specifically, I’m curious. What if we ran our projections for the future of humanity in the opposite direction than the doom default? In other words, what if the future is awesome?

Our feeds show many flavors of doom: AI is coming for the jobs, the planet is cooking, the kids can’t look up from their phones and somewhere a robot is learning to do backflips. I build software for a living, including the kind people are nervous about, so I’m not saying this from a hammock. I’m looking for evidence.

A good place to understand where we’re going is to see where we’ve been—to understand what we care about, what we’re capable of and what direction, as a species, we trend toward.

If you were born in 1800, there was, globally, roughly a 43% chance you’d die before your fifth birthday. Today, it’s about 4%, and in most of the world it’s lower than that. Nearly 80% of humanity lived in extreme poverty two centuries ago; by 2019 it was under 10%, and since 1990 the count has dropped by something like 130,000 people every single day. In 1820, 1 adult in 10 could read. We’re at 87% now.

None of that happened by accident. It happened because somebody figured out that germs exist, that a cowpox sore could stop smallpox, that nitrogen could be pulled out of thin air and turned into fertilizer. Somebody built the sewer. Somebody built the washing machine—Hans Rosling used to call it the greatest invention of the industrial revolution, and his argument was basically that it gave his mother back enough hours to read to him.

My great-grandmother could walk into my kitchen today—hot water out of a wall, a box that keeps food cold, a cabinet of medicine that works, the whole of recorded human knowledge in the pocket of my jeans—and conclude she had died and gone to heaven. To me it’s just Monday, and I’m a little annoyed about the traffic.

The pace never slowed down; the headlines just moved on to other things. The International Energy Agency called solar the cheapest electricity in history back in 2020, and it has kept getting cheaper. Lithium-ion battery packs ended 2025 averaging $108 per kilowatt-hour, down 93% since 2010, which is the reason the grid, my electric car and the battery in my lawnmower are all getting electrified without a modicum of drama.

In December 2023, the FDA approved the first CRISPR-based therapy, for people with sickle cell disease—a disease we’ve understood at the molecular level for decades and could do very little about until now. Twenty-five African countries now give malaria vaccines as part of routine childhood shots, and they’re more than 39 million doses in. This year, four astronauts flew around the moon for the first time since 1972, and an mRNA flu shot got approved after beating the traditional one by about 27% in a trial of nearly 50,000 people.

And AI—which we love to fret about—is mostly busy doing the routable work: the reading, the sorting, the first draft, the thousand small lookups. That raises the value of everything it can’t reach, and most of what it can’t reach is other people.

I spend most of my time now on an above-ground mining startup focused on the critical minerals and materials sitting in the U.S.’s retiring infrastructure—EV batteries, solar panels, telecom copper wires and so forth. A surprising share of the next decade’s supply is already here, waiting to be recovered, and it’s on the verge of happening.

What if we get all of the benefits of technology, and we still love each other, and hang out, and chat with the person at the checkout?

Picture a backyard cookout in 2075. Grandma is 120 and bringing her famous potato salad, because the cataract fix was an afternoon, her hip came out of a printer and she’s been weightlifting her entire adult life. The power for the whole event—the grill, the speaker, the car that drove her over—cost roughly nothing, because sunlight is free and we finally got good at storing it. The toddler running laps around the table will learn of malaria only in history books. Somebody’s cousin runs a three-person company that would have needed 40 people in 2020. And we’re still watching live sports in the living room.

If the lines on the charts to keep going the way they’ve gone for 200 years, and if we keep showing up with a side dish, we might end up better than fine.

We’re all makers and builders in our own unique ways, and we build what we see in our mind’s eye. If the only future we picture is the grim one, we might start driving off the road.

I’m going to say we’re building a beautiful and abundant future, and that things will keep getting better. You may say I’m choosing my facts selectively, and you would be correct. There are too many facts, so we all get our pick.
