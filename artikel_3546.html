Chris Harrison on Launching The Vow, His Faith-Based Reality Show, and Leaving The Bachelor

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

“It really has very little to do with being on The Bachelor,” Chris Harrison, the series’s former host, tells me over breakfast at Manhattan’s Whitby Hotel in mid-October. Harrison orders avocado toast with poached eggs on top, then gently redirects the conversation to The Vow, his new faith-based dating series that premieres November 11 on Fox Nation. “It’s what we noticed, reading articles about this revival of young people going to church more than ever,” he says, turning to his wife and fellow executive producer on the show, Lauren Zima, who is seated beside him. “Here in New York, there are youth nights at the Catholic Church, at the temple, that are selling out thousands of kids. Clearly, this generation is searching for something solid—salvation, foundation.”

The six-episode series airs on the conservative-associated Fox Nation, Harrison says The Vow isn’t meant to have a political slant. “We’re not telling you to believe this or follow this path,” says Harrison. “Good shows look into a niche, this one’s about faith-driven people trying to find love. The hookup culture was a big deal and settling down became a cocktail joke. This generation’s really pushing back the other way.”

Faith-based dating shows aimed at Gen Z audiences have begun to infiltrate the reality TV landscape in recent years, from The Secret Lives of Mormon Wives to Hulu’s Ring by Spring Break, an upcoming series “that drops single Christian college students into a steamy spring break in Cabo, where finding ‘The One’ isn’t a dream…it’s a deadline.” But mixing romance and religion can be tricky business, as evidenced by the indeterminate future of the Mormon Wives and a ready-to-release season of The Bachelorette put on pause following allegations made against Taylor Frankie Paul, who leads both shows.

On The Vow, Jace Cates is a 30-year-old devout Christian mortgage-loan officer from Canby, Oregon that 10 similarly faith-driven women compete to marry. The goal is that, “Jace finds love,” says Harrison. “If it leads to marriage and children, we don’t have enough time for all that to happen on the show, but I want to help try and create the real article on TV. And if we don’t, that has to be real as well because this show is not predicated on the fact that it’s guaranteed to work.” But it’s hard to imagine any contenders stand a chance against Sierra, a virgin who lives 20 minutes away from Jace and attends his same Portland church. Her frontrunner status is cemented with a night-one kiss, the filming of which happened to coincide with Sierra’s 26th birthday. “We cast her before we cast Jace,” says Harrison, which feels as telling a reveal as any about producers’ ability to set themselves up for a successful outcome.

In February, before Fox Nation’s public involvement, Harrison shared a casting call for The Vow on Instagram, seeking single men and women aged 21 and older “who want a traditional, marriage-minded partnership built on commitment and shared values.” The description sparked early backlash on social media, but an initially surprised Harrison says he now understands the reaction. “Because in this day and age, we all have our guard up when we hear something that might be controversial,” he explains. “I’m not going to shy away from the fact that [the show] is about faith-based people saying, ‘I really do want to get married. I really do want to have babies’ Even those that might think it’s polarizing, I hope you watch,” Harrison adds. “You might be pleasantly surprised at how unbiased and uncontroversial it really is.”

The 55-year-old Harrison and Zima, who is 16 years his junior, are themselves “on different stages of faith,” she says as her egg-white frittata arrives. “Not drastically, but a bit. He’s probably more traditionally spiritual than I am.” Harrison was raised by a Jewish mother and grandparents, but grew up in a Protestant household during adolescence in the Bible belt of Dallas, Texas. After getting baptized as a Christian in his twenties, Harrison now says he identifies as “a religious mutt.”

“What matters most in our relationship,” the Chicago-born Zima continues, “[are] our values—family, loyalty, wanting to be good to people—those conversations [about] core things that drive you were what we really wanted people to have on the show.” Seeking “a really diverse spectrum of faith” meant casting Christians who ranged from the recently baptized to a first-generation American from Ghana. And the term “tradwife” means something different to everyone—Audrey aspires to be “a woman who makes her own money” as a penile implant sales rep fromin Wisconsin, while California native Mya is a second-grade teacher who one day wants to “do my husband for a living and have a big family.”

“We are always wanting to bring people together,” Zima continues. “And part of the way you bring people together is conversation—giving them dynamic, interesting, layered things to unpack together.”

The couple is open to devoting future seasons to different religions outside Christianity. “The important thing is finding really good, authentic people who have faith in their heart—whatever that faith is—but then really want to get married,” says Harrison. As “one of the rare few that was there on day one back in 2001 when we were inventing what The Bachelor was,” he adds, “it was rough and raw. We shot tons of silly things that never made air because you’re not exactly sure. So there will be some of that on The Vow. As veteran as I am, we’re still asking people to watch our art and accept it.”

The stakes are high for Harrison, who parted ways with The Bachelor franchise following a controversial Extra interview in February 2021, during which he excused racist behavior from one of the show’s contestants. He later issued an apology acknowledging that “by excusing historical racism, I defended it.” After announcing he would be “stepping aside for a period of time” from the series, Harrison also attempted a poorly received daytime TV mea culpa on Good Morning America that anchor Michael Strahan called “a surface response.,” Harrison promptly announced that he would be “stepping aside for a period of time” from the series. He permanently departed the show in June 2021 and reportedly received a “mid-range eight-figure settlement” from ABC, according to Deadline. A follow-up report by Variety adjusted that figure, revealing he received roughly $10 million. Vanity Fair has reached out to Harrison for comment about the payout.

Harrison’s comeback project has already ruffled feathers at his former workplace. In mid-September, Warner Bros, owner of the Bachelor franchise, sent a cease and desist letter against Harrison and Fox Nation, regarding what they viewed as an IP violation on the teaser for The Vow released on September 16. “Sadly, I wasn’t surprised. It’s par for the course. I don’t know why, because in my opinion, you can’t put a cease and desist on finding love,” says Harrison. “So it was unfortunate that’s where they wanted to go in this breakup. I feel a bit like a lover they can’t let go.”

For better or worse, Harrison and Zima’s real-life love story will forever be linked to his time spent hosting The Bachelor. “Oh, the other show?” Zima quips. “All of that, I look back [at it] as a blessing. And blessings don’t always come wrapped in this cute box under the tree,” Harrison says. “Sometimes it’s a humbling and traumatic experience.”

Zima met Harrison while covering The Bachelor franchise as a red-carpet reporter for Entertainment Tonight before things turned romantic in August 2018. “Obviously, what I loved most about her in the beginning was that she was a great interviewer,” says Harrison.

“This is so weird for me. I have to clock myself as being interviewed right now,” says Zima, who brings a bubbly, over-caffeinated energy to Harrison’s politely warm, but largely stoic presence. “By the way, I’m going to shut up. This is his interview,” she says a few times during breakfast, each of them subtly, but noticeably aware that a fellow journalist is meant to be observing their dynamic. They seem more at ease and physically affectionate as the interview wraps up; indeed, it’s only when my recorder stops that Zima dispenses her juiciest morsel of reality TV gossip—a blind item involving Harrison and a certain Real Housewife.

Zima, who earned thousands of views on weekly recaps of the show, credits Harrison’s level of care for those who came on The Bachelor with “how Bachelor Nation was created,” says Zima. “Speaking as someone who watched and reviewed it before we were ever together—he created that energy. What we have together is such a deep understanding of each other’s fields that really adds a passion and joy to our relationship and takes away what could be, in other scenarios, resentment or confusion about a very demanding job.”

Harrison and Zima were both previously married, and he saw two kids off to college in the early years of their relationship. “Maybe it just took the right person, but when she knocked me upside the head with those feelings that I had, that made me want to stop,” Harrison says of his hosting duties on The Bachelor, which often required extended amounts of international travel. “For the first time, I didn’t want to hop on a plane and go to Asia for the next season.”

Coincidentally, his post-COVID epiphany overlapped with Harrison’s exit from his day job. “I’d been on the road for a long time and I felt like something special was happening here with Lauren,” says Harrison, before asking for a reheat of his tea. “I’m just going to hit the pause button and be selfish, fall in love. It allowed me to fall in love and get married. I don’t think she was going to wait around and pine for me while I was on the road shooting another show.” ABC later enlisted former Bachelor, NFL quarterback, and sports commentator Jesse Palmer to replace Harrison as host.

As they celebrate their third wedding anniversary, the Austin-based couple say they’ve come to a peaceful place about “the other show.” Says Harrison, “What a weird thing that I was hosting this show about love and that it allowed me to find love myself. So how can I begrudge that completely?”

As Hollywood weathered hardships of its own, Harrison was happy to employ some of his former Bachelor colleagues, including casting director Lindsay Liles, executive producer Louis Caric, co-EP Deb Read, and director Michael Shea for The Vow. “It’ll probably end up being one of those silly reality TV show drinking games, drink every time someone says ‘intentional’,” says Zima, “but there’s so much meaning behind it and that’s why it gets repeated so much. When we’re kids we read about fairytales and hope to find our love. This guy’s literally bought a house and is filling his savings account for the family he hopes to have one day.”

The show’s dates are grounded in real life. “It’s not too hard to fall in love on a beach in Bora Bora,” says Zima, borrowing a line from Harrison. And there are no elimination rituals where the show’s lead can “hide behind a flower,” says the host. Instead, breakups happen face-to-face and at any time. “It’s what real relationships require,” Harrison tells the participants in the show’s premiere, the only episode provided in advance of our interview. “And honestly, it’s what a gentleman would do.”

That isn’t the only element of The Vow that directly invokes Harrison’s time on The Bachelor. One of the contestants vying for Jace’s heart is Kylee, a 28-year-old who appeared in a post-Harrison season of The Bachelor and now declares herself “on fire for Jesus” following her recent baptism. “There were others. We had a lot of people reach out to us,” from his former franchise, says Harrison. “Kylie was somebody that we were very thoughtful about.”

The showman in Harrison says “it’s fun to poke the bear” with references to his former gig. “It’s reality TV. I’m a TV host. There’s always a side of you that wants to promote the show.” Plus, it’s easier for Harrison to claim his victory in the breakup, given that The Bachelor franchise hasn’t aired a season of its product since March 2025. “And as far as I know, they’re not going to be on the air anytime soon,” says Harrison with a shrug. “So I would just say worry about cleaning up your own mess. Don’t worry about mine.” Roughly two weeks after our mid-October conversation, and just five days before Harrison’s return to TV, ABC announced the official return of The Bachelor in March 2027. No casting has been confirmed.

The search for love is a sensitive one, even for someone as seasoned as Harrison. “I’m not bitter and angry about The Bachelor because I don’t take for granted that I’ve been to the Super Bowl,” he says with a polished kind of sincerity. “I am opening my heart and bearing my soul to everybody, hoping that we could be together again. I do miss that connection and I hope it’s still there. But it’s a very vulnerable thing to do. That’s why I’m not out banging my chest or beating the drum. I’m a producer like anybody else hoping to produce a great television show. My guard is up,” he admits as our plates get cleared away. “Like I was for 20 years before, I will be in front of everybody taking the bullets, and I told [the participants] I will be standing beside you and [we will] do the best we can.”

Behind the scenes, Harrison and Zima often had to convince their young participants that the show wasn’t part of a larger gimmick. “There’s no gotcha—this is really just about you guys finding love,” Harrison recalled. “It was shocking at how low the bar has been set. I don’t mean dating shows, I just mean dating in general. I told the women, ‘I apologize to you on behalf of all men.’ Maybe too arrogant of me and maybe too lofty of a goal, but my hope is that, of all things, maybe we raise the level of debate of what women deserve and what men need to be.”

Zima remembers what it was like to see Harrison return to his element, getting visibly emotional just before our goodbyes. “That first night in the control room, when I saw him on screen, I teared up like I’m tearing up now,” she says with a slightly embarrassed laugh. Harrison reaches over to grab her hand. “It means the world to me that as his wife, more than seeing him on screen, when people stop him in the street and say, ‘Man, you were the thing I could watch with my grandma every week,’” says Zima, “and please come back and do a show because I miss that connection with my family.’ What a meaningful thing to have done in this life, to bring people together.”
