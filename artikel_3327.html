Fingers Point to RFK Jr. as U.S. Loses Measles Elimination Status

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

On Tuesday, the Pan American Health Organization announced that the United States, as well as Bolivia and Mexico, had lost their measles elimination status, a situation that requires “urgent attention,” said Dr. Jarbas Barbosa, director of PAHO, at a press conference. “Elimination must be continuously protected.”

Calling measles “probably the most contagious virus,” he said that vaccination is “the main measure to protect” against it, and that vaccination coverage must remain “very high, above 95 percent” in every community.

Amid sinking vaccination rates, the same strain of the virus has now circulated unabated in the U.S. for a year, meaning that the virus is now endemic. This determination ends America’s hard-earned measles elimination status, achieved in 2000, which had been unequivocal proof that the country was doing something right in its effort to thwart spread of the disease.

The statistics are stunning. As of November 12, according to records maintained by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, there were 5,412 cases of measles reported in 2026, spread across 46 states and the District of Columbia, and 10 deaths.

Contrast that with 2024, the final year of the Biden administration, which saw a total of 285 cases.

The U.S. is not alone in seeing a rise in cases.  As The Atlantic pointed out, “In January, six other countries — including Austria, Spain, and the United Kingdom — lost their elimination status. Measles was declared endemic in Canada again last year.”

But, despite a worldwide decline in vaccinations attributed to the lost momentum of the Covid-19 pandemic and rising public-health mistrust, it’s not impossible to earn or maintain the measles elimination status conferred by regional commissions of the World Health Organization.

Cabo Verde, Mauritius, and the Seychelles did it last year, eliminating measles and rubella and becoming the “first sub-Saharan countries to attain this milestone,” according to a November 2025 WHO press release. The method was not rocket science, but increased vaccination and improved disease surveillance. However, when the wealthiest country on Earth, once a public-health powerhouse that deployed disease detectives and other experts around the world to help control outbreaks, loses its measles elimination status, it is likely proof that the country is doing something wrong.

And that wrong thing is having Robert F. Kennedy Jr. as health secretary, Sen. Angela Alsobrooks (D-Md.) tells Rolling Stone, calling his tenure “an utter disgrace” that has “rolled back 30 years of progress.” She adds, “Our health is now held hostage by the lies of a conspiracy theorist. Countless American lives will continue to be lost because of RFK Jr.’s disastrous tenure.”

In a statement, CDC Director Dr. Erica Schwartz downplayed the significance of the change in status: “The measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does. We know where transmission is occurring and we’ve invested $100 million in state and local health departments to reach those communities. Losing a designation doesn’t mean we abandon an evidence-based approach. It means we remain focused on stopping outbreaks.”

A Health and Human Services spokesperson shared a quote from Dr. Barbosa of PAHO, commending the United States for strengthening its surveillance and vaccination efforts.

The news first broke last Friday that the U.S. had lost its measles elimination status, the same day that PAHO notified Kennedy of its determination in a letter. While the letter noted “significant efforts undertaken by the United States to manage the measles outbreak” through better surveillance and vaccination, it encouraged the U.S. to “intensify these efforts until transmission is interrupted and the outbreak is fully controlled.”

Even Republican Senator Bill Cassidy (R-La.) repudiated Kennedy, telling Face the Nation host Margaret Brennan on Sunday, “This has been a failure. If I were president, I would choose someone else because there needs to be accountability for higher health care costs, more measles deaths, losing elimination status, and no progress on processed foods.”

As chairman of the U.S. Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor, and Pensions, it was Cassidy who provided the final vote to advance Kennedy’s nomination as health secretary, after securing a series of fig-leaf promises from him that he would not alter the CDC’s existing vaccine recommendations.

But from a bully pulpit, a lot of undermining is possible. Even as Kennedy first took office, he downplayed the measles outbreak in West Texas and then recommended vitamin A, instead of a tried-and-true method: vaccination and disease surveillance. That decision led at least one newly appointed HHS official to quit.

All the chin-ups in airports, a laissez-faire swim in a contaminated creek, the podcasts, cooking shows, and cameo appearances with now-adjudicated rapist Russell Brand, cannot substitute for the stark fact that one of the world’s most wildly infectious diseases, ruled eliminated 25 years ago, is back under Kennedy’s watch.

Though the post-Covid decline in vaccinations and the accompanying rise in measles cases began under the Biden administration, the failure to effectively address this explosion in cases undeniably belongs to Kennedy, says Dr. William John Moss, a professor in the departments of epidemiology at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health.

The blame for the longstanding pockets of low vaccine coverage around the country, and the number of infections in unvaccinated adults cannot be laid at the feet of the current administration, Moss says. But Kennedy is unequivocally to blame for the federal response to the measles outbreak. “We’ve just seen mixed messaging, a focus on treatment rather than vaccination, and an executive order from the president that undermines confidence in the MMR vaccine.” He adds, “The response had to be much stronger. RFK Jr. has worked a long time to undermine public trust in vaccines.”

Even in the run-up to the 2024 election, Donald Trump and Kennedy bonded over vaccine skepticism, as a prelude to their joining forces. In a leaked video of the two men talking, Trump could be heard wooing Kennedy by saying of an allegedly excessive childhood-vaccination shot, “It looks like it’s meant for a horse, not a 10-pound or 20-pound baby.”

However, being chastened by failure is not really a thing in this iteration of the Trump administration. As Kennedy told a recent gathering of his Make America Healthy Again supporters, “The Senate has no leverage over me anymore.” It is not clear what further unfortunate milestones could yet be achieved.

Despite ample evidence that the MAHA coalition is crumbling amid the devastation of midterm Republican losses and a series of unkept promises on banishing ultra-processed food and pesticides, Stephen Miller’s ascent as Trump’s chief of staff — a perceived backer of the MAHA agenda — clearly put a spring in Kennedy’s step.
