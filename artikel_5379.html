The 10 best cities in the world in 2026, ranked by Time Out

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Ten top-ranked cities from Time Out's 2026 list of 50 show how residents' happiness and visitor appeal each rest on different supports.

Most travelers assume that a great city to visit is simply a great city, but the street that thrills someone on a weekend trip is also somebody's daily commute. Travel demand does not spread evenly across the map. It gathers in a few famous places. "This imbalance — too many people in too few places — is at the heart of the issue, and it mirrors global travel patterns," Tim Oakes, managing director of Inside Travel Australia, told ABC News.

The best-known names account for the largest share of that traffic, and New York alone received 65 million visitors in 2025, according to NYC Tourism + Conventions. When the most celebrated cities carry that much traffic, the people who live in them share their sidewalks, trains, and restaurant tables with a steady stream of outsiders. The more useful question in 2026 is not which city is the most exciting to visit. It is the cities that manage to work for visitors and residents at the same time.

What makes a city pleasant to live in and what draws visitors often depend on different things, even when both look strong in the same year. A resident cares about the cost of a weeknight dinner, a park within walking distance, and a commute that works. A visitor weighs hotel rates, the cost of the trip in their home currency, and how crowded the famous sights will be.

Some of those conditions lie outside any city's control, such as hotels cutting prices to compete for fewer guests, visitors whose home currency is unusually strong, growing crowds, or a flood after heavy rain. A city may seem cheap, beautiful, or joyful this year, partly because of circumstances that could change by next year. The useful way to judge any city's standing is to ask what supports it and what could take that support away.

Time Out set out to find the world's best cities by balancing how much they appeal to visitors with how livable they are for the people who call them home, and it took in places as different as Cape Town, Seoul, Edinburgh, and Shanghai. Each city is there for its own reason, whether food, scenery, low prices, culture, or sheer excitement, and no two strike the balance between residents and visitors in the same way. For each one, the questions are: what sustains its strength, and what could upset that balance?

The case for Melbourne, Australia, comes from the ordinary days of the people who live there, not from any single landmark. Time Out ranks Melbourne first among the world's cities, and the firmest evidence for that placement comes from locals, 94% of whom give high marks to the food they eat in their own city. Eating out is part of daily life for Melbourne residents, so a visitor mostly eats in the same places locals already use. What outsiders find in the city comes straight from what the people who live there enjoy. Young people deciding where to settle also think well of Melbourne, which places third worldwide as a home for Gen Z. That suggests its livability matters for the years ahead as well as for today.

Visitors have extra reasons to come in 2026, though those reasons belong to the calendar more than to daily life in the city. Fans broke attendance records at the Australian Open this year, and the NFL is set to stage its first regular-season game in Australia at the MCG. That game gives American football fans who might not otherwise consider a trip a reason to come. Both events depend on a schedule that changes from year to year, while the food and the everyday livability are there every week. Residents' daily life is therefore the steadier basis for Melbourne's standing.

Melbourne's case is weakest after dark. Seventy-seven percent of locals recommend the nightlife, and while that is a clear majority, fewer residents vouch for it than for the food. Residents and visitors share the city's newest rail link equally. Trains have begun running through the Metro Tunnel, and office workers and sightseers board the same line, so neither group gains at the other's expense.

Melbourne's everyday pleasures belong to residents and visitors alike, and that is why both groups are well served. Whether the excitement of this year's events lasts beyond 2026 is still an open question.

For the people who live in Shanghai, low prices are part of ordinary life. Shanghai posted the highest affordability score of any city, according to Time Out, and 88% of its residents call eating out cheap. For most of them, a meal out is a routine expense rather than a treat, and that stays true month after month for anyone who lives there. A visitor's biggest bill is usually a bed rather than a meal, though, and hotel prices depend on different forces.

Cheap meals do not mean cheap nights. Price competition among hotels was clear in Shanghai, as it was in Xinjiang and Yunnan, according to Trip.com data reported by CNBC. Even so, a median one-night stay costs 595 yuan (US$88), which is a real expense in a city where residents find dining out inexpensive. The same competition showed up in regions far from Shanghai and very different from it, which suggests the pressure on hotel prices comes from forces larger than any one city. "While tourism is still a bright spot, [it] cannot escape this broad macro trend," Gary Ng, senior economist at Natixis, said.

Foreign travelers are too small a group to hold up the travel market on their own, even as entry gets easier for some of them. Canadian and British travelers were approved for visa-free entry on February 15, but overseas visitors account for only 12% to 13% of tourism spending in China, according to a Natixis estimate reported by CNBC. The hotel market that visitors use therefore depends largely on domestic travelers, a different group from the residents who enjoy cheap meals.

Shanghai passes the affordability test for the people who live there today. For the people who visit, the value is real, but it rests on Chinese travelers and a wider economy that no single city controls.

Edinburgh's standing rests mostly on the people who live there rather than on the people who fly in. Contentment with ordinary life is close to universal among residents, and 91% of them say daily life in the city brings them joy, which puts Edinburgh among the happiest places surveyed, according to Time Out. The city also ties for the top walkability score, and those results help place Edinburgh third in the world. Residents give high marks to their parks and green spaces as well, and 94% of them approve of what they eat in their own city.

Edinburgh is easy to walk largely because it is small. VisitBritain says that a person can cover the entire city center on foot in a single day. Planners in a sprawling metropolis could not create that kind of compactness, however well they designed its streets. For a visitor, the small center means getting around the way a resident does, on foot, without having to learn a transit system.

Many outsiders come for the festivals, and VisitBritain counts 10 of international standing in the city. The Berliner Philharmoniker is opening the 2026 Edinburgh International Festival. Classical music fans who want to hear the orchestra have a reason to plan a trip around those particular dates on the calendar.

Good food, green space and short walks are there in every month, whether or not a festival is on.

Nearly every Londoner values the city's culture. Time Out reports that 99% of locals rate the city's culture scene highly, and that near-unanimity helps place London fourth. Time Out's own staff agreed with the residents, giving London the most votes in a tie with Tokyo. In London, the culture that tourists come for is the same culture that Londoners value in their own lives, so the city serves both groups at once.

Londoners will have three major new museums to visit this year, and not all of them are in the old center. V&A East is opening in Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park in Stratford, in East London, according to the Museums Association. V&A East is also working with young people as partners. Closer to the center, the London Museum is moving into the former Victorian General Market at Smithfield, and it will give an old market building a new use. Londoners in the east will gain a major museum close to where they live.

Londoners also rate their green space highly, with 94% of locals giving it high marks. Travelers $TRV hoping to see the London Museum in its new home should keep their plans flexible. The museum is expected to open at Smithfield in the autumn, but the Museums Association describes the timing as approximate rather than fixed, and a trip built around a single day could miss it. That uncertainty matters little, because no single opening decides whether London deserves a visit. "You'd struggle to find a place more varied, idiosyncratic and exciting anywhere on earth," Joe Mackertich, Time Out's editor-in-chief for the U.K., said.

For now, London pleases both its residents and its visitors, and the question for 2026 is whether its new museums in the east and the center will win over Londoners as well as tourists.

New Yorkers find their own city more thrilling than residents anywhere else find theirs. Some 78% of locals named New York the most exciting city in the world, according to Time Out, the highest share of any city by a wide margin. New York ranks fifth, and that excitement is what most sets it apart. The praise comes from people who live in the city day after day, not from tourists who see it for a weekend. What it leaves open is whether that thrill reaches the people who come to visit.

Foreign travelers have not answered that excitement with more trips. International arrivals fell 3.2% in 2025, according to NYC Tourism + Conventions, though the decline was smaller than forecast and visits from the U.K., Italy and Mexico rose. The pullback is modest, but it comes from a group that brings the city a large share of its tourism money. "The international visitor market is crucial to our economy, accounting for 50% of tourism spending," Julie Coker, president and CEO of New York City Tourism + Conventions, said. New York's appeal, therefore, rests on two separate groups. Residents supply the excitement, while foreigners whose numbers are wavering supply much of the tourism money.

The city's tourism promoters expect a modest rebound. NYC Tourism + Conventions forecasts 66.3 million visitors in 2026, but that number is a projection from the organization that markets the city, not a count of arrivals. Nothing in it shows whether foreign travelers, a group so important to the city's tourism spending, will make up a larger share of those visitors.

New Yorkers have given their verdict on the city's excitement, and whether visitors from abroad will come back to share it remains an open question.

The people who live in Cape Town, South Africa, make the strongest case for it, and they begin with the scenery they see every day. Locals rate the city's setting above every other city's, with 86% calling it the most beautiful, the top beauty score of any city, according to Time Out. That judgment comes from people who live beneath the mountain and beside the coast, not from visitors holding cameras. Cape Town ranks sixth in the world, and on the residents' side of the balance, its case rests on that regard for the city's beauty.

Visitors come for the price as much as for the view, and Europe supplies most of South Africa's overseas tourists. Selene Brophy, Senior Editor at Time Out South Africa, said that "if there’s one thing Cape Town does exceptionally well, it’s offer outstanding value for a world-class experience." Europeans made up 72.5% of overseas tourists to South Africa in February 2026, according to Cape Argus, while travelers from Asia accounted for only 7.1% of the country's overseas visitors that month. The country's tourism, and Cape Town's visitor trade with it, is therefore heavily tied to European travel.

That heavy reliance on Europe is the weak point in Cape Town's balance. Tensions in the Middle East could reduce the number of European tourists who make the trip, according to economist Dawie Roodt in comments reported by Cape Argus. No one in Cape Town has any say over that conflict, and residents' regard for their city does not depend on who flies in. Their love of its beauty is the steady half of Cape Town's appeal, and the flow of foreign visitors is the half that could change.

Cape Town clearly passes the test of being cherished by its own people, while the number of Europeans who keep coming to enjoy its value is the part still unsettled.

The people of Mexico City value the people who make and sell things in their city above any monument. Residents give their small, independently owned stores and craftspeople higher marks than they give the food, and 85% of locals rate those shops and makers highly, according to Time Out.

Residents treat shopping in their city as part of their own lives, not as a trade set up for tourists. The city's food reputation also comes from people who eat there every day, and 80% of locals rate the dining scene highly. Mexico City ranks seventh out of 50 cities worldwide, and residents' high regard for their shops and food is part of what supports that standing. A visitor here largely uses the same city a resident does, eating at the same tables and buying from the same small shops.

In 2026, people from far beyond the city will judge what its residents have built. Fans from around the world will come to Mexico City, a host city for the FIFA World Cup this year, and they will find the shops and dining rooms that locals already prize. The welcome offered to those fans rests on things residents value, though it leans more on food, culture, and nightlife than on the small makers locals rate highest. Mauricio Nava, Director Editorial of Time Out Mexico City, said, "we’re ready to welcome the world with our incredible gastronomy, culture and nightlife." Hosting a global crowd brings in new customers for the city's independent shops, and it also brings far more people who have never lived there.

Nobody can yet say whether the independent makers locals prize most will keep their local character once that crowd arrives.

Bangkok's appeal rests on how good an ordinary day feels to the people who live there, not on a skyline or a set of monuments. Asked about their daily routines, 79% of residents say those routines bring them joy, according to Time Out, and that contentment with ordinary days earns the city eighth place.

Much of that pleasure comes from eating, and 81% of residents rate the food scene highly. A visitor who eats where locals eat shares the same enjoyment. What travelers come for and what residents value are largely the same: good food and ordinary daily life.

That kind of contentment depends on the city staying usable, and in late September 2026, much of it flooded. Nearly 300 millimeters of rain fell in 48 hours, ABC News reports, and the worst flooding was near the canals. About 4,200 people were staying in shelters, and for those households, the ordinary days that residents praised had stopped. The danger went well beyond spoiled plans. The flooding "poses a risk to people's lives," Bangkok Governor Chadchart Sittipunt said. Knowing that residents are happy on a normal day reveals little about how safe and usable Bangkok is during a storm. That question now matters for anyone deciding whether to live in the city or travel there.

Visitors kept their main link to the outside world through the flood. Suvarnabhumi Airport remained in normal operation, according to ABC News, and flights could keep arriving and leaving while parts of the city were underwater. For a traveler, reaching Bangkok was easier than moving around the neighborhoods that took on water.

Bangkok passes the test of serving both residents and visitors on an ordinary day, but the outcome is not settled. No one can yet say how quickly residents will get those days back.

The people who live in Seoul, South Korea, and the people who judge cities for a living largely agree about it. Time Out reports that 79% of locals say Seoul makes them happy, so the city's appeal holds up for residents who live with it every day as well as for visitors. The experts who compare cities professionally reached the same verdict, tying Seoul for their third-favourite city. Those two verdicts rest on different grounds, which raises the question of what supports each one and whether they will stay aligned.

A wave of cultural building is giving visitors a new reason to come to Seoul, and much of that building is in the capital. Four of the new cultural landmarks opening in South Korea in 2026 are in Seoul. The most prominent of them, the Centre Pompidou, is set to open this year in Seoul's 63 Building with funding from Hanwha Group's cultural foundation, according to Smithsonian. The museum's backers want Korean art to help shape the global conversation, not simply receive art from the West. The museum is "not intended as a one-way introduction of Western art, but as a reciprocal exchange in which Korean and Asian art can generate new interpretations and questions within global art discourse," a spokesperson for the Hanwha Foundation of Culture said. That aim matches the national plan, since South Korea has named 2026 its Year of Art. The Pompidou has not opened yet, though, so it gives visitors and experts something new without accounting for the happiness residents already report.

Large museum projects do not always win over the people who live near them. Residents of Busan, South Korea, protested a proposed museum project there over ecological concerns and cost, according to Smithsonian. The Busan protests show that residents can turn against a cultural project when they judge its cost or its effect on the environment to be too high.

Nothing suggests that Seoul's residents are turning against their city's new museums. For now, the city's balance holds because locals and experts agree, and the test ahead is whether residents welcome the new landmarks or come to resent their cost.

Tokyo's young residents are happy with their city even as it fills with visitors. Among residents under 30, 91% say Tokyo makes them happy, according to Time Out. That figure is the verdict of the city's Gen Z residents, not of every Tokyoite, but it shows that Tokyo serves both the people who live there and the people who visit. That contentment earns Tokyo its place, and so far it has held up under the pressure that visitors bring.

Tokyo's crowds reached a new high in 2025, when the city set a record for international tourists. Those travelers gather in a few famous places rather than spreading out. Hayato Nagai, an associate professor at Ritsumeikan University Business School, says the strain is confined to certain spots and does not extend over the whole country. Tokyo has an extensive subway network, which Kyoto does not, ABC News reports, but Tokyo still sits with Kyoto, Osaka, and the area around Mount Fuji among the destinations under the heaviest pressure.

The pressure on Tokyo is likely to grow because Japan's government wants more visitors, not fewer. Only cars earn Japan more as an export than tourism does, ABC News reports, and officials aim to reach 60 million visitors by 2030, well beyond the record 42 million who came to Japan in 2025. With that much money at stake, the government has little reason to slow the boom. "Tokyo’s secret may be out, but the city remains a truly singular experience," Ili Saarinen, deputy editor of Time Out Tokyo, said. The distinct character Saarinen describes is the part of the city most exposed if Japan reaches its goal.

Tokyo passes the balance test today, and whether its young residents still feel as happy once Japan pursues its larger visitor goal remains an open question.
