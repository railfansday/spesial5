Crunchbase Data: Less Than 2% Of 2026 Venture Funding Went To Post-Election Red States

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

For the past few decades, the vast majority of U.S. startup funding has consistently gone to companies in blue states or purple states, where Democrats control all or some statewide offices.

But with Tuesday’s election results, the investment divide between blue, purple and red states has grown even more pronounced.

So far this year, less than 2% of all startup investment has gone to companies in post-election red states, defined as those that will have both a Republican governor and two Republican U.S. senators.

The red states’ share is much smaller than in years past due to five states shifting to purple. Of those, the largest venture hub is Texas, which shifted to purple with the election of Democratic Senator James Talarico and Governor Gina Hinojosa, followed by Ohio.

This year, as of Election Day, startups in the remaining 17 red states 1, which are home to nearly a quarter of Americans, had collectively pulled in just $8.6 billion in venture funding, with Florida, Tennessee and Utah among the largest contributors. To put that in perspective: It’s less than the typical nationwide total in a single week.

The 10 purple U.S. states2, meanwhile, are home to just over a quarter of Americans and pulled in about $28.6 billion this year as of Election Day. That’s much higher than the red state tally, but still less than 6% of all U.S. startup funding.

Beyond the aforementioned newcomers Texas and Ohio, several other purple states regularly pull in sizable venture funding, including Pennsylvania, North Carolina and to some extent Wisconsin.

Also worth noting: The divide between red, purple and blue states is somewhat less stark when it comes to round counts. About 12% of startup financings of $1 million or more went to purple state companies this year, and about 9% went to red states.

Broadly, it looks like backers are still finding plenty of investment-worthy talent outside of blue states when it comes to smaller deals. However, they are concentrating most of their capital into big AI rounds for California and New York companies.

This brings us to our next point: Blue states receive the overwhelming majority of startup funding, of which most goes to California.3 Per a Crunchbase analysis last week, California companies had pulled in more than three-fourths of all U.S. startup funding in 2026. Most of that stems from mega-financings for San Francisco-based OpenAI and Anthropic. While no other state comes close to California for attracting investment, the nearest runners up are also predominantly blue states, including New York and Massachusetts. In total, blue states have pulled in a staggering $481 billion in startup investment this year as of Election Day, per Crunchbase data, a new record. Politics and what makes for a venture hub So is it politics, or coincidence? Broadly speaking, the qualities that geographic startup hubs share — such as a deep tech talent pool, urban environs, leading tech companies and top research universities — aren’t specifically the domain of blue states. That said, predominantly rural states, which tend to lean red, are less likely to have those ingredients. Even so, there’s no rule against red state startup prowess. Austin, for example, managed to cement its status as a major tech hub even while Texas remained solidly under Republican control. And Utah also boasts a sophisticated startup scene. But following a Democratic electoral sweep, it’s hardly a surprise that more startup hubs now find themselves located in blue or purple states.
