DARVO at Work: Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender

Simone had done everything right. She had led her department's most impactful projects, trained half the team, and been told twice that she was "next in line" for a senior manager role.

Then the promotion went to Mark. He had been with the company for about a year, created extra work for others by turning in slop, and, as nearly everyone knew, had been in a relationship with their boss, Jody, for the last 6 months.

Simone didn't accuse anyone of anything. She politely asked HR whether the promotion process had been reviewed for a conflict of interest. Before long, Simone was labeled "a troublemaker," and somehow Jody was a victim of "harassment about her private life."

How does the person who raised a legitimate concern end up as the problem, while the person who created the problem ends up as the victim?

Jennifer Freyd (1997) coined the term DARVO— for Deny, Attack, and Reverse Victim and Offender—to describe how people often respond when confronted about wrongdoing. It was first studied in the context of domestic violence and abuse, but it occurs anywhere accountability is brought up, including the workplace.

DARVO can be deliberate and calculated, or reflexive and emotion-driven. Either way, the person who dared to speak up is vilified. Here’s how it might have played out with Simone and Jody.

The organization's attention switches to protecting Jody and Mark, the people with power, and away from advocating for Simone, someone originally harmed by that power. And the original concern, a manager granting a promotion to her lover in violation of conflict-of-interest policy, has disappeared from the conversation. It’s now about "You are attacking my private life and hurting my feelings."

DARVO is used so often because it is effective. Research shows it. In a 2020 set of experiments, people read the same incident with or without the accused person's DARVO response. When DARVO was present, readers judged the victim as less believable and more to blame, and the perpetrator as less responsible (Harsey & Freyd, 2020).

DARVO can also gaslight the original target. Harsey, Zurbriggen, and Freyd (2017) found that the more DARVO people encountered when confronting someone who had wronged them, the more they blamed themselves. Self-blame can lead to self-silencing, and Simone might come to wonder if she really is "just bitter" and should say nothing.

From the inside, DARVO can spur confusion. Simone starts editing her own memory: Was her tone too sharp? Did she misread something? Was she, deep down, just jealous? Each doubt could make sense, and that’s what makes them dangerous. Over time, that self-doubt can harden into silence—a coping strategy that may cost you who you are. So, if you find yourself wondering whether you were the problem, keep asking yourself: What actually happened first?

Research also offers some good news: People who were taught to recognize the DARVO pattern were less swayed by it. They saw the victim as more credible and the perpetrator as less so. Simply knowing the pattern's name weakens its grip (Harsey & Freyd, 2020). Of course, recognizing the pattern requires paying attention. The clearest signal is a subject change. If a question about a decision turns into complaints about the questioner's tone, personality, or propriety, ask why.

DARVO succeeds by making us forget the original question. In Simone's case, it was simple: Was there a conflict of interest? Everything that followed—the denials, the attacks on Simone, Jody's claims of being a victim—was meant to bury it.

Do not be distracted. Hold on to the original concern. Keep asking “what happened first.” And insist on the resolution of the original question.
