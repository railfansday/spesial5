7 novels where Americans in Paris solve mysteries

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

“Paris is so very beautiful that it satisfies something in you that is always hungry in America,” wrote Ernest Hemingway in a letter to his mother. The pull to Paris from across the ocean has always been strong. Since the 1800s Americans have traveled to the City of Light in search of artistic, intellectual, and personal freedom. They found better (but not perfect) racial equality, the mental and physical space to write or paint, affordable rent (in the good old days), cheap red wine, ethereal croissants, and very often romance.

The experiences of real-life Americans like Hemingway, Josephine Baker, Julia Child, and James Baldwin inspired a trove of fictional stories. In books, movies, and TV series these characters’ storylines often followed a similar trajectory. Once in Paris the Americans became more sophisticated, more assured, more themselves. Think of Frank Mulligan, Gene Kelly’s character in An American in Paris, Audrey Hepburn’s Sabrina, or the titular Emily in the Netflix hit Emily in Paris.

In the popular imagination, Paris is a place of joie de vivre, of pleasure. But, the rose-colored glasses through which we Americans often view the city also makes Paris an ideal setting for mysteries and thrillers. Who doesn’t love experiencing the frisson of danger lurking behind a pretty façade?

The main character in my new mystery novel The Foreign Correspondent’s Wife finds that very darkness in the City of Light. It’s 1949, and Kat Kelly is a whip-smart reporter who’s run out of the profession when men return home from the war. She comes to Paris with Charlie, her foreign correspondent husband, and enrolls in culinary school, eager to make a fresh start. But when one of her classmates, a former GI, is brutally murdered, she becomes obsessed with solving the crime, soon suspecting that the killing has its roots in the Nazi Occupation of Paris during World War II.

Like Kat, most American characters in mysteries or thrillers set in Paris have much to learn from the city. But, often, they bring a uniquely American perspective to whatever shenanigans they find themselves embroiled in—a can-do attitude, a refusal to take no for an answer, a moxie that makes them the ideal person to ferret out the truth.

Kat and Charlie join a long line of Americans starring in mysteries and thrillers set in Paris. Here are a few of my favorites.

No, this 2026 novel isn’t about the 2025 Louvre heist. But what timing! Piazza’s most recent page-turner features dual timelines, both revolving around the art of Vincent Van Gogh.

In the first, set in the 1990s, Emma is an American art student forced to clean apartments when her École des Beaux-Arts scholarship is canceled. Soon she gets drawn into the mysterious world of her employer, the disinherited matriarch of a stupendously rich—and potentially criminal—family of art dealers.

In the second, we follow Jo Van Gogh, the painter’s real-life sister-in-law as she struggles to build a market for Vincent’s work after his death. The stories braid together beautifully, and with fraud, assault, a soupçon of romance, and of course a heist, this one’s a real work of art.

The Maggie Hope series is anchored in World War II-era Britain, but in the seventh installment, the Massachusetts native finds herself in occupied Paris on a dangerous mission. The mathematician-turned-spy must discover the fate of both a fellow agent and her half-sister.

The narrative is teeming with fascinating historical details and real-life characters, but it never feels heavy-handed.The Paris Spy is a smart, fast-paced story that’s full of both action and heart.

The second entry in Pavone’s Kate Moore series finds Kate living in Paris with her family. She’s overseeing a sleepy CIA substation, attempting to shore up her flailing marriage, and planning dinner parties in an effort to fit in better with the other international moms.

When seemingly unrelated incidents flare up across the city—a bomb threat at a train station, a suicide bomber near the Louvre, an American billionaire spirited out of his apartment—Kate realizes that everything is connected and she may be the only person who can unravel the plot before it’s too late.

Packed with cameos from the city’s landmarks and biting asides, The Paris Diversion is the rare espionage thriller that’s as witty as it is twisty.

Mark Pryor, The Most Mysterious Bookshop in Paris

When Pryor launched the Hugo Marston series in 2012 with The Bookseller, his main character and our de facto detective was head of security at the U.S. Embassy in Paris. When his friend, an antiquarian bookseller, is kidnapped, Marston can’t help but pursue the case.

Nine books later and Marston has retired from the embassy to open a bookshop of his own. But when blackmail and murder come for a high-end chocolate maker located in an ancient Parisian chateau with a troubled history, Marston is drawn back into the city’s dark web of secrets.

While Black’s popular Aimée Leduc series wouldn’t make this list—Aimée may live in Paris, but she’s not a Yank—this spy thriller does. Set during World War II, Three Hours in Paris features Kate Rees, an American markswoman who is dropped into the Nazi-occupied French capital by the Brits with an assignment to assassinate Adolf Hitler on his visit.

When the plan falls apart, Kate must evade capture in a city teeming with danger while trying to figure out who betrayed her.

Colleen Cambridge, In the Spirit of French Murder

The fourth book in this post-World War II cozy mystery series is a gem. It finds American Tabitha Knight once again investigating a murder alongside her larger-than-life, food-obsessed pal Julia Child. This time, the victim is an elderly man who’s friends with Tabitha’s grandfather and his partner.

Eerie encounters with a medium, forays into dangerous parts of the city, and mouthwatering culinary descriptions keep this clever confection of a book moving at a brisk, entertaining clip.

Okay, this is cheating because the characters in this page-turner are Canadian, so North-American, but it’s too much of a favorite to omit. Number 16 in the Chief Inspector Armand Gamache series, which is mostly set in Quebec, this installment finds Gamache and his family in Paris on vacation. When his billionaire godfather is struck and gravely injured in a hit and run, Gamache realizes it was no accident.

The act of violence sparks a ticking clock mystery that sets the chief inspector and his son-in-law (and former deputy) Jean-Guy Beauvoir criss-crossing the city, unsure whom to trust and desperate to uncover the truth before it’s too late for their family.

Jenna Helwig is the author of The Foreign Correspondent, the food director at Real Simple, a five-time cookbook author, a culinary school graduate, and the creator of the Cookbookery Collective Substack.
