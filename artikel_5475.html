Single-Family Construction Rebounds in November, But Permits Nosedive

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Construction of new single-family homes rose a bit October, but permits fell sharply, the U.S. Census Bureau reported on Wednesday.

Single-family housing starts rose 4% last month from September, to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 864,000. On an annual basis, single-family starts were down 3.2% from a year earlier.

The monthly increase comes from upticks in the West and South, while the Northeast saw a larger increase in starts and the Midwest a slight decline. Single-family starts rose 15.7% on the month in Northeast, 4.2% in the South, and 3.4% in the West, but fell 0.8% in the Midwest.

Total privately owned housing starts, including more volatile multifamily units, rose 9.9% from September and 2.8% from October 2025. The month extends the uneven year for housing starts, which after after a brief August rebound, then fell sharply in September.

Meanwhile, new building permits in October fell 7.4% month over month and 8.7% year over year to an annualized 1,295,000 units.

At the same time, housing completions increased 6.5% from September but fell 10.4% compared to last year.

"The improvement came for both single family and multifamily starts. Single-family starts are still below pace for last year’s total, but the multifamily pace exceeds its total from 2025," Realtor.com economist Joel Berner said. "The starts pickup was strongest in the Midwest and South, but both of those regions saw more multifamily growth than single family."

Even so, the slowdown of building permits reflects greater pessimism in the housing market for builders. Many of the nation's major homebuilders have reported large year-over-year declines in revenue.

This week, the National Association of Home Builders/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index (HMI) plunged to a 14-year low, reflecting the industry's growing pessimism amid high mortgage rates and strained affordability.

With mortgage rates above 7%, housing affordability is a major constraint on buyers, said Selma Hepp, chief economist at Cotality. Hepp pined that on high rates that keep payments elevated, high construction costs and high insurance premiums and property taxes. Then add in everyday bills.

"For housing, the key question is not simply whether mortgage rates decline, but whether they fall enough to meaningfully improve purchasing power and bring more buyers and sellers back to the market," Hepp said. "Until then, I expect activity to remain relatively subdued, making the spring homebuying season an increasingly important test of whether the market can finally turn a corner."
