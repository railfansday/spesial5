I Hate Video Game Romance – Here's Why Grand Theft Auto VI Is The Exception

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Generally speaking, I do not like romance mechanics in video games. I never find them convincing. Relationships build way too quickly, and usually all it takes to win someone over in an RPG or farming sim is a couple of conversations and a few gifts. In Grand Theft Auto VI, however, I'm extremely invested in the romance between Jason and Lucia, and it comes down to the game's quieter moments and slice-of-life mechanics.

If you've played Baldur's Gate 3, Stardew Valley, Date Everything, or practically any other game with romance options, you're familiar with the gameplay loop. I ask an NPC about her hometown, sympathize with her tragic backstory, promise to be there for her, and then give her the book, cake, or mining resource she likes. Do it enough, and one day she'll approach me with the uncharacteristically heartfelt confession that she can't stop thinking about me. The process is too transparently mechanical: I can scan the dialogue options and instantly know which of the three sentences will boost my affinity rating the most. It feels less like an actual relationship and more like a long-term scheme to program someone into dating.

Many games structured like the ones above are written as well as they can be. The issue is scope and structure. Romantic relationships are nuanced and unique, but there often aren't enough resources during a game's development to represent more than the basic building blocks. Players are meant to suspend disbelief and fill in the gaps – even though you technically only spoke to that villager a few dozen times, you're married with a child, so you imagine it's actually been years.

Of course, this scope is the difference between Grand Theft Auto VI and these other games – Rockstar made time and space for everything in this world, and romance is no exception.

Since they're dating when the game begins, Jason and Lucia admittedly skip the courtship phase that many games focus on, but their relationship is still conditional and malleable – if you want them to like each other, you should be acting in their best interests. And while the mechanical aspect of their romance is still plenty visible, the actions to increase that meter are vastly expanded. There has been a lot of discussion about the early game, when you can prep for Lucia's release from prison by buying flowers or groceries, and while I like those gestures, they're not what sold me on the relationship. Say what you will about modern dating standards, but flowers and food are too easy – too obvious for me to feel connected to and invested in a couple.

When I played this section, I felt like Jason's hair looked a little unkempt, so I got a shave and a haircut. And, to my surprise, this improved his relationship with Lucia. She wasn't around and wouldn't see the results until later, but it still scored me partner points, as did repairing the roof, painting the bedroom walls, and even giving Jason's car a new paint job. These unexpected, specific tasks don't just fill up the relationship gauge – they communicate information about both characters. Lucia is really looking forward to getting out of prison, and having a finished house and a clean, put-together partner sets her in a better mood. Meanwhile, my actions establish that my version of Jason knows she likes these things and would do them without even asking.

Minor, specific tasks like these are romantic because they imply a degree of thoughtfulness that sidesteps the grand gestures expected of a long-awaited reunion. Yes, there should be apples in the fridge, but that's a basic need; knowing the exact conditions Lucia wants the house to be in makes me believe Jason actually knows her. It becomes even more effective – both in real relationships and in the credibility of the in-game partnership – when thoughtfulness is demonstrated on a day-to-day basis, in the quiet moments.

Grand Theft Auto VI is a long game with a slow, steady pace, leaving room for the intimate interactions other games wouldn't have time for. If you come home at the end of the day and see your partner watching TV, you can sit next to them and get a bonus. When they go to sleep, they'll call out to you and ask you to lie in bed and scroll on your phone with them. You can hold hands in public, buy each other clothes, and remember to text back promptly. There are large, dramatic moments and professions of love, yes, but players are likely to experience vastly more of these minor interactions than those scripted cutscenes.

Romance can be flashy, but it has to be more than that. It's nice to see the starring duo dance, drive, and shoot at foes during cutscenes. But it's the long car rides when Lucia reassures Jason that her mom likes him, or the evening on the couch when Jason talks about how he likes the way Lucia laughs, that make this relationship work narratively.
