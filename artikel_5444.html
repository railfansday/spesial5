Before the SS United States becomes a reef, 10 lessons from artificial reefs

Do artificial reefs work? Okaloosa County's plan for the SS United States raises the question, and 10 points from oil rigs to tire dumps help answer it

The SS United States won the Blue Riband for the fastest Atlantic crossing on its maiden voyage in 1952. Decades after it carried its last passengers in 1969, the roughly 990-foot liner is slated for a very different job. Under an agreement reached in 2024, Okaloosa County, Florida, is set to acquire the ship and make it an artificial reef off the Emerald Coast, near Destin and Fort Walton Beach.

The plan puts a familiar question back in front of anglers, divers and marine scientists: do artificial reefs work? The answer depends on what "work" is supposed to mean. A sunken ship can give recreational divers a new site to explore. It can give charter boats a known spot to find fish. It can also serve as a way to retire a large steel structure that has no other use. Those are three different goals, and success at one does not guarantee success at another.

The scientific debate centers on a narrower point. Fish gather around sunken structures, from oil platforms in the Gulf of Mexico to concrete modules placed on sandy seafloor. Researchers have argued for decades over whether those structures produce new fish or simply pull existing ones into a smaller area. The difference matters. A reef that concentrates fish without adding to their numbers can make them easier to catch. That result works against the goal of rebuilding fish stocks.

Environmental risk is the other half of the story. Ships can carry fuel, oils and PCBs. U.S. agencies, including the EPA and the Maritime Administration, issued guidance in 2006 on removing those contaminants before a vessel becomes a reef. Not every project has gone to plan. Off Fort Lauderdale, bundled tires dumped at the Osborn Reef starting in 1972 broke loose and scattered. A removal effort followed years later.

The 10 points below look at how artificial reefs change over time, where they can help fish and where they have failed, and how the SS United States fits into that record. They also take up a related question: whether artificial reefs are bad for the environment, and under what conditions.

The central scientific question about artificial reefs is whether they create new fish or simply gather fish that already exist. Fish crowd around sunken structure. That crowding alone does not prove a reef has added anything to the wider population.

Researchers call this the attraction-versus-production debate. Under the attraction view, a reef works like a magnet. It pulls fish in from surrounding waters and nearby natural habitat. The total number of fish in the region stays the same, and only their location changes.

Under the production view, a reef adds habitat that lets more fish survive and grow. The region would then hold more fish than it did before the structure went down. A reef that does this increases the supply of fish rather than reshuffling it.

The two outcomes look identical to a diver or an angler. A wreck crowded with snapper or grouper could reflect new production. It could also reflect fish that moved in from somewhere else. Counting fish on the reef itself cannot separate the two. Answering the question means looking at the population across a whole region, not at a single site.

J.A. Bohnsack set out the problem in a 1989 paper in the Bulletin of Marine Science. Its title asked whether high densities of fishes at artificial reefs result from habitat limitation or behavioral preference. That question set attraction and production side by side as competing explanations. It gave researchers a hypothesis to test rather than an assumption to accept.

Grossman, Jones and Seaman returned to the evidence in a 1997 paper in the journal Fisheries. The title put the debate plainly: "Do artificial reefs increase regional fish production?" The word "regional" points to the scale at which the answer has to be found.

Neither paper settled the question for artificial reefs in general. The answer varies from case to case. It depends on which species is involved, where the reef sits and how heavily the surrounding area is fished.

That conditional answer shapes how reef projects are judged. A structure that adds fish of one species at one site may only concentrate a different species at another.

For anyone asking whether artificial reefs work, attraction versus production is the starting point. A reef can draw crowds of fish without adding a single one to the ocean. Whether it also builds fish populations has to be answered one reef, one species and one region at a time.

Okaloosa County, Florida, agreed in 2024 to acquire the SS United States and turn the retired ocean liner into an artificial reef. The county has announced plans to place the ship in the Gulf of Mexico off the Emerald Coast, near Destin and Fort Walton Beach.

The agreement moves the liner from the SS United States Conservancy, the nonprofit group that had owned it, to a local government. A reef gives the hull a use that does not depend on keeping it afloat and maintained.

The Emerald Coast is the stretch of the Florida Panhandle that faces the Gulf of Mexico. Destin and Fort Walton Beach both sit within Okaloosa County. Charter fishing boats, dive shops and beach tourism are central to the local economy. The county's stated aims for the ship serve two of those groups. It wants a destination for recreational divers and a large structure that draws fish for anglers.

The ship's design shapes what kind of reef it could become. The SS United States is a multi-deck passenger liner. On the seafloor, its hull would hold decks, passageways and interior compartments stacked well above the sand. That vertical relief gives reef fish places to shelter. It also gives divers more to explore than a flat bottom offers. Farther west along the same coast, the USS Oriskany, sunk off Pensacola in 2006, already serves as a dive and fishing site built from a large retired hull.

The plan still has required steps ahead of it. Florida's artificial reef program is coordinated by the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission, which works with coastal counties on reef sites. A reef deployment in U.S. waters also needs a permit from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. National policy on artificial reefs traces to the National Fishing Enhancement Act of 1984, which called for a National Artificial Reef Plan. The ship must also be stripped of contaminants before it can be sunk.

Until those steps are done, the SS United States reef remains a plan the county has announced rather than a finished site.

The SS United States is an ocean liner built at Newport News Shipbuilding in Virginia, launched in 1951 and placed in service in 1952. It won the Blue Riband on its maiden voyage, carried passengers until 1969 and passed to the SS United States Conservancy in 2011.

The Blue Riband is an unofficial title held by the passenger liner with the fastest recorded crossing of the Atlantic. Shipping lines on both sides of the ocean competed for it. Taking it on a maiden voyage meant the SS United States set the record the first time it sailed with paying passengers.

The result carried weight for an American ship. British, German, French and Italian liners had traded the record for decades before 1952. The ship that held it immediately before the SS United States was the Queen Mary, a British liner operated by Cunard.

Naval architect William Francis Gibbs designed the liner. He gave it an aluminum superstructure to cut weight. He also used fire-resistant materials in place of wood across most of its interiors. The U.S. government helped pay for construction. In return, the ship was designed so it could be converted into a troopship in wartime.

That link to defense planning suited the builder. Newport News Shipbuilding, in Virginia's Hampton Roads region, is better known for building warships and aircraft carriers for the U.S. Navy. The finished liner is about 990 feet long. United States Lines operated it on the transatlantic route between New York and European ports.

Its passenger career ran from 1952 to 1969. By the late 1960s, jet airliners carried most travelers across the Atlantic, and ocean liners had lost much of their business. The SS United States was withdrawn from service in 1969 and never carried passengers again. Over the following decades it passed through a series of owners. Several plans to return it to service or redevelop it did not go forward.

In 2011, the SS United States Conservancy, a nonprofit formed to preserve the ship, took ownership of the liner. Okaloosa County's announced reef plan would give the vessel a different purpose. Its long steel hull, stacked decks and enclosed interior spaces are the features that plan would put to new use on the seafloor.

A ship must be stripped of fuel, oils, PCBs and other contaminants before it can be sunk as a reef in U.S. waters. The main reference for that work is a 2006 best-practice guidance document from the Environmental Protection Agency and the Maritime Administration.

The document is titled "National Guidance: Best Management Practices for Preparing Vessels Intended to Create Artificial Reefs." Congress directed the two agencies to write it. It gives agencies and reef sponsors a shared standard for getting a vessel ready to go to the bottom.

Fuel and oil come first. Tanks, bilges, engines and piping can hold residues long after a ship stops sailing. Oil left aboard can leak as the hull settles and corrodes. That would turn a habitat project into a pollution source.

PCBs are a harder problem. Polychlorinated biphenyls are industrial chemicals once used widely in electrical equipment, cable insulation, gaskets and paint. They persist in the environment and build up in the fatty tissue of fish and the animals that eat them. U.S. manufacture of PCBs ended under the Toxic Substances Control Act. Older vessels are therefore the ones most likely to carry them.

The guidance also covers asbestos, paint that can flake or leach, and loose debris. Floatable materials such as foam, plastics and wood are taken off the ship. Left aboard, they could break free and drift away after the sinking.

The USS Oriskany shows why these details matter. The retired aircraft carrier was sunk off Pensacola in 2006. Its preparation drew scrutiny because some PCB-containing materials stayed aboard under an EPA risk-based approval rather than being removed entirely. The carrier became a reference point in arguments over how clean a reef vessel needs to be.

The cleaning stage ties directly to a common question: are artificial reefs bad for the environment? A well-prepared hull offers hard structure for marine life without a chemical legacy. A poorly prepared one can release contaminants into the same food web it was meant to support.

Preparation also shapes the schedule of any ship reef project. Every cable run, fuel tank and painted surface that needs attention adds work before a vessel can be towed out and sunk.

Rigs-to-Reefs programs turn retired oil and gas platforms in the Gulf of Mexico into permanent fish habitat instead of hauling them to shore for scrap. The practice dates to the 1980s. Louisiana launched its formal program in 1986.

The idea starts with what a platform already is. A steel jacket rises from the seafloor to the surface, crossed by legs and braces. That frame offers hard structure through the entire water column.

Much of the northern Gulf seafloor is mud and sand, with little natural hard bottom. A platform adds vertical relief in a place that otherwise has almost none. Platform structures host dense fish communities. Recreational anglers and divers have sought them out for decades.

Federal rules normally require an operator to remove a platform once its lease ends and production stops. Full removal means cutting the structure free and bringing it ashore. Rigs-to-Reefs offers another path. A state agrees to take responsibility for the structure, and it becomes part of a permitted reef site.

Conversions generally follow one of three methods. In a tow-and-place conversion, the jacket is cut loose and towed to a designated reef area. In a topple-in-place conversion, it is laid on its side at the original location. In a partial removal, the upper section is cut away. That leaves enough clearance above the remaining structure for ships to pass safely.

Money shapes the arrangement. Leaving a jacket on the seafloor generally costs an operator less than full removal. In Louisiana, operators share part of those savings with the state. The state uses the funds to run its artificial reef program. Texas operates a similar Rigs-to-Reefs program off its own coast.

The approach has limits. Platform reefs can only go where platforms already stand or where they can be towed. That means site selection follows oil and gas geology more than fish biology.

Removal work also carries a cost to marine life. Some operators use explosives to sever platform legs, and those blasts can kill fish living on the structure. Tow-and-place and topple-in-place conversions still require cutting the structure loose, which can involve the same severing methods.

Reef Balls and similar purpose-built modules are concrete structures designed from the start to serve as reef habitat. Unlike sunken ships, they are shaped to stay put on the seafloor and to give marine life surfaces and openings to use.

A Reef Ball is a hollow, dome-shaped concrete unit with holes cut through its walls. The holes let water move through the structure. They also create sheltered spaces where small fish and invertebrates can hide. The rough concrete surface gives algae, sponges and other encrusting organisms a place to attach.

The design addresses a basic engineering problem. An artificial reef has to stay where it is placed through storms and strong currents. A heavy, wide-based concrete module sits low on the bottom and resists being moved. A builder can engineer that stability into a purpose-built unit from the start. It is much harder to guarantee with a structure that was designed for another use.

Material is the other difference. Concrete made for reef use contains no fuel tanks, wiring, paint or insulation. That means there is no hazardous material to strip out before deployment. Modules can also be made in different sizes and set out in planned arrangements. That lets reef managers decide how much structure goes where.

The trade-off is height and scale. A field of concrete modules generally offers low relief compared with a ship or an oil platform. Those larger structures can rise far up the water column. A low concrete reef serves different purposes than a tall wreck sunk partly to attract divers.

Purpose-built modules contrast with an older approach that relied on materials of opportunity. Texas, for example, sank decommissioned Liberty ships — the mass-produced cargo vessels of World War II — as reefs. Ships of opportunity deliver a large volume of structure in a single deployment. Designed modules give up some of that bulk in exchange for predictability.

For a reef planner, the choice between a module field and a sunken ship comes down to what the reef is for. The options are fish habitat at a chosen spot, a dive attraction or a mix of both.

Florida has turned three retired military ships into artificial reefs. The USS Spiegel Grove went down off Key Largo in 2002, the USS Oriskany off Pensacola in 2006 and the USS Vandenberg off Key West in 2009.

Each ship had a different military career. The Spiegel Grove was a Navy dock landing ship. The Oriskany was an 888-foot aircraft carrier that served during the Korean and Vietnam wars. It carries the nickname the Mighty O. The Vandenberg began as a World War II transport and later tracked missile launches for the Air Force.

The Oriskany lies off Pensacola, at the western end of the Florida Panhandle. That places it on the same stretch of Gulf coast as Destin and Fort Walton Beach. The other two ships sit at the opposite end of the state. Key Largo lies in the upper Florida Keys, and Key West sits near the end of the island chain.

The Spiegel Grove's sinking did not go as planned. The ship went down early and came to rest on its side rather than upright. Hurricane Dennis shifted the hull upright in 2005. The episode showed that a large hull does not always settle where its planners intend.

The three wrecks show what large ship reefs are meant to do. A hull this size gives divers decks, passageways and a shape they can recognize on the seafloor. It gives fishing boats a fixed, mapped target in open water. Neither use requires the ship to produce new fish. Both rely on the ship drawing people.

Backers of ship sinkings have offered a further reason. They argue that a large wreck can draw divers away from natural coral reefs already under stress. That is a stated aim, not a measured result. A ship on the bottom does not by itself show how many divers changed their plans or whether pressure on natural reefs eased.

For Okaloosa County, the Oriskany is the nearest point of comparison. The county's announced plan for the SS United States would put another large former ship off the Panhandle coast, aimed at the same divers and anglers.

The Osborn Reef off Fort Lauderdale, Florida, is a case where an artificial reef did not work. Tires bundled together and dumped on the seafloor starting in 1972 broke loose when their bindings failed, and the scattered tires became debris rather than habitat.

The project rested on a disposal rationale. Old car tires were a waste problem on land. Bundled together and placed offshore, they were expected to give fish and marine life hard structure to use. The same plan would keep those tires out of landfills.

The plan depended on the bindings. Each bundle had to stay intact for the tires to function as a fixed reef. Once those bindings gave way, the individual tires were free to move. The site no longer held together as a single structure.

Loose tires on the seafloor do not behave like a reef. A stable reef stays where it is placed. It gives organisms a fixed surface to settle on and gives fish shelter they can return to. Scattered tires offer neither of those things in a dependable way.

The failure turned a disposal project into a cleanup project. Years after the dumping began, a removal effort followed to take tires back out of the water. That work reversed the original logic of the site. Material sent to sea to avoid disposal on land had to be brought back to land.

Osborn illustrates a point that runs through artificial reef science. Whether a reef works depends partly on what it is made of and whether it stays in place. A structure that breaks apart cannot provide the stable shelter and settling surface that reef fish need.

The case also bears on a common search question: are artificial reefs bad for the environment? At Osborn, the answer was yes. The structure was not secured well enough to stay in place. The problem lay in how the reef was built and held together, which is a separate issue from whether added structure can serve as habitat.

Artificial reefs are most likely to increase fish populations where a shortage of hard habitat holds those populations down. Where heavy fishing is the main limit, added structure tends to gather existing fish in one place and make them easier to catch.

The distinction starts with how reef fish live. Many species spend their early days as larvae drifting in open water. To survive past that stage, they need to settle onto hard bottom, where crevices and ledges offer shelter from predators. Older fish also rely on structure for cover and, for some species, for feeding.

On a flat sand or mud seafloor, that kind of shelter can be scarce. If larvae arrive in good numbers but find few places to settle, the habitat itself becomes the bottleneck. Adding rock, concrete or steel in that setting can give more young fish a place to survive. That is the scenario in which a reef can add fish rather than simply move them.

The picture changes when the bottleneck is fishing. If a population is held down by how many adults are removed each year, new structure does little to raise its numbers. It can instead draw fish into tight groups at known spots. Many state reef programs publish site coordinates for anglers. That makes those groups easy to find.

Tropical tuna fisheries offer a parallel. Fishing fleets use fish aggregating devices, floating objects that tuna gather beneath, to concentrate schools and improve catches. Seafloor reefs can work the same way for bottom-dwelling species.

Red snapper in the Gulf of Mexico sit near the center of this argument. The species is commonly caught around artificial structures. How much those structures add to the stock, and how much they simply concentrate it, has been a long-running point of dispute among fishery scientists and managers.

For reef managers, structure and harvest rules interact. A reef placed in a habitat-poor area with limits on catch has a stronger chance of adding fish. A reef dropped into a heavily fished zone with no controls has a weaker one.

An artificial reef is not finished on the day it reaches the seafloor. Corals, sponges and other encrusting organisms settle on the structure over years. The reef slowly turns from bare steel or concrete into living habitat. Even so, it remains distinct from a natural reef.

The first layer is microscopic. Soon after a structure is submerged, bacteria and microscopic algae coat its exposed surfaces in a thin biofilm. That film changes the texture and chemistry of the surface. It also gives the drifting larvae of larger organisms a cue to settle.

Fast-growing filter feeders tend to arrive next. Barnacles, hydroids, bryozoans, tube worms and tunicates claim open space early. They feed on plankton carried past the structure by currents. Their shells and tubes add small-scale texture to surfaces that started out flat and smooth.

Sponges, soft corals and hard corals take longer. Reef-building stony corals grow slowly. Their colonies spread across a wreck or module over many years rather than a single season.

Which organisms take hold depends on depth, water temperature, light, current and the larvae that drift past. Reef-building corals host symbiotic algae that need sunlight to supply them with energy. On deeper or murkier sites, sponges, hydroids and other animals that do not rely on light often cover more of the surface.

The material underneath shapes the community too. Steel hulls corrode over time. Thin plating eventually weakens and collapses. That collapse changes the shape of the habitat and the shelter it offers. Concrete is more chemically stable in seawater and tends to keep its form.

A heavily colonized artificial reef still differs from a natural one. Natural coral reefs form over thousands of years. Generations of corals lay down calcium carbonate skeletons that pile into a complex framework full of crevices and caves. An artificial reef offers a ready-made shape but lacks that age and that layered structure.

The species mix differs as well. A sunken ship or platform often rises steeply from flat sand or mud, in a spot that had no hard bottom before. That tall profile and isolation can attract a different set of fish and invertebrates than a low, sprawling natural reef.

Artificial structures can also give non-native species a foothold. Tubastraea coccinea, an orange cup coral native to the Indo-Pacific, has spread across oil and gas platforms in the Gulf of Mexico.

For anyone weighing an artificial reef against a natural reef, the comparison shifts with time. A newly placed structure is mostly bare surface and visiting fish. Years later it can carry dense growth, but it remains a distinct habitat shaped by its material, depth and location.
