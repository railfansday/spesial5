Yield-Bearing eZAR Blurs the Line Between Stablecoin and Investment Product

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

AI-assisted summary, reviewed by NullTX editors.

eZAR opened its rand-pegged token to the public on November 2 with R303 million in circulation. Its reserve yield is the feature that puts the stablecoin label to the test.

Most stablecoin launches promise a token that never moves. eZAR's disclosures say its holders can make money or lose it. The rand-pegged token went live to the public on November 2, and its fine print reads more like an investment product than a payments coin.

According to launch reports, eZAR is now open to the public and to new institutional partners after almost a year of pilot operations. The company says R303 million worth of tokens had been issued and were in circulation as of October 30.

The project is a partnership between eZAR Ltd, asset manager Ninety One and AVBOB Financial Services, with AVBOB acting as lead institution and liquidity partner. CEO Sean Emery said the team put "getting it right" ahead of "getting it big" during the pilot. The message now is that eZAR is ready to scale.

The model also departs from the typical single-issuer coin. Neobanks, retailers, insurers and payment firms can plug the token into their own branded wallets and share in the economics. Transfers between participating wallets settle within seconds at any hour and, according to the company, cost nothing.

The reserves backing eZAR are invested through a money market strategy run by Ninety One, mainly in the Ninety One Money Market Fund. Returns are distributed daily to partners after partnership fees, and each partner then decides on what terms its own customers receive a share.

Ninety One's head of digital assets, Adam Duxbury, described it as new rails under an old discipline. His firm has run money market strategies for 25 years, he said, and South Africa should build its own digital rand rather than import a dollar coin.

The framing is clever, since it presents the yield as a feature of "digital cash that can grow." Yet that same feature makes the classification question hard to avoid.

The risk language is the most revealing part of the launch. eZAR describes itself as a crypto asset issued by the eZAR En Commandite Partnership. It states that the token is not legal tender, not a bank deposit and not a banking product, and that it carries no guarantee.

The details that follow set it further apart. The R1 redemption value depends on available liquidity. Money market returns can be positive or negative, so capital is at risk. Holders also own no units in the underlying fund and have no direct claim on it.

"Capital at risk" rarely appears in stablecoin terms. A payments token is meant to hold its value and let users redeem at par, yet eZAR's own documents hedge on both counts. Back in March, a market survey from Blockhouse had already noted that eZAR doesn't qualify as a stablecoin under most regulations. The live launch now tests that observation against a real product.

In the major rulebooks, the yield is the sticking point. The US GENIUS Act bars permitted payment stablecoin issuers from paying holders any form of interest or yield solely for holding, using or retaining the token. The EU's MiCA framework takes a similar line on interest. The logic is that a payment token paying a return starts to function like an unregulated deposit.

The OCC issued its final GENIUS Act rules on October 21, and they confirmed that ban for covered issuers. The contested ground lies elsewhere, in who sits between the issuer and the holder. Earlier OCC proposals set a rebuttable presumption against yield arrangements run through affiliates and related third parties, whereas the statute itself names only issuers.

eZAR's design lands squarely in that gray zone. Returns flow to partners, and the partners decide how customers share in them. Whether that amounts to an issuer paying yield or a partner passing on profit is precisely the kind of question US regulators are now wrestling with, and it would be a difficult one to argue before the OCC.

eZAR doesn't operate under the US or EU regimes, so none of those rules apply to it directly. eZAR Ltd is described as an authorised financial services provider responsible for the main technology and services behind the partnership. That technology also draws on the South African Reserve Bank's Project Khokha 2, where eZAR's parent company, Block Markets Africa, was a technical contributor.

This background signals a serious, institution-backed build rather than a speculative launch. Even so, the rand stablecoin field is getting crowded. ZARU launched earlier this year as an institutional-grade rand coin, and a pattern is emerging of different models competing for the same local payments niche.

The bigger question is whether South Africa will define stablecoins narrowly, as the US and EU do, or leave room for hybrid products like this one. Because the network is permissioned, the reported circulation figure also rests on company disclosure rather than a public ledger, which makes the regulatory and disclosure framework matter even more.

For everyday users, the appeal is easy to see. A rand token that moves in seconds, costs nothing to send and might earn a daily return makes an attractive pitch. For partners, shared infrastructure with a revenue share offers a way into digital payments without building a coin from scratch.

The trade-off sits in the disclaimers. A holder who treats eZAR like a bank balance could be caught off guard by liquidity-dependent redemption, or by a money market return that dips below zero. That gap between how the product feels and how it is legally defined is where regulators tend to step in.

eZAR is best understood as a hybrid: a payments token with an investment return attached. It may well succeed on those terms. Still, as more jurisdictions tighten the stablecoin definition around no-yield, par-redemption tokens, products like this will increasingly be judged on what they are rather than what they are called. How South African regulators treat eZAR in the coming months will show which way that debate is heading.
