Mamdani’s NYC public housing rebuild plan skirts DSA rebuke

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Delegates debated and ultimately rejected a resolution opposing redevelopment at two Manhattan public housing properties. Critics cite privatization concerns tied to a shift to project-based Section 8, while the city argues rebuild costs less than renovation.  AI Summary

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s quest to replace aging public housing with new buildings faced a potential political reckoning this past weekend. But Mamdani survived a challenge from like-minded Democratic Socialists.

Delegates to the city’s Democratic Socialists of America convention on Saturday voted down a resolution to oppose the city’s plan to demolish and rebuild two Manhattan public housing developments. It was the only one of nine resolutions to fail at a convention attended by more than 430 delegates.

Mamdani, who didn’t attend the convention, won a year ago on his vow to make New York more affordable. Delivering on that promise has meant sometimes walking a fine line. By proceeding with a plan developed under Eric Adams, a mayor critics accused of being in developers’ pockets, he risks being lumped in with his predecessor.

Democratic socialists now face similar choices elsewhere. In Los Angeles, DSA member Nithya Raman ousted outgoing Mayor Karen Bass on a pledge to build more homes faster. She will likely draw fire from neighborhood advocates opposed to higher density and zoning changes required by new state laws.

The plan is to demolish 2,056 public housing apartments in Fulton and Elliott-Chelsea Houses and rebuild them, with the New York City Housing Authority still owning them. Private developers would then add about 3,500 mixed-income apartments. Current tenants would get new apartments under private management, with rent held at 30% of income.

Had the DSA resolution passed, Mamdani could have faced stiffer backlash against a multi-billion project than he was already facing from tenant rights groups. Instead, Mamdani hopes the project stands as a model for rebuilding other decaying public housing properties elsewhere in the city.

Fulton and Elliott-Chelsea’s 18 buildings were built in phases from 1945 to 1965. Construction largely occurred under Robert Moses’ tenure as the city’s powerful unelected master builder.

Moses supported tall buildings, parks, highways and slum clearance to ease the path, including the site now being redeveloped. He dubbed the area’s tenements and factories blight.

But like public housing across the country, flawed maintenance policies left years of issues unresolved. Pruitt-Igoe in St. Louis began deteriorating within years of the 1954 completion of its 33 towers. The city demolished them by 1976.

Mamdani and DSA members in his administration say the Fulton and Elliott-Chelsea buildings are in such bad shape that tearing them down costs less than renovating them. This approach also adapts to shrinking federal money for public housing.

The administration says NYCHA faces about $78 billion in repair needs. Conversion cash flow is one of the few ways to pay for them.

“Too many leaders have walked away from public housing,” Matt Rauschenbach, a Mamdani spokesman, said in a statement last month. “We are not going to.”

Cea Weaver, who leads the Mayor’s Office to Protect Tenants and is a DSA member, told Gothamist that leaving the housing in its current disrepair is unacceptable given her socialist values.

The fix has been circulating for years, first under former Mayor Bill de Blasio. Under Adams, NYCHA announced in December 2021 that resident leaders had selected Related Companies and Essence Development as its development partners. Related is best known for Hudson Yards, the 28-acre Manhattan megaproject it built with Oxford Properties.

NYCHA began issuing relocation notices in July 2025, and two buildings are designated for demolition in the first phase.

Critics, including the tenants’ DSA resolution, point to Hudson Yards as evidence that a for-profit firm has no place managing public housing.

“Related Companies is scheduled to make hundreds of millions of dollars from federal funding as well as rents through this project, while we, as socialists, know that a billion-dollar company like Related has no business anywhere near our city’s public housing,” the DSA resolution states.

The resolution says residents have called the project a “land grab.”

Three tenants joined DSA and were elected delegates with the backing of the chapter’s Tenant Organizing Working Group. They argue the plan privatizes public housing. The buildings would move from the federal Section 9 program to project-based Section 8, in which the government subsidizes rent through vouchers.

“We have no interest in being privatized,” Celines Miranda, one of the new delegates, told Gothamist last week. “We have no interest in being demolished.”

The resolution called the buildings structurally sound and criticized developer Related Companies for withholding structural analyses. More than two dozen families, including many seniors, are refusing to move.

Tenants and neighbors also tried to stop the project in court. In July, a state appeals court upheld rulings that denied their requests for preliminary injunctions. The court determined that their challenge was filed after the four-month deadline that began when NYCHA issued its July 2025 decision to proceed.

The court also ruled that relocation, while “inconvenient and difficult,” especially for seniors, did not amount to irreparable harm.

Weaver said the activists do not speak for all residents and that many tenants want the project to proceed.

Only 37% of delegates backed the resolution in a pre-convention survey. It won 44% on the floor, suggesting tenants flipped some votes but not enough to win.
