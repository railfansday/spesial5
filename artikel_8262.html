Overview: The best backcountry ski packs of 2027 include the Ortovox Venet 30 ($170) for most skiers, the Raide EX55 ($599) for expeditions, the Dynafit Radical 30 Snow Pack ($190), and the Rab Descendor 40 Ski Pack ($200) for ski mountaineering. Our 14 testers—men and women—evaluated 18 packs over a full season in Colorado, Utah, Alaska, the Alps, and more, judging fit, weight distribution, access, and durability.

When traveling in the backcountry, it’s not just what you bring but how you carry it. Intuitive access points make finding and stashing items easy, and externally fastened gear needs to stay secure without blocking pockets. But making the most of capacity is arguably the most critical feature. As our testers make tracks farther out in the mountains and become more self-reliant, they tend to reach for higher-volume packs that can fit everything they need without overloading them to the point of a skin track gear explosion. We factored comfort into all of it while testing 18 packs, narrowing the field to the best backcountry ski packs of 2027 for every type of ski adventure.

Ski packs work best when you can avoid taking them all the way off and rooting through the main compartment when you need to pull out gear. That means shorter stops in the skin track—if you stop at all—which helps you keep momentum and body temperature up on cold days. The Venet 30 features triple-zipper access to the main compartment, which let our tester quickly grab his water flask while skinning around Hemavan, Sweden, and Helgeland, Norway. The large hipbelt was also a tester favorite. “While I loved the elastic waist on the Ortovox Freerider, the normal hip strap with the pocket on the Venet was a huge plus for general touring,” said one. “On longer days I kept snacks in it and on more freeride days I kept a GoPro, for which easy access allowed me to actually remember to pull my cam out before descents.”

Testers called this the most ergonomic and versatile pack they’ve used from Ortovox, thanks to its simplicity, the comfortable adjustability of its straps, and its full-contact back panel. The mini top pocket was well sized—not too deep and easy to access—and the hidden mini pocket in the avalanche tool compartment was useful for carrying an emergency bivy and small med kit, keeping them “out of the way” of the rest of your gear.

This pack’s ideal use is general touring with the occasional bootpack, but it’s capable as a more technical bag when ropes may be required. It performs well as a freeride sidecountry bag or on longer tours—comfortable for mellow days and functional for technical ones.

Features that would make this pack even more user-friendly: a small zip on the right back side to mirror the bottom zipper for water access, the ability to carry two ice axes (the elastic cord holds only one), and easier access to the pack when using side ski-carry.

Schlepping all your gear on a big hut trip or expedition-style endeavor has long presented a dilemma: how to carry a pack big enough for everything at basecamp while also having a daypack small enough for lighter missions. Bringing two packs is inefficient, but skiing with a few items jiggling loose in a giant frame pack feels cumbersome. The Raide EX55 expands to 75L and carries enormous loads, then cinches down once you’ve emptied most items out, so it skis like a little daypack. “During our big four-day ski traverse out of Bishop in the Eastern Sierra, I’d get to the base of a couloir, fill a bivy sack with all our junk, cinch the pack down, and boot up like I had a regular ski pack on,” said our Mammoth-based tester. “It skis incredibly well given how much gear it can swallow.”

Raide accounts for the added weight with thicker shoulder and hip straps and better load lifters than its smaller counterparts, the LF30 and LF40. All of it is ideal for heavier hauls. The EX55 doesn’t just turn into a giant sack and skimp on key features. You can still deploy your probe without taking off your pack—ideal for glacier travel of any kind—and the same goes for the shovel with the pack on one shoulder. Testers called the ski carry systems excellent, and being able to grab an ice tool without removing the pack is invaluable in steep bootpacks. “This pack strips backcountry skiing down to its absolute essentials but lets you do it on a bigger scale,” said one.

The one complaint we heard was that the EX55 lacks the external drop sleeve found in the LF30 and LF40 for carrying helmets and skins, so when the pack is loaded for overnight carries, it’s tough to stuff skins in and out of the front zip pocket. It also makes the pack very tall when fully loaded with a helmet inside.

Packs come in various sizes to fit different torso lengths, so measure your spine from the base of your neck to the top of your hips and choose your size accordingly. Torso lengths generally range from 15 inches, or size XS, to 20-plus inches, or size L. Because fit is the single most significant factor in pack comfort and carry, we recommend going to your local gear shop wearing the layers you plan to tour in and trying packs on. The hipbelt should securely wrap your iliac crest, and the back panel shouldn’t hit the back of your head when you’re wearing a helmet.

If half-day backcountry tours are your norm, choose a lower-volume pack, 20 to 30 liters, that fits just the essentials—shovel, probe, water, extra layers, snacks. If you typically spend full days in the backcountry, you’ll want a higher-volume pack, 30 to 40 liters, with room for the extra supplies you’ll need out in the elements. For overnight trips and expeditions, look for a 50-plus-liter pack.

Also make sure your avalanche shovel and probe slip easily in and out of the dedicated avy pouch in an emergency, and that the pack is big enough and organized enough for how you tour. Don’t be afraid to size up if you’re unsure how much space you’ll need—a few extra liters of empty space won’t weigh you down much. If you want to secure your skis to your pack, look for an A-frame or diagonal ski-carry strap system and back-panel suspension that can support the extra weight. The same goes for your helmet: most packs have integrated helmet carry, but not all do, so double-check if it’s essential to you.

Always carry avalanche safety equipment when traveling in the backcountry. That means wearing a beacon and carrying a shovel and probe in your pack. You’ll also need extra layers, a helmet, sufficient hydration—including something hot in an insulated bottle on longer backcountry days—snacks, a first-aid kit, and a basic repair kit.

To nail down the best ski packs of 2027, we seeded 18 products with 14 different testers. Each tester was matched to packs according to their planned objectives, body type, ability level, and testing environments. Then we set testers loose in the mountains, mainly in Colorado, Utah, Wyoming, Montana, Washington, California, and Alaska, with a few trips to Scandinavia and the Alps. Testers jammed gear into packs, stuffed them shut, and pushed them to their limits on both rugged and mellow ski ascents and descents.

We focused on newly designed packs with practical, ski-specific features that make traveling on snow easier, safer, and more enjoyable. We also brought tried-and-true, unchanged models into the mix so you’re seeing the best of what’s available. Comfort is a must, even for packs meant to carry smaller, lighter loads, as is durability, so we asked testers to evaluate straps, back panels, and hipbelt carry alongside the quality of materials.

Every time a pack went out, testers filled out feedback forms assessing fit, weight distribution, ease of access, and even pocket size and placement. Testers noted how easy it was to find what they needed, transition in the skin track, and carry their skis and boards on their backs while climbing steep couloirs. After a full season of rigorous testing, we selected the cream of the crop and reviewed those packs here.
