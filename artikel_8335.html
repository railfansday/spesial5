20. Bootheel Blues

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

The land turned flat. Mountains broke the horizon here and there, but our journey through them was over. Now we were in the Chihuahuan desert, and everything felt different.

The land was so flat that we might cross the dry wash between two mountain ranges and find it no more than a foot deep. This was a land without running water, a land almost beyond water itself. And yet it was green. Creosote dominated the dusty ground, a fragrant plant without thorns. The late blooms of barrel cactus and globemallow gave a little color here and there. Soaptree and swordbladed yucca rattled in the wind, and the long spiked tendrils of ocotillo reached up from their root like the creeping arms of an octopus, dried beyond reconning but no less capable. We might see one juniper tree all day, and its shade would be an oasis for hikers.

Colonies of red ants sallied out from their cratered mounds. A jackrabbit the size of a small child bolted away, ears flopping. Rough red earth and green leaves of the vegetation that clung to life here. A coyote startled from the brush went bounding over the open ground. Marks left in the sand where a snake had crossed the trail and gone on.

We slept out and watched the sunset and the stars. Condensation had its say and we woke wet in an amber dawn. In the north the low clouds burned and melted, broke and joined and distorted in the heat, making mountains that floated a mile above the earth or tables and towers of rock that could never stand, and yet did.

Horses stood taking the sun, coats steaming, some branded and some not. Most moved away when approached, but one stood. A huge dark eye, all pupil it seemed, watched my hand move closer and allowed it. Warm forehead, brown with a white diamond, completely still and yet churning with life.

Far out there in the south, the blue mountains of Mexico moved like apparitions. Thousands of feet above us the Sandhill cranes move in their winter migration. A ‘V’ that broke into organized chaos at the intrusion of a hunter. Hawk? Eagle? Too high and too far to be sure. The travelers broke ranks and swirled like a school of fish. The croaking calls of the cranes fell upon our ears like pebbles in a pond.

“D’you reckon a bird migration is their version of a thru hike?” “Other way around, I guess.”

The pan of the desert began to heat as the morning reached its natural end, and even the near horizon began to wobble and shift. We kept moving.

Groups of hikers had merged, units becoming interwoven by the many connections, small and large, until there were suddenly over a dozen of us in the greater mass. The lot of us would string out in the day and reform as a mass at key locations: water, shade, camp or, best of all, the car. One of us had a family member in the area with a rental car full of fresh fruit and cold beer that all were invited to. We sat in the dust a few yards from the road to make dinner. All that passed were semi trucks hauling steel for the wall.

Wheelz is the unofficial mayor of the group, and around her mat the circle of red headlamps spread and incorporated more and more until all were seated before their flames. Hikers told about where they had met others, and what had happened there. Hikers asked about others, known in friendship but not in location. Hikers offered and accepted items of food.

We woke dry and glad of it, and walked into the ghost town. A double-walled storehouse with the roof caved in. A machine shop of fallen arches. Little rings of barbless wire around open mining pits so deep that you could count the seconds before a dropped stone touched anything at all.

Hikers spread put to enjoy the solitude of the desert, yet none wanted to be alone too long, and sooner or later all sought the company of those others they had befriended and grown to love along the way.

Another road. Ran out of water five miles ago and feeling it – when the cooler opened it felt like a blessing.  Two capri suns back-to-back, half a liter of water, a bunch of grapes with perfect surface tension, a ginger ale, another half liter or water, a Mexican lager.

Great vehicles lumbered and lurched over the road. Tankers and oversize loads with signs that said so. Trucks hauling pieces of equipment so large we couldn’t guess at their origin, let alone purpose. There was only one possible destination for any of it. Cement trucks without end, their barrels slowly turning and churning their burdens. Flatbeds of steel beams, pieces of the wall. Tractors two or three or maybe six times the height of a man. All moved down the crushed gravel road, and dust was conquered by the sprayer trucks that led and followed the convoys.

Flat horizon but for the Big Hatchet Mountains. The clouds and peaks that lay in sun glowed like embers, and those that lay in shadow held their purple robes around them tightly. I have written much of sunset, but this must have been the grandest of all I saw on trail. Perhaps it was simply that it was to be the last.

Another gathering of red lights. The last of the trucks rumbled by in the last of the light. The final driver stopping to joke: “got any extra? I’m hungry!” Someone threw him a pack of chips and a wave. The night quieted but for the skipping yowl of a lone coyote. Scorpio rose above the horizon.

Conversation came to favorite parts of the trail, least favorite parts, hopes for the following day of the future beyond it. We all had our reasons for coming our here, and we all had our reasons for staying.

Tomorrow we would go to the end of the line. Tomorrow we would finish the trail.
