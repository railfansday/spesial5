Vermilion Valley to Nüümü Hu Hupi — John Muir Trail Day 17

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

I slept really well in the hostel at Vermilion Valley Resort (VVR).  Although I got up four or maybe five times to pee.

The first time I went to pee, I walked a longer distance down to the bathroom, where I discovered that the generator had been shut down for the night.  With no light in there, it was a bit challenging, even with my Petzl headlamp.

Rather than miss the toilet, and make a mess in the bathroom, I decided just to pee against a tree in the little grove nearby.  And that’s what I did for the rest of the night.  Each time I went out for that purpose, on that calm, clear, moonless night, I “looked up in perfect silence at the stars.”*

No one else ever joined us, so Ninja Queen and I remained the only ones in the facility.  I just think other hikers didn’t know the hostel was available.  It didn’t offer much, but a bed and a roof for $25 was a good enough deal for me!

It did get a little chilly in the night, but no more than my tent would have been.

Unfortunately, at some point my Petzl rechargeable battery died.  I realized that the headlamp had turned on in its bag and drained during the night.  In the morning I charged it again and Ninja Queen showed me how to prevent it from accidentally turning on again.  That was a good lesson.

Breakfast was awesome!  I couldn’t even finish it.  Two huge slices of French toast, two eggs over easy, bacon, and an enormous portion of home fries.  I ate a lot, to give myself a little pre-hike boost.  Believe it or not, Ninja Queen ate all of hers.

Modeling a breakfast fit for a queen — a Ninja Queen that is!

Then we packed and got ready to go.  My headlamp battery was sufficiently charged to get me to my next stop in Red’s Meadow.

As I went to pay the bill, I saw Amy one more time.  She was cordial enough, but not really effusive.  I was left wondering if maybe Josh didn’t tell her my role in securing her spot on the shuttle the previous afternoon.  Anyway, it wasn’t important; I didn’t do it for the gratitude.

The bill was about $110 before tipping.  I had to contrast that with $375 at Muir Trail Ranch for basically the same services.  The explanation is supply and demand economics.  Heading south, MTR is that last chance before a long stretch of trail without amenities.  Heading north, it is the first taste of “silly-vization” after completing that same long stretch.  Either way, you are a captive audience.

The night before, Ninja Queen and I had agreed to start off together.  We would stay linked at least until we got on the correct trail uphill to Goodale Pass, hiking together to make sure that we either both got lost, or we both did not get lost.

At first we passed through an area where the low level of the lake was unmistakable.

Then she had laid out a trail on her app that went through a burned area that had no real trail in it.  Maybe it had been erased by the fire?  Finally, I looked for the trail on my AllTrails app and realized we had to bushwhack uphill to reach it.  This was a little hairy because the fire had created a lot of fallen trees that we went around or stepped over.

You can imagine that walking through this terrain was not the easiest!

While a little tricky, it was not a great distance.  We found the trail on that devastated hillside and resumed our journey.

We were sad to see all the dead trees.  It also became quite hot in the sunshine, because the fire removed the cooling cover of vegetation that had been there before.  But there was an eerie beauty in it too.  The fire left some of the trees unscathed and others in interesting configurations; odd shapes created as the fire burned part of the tree but not all of it, or the trunk twisted and reshaped in the heat.

Interesting how some trees here are fine, some are struggling, and that one in the center is… gone.

At one point there was a little detour, and the trail went over a bridge.  It was exceptionally well-constructed and attractive, apparently added after the fire.

A well-made bridge, but will it survive the next fire?

Finally, we got to the place where the trail splits.  Going straight would continue along Edison Lake and eventually cross Silver Pass on the John Muir Trail.  We took the road less traveled, turning left, to go up to Goodale Pass on – you guessed it! – Goodale Pass Trail.  This route rejoins the John Muir Trail a bit further north, with slightly more elevation gain and slightly less total mileage along the way.

We had agreed that at the split we would begin hiking separately.  So, I took off.  I thought we might leapfrog, but I didn’t see Ninja Queen for the rest of the hike.

The elevation gain was well over 3,000 feet, and it came in three distinct stages.

At first, I remained in the burned forest for about three miles, with only occasional patches of green trees that the fire had miraculously missed.  The ground rose fairly steeply, but not exceptionally so.  Maybe I gained about a thousand feet of ascent there.

The next three miles and 1,000 feet were mostly in forest, following a stream.

I had my first lunch at that stream and filled up my water bottles.  I was really trying to keep hydrated.   I was surprised Ninja Queen did not catch me there.

Not far beyond that stream I ran across two people descending and they said, “Oh, you’re the first person we’ve seen all day!”

Then I replied, “Oh, you’re also the first people I’ve seen all day!”

Clearly Goodale Pass Trail was not a much-traveled route, because I ended up seeing only five people total the whole way.  And each of them said that I was the first person they had seen.  I knew they would encounter Ninja Queen behind me, but I had no idea if they would see anyone else.  As a tease, I told them to greet her by name as a long-lost friend when they encountered her.

Higher up there were meadows and wild flowers.

I thought I might see a bear in one of these meadows, but I never did.

The third stretch is when it really got harder.  That last fifteen hundred feet of elevation gain was steeper and rockier and more exposed, lying above the tree line.  I just pushed and pushed to keep going.

I was using my technique of counting.  I usually try to count to 60 steps, where left-right count as one step.  Then I stop and take deep breaths.  How many I take depends on how I am feeling.  I may only do four, or six, or I may do eight.  If I’m really knackered, it could be 10 or 12.  But then I keep going.

Occasionally, if it’s really, really steep, or at a higher elevation, as it was at the end on Goodale Pass, I will do 30 steps instead of 60 before stopping to breathe.

This technique helps me physically, to just keep going, and psychologically, to focus on the next 60 steps instead of thinking about the next five miles.

In this way I gradually went higher and higher, until I finally reached the pass.  Hooray!

Despite the jagged peaks around, the pass itself was quite flat.

Before leaving, I wrote “NINJA Q” with my pole in the sand, hoping she might see it and take courage from it.

Then it was time to navigate the equally steep descent.  I was quite nervous because there was a lot of loose sand and gravel, so it was an easy place to slip and fall.  Starting to feel tired, I was being especially careful.  I did not need another serious fall!

At the base of the slope, on the other side of Goodale Pass, there were three lakes.  They were named Papoose, the one the trail goes right by, Lone Indian, and Big Chief.  It occurred to me that these names were not especially politically correct, and I wondered if any thought was given to renaming them. Of course, that would be unlikely in the current political climate.

It was already late afternoon when the Goodale Pass Trail finally met the John Muir Trail.   This was a big celebration because I had completed the VVR detour and was back on the main trail.  Yay!

I also thought, “I now have less than a hundred miles to go.  I am unlikely to leave the main JMT beyond this, unless I get a permit to climb the Half Dome in Yosemite.”  It was the first time I really thought about the finish line in a concrete sense.

On the map that morning I had spotted Nüümü Hu Hupi.  It had several things going for it.  One, it was at the right distance as a camping goal, about 15 miles from VVR.  Two, it had a lake with an exceptionally scenic backdrop.  Three, it had the coolest name of any place on the JMT.  I did not know this then, but from Artificial Intelligence I learned…

I also learned that Nüümü Hu Hupi used to be called “Squaw Lake.”  So I guess they changed some of those offensive names after all.

Now I was really tired, so it was disappointing to see that Nüümü Hu Hupi appeared full.

Rather than just walk on by, I asked a guy camped near the trail, “Do you know of any spots here?”

He led me away from the trail for a few hundred yards and, sure enough, there was a beautiful spot, not that far from the lake.

The location also had a sandy floor, which made me hope to avoid condensation on my groundsheet in the morning.

I thanked the guy and then described Ninja Queen.  “If a hiker meeting this description comes by, let me know.”  He agreed to watch out for her.

I set up my tent and started soaking my dinner.  With enough sunlight left, I found a secluded spot, took off my clothes, and went for a little swim in the lake.  After a long, hot day it felt so great!

I got out, dried off, and then went off to dig my cat hole for the next morning.

For me, this is an important task to take care of the night before.  That way the next morning, if you suddenly have an urgent need to go, you’re not in a situation where, “Oh no!  Where am I going to go?   What am I going to do?”

When I dug my cat hole at Nüümü Hu Hupi, I had climbed up one side of a remote boulder.  After all, you want to pick a place that’s far from the campsite.  But after digging, I came back down on the other side of that boulder and… oops!  I saw that there was kind of a viable campsite out of sight, but pretty close to where I dug the cat hole.  I wasn’t going to fill it in, but now I was not so keen on that spot.

I came back to my tent to eat dinner and just then, in deep twilight, Ninja Queen arrived and the guy pointed her in my direction.

I told her the site was pretty much full, but I thought she had a couple of options.  First, I said, “Look, my site is big enough for two tents.  It’ll be uncomfortable, but we could do it.”

I replied, “Yes, I know it’s not ideal.  However, I found another site.  Let’s see what you think of that one.”

I took her up near my abandoned cat hole and she said, “Yeah, this will work great!  Now we’re even.”  She meant that she had helped me get the hostel at VVR, and now I had found her a camp site at Nüümü Hu Hupi.

So, she perched up there, I ate dinner at my tent, and by then it was dark.  Time for sleep!

*My quote comes from Walt Whitman’s wonderful short poem:

When the proofs, the figures, were ranged in columns before me,

When I was shown the charts and diagrams, to add, divide, and measure them,

When I sitting heard the astronomer where he lectured with much applause in the lecture-room,

How soon unaccountable I became tired and sick,

Till rising and gliding out I wander’d off by myself,

In the mystical moist night-air, and from time to time,

Look’d up in perfect silence at the stars.
