You can open most accounts for your child years before they could open one alone. Match each to their age and goal: a 529 plan and a Trump account from birth, a custodial Roth IRA once they earn, and credit building near 18. Before each deposit, check whether the money stays yours or becomes your child’s.

The newest is the Trump account, and by Oct. 1, 2026, every child under 18 with a Social Security number had one, after the Treasury opened more than 60 million of them automatically. U.S. citizens born from 2025 through 2028 also get a one-time $1,000 federal deposit once a parent claims the account and elects it on Form 4547, and family and friends can add up to $5,000 a year.

It joins eight older accounts worth opening for a child, each suited to a different age and purpose. What separates them most is control: whose money it is, who decides how it’s spent, and when your child takes over.

A baby born after Oct. 1, 2026, may not have one yet, and Form 4547 opens one in any year before the year a child turns 18. Either way, claim it in the Trump Accounts app, where you also elect the $1,000, so you can manage it and family and friends can contribute. You run the account as its custodian, and at 18 it becomes your child’s to manage.

Contributions aren’t tax-deductible, and money from an employer counts toward that $5,000 cap. Until the year your child turns 18, the money stays in an index fund of mostly U.S. stocks and can’t come out. After that, the account follows traditional IRA rules. What family and friends put in comes back untaxed, while everything else, the $1,000 and all growth included, is taxed as income. Withdrawals before 59½ also owe a 10% additional tax unless an exception, such as college costs or a first home, applies.

Open one whenever you start saving for your child’s education. The money grows untaxed and comes out tax-free for qualified costs, from some K-12 tuition to college. Many states also give a tax break for contributing, sometimes only to their own plan. Money withdrawn for anything else owes income tax plus a 10% penalty on its earnings. There’s no yearly federal limit, but contributions can’t exceed what your child’s education will cost.

The account stays in your name, not your child’s, so you decide when money comes out, and you can switch the beneficiary to another family member without owing tax. Compare 529 plans before you pick one, starting with your own state’s.

You can open one from birth at banks and credit unions that take young children, with a parent or guardian on the account until the child is an adult. On a joint account, you both own the money, and either of you can withdraw it. On a custodial account, the money is your child’s, and you approve each withdrawal until they come of age. It has no tax break, and its interest is taxable income. Look for savings accounts for kids that charge no monthly fee.

Open one at any age to invest gifts for your child’s future. Any adult can open one, with no contribution limit, no withdrawal penalty, and no rule on spending beyond using the money for your child’s benefit. Each deposit is a gift you can’t take back. You manage the investments until the age your state sets, often 21, and then the account is your child’s to spend on anything.

Its earnings count as your child’s income, and above a yearly threshold, the kiddie tax can apply your tax rate to them. Choose among custodial accounts by their fees and the funds they offer. It’s also how a teen can invest before 18, with you placing the trades.

Open one in the teen years, once your child is ready to make their own purchases with a debit card. Until they’re an adult, 18 or 21 depending on the state, you’ll be a joint owner, which lets you keep an eye on their spending. Find teen checking accounts with the parental controls you want.

Open a custodial Roth IRA once your child earns money from work, whatever their age. Contributions can’t exceed what they earned that year or the annual IRA limit, $7,500 this year, whichever is less. They aren’t deductible, but withdrawals after 59½ from an account at least five years old come out tax-free. You manage the account until the age your state sets, and then it’s your child’s. Open it with one of the IRA providers that offer a custodial version.

Add your child to your own card as an authorized user once you trust them to use it, and first ask your issuer whether it reports authorized users to the credit bureaus. Issuers usually do, so your card’s record, missed payments included, can start your child’s credit history before 18. Every charge stays your debt, and the account never becomes theirs. At 18 they can apply for a card of their own, but until 21 they’ll need income of their own or a co-signer.

This one waits until your child is an adult, 18 in most states, since a minor’s loan agreement may not be enforceable and a lender can require a borrower old enough to sign a binding contract. The lender holds the loan amount in a locked savings account or certificate of deposit while your child repays it, usually over six to 24 months, and reports each payment to the credit bureaus. The money is theirs once the loan is paid off. Compare credit builder loans for the lowest cost.

A child anyone can claim as a dependent can’t have a health savings account (HSA), though your own HSA can already pay their qualified medical bills tax-free. Once no one can claim them and they’re covered by a high-deductible health plan, theirs or yours, they can open one. Compare HSA providers with them before they do. Anyone can contribute for them, you included, up to the yearly HSA limit, and the deduction is theirs. The money then grows and comes out tax-free for medical costs.

Whatever your child’s age, keep money you may want to control or take back in your own name or a 529 plan, not a custodial account your child can spend however they like once it’s theirs.
