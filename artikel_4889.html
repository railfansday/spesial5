Why You Treat Your Future Self Like a Stranger

Posted November 6, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

Do you care about yourself and want the best for yourself? Most people would say yes. But that isn’t how we treat ourselves, especially a specific aspect of ourselves—our future self.

The evidence for that isn’t hard to find. You drink too much and wake up with a headache and a hangover. You push off an unpleasant task and have to deal with it the following week. You spend too much money and then stress out about paying next month’s bills. You skip the gym and eat foods you know are bad for you and saddle your future self with health problems. In short, we are pretty inconsiderate today of the person we’ll be tomorrow, and unsurprisingly, research confirms that we actually treat ourselves no better than we would a stranger.

In one study, participants were told they would have to drink a terrible-tasting liquid either immediately or later in the semester and were asked to decide how much they would drink. Those who chose to drink it immediately agreed to down about two tablespoons. Those choosing to drink it later sign on for half a cup—far more than they were willing to drink that day. When participants were asked how much of the unpleasant brew they would assign a stranger to drink, they chose about half a cup again.

This is why we postpone unpleasant tasks, ignore future consequences, and make decisions that saddle us with problems later—because we treat our future selves like strangers. But we can change that.

Another study found that people behaved differently toward their future selves when they felt more connected to the person they would become. Participants were shown computer-generated images of what their faces might look like in a few decades and then decided how much money to allocate to their retirement savings. Those who saw their faces digitally aged allocated more money than those who did not. Seeing the image made the future version of themselves feel more real, and that helped participants consider their long-term well-being.

I was excited when I first read these studies because this is something I’ve been doing for many years without knowing there was research behind it. How I do it might sound silly—mostly because it is—but I’ve found it remarkably useful. Whenever I catch myself about to postpone an unpleasant task, I say to myself: That’s going to make next week harder for Next-Week-Guy. Don’t do that to him. Or, Tomorrow-Guy will be very grateful if I take care of this now. And then when the next day or week arrives, and the task is already done, I take a moment to thank the earlier version of myself for taking care of it and freeing me from the burden, even if it’s small.

So yes, I’m having imaginary conversations with my future self, but doing that makes that version of me feel more real, and that helps me act with greater consideration for him. It’s especially useful for things like procrastination. Postponing tasks doesn’t eliminate the stress of doing them, and it often increases it when an unpleasant task that could have been completed in a few minutes today becomes a source of worry for days or weeks. Being considerate of the person you’ll be later and doing them the favor of taking the unpleasant task of their shoulders also spares them (you) unnecessary stress.

1. Give them an identity. Instead of thinking vaguely about doing something “later,” picture the person who will have to deal with it. Call them Tomorrow-You, Next-Week-You, or use your own name (I use tomorrow-Guy or next-year-Guy). The more real that person feels, the more you will care about them and want to make life easier and better for them.

2. Consider the consequences for them. When you’re about to postpone something, ask yourself: How will I feel about having to do this under pressure because now it’s urgent? Or, What if other important things come in and having to do this next week becomes really stressful? You might still decide to postpone it, but considering the consequences might motivate you to do it now.

3. Thank your past self. When you benefit from something you did earlier—doing the unpleasant task, packing for the trip ahead of time, or skipping the dessert you were kind of too full to have anyway—take a moment to acknowledge it. Thanks for taking care of me, Yesterday-Guy. It might feel strange at first, but having a better relationship with your future self and treating that version of yourself like a friend instead of a stranger can make your life easier and it can make you happier and healthier.

We usually think of self-care as something we do in the present, like relaxing, exercising, or taking a break. But I believe that some of the most valuable acts of self-care are actually acts of kindness toward our future selves. And they will be very grateful to you for it.
