Unraveling All the Details Behind <i><em>Wicker</em></i>’s Wicker

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

We asked the artists behind Alexander Skarsgård’s woven-to-order suit how they built it—and kept his appeal intact.

“It will take her another lifetime to understand wicker,” Marella Agnelli reportedly said of fellow socialite Mercedes Bass’s apartment. With Alexander Skarsgård’s help, some of us are getting there a little faster.

In Wicker, the romantic comedy based on Ursula Wills’s short story “The Wicker Husband,” Olivia Colman plays a fisherwoman who commissions a husband from the local basketmaker (Peter Dinklage). Skarsgård plays her woven-to-order spouse, whose resemblance to patio furniture does surprisingly little to diminish his sex appeal. Directed by Eleanor Wilson and Alex Huston Fischer, the film comes out in theaters today.

Behind the wicker transformation is Wētā Workshop, the New Zealand design and effects company whose credits include The Lord of the Rings and Avatar. Its artists make the costumes, props, and creatures that give imaginary worlds something tangible—and, in this case, marriageable.

For prosthetic makeup designer Joe Dunckley and lead makeup effects artist Sarah Rubano, the assignment was to turn Skarsgård into a convincing wicker husband without losing the expressions that make him recognizable. The team spent 18 months developing and manufacturing his suit and facial prosthetics, translating his features into a weave, right down to his furrowed brows.

Naturally, we had questions. How do you make wicker sexy? What happens to the eyelashes at lunch? And could anyone involved look at a household basket the same way again? Below, Dunckley and Rubano explain the work behind the wicker.

Before we get to the cheekbones, how did this assignment arrive?

Dunckley: We were approached by Eleanor Wilson and Alex Huston Fischer about 18 months out from filming. Initially, they were after a piece of concept art—a beautiful piece that they could take to potential cast members. This was part of their development process, so they had a tool for helping show their vision for the project.

Pretty quickly after that came the question: How are you going to make this wicker husband character? So much of the film’s success hinges on the success of that character. They always wanted to make the wicker husband a physical prosthetic character. That led us into a process of research and development. It was an 18-month period of R&D and manufacture to get to the final product.

A husband made to order. What, exactly, was in the order?

Dunckley: About 95% of the prosthetic and fabricated suit is made out of a 3D-printed material. Most prosthetic materials are manufactured to imitate human skin, but the wicker husband is built of wicker, not flesh and bones, so we knew we needed something different.

Sometimes we found the limits of existing programs and had to write our own code for weaving. Weaving is such complex geometry that it was very challenging for sculptors to weave in 3D.

The prosthetic appliances were then printed directly, without needing to go through the mold-making process.

Rubano: On [Skarsgård’s] body, we had about 50 individual pieces stitched and anchored onto thin spandex or mesh. The face, including the wig, was about 10 individual pieces. We also used foam latex for the lips, eye bags, and underlip and chin piece.

Did anyone get to weave an actual basket, or was this strictly a digital operation?

Dunckley: Absolutely. We had a team of weavers who had just come off Avatar, where they’d been weaving costumes for the Na’vi. Our design team would block out the shapes that we liked, and our lead weaver would do a physical weave sample using flax, giving us those shapes.

We’d print, do a makeup test, and learn what we learned: This one’s too loose. This one’s too tight and it binds. We’ll need more movement here. Slowly, we developed what you ended up seeing in the film.

I had a crush on the wicker husband. How do you make wicker handsome?

Dunckley: One of our main objectives was to make sure Alexander shines through the makeup. He’s a handsome man, and he has an expressive quality that’s really unique and recognizable.

We wanted to get those qualities into the weave. It was a matter of deconstructing what it is about those expressions that are endearing, that are heart-melting, and then reconstructing them in a weave pattern and enabling that movement for him through the prosthetic.

How long did it take for Alexander Skarsgård to become the wicker husband every day of filming?

Rubano: We got the whole morning process down to an hour and a half. That was the brief at the beginning, because he was working every day. It was an ambitious shooting schedule, so all the build techniques supported being able to expedite it.

He would come in, and we would put on a partial fabricated undersuit. He would come to the makeup chair, then go back and get more of the body suit on. Then we got into gluing the appliances to his face, the eyelashes, the wig, the styling, the makeup around the eyes—all the finishing touches.

And at lunch? Surely even a wicker husband gets a break.

Rubano: About halfway through, once we were really confident with our processes, we started giving him a bit of relief at lunchtime. I would take off the eyelashes because they’re quite robust, and he’s not used to wearing fake eyelashes. We would wrap up his beard and put a little bib over it so he could eat and be comfortable. We’d take off the gloves—just all the things we could do to give him some reprieve. After lunch, we’d build him up 100% again. The cleanup at the end of the day was about half an hour. The real challenge was keeping him cool. He runs hot, so we had fans and ice packs.

How do you feel about all the online chatter about “sexy wicker”?

Dunckley: We never would have expected this kind of level, to be honest. It’s been quite a ride to watch the enthusiasm, all the excitement, all the memes. We sat around watching memes for hours and laughing our heads off.

Rubano: We’ve built so many different types of characters for so many different types of films over 30-plus years. Sometimes they strike a chord as hoped, sometimes they don’t. Sometimes the chord has been struck louder than you could ever have expected. This is an example of that.

Has this changed how you look at the wicker in your own home?

Rubano: I have this tiny little wicker basket, and it’s not necessarily sexy, but I do love it. I look at it with love and adoration. It’s just this tiny little basket I put all my jewelry bits and pieces in. But, of course, the hottest basket of all is Alexander Skarsgård. Definitely.
