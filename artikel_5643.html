Most Lupus Patients in Remission Can Safely Stop Steroids, Study Finds

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

— No increase in flare risk after stopping, although certain patients may benefit from continuing

For patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) in remission, flare risk was not increased after stopping all glucocorticoids in a small Italian study.

Among 480 SLE patients in a prospectively followed cohort achieving remission, of whom 75% had steroids withdrawn, rates of disease flares were actually a bit lower in the off-steroids group compared with a propensity-matched group on low-dose maintenance (2.07 vs 3.44 per 100 patient-years), according to Margherita Zen, MD, PhD, of the University of Padua in Italy, and colleagues.

A propensity-matching analysis confirmed that the rates didn't differ substantially, with a hazard ratio of 0.767 numerically favoring the off-steroids group (P=0.45), the researchers reported in Rheumatology.

But this overall similarity didn't apply to certain subgroups. Zen and colleagues found that patients with cutaneous features, arthritis or myositis, leukopenia, or vasculitis faced statistically significant increases in flare risk with steroid withdrawal versus maintenance, with hazard ratios ranging from 1.9 to 3.5.

These results are potentially important insofar as they can inform clinical decision-making on whether patients with SLE in remission should try having steroids withdrawn. Long-term use, of course, comes with a raft of adverse effects that would be best to avoid. But clinicians have had to balance that with the risks from SLE flares, which can lead to irreversible organ damage along with the pain and discomfort associated with active lupus.

Zen and colleagues cited several previous studies that also found no increase in flare risk with steroid discontinuation, but a 124-patient randomized trial published in 2020 found a fivefold increase in flares with discontinuation. However, Zen's group also pointed out that, in this trial, steroids were not tapered but halted abruptly, which isn't normally done in routine practice. This conflicting evidence suggested another study is needed to provide clear direction.

Theirs isn't exactly that study -- it was not randomized but rather compared patients whose clinicians decided on their own judgment whether to withdraw steroids for SLE patients in remission. Propensity matching certainly helps make the comparison meaningful, but the observational design remains an important limitation.

Patients in the study were members of the prospectively assembled Padua Lupus Cohort, which had enrolled 541 SLE patients as of 2023. The group focused on the 480 who had achieved remission, defined as a SLE Disease Activity Index-2000 (SLEDAI-2K) score of zero on stable drug therapy including steroid doses no greater than 5 mg/day in prednisone equivalents. Any increase in SLEDAI-2K score or change in background medication during follow-up, including restarting steroids, was considered a flare. A total of 358 remitted patients had their steroids tapered, with no prespecified schedule; the other 122 remained on low-dose steroids.

On average, patients taken off steroids differed in a few ways from those chosen to remain on maintenance: they were somewhat younger (mean age 41 vs 46) and had shorter disease duration (mean 12 vs 15 years). Also, leukopenia was more common among those on maintenance (prevalence 49% vs 35%), as was a standard overall measure of organ damage accrual. To make the groups more properly comparable, the propensity-matched analysis winnowed them down to 314 in the withdrawal group and 114 on maintenance.

Flares were relatively uncommon: across 3,423 patient-years of follow-up for the 480 participants in remission, just 86 flares occurred, more often in the maintenance group.

A key concern with flares is the extent to which they cause new organ damage. In the withdrawal group, Zen and colleagues found a numerical association between flares and damage accrual that fell short of statistical significance (incidence rate ratio 1.598 comparing flared vs nonflared patients, P=0.227), and thus not entirely reassuring. But damage accrual was worse in the maintenance group, at an annual rate of 7.14 units per 100 patient-years, versus 3.89 per 100 patient-years after withdrawal -- not unexpected given the higher numerical flare rate with maintenance.

Notably, about half the maintenance group attempted tapering unsuccessfully. Among those who did succeed, the median tapering duration was 17 months (IQR 8-35), suggesting the process was not always easy.

Besides the nonrandomized study design and small numbers of events, other limitations included the lack of prespecified tapering schedules or characteristics of patients for whom to attempt steroid withdrawal. In addition, most patients were white. Zen's group also noted that the baseline for organ damage accrual was set well before tapering attempts began.
