The Out-of-Touch Adults' Guide to Kid Culture: Looksmaxxing With Dangerous Drugs

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

A weekly deep dive into the current trends, slang, and viral videos of youth culture in terms that even the squarest can understand.

This week's column is bleak. First, there's the looksmaxxing trend where teenagers are taking dangerous chemicals to correct perceived physical shortcomings. Then there are the AI job interviewers. Depressing, but at least some young people are having a little fun with their dystopian hellscape.

You can judge a generation by the illegal drugs it pioneers. In the 1960s, hippies smoked weed and dropped acid to expand their consciousness; the yuppies of the go-go '80s fueled their drive to acquire with Colombian cocaine; but kids today don’t want to go deeper or work harder. They have a smaller, sadder goal: They take drugs to look better. The substance of choice for a growing number of teenage boys are steroids, peptides, and other exotic chemicals designed to enhance appearance.

Would-be muscle-men have been injecting themselves with black market ‘rhoids since the Eisenhower administration, but that was a small cult of adult weirdos with a specific goal. In 2026, kids are into it, and they aren’t using steroids to win a $500 prize in the Mr. Topeka contest; they’re using unapproved research chemicals to looksmaxx. Looksmaxxing is an online movement of young men trying to maximize their physical appearance. Teenagers are taking the normal desire to look their best to dangerous, pathological levels. Driven by a combination of undiagnosed body dysmorphia and relentless social media algorithms that turn amoral “red pill” kooks into gurus, teenage boys are ingesting a panoply of exotic and dangerous compounds in a quest to fix the angle of their jawlines, change their skin tone, lose weight, grow taller, and otherwise “correct” perceived physical problems.

In the "good old days" of steroid abuse, people generally stuck to abusing anabolic-androgenic steroids that had at least been tested for decades. Teens today are buying SARMs (Selective Androgen Receptor Modulators) and synthetic peptides marked "not for human consumption" from shady laboratories online. Steroids primarily affect the muscles, but many of these newer substances target different biological systems. A recent Rolling Stone article reports that kids are “heightmaxxing” by swallowing erdafitinib, a drug that treats advanced urothelial cancer, because the drug is known to trigger growth spurts (that can result in a curvature of the spine and other nasty side effects). They’re also trying MK-677 to get taller, an experimental growth hormone secretagogue that carries the risk of insulin resistance, water retention, and high blood pressure. Others are injecting Melanotan II, a synthetic peptide that forces a long-lasting deep tan, with side effects of extreme nausea and the sudden, aggressive darkening of moles. (And they're doing steroids too.)

In the looksmaxxing community, softmaxxing is doing generally-considered-acceptable things to look better, like getting enough sleep, exercising regularly, and eating a balanced diet. Hardmaxxing is taking more extreme measures like plastic surgery, smashing your face with a hammer, or taking experimental drugs.

In the bodybuilding and looksmaxxing community, “natty” refers to a natural, non-steroid-enhanced physique.

Here's something more lighthearted than the growing popularity of illicit self-esteem chemicals: the bicep heart and waist heart trends. The Bicep heart came first. Thirst trap videos started appearing on Instagram and TikTok in October in which dudes make a heart with their hand and bicep. Then people started posting variations where they frame the shot with someone they love inside the heart. The bicep heart quickly led to women posting the waist heart.

Okay. So maybe it's not that lighthearted of a trend. While there are some videos of dudes with skinny arms doing the bicep heart, it's mostly jacked dudes. And the waist heart doesn't seem like it would work at all unless you are a specific shape, as this video illustrates. So maybe this isn't a silly meme but an illustration of how young people are inundated with content that can trigger feelings of physical unworthiness, even if they're just looking for silly memes.

As if searching for a job wasn't alienating and horrible enough, some companies have begun using AI agents to conduct interviews. Begging a robot to let you work in a cubicle farm is as dystopian as anything Phillip K. Dick ever imagined, but don't count humanity out yet. Young people are sticking a finger in the eye of the machine by creating comedy videos that focus on trolling these AI job interviewers. They're hilarious because AI is the ultimate straight person in a comedy team, and the raw compute costs to run these interviews means corporate money is burning with each second of wasted time. Here are some of the top creators in the genre.

Bo Bongo: This YouTuber shows up to AI interviews shirtless but insists he's wearing a "corporate tube top," then focuses on daring the interviewer to notice he's doing things like smoking a cigarette or eating a rotisserie chicken.

Cam Whitt likes to wind up AI by arguing, interrupting, telling outrageous lies, and asking the dumbest questions imaginable, like, "When does the interview actually start?" and "How do I figure out what my name is?"

Grahamn Raabe: Among this YouTuber's schticks is speaking only in nonsense, like describing his professional background as "freight cadence, walnut, a little bit of Richard, spruce wood—six years of spruce wood."

Nick Reid: Is so obnoxious, he seems to make the AI furious at him, even though it's a computer without emotions. Two thumbs up!
