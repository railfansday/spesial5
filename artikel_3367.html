The 10 Best Alternative Rock Albums of the 21st Century, Ranked

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Looking over alternative rock music, most of the greatest of the greats can be found in the final couple of decades of the 20th century. Like, the 1980s and 1990s was the time when alternative rock was at its most classic and dominant, like how classic rock, you know, dominated (and felt “most” classic) in the second half of the 1960s and then throughout the 1970s.

That doesn’t mean you should only look to the 1980s and 1990s, though, if you want to hear great alternative rock. There have been more than enough artists tackling that genre so far throughout the 2000s and a little beyond as well. Case in point, these ones, which can count themselves among the very best alternative rock albums of the 21st century so far (more of them in the 2000s and the first half of the 2010s, sure, but rock – alternative or otherwise – just hasn’t thrived in quite the same way since about the mid-2010s onward).

It’s obvious to point at Interpol and say, “Hey, that band sounds quite a lot like Joy Division,” but maybe that’s okay. After all, there are only a couple of Joy Division albums, so there’s more territory with the whole post-punk sound to mine, but also, Interpol do some interesting things with that sound and aren’t just like a Joy Division cover band. Also, of course there’s going to be similarities, since Interpol are sometimes lumped in with the post-punk revival genre.

That’s what you'd expect it to be: post-punk returning with a bit of a sheen a couple of decades on from its heyday. Turn on the Bright Lights is the sort of definitive album you can listen to not just if you want to work out whether you'll like Interpol, but whether you'll like this whole sub-genre of alternative rock. It’s an album with a ton of hype behind it, but it thankfully lives up to all that.

Listing off highlights in terms of individual tracks feels hardest for Radiohead’s In Rainbows, owing to how well the album flows, and how consistent it is. Once you mention “15 Step,” “Weird Fishes/Arpeggi,” “Jigsaw Falling Into Place,” and “Videotape,” you feel obligated to mention all the other tracks, and that won’t be done. They're all great. They're all worthy of mentioning. That’s the main thing.

In Rainbows is a little more rock-adjacent, definitely came out in the 21st century, and it’s almost as good as Kid A, so it’s going here.

If Kid A counts as a 21st century album, then maybe it’s a little better than In Rainbows, but not by much. But the year 2000 is kind of the end of the 20th century rather than the start of the next one. Oh, and Kid A doesn’t have a lot by way of alternative rock. In Rainbows is a little more rock-adjacent, definitely came out in the 21st century, and it’s almost as good as Kid A, so it’s going here. It feels right.

Tame Impala is easily one of the best rock acts of the century so far, and it’s easier to call Tame Impala an act or a project than a band, since Tame Impala is – as the meme says – just one guy (Kevin Parker). The same can be said for The Cure (Robert Smith), though, so it’s not unheard of. Anyway, Tame Impala is great and/or Kevin Parker is great. Either way, same greatness.

Lonerism is the easiest album to label as “rock,” and probably the strongest overall, too, even if the slightly poppier Currents (2015) does have some undeniable highlights track-wise. Like, “Let It Happen” and “The Less I Know the Better” might be better than the best song on Lonerism, but Lonerism has extra appeal as a coherent album, with all its tracks bleeding into each other so well, making for a seamless listen for the 50-ish minutes it goes on for.

As the title might suggest, Songs for the Deaf is kind of a concept album, but not really the sort that goes far enough to have an in-depth narrative or a particularly notable number of themes it really hones in on. It’s more of an experience kind of thing, designed to replicate the feeling of cycling through a bunch of strange and/or unfamiliar radio stations during a long drive, and hey, it does do that premise/concept pretty well.

You’ve got a few interludes and snippets of audio contributing to this concept, but mostly, Songs for the Deaf stands out for being very generous with a ton of great alternative rock songs. Queens of the Stone Age peaked here, and that’s not so bad when the peak is this high (and, for what it’s worth, there are definitely other albums by the band worth checking out if you got something out of Songs for the Deaf).

You might not like this particular realization dawning on you, but if American Idiot were a person, it would've been able to drink legally for a year, and it’s been able to vote for about four years. It is old. Maybe that makes you feel old, too. But hey, it’s held up well. Maybe you’ve held up well, too. Best to stay positive (maybe not during September, though, if you want to be woken up when it ends).

American Idiot isn't really a positive album, though, for what it’s worth, since it’s an angry album, and one focused on reflecting feelings that were felt during a confusing and politically charged time in history. Time passes, though, and maybe even that simpler kind of anger and passion feels a little nostalgic. Anyway, this album is very much a time capsule for alternative rock in the 2000s, and maybe even the 2000s overall, so it’s well and truly got its place in history, all the while still sounding pretty great when listened to today.

Fever to Tell might be one of the best punk rock albums not released during that genre’s heyday, though it’s got a bit more to it than just punk, and so it fitting within the pantheon of great alternative rock albums also feels fitting. This Yeah Yeah Yeahs album definitely has that punk energy throughout, but its indie/alternative rock elements are implemented flawlessly, and it does indeed feel like rock a good deal of the time.

Counting the hidden track, there are 12 songs here, and the album isn't long, so the average track length is only about three minutes, meaning nothing really risks overstaying its welcome (a couple of songs even clock in at under two minutes in duration). Though it sounds of its time, Fever to Tell has aged surprisingly well and still sounds awesome, all the while also being able to count itself as easily the best album by Yeah Yeah Yeahs to date.

Quite a bit of what could be said about American Idiot can also be said about The Black Parade, with the two being rock operas/concept albums that blended alternative rock, pop, and a bit of punk. With The Black Parade, you can also lump it in with the emo genre, but it’s a cut above a ton of the other stuff associated with that particular genre/movement, all the while being the best and most ambitious of all the My Chemical Romance albums to date.

And then like American Idiot once again, nostalgia could have some impact on The Black Parade feeling as great as it does, but not wholly. The Black Parade is a genuinely great concept album dealing with all sorts of heavy things, and it goes more in-depth on those topics than you might expect, based on the genres it’s associated with. It’s something that was operating on a whole other level compared to all the comparable albums from around this time, and it’s aged exceedingly well all these years later.

The National had a good run throughout much of the first two decades of this current century. Like, there’s Alligator (2005) and Boxer (2007) often being fan favorites, and then the likes of Trouble Will Find Me (2013) and Sleep Well Beast (2017) are also excellent, being close to the standards of those other two albums. Yet you can see The National and their very best right between these two pairs of albums.

It’s High Violet (2010) that makes that halfway point, being a pretty much perfect album that encompasses everything great about The National within a single album. It is a somewhat dreary and downbeat listening experience, but there’s thankfully a lot of passion here alongside all the sadness, so it’s not miserable or too much. For something oftentimes sad, it’s also got a surprisingly high number of earworms and moments of catharsis.

With Arcade Fire, it’s a bit of a Radiohead kind of situation where the album here might not be the band’s very best, but it’s also not far off, and it’s here because it fits within the confines of “alternative rock” to the greatest extent. It’s The Suburbs, which is the band’s third album overall, and one where they really seem to tackle Americana, doing a bit of a Bruce Springsteen thing in parts alongside referencing and paying homage to some other great classic rock acts.

It’s eclectic, though, as can be expected from an Arcade Fire album, with the best song here sounding more like Blondie’s “Heart of Glass” than any classic rock song (the song in question is “Sprawl II (Mountains Beyond Mountains)”). Everything here is pretty great, though, and it could well be the most approachable album of the band’s, too.

If you're into indie/alternative rock, you'll pretty much guaranteed to like Wilco’s Yankee Hotel Foxtrot, but if you also like more old-fashioned sorts of rock, you'll likely also find things to like here. It’s just a great album, right up there with the century’s best so far, and that’s probably the easiest thing to say. It’s a more mellow sort of rock, sure, but rock nonetheless, maybe scratching a similar melancholic itch to some of the best The National albums.

Some sadness is inevitable when the album begins with a song named “I Am Trying to Break Your Heart,” so that one sets the stage quite ideally. Then there are other songs here that lyrically go beyond more personal topics like love, loss, and loneliness, with the variety being appreciated, of course. Some of the songs here are quite long, and Yankee Hotel Foxtrot also clocks in at over 50 minutes all up, but you never really come close to feeling the length here in a detrimental way.
