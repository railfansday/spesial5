The Appalachian Trail: ECT Week 9

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

The day has finally come to wrap up the International Appalachian Trail. It has definitely been a unique experience though there was a lot more road walking than I would have preferred. For now, as stage one in my grand journey I will appreciate it for what it was. I’m up and out of camp by 5:40am. Though today will be shorter I have made it my goal to get into the town of Millinocket before the post office closes at 4pm to grab a box my parents sent me. All that stands in my way is about 20 miles of hiking and a 40 minute drive into town. I am most worried about that drive into town. The trailhead where I will pop out after my hike is a bit out of the way though any cars that pass by will only have one option to go into town. There just has to be cars that pass by in the first place.

Today begins with a series of exposed rocky sections. At the top of a few it becomes a bit confusing where to continue on as the cairn stones are very tiny, but on one of these sections I am granted my first view of the backside of Katahdin. Mama K. I am getting so close to the Appalachian Trail now. Not much stands in my way. Hopefully in a day’s time I will be standing atop of Katahdin getting ready to begin my journey along one of the oldest hiking footpaths. I cannot overstate how much I have been dreaming and looking forward to finally starting this trail so seeing the mountain on which it begins has me feeling very emotional. Even though I have 1,600 miles under my belt so far on this journey the IAT was just the warm up. The AT is the main event which will have me, Lord willing, standing atop Springer Mountain in Georgia 2,200 miles away as a triple crowner: a backpacker who has hiked the AT, PCT, and CDT. For now, that seems a long ways away so I just focus on getting into Millinocket on time.

I have one river ford to deal with before making my way along a series of long overgrown and neglected old forest service roads. I make good time until I am confronted by a juvenile moose. I try slowly encroaching into his area to get him to push on, and he runs away. I turn the corner to find him looking over his shoulder with what can only be interpreted as a “now what?” face as he hides behind his much larger mother who is fully facing me and staring me down. Alright, you win. Take your time. I’ll just be here taking a video of you. Wait. Come back. If you ever want wildlife to get going just pull out your phone to try and record them. They always seem like they thwart any plans to get a cool video.

Another half hour has me standing at the intersection of three roads where the IAT ends. No cool sign or terminus. Just one last blaze I take a picture next to. Well, that’s over. Add it to the list of trails I have completed. I have had time to digest the trail since finishing, and I have to say it is probably my least favorite trail I have completed. I don’t hate it, and I am happy to have the experience of hiking it. Though, too often, I found myself wondering what I was doing while walking along a thin road shoulder as cars zoomed by. If you are out there to find a true nature experience it will be few and far between on this one. Then when you are on trail for the most part it’s fairly swampy. Except for Quebec. Quebec had the best of everything. Great trail, great huts, great national parks, great food. Anyone looking to get a taste of Canada via the IAT should just do that 400 mile section.

I’m not trying to be too negative. I really enjoyed the people I met along the way. I enjoyed walking into and out of pretty much every town for resupply. In fact, I only ever had to hitchhike to the northern terminus in Newfoundland and into Charlottetown, PEI. I even enjoyed some of those lonely stretches of road which probably hadn’t seen a car come through in quite some time. Frequently, I would get the impression I was discovering some long lost fishing village, or that I was the first person to step foot in a place for quite some time. I like that feeling of being on the outskirts of civilization looking around with fresh eyes. You tend to notice more.

Mostly, I will miss the water. They don’t call some of those provinces the maritimes for nothing. I will miss having the steady presence of the sea. In the beginning in Newfoundland, I looked out at the horizon and wondered how far away Greenland was (turns out about 800 miles as the crow flies). I would see icebergs floating by. The constant presence of fishing boats going about their business, and the gulls that would circle by looking for scraps. Many of the roads I walked along would be littered with lobster traps and other fishing paraphernalia. Oh, and the seafood! One could get spoiled by the amount of seafood I consumed.

But I left all that behind when I turned inland in Quebec. I will have to walk a pretty far ways down into Florida before all that returns. For now, I have the mountains and the green tunnel of forest that surrounds them. I have an odd 14 miles or so of no man’s land between the end of the IAT and the start of the AT. During this section I walk along Katahdin Lake and marvel at the beauty of one of the shelters there. It has canoes at the shelter for people to use during their stay. Another few miles finds me waving in and out of dense overgrown trail following a series of flags and cairns. At this point, I am so used to poorly marked trail I don’t even slow my pace as I weave in and out, up and around as if I know perfectly where I am going. It seems to work as I reach an intersection with a nice, wide, easy two track. You can always tell you are getting close to the parking lot when things become incredibly easy. Also, when you start seeing more trash.

Katahdin Lake in the background. Maine has quite a few lakes to enjoy.

Here, I befriend an older gentleman who is just finishing up some volunteer trail work. We walk together for a bit before I say my goodbyes and try to push onwards to the trailhead parking lot. Well, I should have just hiked with him as he comes wandering out of the trail as I am still trying to hitch and offers me a ride into town. It is 3pm and it takes about 40 minutes to drive to town. The post office closes at 4pm. I swear it is always a race against time with the post office on trail. Luckily, I make it with 15 minutes to spare. He then drops me off at the hostel where I check in.

Thanks to the AT Hostel for all their help. A great place to stay.

I’m staying at the Appalachian Trail Hostel for two nights. I have worked it out so I will get a shuttle back into Baxter State Park in the morning, climb up Katahdin via the backside, traverse the Knife’s Edge, summit Katahdin and start the AT, summit Hamlin Peak (my first AT side quest), then descend down the far side and get picked back up and taken into town. The next day I will get dropped off where I left off. I will then attempt to hitch to the west side of Baxter State Park to climb North Brother mountain and then hitch back again and continue on to the start of the 100 mile wilderness. These two side quests have been stressing me out for a while. A large part of me wants to attempt to do all of this in a day and not go back into Millinocket, but I know that would be a very long day both in miles and especially in elevation. I think I better play it safer in the middle of a large thru hike.

So I do just that. I play it safe. Though after everything is said and done I think I could definitely have done it as one big day. Going up the backside of Katahdin was fairly easy. I would say it is easier than what northbound hikers have to do on the side of the mountain I descended. The Knife’s Edge wasn’t too exposed save for one short saddle section where I had to descend and then climb back up a steep 40 feet. There was one easy rock climbing move to do that would have been disastrous if I fell. The whole time I traversed the Knife’s Edge it was a complete white out. Finally, I got to Katahdin, Baxter Peak. The start of the AT. I got my picture taken at the famous sign and ate some snacks up there, but even though it is only August it got pretty cold so after 30 minutes I decided to make my way down.

Unfortunately, it was still pretty foggy at the top of Baxter Peak.

Exactly one mile into the AT I turned off to start my side quest of Hamlin Peak. This was a two hour detour which involved going down and then up a saddle, summiting, and then going back down and up the saddle. Fun times. With that taken care of I continued my way down the mountain. There were a lot of sections with rebar drilled into the mountain as makeshift ladders. It was pretty steep. This is definitely the harder side. The sun attempted to come out at times, and by the time I made my way down the mountain it was actually a pretty nice day. I’ll have to come back sometime for better views from the top. I made it to Katahdin Stream Campground just in time to get a shuttle back to Millinocket.

The next day I shuttled back to where I left off except now I will have to try and hitch to the West side of Baxter State Park where North Brother resides. This side quest proved lengthy in time as I stood out there for an hour trying to hitch. Once I got picked up it was about a twenty minute drive. The actual hike was three hours round trip with another hour of trying to get a hitch and twenty minutes of driving back to Katahdin Stream Campground. With that six hour detour finished I continued along where I made it out of Baxter State Park to Abol Bridge Campground. Here I picked up a few items at the store and bought a very large ice cream cone. It was getting late so I hiked another three miles into the 100 Mile Wilderness and called it a night at the first shelter, Hurd Brook Lean-to. I’m not getting very far these first two days so I’m going to have to pick up the pace tomorrow.

I woke up fairly early the next day to get my miles in. I was on trail at 5:40am. The 100 Mile Wilderness is a famous section of the AT though I was surprised when I actually hiked it. Due to its name, it conjures up thoughts of pristine wilderness without any signs of civilization for miles and miles on end. While it might be true that you are far away from civilization, access to civilization and all of its allure still runs heavily through this section. There are some great hostels in the area that will slack pack you for a price. Slack packing is essentially when they hold onto your backpack and you hike your mileage for that day with just a day bag or just the essential gear. They will then pick you up at an agreed upon spot, and take you back to the hostel for a night of town food and a bed. There are many spots along the AT that do this, but here in the 100 Mile Wilderness it seems especially noticeable. There are even packages that can be purchased where you do this over ten days with a meal and bed every night. Such is the fame of the 100 mile wilderness that it brings in all types seeking to “conquer” it. Or at the very least be chauffeured through. Add onto this, there are many active logging roads to be crossed where logging trucks zoom by and the 100 Mile Wilderness is no longer the peaceful serene experience you might imagine. In fact, it is quite popular and crowded to the point where the mice have got in on the act. My first night’s campsite I had a visitor who chewed up a Butterfingers wrapper I had in a pocket. Fortunately, no damage was done to my pack, but this is fairly rare if you’re not staying at a shelter. I guess the campsite was too established.

Caution, unless of course, you want to pay for every luxury.

The 100 Mile Wilderness is beautiful. I can see why people flock to it even when they’re not doing the whole AT. While I did get a heavy taste of rain during my stay, the Maine wilderness is stunning. I’ve never backpacked in Maine before despite visiting many times. The forest is enchanted. There is moss and mushrooms everywhere. Many lakes dot the landscape. And I have arrived during blueberry season. I find at the top of every mountain there are more berries than I can pick. And I try. Believe me I try.

Another great place on the AT. I hope to be back someday.

They run a tight ship at Shaw’s. The owners, Hippie Chick and Poet, have everything down such that, upon being given the tour and asked if any questions, I am just left speechless as to how they distilled all the information I needed so clearly, concisely, and quickly. I was also given a free soda and ice cream sandwich upon arrival which was nice. One of the employees took me to the store later where I bought a very large pizza, jug of chocolate milk, and other goodies for resupply. I was very full. Shaw’s also has a gear store I perused with a nicely curated collection of backpacking gear including some more niche brands you cannot get online or through other big retailers. The one bummer is right before leaving I left my AT branded Darn Tough sleep socks somewhere in the room where I stayed, and by the time I realized and had service to call and see if they were still there several days later someone had swiped them. That was a darn tough pill to swallow as they were very new and those socks cost $28 as they have a lifetime warranty. You can’t really use that warranty without said socks in hand, however.

Famous stack of blueberry pancakes at Shaw’s.

The morning of leaving Shaw’s I had their famous breakfast before hitting trail again. Eggs, bacon, toast, and all you can eat blueberry pancakes. You will leave satisfied. I finish my last day of this week setting myself up for the Kennebec River crossing tomorrow. This is the only river along the AT wide enough that you must get ferried across. The Appalachian Trail Conservancy actually pays someone to ferry hikers across all day in season. The canoe even has a white blaze on it. After this it will truly be all hiking until Key West.

Maine has been such a treat to walk so far. I am excited for more enchanted forest.
