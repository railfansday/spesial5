Will Pochettino fix Manchester United? Will Unwin is answering your questions – live

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

As Mauricio Pochettino prepares for his first game in charge at Anfield, the Guardian’s Will Unwin is taking your questions on Manchester United’s new head coach, the crisis he inherits and what comes next.

Will Unwin is online now answering all your Manchester United questions. It’s not too late to get involved, ask yours here.

walmai asks: Do you think fans of clubs (including Man United) put too much store by success on the football pitch, or has sentiment towards clubs and players become unrealistic and damaging? Do people’s expectations weigh that heavily on players, or on clubs such as Man United? Is it all a bit unhealthy?

Will: I think there is one key problem with focusing on success … only one team can win the league each season. Not winning a trophy is seen as failure for many but there are only four to go around, and that is if you are in Europe. Supporting a football club in general is pretty unhealthy, it affects your mood and happiness constantly. Being able to disconnect from it is difficult in the bad times.

For players, pressure weighs heavily on them. United is supposed to win every game according to the history of the club but that is clearly not the case. Performing in front of more than 70,000 people each week is a tough task for anyone’s mentality, let alone if they are jeering. I cannot imagine what it takes to cope with that. Fans can inspire but also negatively affect players. It is a black and white industry, winning is good and losing is bad. Football will never change, it is all about who adapts best.

kiya asks: What do you wish fans or the public knew or understood about United but don’t, or most often get wrong about the club and what goes on there?

Will: This feels like the hardest one to answer. I think that among all the poor decisions being made, it should be said that United get a lot of things right when it comes to the academy and have a lot of talented staff, many underpaid because there is a theory that working for a football club is a privilege. They are equally hurt as everyone else when the team is struggling.

Cojones70 asks: Fernandes and Martínez refuse to renew their contracts, JJ Gabriel leaves for Madrid at 16 years old. Even the players can see Man U is a sinking ship and are jumping off … How does the club get back to winning the league?

Will: We should look at each case individually but I do not think they are the same.

Fernandes: Clearly there are frustrations, as noted in his criticism of Carrick, but he is club captain and someone who has had opportunities to leave before and stayed. He is 32 years old and this will probably be his final big contract, so wants to get full value from it, and he can rightly point to his achievements at Old Trafford, something few others can do. United, on the other hand, have to weigh up his age and whether he can be a force in two to three years, and the value that comes with that. There is a confidence that an agreement will be reached between all parties.

Martínez: Looks like someone eager to go, his comments aimed at Wilcox will discourage the club to trigger the year extension they have in their favour. He will be 29 when his deal expires, and will be highly-desirable to clubs the world if he is available for nothing next summer, so may see this as his final chance to join a club capable of winning trophies.

JJ Gabriel: He never looked like staying to me. He would have been given chances to play, so it is slightly odd that he was so desperate to leave. United tried everything they could but Gabriel and his advisers were always reviewing his options.

salfordexile66 asks: United follower since 1974. For the first time that I can remember I have no sense of optimism at the arrival of the new manager. Don’t get me wrong, I think he is a capable guy, but the manager isn’t the problem. It never has been since 2005. I feel that the only way to get the owners to quit is a full-on boycott of games by season ticket holders, ie leave just after the game starts and don’t return, coupled with ceasing to buy merchandise for the foreseeable future. What do you think?

Will: Fans are very loyal, and the badge of honour that comes with attending every game is a difficult one to shake, especially when clubs threaten to take away season tickets from those who don’t attend enough matches. United, and other clubs, know that.

United are bigger than a football club and have a plethora of revenue streams. They have been an underwhelming team in a big stadium for a long time, and their global fanbase has not been affected. But I do think one thing that would damage the product is swathes of empty seats at Old Trafford. It is a sports entertainment business at the Theatre of Dreams, and if the seats are empty, it becomes more depressing and harder to buy into. So a mass boycott would have a huge impact on the club.

Fan power has been decreasing with each passing year, which means bolder plans of action are needed. United supporters have done a fine job with their marches and banners, so it does feel as though they will eventually take further steps.

MademoiselleK asks: Do you think Pochettino will see Rashford as a key player in his plans, or want to bring in a replacement?

Will: Pochettino will certainly give Rashford every chance to prove himself. For the players it is a chance to start again but Rashford needs to show he can maintain professionalism and produce on the pitch. He is a quality player, who has the capabilities of being a gamechanger at the top level.

Pochettino will not write anyone off without giving him a go. Rashford had a decent season at Barcelona and has offered a glimpse or two since returning but without any notable output in terms of goals and assists. His contract is long and expensive, making him difficult to sell, so the player and club need it to work.

dNazareth asks: I remember reading that one of Sir Alex Ferguson’s greatest achievements was transitioning into more of a director-level role, allowing his assistants to implement their own ideas about how the team should play and modernise the tactics without losing the club’s identity. Is the director of football role the weakest link in Manchester United’s current hierarchy?

kiya asks along similar lines: Given how often Poch referred to himself as United’s “head coach” (a point of friction at Spurs), how often do you request interviews with the real power at the club – Wilcox, Berrada or Ratcliffe? What do you think of Wilcox and Berrada? Is it true they were appointed mainly on their success at Man City?

Will: I have written about the role of Jason Wilcox few times before. Martínez thrust him into the limelight in recent weeks, and he has faced the ire of fans, whose chants about the director of football have featured some choice language. The results on the pitch show there is a problem, but the blame has fallen on successive head coaches despite clear failings in how the squad has been recruited.

Wilcox desperately needs Pochettino to be the catalyst for success. Otherwise he will face a lot more questions, and may find his position untenable sooner rather than later. He had little experience of a senior first-team role before joining United, and little he has done since suggests he is capable of being a leadership figure at a Champions League club.

When it comes to signings, few come from outside the Premier League, which means United are always going to have to overpay. Brighton, who are second, have been built on clever scouting and data analysis, while United look at who did well at Brentford or Wolves the previous season. United need to change something about their recruitment, and someone like George Syrianos at Nottingham Forest, who thinks outside the box, would be worth considering.

Requests have been made to speak to Wilcox, but it feels like a futile gesture. As for the City connection, I think United saw the success there and wanted some of that influence.

Whosdatdandare asks: With Poch becoming the new fall guy who will get a tune out of the team before asking to be backed in the transfer market, being told we can’t afford player X and then leaving, how does the club remove the true issue? … So, how do you solve a problem like the Glazers and Ineos? No change of manager will do it.

Will: The fans point of view will be to remove the ownership, as they have made the case for. They are seen as a problem for numerous reasons, from taking money out of the club to moving fans out of their seats to make way for more lucrative hospitality clients. Nothing will really change at United until the team becomes the full focus and the right football brains are in the most significant jobs. With the current setup, the only way of things getting better is if there is constant success but the club is not built on such foundations.

Books have been written on the subject of United since the Glazers came to Old Trafford, so will offer some further reading:

We’ve had many questions about United’s finances. kapsiolaaaaa asks: Is there still the spending power to support Poch? Can United go out and buy a £70m player in January without selling someone? What about wages?

Dmitriverlouanne asks along similar lines: With United’s finances as they are, what can we realistically expect in terms of new signings in January and in the summer?

And willconqueror has a warning: Will United continue to buy second-tier players while expecting to compete at every level? If they do, then Poch would have to consider unemployment within six months.

Will: Spending wise, we can look at the summer window where they were outspent by promoted sides Ipswich and Hull, which sums up the situation. United are in the Champions League and money will come in through that but there is not much to go around. The club’s quarterly figures were released, showing “reported a quarterly ⁠net loss of £18.1m, compared with £6.6 ​in ​the same period a year earlier. Revenue rose 4% to £146m in the three months ​to the end of ​September.” It is an overall indication of finances.

Pochettino will want some new players, it is normal for a coach starting in a job, so United will find a few quid – but not £70m – to spend in January, which is a notoriously difficult time to do business. Anyone that does come in, I suspect, will plug a hole or be a long-term investment.

One problem is they have not been good at selling in recent years, making losses on those acquired for plenty. Antony was a financial flop, Jadon Sancho left for nothing after joining for £75m. The question is more whether the United hierarchy can be trusted with the money available or if Pochettino will get more say than his predecessors on who comes through the door. Sitting 18th makes a return to the Champions League next season very difficult, and every year United are not at Europe’s top table, it has a huge impact on spending.

tetters asks: Every few months there is the report of a new “wonderkid” being signed: a South American full-back, a Scandinavian midfielder, a flying winger etc. But then they never seem to make the squad, let alone the first team. The same for our academy, which wins trophies regularly. Are there real prospects who could supplement or even improve the team? Is it too difficult to introduce them into Premier League games? Are they just investments for future sell-on? And why did JJ Gabriel leave?

Will: The simple answer for many, and we can take the Paraguayan left-back Diego León as our example here, is that they are plainly not good enough and signing them has been poor judgment. Some will take time, Sekou Kone was brought in from Mali and is out on loan at Lausanne to gain experience. Developing footballers is not an exact science and bringing them in from all around the world is a complex process to begin with. It is reliant on human factors outside football, of whether they can quickly adapt to life in a new country before even thinking about what they do on the pitch.

United’s academy is successful and they bring through quality players, such as Kobbie Mainoo or Shea Lacey but it is more difficult to introduce them into a struggling environment than a successful one. Coaches always rely more heavily on experience in tough circumstances, leaving fewer opportunities for youngsters, further exacerbated by United’s repeated failings in cup competitions, where many clubs blood their finest homegrown talents. At other clubs, there is always an eye on value of youngsters, and it makes them a lot of money but United have never thought like that, it is all about the first team, which is why they end up selling those that do not make the grade for cheaper. City are known for selling their unwanted teenagers for good prices, whereas United struggle in this department, losing them on the cheap.

In terms of JJ Gabriel, few inside United expected him to stay to sign a scholarship or professional terms. The plan from his side was always to move abroad and Real Madrid looks like his destination. United cannot be criticised, they did all they could to persuade him to stay but, ultimately, they were just a stepping stone in his journey.

CuriousOtter asks: Something I found strange with the team themselves was when they said they lacked in certain areas, in interviews. Calling out sporting directors for not investing in more players – isn’t that calling out your teammates? If your centre-back says the team needs to buy a right-back, what does that communicate to the squad that’s already there? Is this common across teams? Or is this coming up for United indicative that the players are as much part of the problem as the manager and higher-ups?

Will: This is referring to Lisandro Martínez’s comments where he took aim at director of football Jason Wilcox. The recruitment has been poor, no one can argue with that, leaving United short. Martínez has won a World Cup, he knows what is required to win at the elite level. The players will know that from experience where issues lie and they take the brunt of criticism, so Martínez was eager to make others be the focus of ire. Maybe he did not go the best way about it, using a post-match interview after losing to Atlético Madrid but if you are supposed to be at one of the best clubs in the world, then it should not be the case that a young centre-back is being played out of position in the Champions League because the hierarchy failed to buy a backup for an injury-prone left-back.

United spent £150m on central midfielders in the summer and it was an area everyone knew they wanted to invest in after Casemiro left but spending £50m on Andrey Santos to sit on the bench while there is no natural alternative left-back or striker, with Benjamin Sesko injured and Joshua Zirkzee barely trusted, it must grate on players who want to challenge for trophies. United are a club wanting to challenge in the Premier League and Europe but the squad is not deep enough to cope with a hectic schedule.

Diggedy asks: I know it is easy to blame the Glazers but over the last 10 years Man U is the third-highest spending club. How much is the actual players’ fault rather than the club structure?

Will: There is plenty of blame to go round from the Glazers taking money out of the club to coaches not having the right tactics. It is bizarre that almost all of United’s most expensive signings, Paul Pogba, Romelu Lukaku, Jadon Sancho, Anthony and Ángel Di María, to name a few, have all failed to live up to expectations, suggesting there is a wider problem. The recruitment has been dreadful for a long period of time, leaving the squad often unbalanced because too much money was spent in the wrong places. Players will always receive a lot of the blame, as witnessed recently when they were booed off after losing 3-0 to Bournemouth, while Carrick was cheered.

This team is clearly not performing to its maximum, that is for certain. Too many players have enjoyed the good life for too long at Old Trafford without performing. There is a certain pressure that comes with playing for United and many cannot cope, but it weighs even heavier when they know they cannot match expectations because there is a shortage of quality.

If we are being honest, this team should finish around sixth or seventh, so being 18th is an embarrassment. Without Champions League football, life was straightforward for all involved, aiding their surge to third under Carrick. When things are going badly, however, few are willing to step up and take responsibility. Fernandes, Cunha and Maguire will but others like to go hiding, a matter Pochettino will need to change.

Chloe asks by email: Who should we expect to be key figures under Pochettino’s new system? Which young players may be given a chance to shine, and which squad members may have their form revitalised?

Will: Hi Chloe, If he is fit, Luke Shaw will become very important to Pochettino before he worked under the coach at Southampton earlier in his career, so will know the demands of the Argentinian.

Kobbie Mainoo in midfield will be integral to everything going forward because he is someone that should be at the club for the next decade and has the ability on the ball to utilise it how Pochettino likes in a progressive manner.

Naturally, Bruno Fernandes will be a focal point and considering Pochettino likes his No 10s to roam and create chaos where they think they can cause the most damage, it will be interesting to see how the skipper will perform the role. Fernandes criticised Carrick’s tactics, implying he was not happy in general, so a new start for him under Pochettino could hail a return to form.

Shea Lacey’s development as a winger will be intriguing because he has the capabilities to establish himself. Noah Ajayi from the academy is another who could quickly get on to Pochettino’s radar.

Patrick asks by email: Poch is associated with enthusiastic, young players, a high press and a mobile defence playing a high line. Given the lack of dynamism and quality in the current squad, what’s his plan B going to look like?

Will: Hi Patrick, for starters, it should be said that Pochettino will be happy to delve into United’s very successful academy to find young players with the right attitude and attributes, if the senior players are unable to perform the duties they want.

Under Michael Carrick, United showed they are often best playing on the counterattack and they have the players to do this but it is not the ‘United way’. Pochettino has spoken of his love for Sir Alex Ferguson and will be desperately hoping that he can follow in his footsteps with attacking play, inspired by a relentless desire to win. He said on Thursday that he wants United to be “protagonists” on the pitch, it feels as if he will try to honour Sir Alex’s legacy. I suspect it could be a few months before we find out if there is a pragmatic plan B. If it was up to me, I would stick to playing on the counter but Pochettino is unlikely to listen to my views.

RobRoy1975 asks: Pochettino has, at best, a chequered track record … What exactly would he bring to United that Moyes, Van Gaal, Mourinho, Solskjær, Amorim and a host of interim managers didn’t bring?

Will: It is an intriguing one. I am not sure it is possible to pinpoint one individual trait that others did not have. He is very self-confident like Van Gaal, Mourinho and Amorim but that did not equal success. He has a clear philosophy that has been tested over the years but so did the others.

I think, at the moment, he was the strongest candidate based on his record in England in particular and taking on a long-term project at Tottenham. If anything, maybe he has the political intellect to enjoy a good relationship with the players and the hierarchy. He has had to deal with Donald Trump after all.

PurvesGrundy asks: There is a strong case to be made that the last United coach who truly coped well with a “normal” season for a big club (ie two games a week, four competitions, no empty Covid stadiums) was José Mourinho. That’s a long time. The club has changed dramatically, there’s years of scar tissue and failure since then. Can anyone realistically expect Poch to be the first one to get it right?

LouisLou adds: Is the answer to the question of whether Pochettino will fix Man Utd just “depends how long he gets”? A string of people have gone through the doors now, any one of whom might have sorted things out, all sacked, some with less than a year in charge. What can anyone realistically do in that time unless they’ve been left with a squad in really good shape? What usually happens (with any half-decent manager) is there’s some sort of bounce, some sort of lull after a bounce and then things start to pull together. So far, have Man Utd ever got past the lull phase without overreacting?

Will: A coach cannot cope with the supposed ‘normality’ of playing in the Champions League and Premier League, bringing two games a week, because the squad lacks the quality and depth to do so.

At this stage, you probably have to write off this season because it looks unlikely that anything can be achieved beyond steadying the ship. A lot of changes need to be made at United, they have started in the dugout but there is clearly a requirement to bring in new players and move on those that fall below the standard.

Elsewhere director of football Jason Wilcox and chief executive Omar Berrada are under intense scrutiny because they have failed to oversee any improvements during their tenure but retain the backing of Sir Jim Ratcliffe. Signings have been underwhelming for far too long and United consistently struggle to find value for money in the market, leaving the personnel available to a coach short of what is demanded by a club with such history. If Pochettino is unable to cope, then it will be further proof that the coach is not the problem.

leroyhunter asks: Do you believe Poch is still an “elite” manager, as United fans seem to believe? His best work (at Spurs, under circumstances impossible to recreate at Old Trafford) was nearly a decade ago. He has drifted since.

Will: Hi Leroy, Obviously his finest work is still associated with those years at Tottenham but he has since worked at PSG and Chelsea with some incredible talents, and will have learned plenty from those environments even though the silverware was lacking. After a couple of years on the international beat with the USMNT, not to mention being 54 years 0ld, this is a pivotal moment in Pochettino’s career, the final shot at a “big” job in the Premier League.

His tactics have slowly evolved over time, but there are non-negotiables, in terms of the high press and energetic style throughout his tenures at clubs. At United, there is a sense he will need to adapt because this team has been unsuccessful playing on the front foot and he will seek a balance, I suspect, to get the† best out of what is available to him. What Pochettino has – and will never leave him – is a strong personality, a no-nonsense attitude to discipline and someone who can cope with the pressure and the circus that comes with United, which could be his most important trait.

Manchester United have a new head coach. Mauricio Pochettino has signed a two-and-a-half-year deal to replace Michael Carrick, who was sacked after leading the club to their worst start to a season in four decades.

In a tearful farewell to the US men’s national team, Pochettino called the move “one of the most difficult decisions” of his career. But the job he inherits may be harder still. No team in Europe’s top five leagues has conceded more goals in all competitions this season. Bruno Fernandes has rejected a second offer of a new contract. And fans have turned their anger on the hierarchy above the dugout.

In his analysis of what the new head coach must do, Will Unwin wrote that whoever took over would have to find solutions on the pitch while putting pressure on those above him: “United fans know the person in the dugout cannot take all the blame and someone else has to be publicly accountable.”

Pochettino’s first game is at Liverpool on Sunday, then Sporting away in the Champions League. So is he the right man for the job? Can he get a tune out of this squad, and who will thrive under his high-intensity style? And will the club’s owners give him the time and the tools he needs?

Will, who covers Manchester United for the Guardian, will be live here at 2pm GMT on Thursday 19 November to answer your questions.
