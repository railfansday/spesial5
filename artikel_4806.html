Why Do Therapists’ Marriages Fall Apart?

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

It would seem intuitive that therapists, having spent many years learning about relationships and how the psyche works, should have strong marriages. They would serve as a personal example and pass on to patients a solidified experience of building robust relationships, bolstered by professional training. However, landmark research by McCoy and Aamodt (2010) showed that among 449 occupations, therapists reported being divorced or separated at a rate higher than the 16.96 percent average (therapists 24.20 percent, social workers 23.16 percent, counselors 22.49 percent).

As I was strolling in the evening, thinking about some personal conundrum, I suddenly came to the realization that I tend to turn every crisis into a lesson. I'm not alone; this is how the therapeutic mind works. Therapists tend to be highly inquisitive; they often love reading; some, like myself, write; and they like to reflect on whatever happens.

This is what drives them in the profession as well. They love helping people reflect on their lives and their relationships and turn their problems into solutions, learning along the way.

However, a therapeutic relationship is very different from a personal one. You could be a therapist married to a spouse who doesn’t see a series of crises as an ongoing invitation to analyze them and extract the lessons.

So what are the difficulties in a therapist’s marriage that could lead to divorce?

Therapists are taught to tackle problems head-on, behaviorally or emotionally, depending on the approach. Therapists are usually comfortable engaging in an uncomfortable dialogue about situations, stimulating feelings, often unpleasant ones, or working with deep bodily responses. While all that can serve as great therapeutic tools, in a marriage, the partner might oppose all of them.

The spouse could belong to a large category of people who don't want to deal with problems head-on at all or through processing their emotional response to them, and they postpone dealing with problems until it's impossible not to.

Some therapist-spouses see it as avoidance. They interpret their partners' response as repression or denial, which are considered psychological defenses, so they often attempt to draw out their partner's response whilst the partner is not yet ready to talk about it, or firmly acknowledge the problem. The partner reads this professional bias as impatience and intrusion on boundaries, and its presence multiplies conflicts in a couple.

The therapist's work consists of being empathetic and compassionate, and of caring about other people's feelings. Their spouses believe that this compassion will extend to family members as well. However, having worked with compassion as a sharp tool—often the main tool—therapists find that it tends to get blunt by the evening, by the weekend, or by the end of the year.

Therefore, many therapists can be withdrawn or emotionally exhausted once they get home to their family. They want to be cared for, or they want to be left alone. However, their spouses, and rightfully so, ask for and need their attention and emotional care as well.

It creates the emotional imbalance that many therapists can help their clients deal with quite successfully, yet lack the energy to handle within their own families.

Compassion fatigue goes beyond simply being tired from work. Figley (1995) described it as secondary traumatic stress stemming from having to care for people with trauma and anguish. Common symptoms of compassion fatigue also include sleep difficulties, irritability, general emotional exhaustion, emotional withdrawal, and difficulty in creating genuine empathetic relationships.(3) That's why being in therapy as a therapist can be immeasurably important for work-family balance.

Yes, therapists, especially marriage therapists, learn how to handle relationships, why relationships fail, and how they can be helped. But this very thing causes them to fall into several traps.

Therapists could talk about relationships a lot, and the psychology of relationships as well (the name of my blog, duh), which irritates their partner to no end.

While the subject of relationships might interest therapists a lot, they tend to analyze their own relationship as well, along with their partner's behavior patterns.

What looks like curiosity and a willingness to engage in conversation to the therapist often looks like nit-picking to their partners. The partner often regards the "relationship analysis" as criticism and patronizing.

And here comes another trap, as the therapists' partners often want the opposite of what therapists usually provide to clients. While therapists give their patients containment, holding, therapeutic neutrality, and assistance in creating the environment for the client to grow and change, without the therapist being directly involved in the client's life, the spouses want concrete involvement, actions, and proactive solutions.

For many therapists, the motivation to enter the profession lies in their own problematic family story, such as emotional abuse, parental narcissism, dependencies or addictions among family members, or generational traumas. As research shows (see Deacon et al.), marriage therapists especially crave rewriting the family history, often being the first in generations to disrupt destructive patterns.

However, the therapists' spouses are often not at all motivated or driven by the constant urge to change internally or fix their family story and current relationship. They might want to keep things as they are, appreciating the long-term stability, or they might not want to deal with past traumas at all. So, the drive to change that defines many therapists can be draining for them.

This drive might also make the therapist forget about the role of the partner, and unknowingly assume the role of the therapist within their relationship, putting their spouse and other family members in the role of the client.

Helping with relationships of other people and maintaining one's own long-term relationship differ. That is not to say that the therapist-partner is to be blamed for anything that goes wrong in the relationship—it always takes two to tango—but rather to understand the peculiarities of being a therapist in a marriage or being married to a therapist.

Individual therapy is recommended for the therapist, or for both partners if they deal a lot with people and their emotions outside their relationship. Couples therapy will also be helpful in building supportive communication patterns and restoring the roles of spouses instead of therapist-patient dynamics.
