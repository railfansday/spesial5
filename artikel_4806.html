Is Psychology Measuring Introversion All Wrong?

Posted November 13, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

It used to be that if you wanted to know if someone was an introvert, you handed them a personality questionnaire, and oh, by the way, it was definitely built on the Big Five model, the dominant framework in personality science since the 1980s. Their score on the extraversion scale would have told you what you wanted to know.

But newer research is challenging the Big Five’s position as the best measure of introversion. If the critique holds, it could mean that decades of findings about introverts may have been skewed in ways I’ll explain.

The biggest problem is that the Big Five doesn’t actually measure introversion directly, which very well may mean, in effect, that it is not measuring introversion at all. You see, what the Big Five does is measure extraversion and then, believe it or not, treats introversion as the absence of extraversion. So introversion, within this model, is the low end of the extraversion dial. But that kind of makes sense, right?

Yet it paints a certain picture. A person with low extraversion is (look closely at the words I’m about to use) reserved, inhibited, quiet, or withdrawn. In the words of researchers Valerie Thomas and Pauline Nelson, the Big Five assigns “solely negative characteristics” to introverts. Oh great. Thanks. In other words, it defines introverts by what they lack rather than by the qualities and functional characteristics that they actually possess.

That’s like defining calm as “low anger,” by the way. Which would miss something essential about the experience of being calm, wouldn’t it?

So, in 2014, psychologist Jonathan Cheek and colleagues proposed the STAR Introversion Scale, measuring four distinct flavors of introversion (Cool, Jon et al., 2014):

In a 2025 study published in the Journal of Personality, Thomas and Nelson put these two approaches head-to-head, examining which better predicted solitude-seeking behavior, arguably the most signature introverted tendency (I also so argue).

Big Five introversion showed no relationship at all with positive motivation for solitude. None. Zero. Zilch. It didn’t predict wanting to be alone, and worse, under the Big Five, it was low introversion (meaning higher extraversion) that predicted actually spending more time alone. Crazy, eh? This study found that, by the Big Five’s very own logic, extraverts are the ones who are out there in the world seeking solitude for themselves, so something had clearly gone sideways.

The STAR model, on the other hand, told a coherent story about solitude. “Social introversion” predicted both the desire for and the amount of time spent alone, while curiously but understandably, “thinking introversion,” and only thinking introversion, was associated with genuinely wanting solitude, and “anxious introversion” predicted wanting solitude for negative avoidance reasons, which all makes sense to me.

When the study incorporated STAR and Big Five data and analyzed various fancy comparisons statistically, the conclusions that resulted began to indicate that perhaps where the Big Five had claimed introversion, it had actually all along been capturing something much closer to what would be better described as neuroticism. (Whoa.)

I can answer that! Well, you see, if clinical screening tools rely on Big Five-based formulations of introversion, a therapist might interpret a patient’s solitude-seeking as a symptom of a disorder rather than merely their preference. They might cast a thinking introvert who recharges alone as socially withdrawn. Alternatively, they might underestimate the functional impact of an anxious introvert’s distress given that it’s literally part of a normalized trait score, when in fact, it is an indication of clinically concerning functioning and treatable patterns. Ah, you see now?

In workplaces, the Big Five framework feeds a narrative in which introversion is a deficit to be managed, such as the stereotypical quiet employee who needs to “come out of their shell.” In reality, there is not necessarily a defensive shell at all but, rather, likely a vital interior life. Such confusion risks a perpetuation in varying forms and degrees of, in effect, pathologizing perfectly functional ways of being human.

To me, the STAR’s great contribution is carving out space for thinking introversion, a dimension completely invisible to the Big Five, for introverts who don’t avoid people out of anxiety but do also gravitate toward ideas, reflection, imagination, and, thus, toward solitude. This is arguably at the heart of what most self-identified introverts mean when they say “introvert” to describe themselves (can I get an amen?).

But still, clearly the STAR model has its own vulnerabilities. It is relatively new, developed by a small research group, and has had as-yet-limited independent validation across diverse populations (equity!). And “anxious introversion” is easily debatable. If it so strongly correlates with neuroticism, is it even an aspect of introversion, or is it really something altogether different, albeit related, that should be measured separately?

Whatever the case on the nuances, it is clear enough that the STAR is bringing a serious challenge to whether the Big Five, for all its strengths, has introversion right.

As I’ve alluded, emerging evidence suggests it does not—that it has been measuring only a shadow of introversion, defined by deficit and tinged with pathology, while missing the fuller, more textured reality, so I am appreciative of new perspectives.

This new conversation is just beginning, but for the many millions (including myself, hi) who have long felt that their introversion is something richer than “not extraverted enough,” the science may thankfully, finally, hopefully, be catching up.

Cheek, J. M., Brown, C. A., & Grimes, J. O. (2014). Personality scales for four domains of introversion: Social, thinking, anxious, and restrained introversion. Unpublished manuscript, Department of Psychology, Wellesley College. (The original STAR Introversion Scale.)

Thomas, V., & Nelson, P. A. (2025). The effects of multifaceted introversion and sensory processing sensitivity on solitude-seeking behavior. Journal of Personality. (Head-to-head comparison of the STAR and the Big Five Inventory in predicting solitude-seeking; source of the finding that Big Five introversion fails to predict positive motivation for solitude.)
