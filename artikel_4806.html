Healthy on the Outside: The Silent Risk Dr. Nancy Snyderman Never Saw Coming

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Nancy L. Snyderman, MD, spent decades helping millions of Americans understand their health. But when it came to her own health, it was a nudge from a friend that finally pushed her to get the care she really needed.

Nancy L. Snyderman, MD, began her career caring for one patient at a time. After medical school, Nancy became a board-certified surgeon, working at academic medical centers, and she kept running into the same gap: the distance between what doctors knew and what patients actually understood about their own health. Television, she realized, could help close it. “A big part of being a doctor, for me, was putting humanity back into medicine,” she says. So she traded caring for one patient at a time for caring for all Americans.

For nearly three decades, Americans welcomed Nancy into their homes through their televisions. As a chief medical correspondent, she covered wide ranging medical topics and traveled the world extensively, reporting from many of the world’s most-troubled areas. Every day, she expertly translated complex medical news into easily digestible—and actionable—information for people across America.

“I spent my whole career being the person who talked about health to help others,” she says. “I never thought I’d be the one in need of help.”

Nancy has always been active. Even now, she hikes, travels and keeps up with her young grandchildren. But being active and feeling good doesn’t always mean you’re healthy. No one knows this better than Nancy, who had no symptoms of heart disease before her diagnosis. It started when she was still a national medical correspondent, working on a story on CT angiograms and a then-new test that provided a coronary artery calcium (CAC) score. As part of her report, Nancy had the scan done herself and her score came back at 100. “I thought, ‘Oh my gosh, this is great, I aced this test’,” she says—only to learn that acing it means a score of zero.

What is a CAC Scan?A coronary artery calcium (CAC) scan is an imaging test that measures the amount of calcified plaque built up in the heart's arteries.1 It can help doctors and patients understand cardiovascular risk—even before symptoms appear.1

At the same time, she was struggling to keep her LDL-C, or low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, often called “bad” cholesterol, in check. High LDL-C is one of the leading risk factors for a heart attack or stroke, but also one of the most modifiable.2 To lower her LDL-C, Nancy had been taking statins—which are a commonly prescribed class of LDL-C lowering medication—for decades. “I knew what to do, and I thought I was doing the right things,” she says. “But I was never able to really get my bad cholesterol to my doctor-recommended level.”

What finally changed everything was a conversation. After reconnecting with an old friend, who also happens to be a cardiac nurse, Nancy mentioned her CAC score and stubborn LDL-C. Her friend insisted that Nancy see a new cardiologist. After some additional testing, the results were nothing Nancy ever expected to hear: severe coronary artery disease, with significant blockages in the arteries feeding her heart.

What is LDL-C?LDL-C stands for low-density lipoprotein cholesterol—often called “bad” cholesterol. When too much of it builds up in the blood, it can settle into artery walls, narrowing them and raising the risk of a heart attack or stroke over time.2

“My diagnosis was a huge wake-up call,” she says. “I thought I was doing the right things, but even I, as a physician, needed an advocate.” She’s quick to point out why that mattered: “A lot of the risks women face with heart disease can be managed,” she adds, “but only if we understand them and speak up for ourselves.”

Nancy’s experience is far more common than many women realize. Heart disease remains the leading cause of death for women in the United States3—yet only about half (56%) of women recognize it as their number one threat.3 “One of the most dangerous things about heart disease is how quietly it can develop,” says Sirisha Vadali, MD, a board-certified cardiologist in Scottsdale, Arizona. “It can progress silently, even when you feel completely healthy,4 and women in particular may not have the obvious warning signs we’ve all been taught to watch for.”

So much of that hidden risk traces back to a single number: LDL-C. When there’s too much of this “bad” cholesterol circulating, it doesn’t just sit in the blood. It works its way into the artery walls and builds up as a sticky plaque, narrowing them and raising the risk of a heart attack or stroke over time.5,6 “It isn’t only how high your LDL-C is today,” Dr. Vadali says. “It’s how high it has been, and for how long. The longer it’s too high, the more cardiovascular risk accumulates.”⁵

For Nancy, the lesson was humbling: expertise is not immunity. “You can be the most informed person in the room and still miss a health warning in yourself,” she says. It sometimes takes a push from a loved one to take the necessary action.

After her diagnosis, Nancy did exactly what she had spent years urging her viewers to do: she partnered with her cardiologist and built a plan. Together, they assessed her individual risk factors and set a clear target for her LDL-C to help lower her cardiovascular risk.

Getting there was more challenging than Nancy realized. “Despite standard treatment, diet, and exercise, my LDL-C was still too high, and we needed to lower it to my doctor-recommended LDL-C goal,” she says.

That gap—between lower and a guideline-recommended target—is one Dr. Vadali sees constantly. “When it comes to LDL-C, lower is better, but ‘lower’ isn’t the same as reaching the guideline-recommended goal for your unique risk factors,” she says.

What are the Guideline-Recommended Goals?The 2026 ACC/AHA/Multisociety Guideline on the Management of Dyslipidemia recommends specific LDL-C goals based on individual risk. For example, below 70 mg/dL for people with diabetes and multiple cardiovascular risk factors, and below 55 mg/dL for those with cardiovascular disease at very high risk.7,*

“Knowing your LDL-C number—and talking with your doctor about what’s right for you—is one of the first steps toward understanding your risk level,” Dr. Vadali adds.

She’s also quick to retire a persistent myth. “For a lot of people, especially those already at increased cardiovascular risk, diet and exercise alone may not be enough to get LDL-C to the guideline-recommended goal,” Dr. Vadali says. “That’s not a personal failing. It’s biology. Sometimes the body needs additional help, and that’s worth a conversation with your doctor.”

For Nancy, that additional help became part of her plan. Working with her cardiologist, she started treatment with REPATHA® (evolocumab) to help lower her LDL-C and reduce her risk of a major cardiovascular event.

These days, treatment is a welcome part of Nancy’s routine. “Every two weeks, I give myself an injection and have built a small ritual around it as part of my treatment plan to help keep my LDL-C at the goal my doctor recommended,” she says. “I can take it in the comfort of my own home or on the go† which is a treatment that can fit into my routine.”

Nancy is careful to say her path is her own. “I’m sharing my experience as a patient, but everyone’s situation is different,” she says. “What mattered was working with my doctor to figure out what was right for me. That’s what I want for every woman reading this to consider.”

Dr. Vadali says REPATHA is a treatment she has consistently recommended for appropriate patients. “I prescribe REPATHA because we know it starts working quickly,” she says. “For high-risk patients, keeping LDL-C lower will help reduce their risk of a life-altering event like a heart attack or stroke.”

If one thread runs through Nancy’s story, it’s the one so many women will recognize: the tendency to take care of everyone else first. “Women wear a lot of hats, and most of them involve caring for other people,” she says. “We don’t always stop to care for ourselves.”

That’s the habit she’s determined to break—in herself, and in the women who’ve heard important health messages from her for years. Her message today isn’t fear; it’s agency. “I want women to proactively understand their cardiovascular risk before something forces them to,” she says. “Ask the question: What’s my risk, and what can I do about it?”

Nancy’s ask is simple: Talk to your healthcare provider about your LDL-C and your personal cardiovascular risk factors. Get your LDL-C tested, know your doctor-recommended LDL-C goal and—if needed—make a plan with your doctor to help you reach it.

“This is the health story I never expected to tell,” Nancy says. “But if telling it means one more woman picks up the phone and calls her doctor, then it’s the most important one I’ve ever shared.”
