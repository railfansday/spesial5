100 Years of Pooh

Updated October 16, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

A. A. Milne wrote Winnie-the-Pooh for his son, Christopher Robin. It was published on October 14, 1926, and sold 150,000 copies before the year was out. Pooh books have since sold more than 45 million copies in the United States alone, and have been translated into over 50 languages.

Milne was already a well-known humorist at Punch magazine and a successful playwright. Pooh himself made his first appearance in a London newspaper on Christmas Eve 1925.

Milne had served in the First World War, and Britain was still grieving. Into that world came a quiet forest where the biggest crisis was a lost tail or a bear stuck in a doorway. Pooh offered a refuge then, and he offers one now.

Part of the pleasure is knowing these characters so well. We can almost predict what each will say. Perhaps we recognize someone we love in many of them. Perhaps, we may even recognize ourselves.

These lovable characters resemble those with mental conditions found in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders Fifth Edition (DSM-5-TR).Pooh's forgetfulness is a loose match for inattentive ADHD. He may lose track of a plan, especially when honey is involved, but he never loses his affection for a friend.

Piglet approaches life cautiously. His tendency to anticipate trouble resembles the worry of generalized anxiety disorder. Yet he is loyal and willing to help. He reminds us that bravery can come in a very small package, and that feeling afraid does not stop us from doing something meaningful.

Eeyore expects disappointment. His gloom brings to mind persistent depressive disorder. But his dry humor makes him much more than the sad member of the group. His friends keep including him. He does not have to become cheerful to deserve their attention.

Tigger is movement, excitement, and impulse, much like the hyperactive side of ADHD. He can be exhausting, but he brings vitality. Most of us know someone whose enthusiasm occasionally overwhelms us, and whose absence would leave the room noticeably quieter.

Rabbit likes order and likes to be in charge, which resembles obsessive-compulsive personality traits. Owl enjoys being the expert, a playful nod to narcissistic traits, though wanting to sound knowledgeable is hardly a personality disorder.

Little has changed in terms of human characteristics in 100 years.

Milne made a quietly radical choice for his time. In the Hundred Acre Wood, the child is the capable one. Christopher Robin solves problems, organizes expeditions, and reassures his anxious friends, while the animals around him are smaller and more muddled.

For young readers, this is empowering. Children spend most of their days being told what to do. In Pooh's world, they get to be the steady, kind, competent one. Perhaps that is why so many of us felt a little braver after reading these stories.

In 1953, the pediatrician and psychoanalyst D. W. Winnicott described what he called transitional objects: the beloved teddy bear or blanket that helps a young child move from total dependence toward independence. The object is comforting because it carries the feeling of being cared for even when a parent is out of the room.

Thirty years earlier, Milne took a transitional object and gave it a voice, a personality, and a whole forest. Every child who has hugged a stuffed animal at bedtime understands Pooh instinctively. In a sense, the books do for readers what the bear did for Christopher Robin. They offer comfort we can carry with us.

The friends keep making room for one another. They become annoyed. They misunderstand. Their efforts to help do not always go smoothly. These trials are what make their friendships believable.

Eeyore matters here. People with depression sometimes withdraw because they believe they will bring everyone else down. Friends, unsure what to say, may pull away too. The distance grows just when connection is needed most.

We can take a gentler approach: Keep inviting. Offer a walk, a cup of tea, or a little company. Allow room for quiet. In Pooh's world, a friend is someone worth checking on.

The Hundred Acre Wood offers a different pace. There is time to visit, walk, wonder, and become absorbed in something small. A modest adventure is enough to fill the day.

In 1982, Benjamin Hoff's bestseller The Tao of Pooh presented the bear as a model of simple, present-moment living. Long before mindfulness became a wellness trend, Pooh was savoring honey, noticing the weather, and enjoying his friends without needing to accomplish anything.

The word nostalgia was coined in 1688 by a Swiss physician who considered it a disease of homesick soldiers. Nostalgia tends to lift mood, strengthen our sense that life has meaning, and remind us that we are loved. In one study, people who felt lonely drew on nostalgic memories to restore their sense of connection. Looking back, it turns out, can help us move forward. The original stuffed animals that inspired Milne now live at the New York Public Library, where they receive 750,000 visitors every year.

The last lesson, and it is a sober one. The real Christopher Robin did not always enjoy being famous. As he grew older, he was teased at school, and as an adult he wrote with mixed feelings about the boy in the books. His father eventually stopped writing about him, explaining that his son had "already had more publicity than I want for him."

These stories hold the wish to be known, accepted, and remembered. Near the end of The House at Pooh Corner, Christopher Robin asks Pooh to promise he won't forget him, "not even when I'm a hundred."

A century later, we have kept that promise too.
