What Are Your Emotional Triggers at Work?

What is the one thing that could happen at work to ensure that you have a “bad” day? If someone really wanted to upset you at work, what should they do? When do you feel the most angry, disappointed, frustrated, stressed, or hurt at work? Answers to questions like these point to your specific emotional triggers at work.

What is the value of identifying your emotional triggers? Identification is the first step toward recognizing patterns and commonalities across instances. That recognition allows you to begin considering what is going on internally (in you) when these situations occur. That is, how do you interpret the events that prompt a particularly strong emotion in you?

The goal is to answer the question, “Why do I feel strong emotions when this happens?” The answer may seem obvious at first. For example, suppose that a trigger for you is a coworker not pulling their weight. The obvious answer is “I get angry because it’s unfair. I’m working harder than they are.” True, it is unfair. But why does that unfairness upset you?

Someone else might react to the same situation by concluding that the important thing is whether they themselves are doing a good job, and perhaps they believe that slackers will eventually experience negative consequences. The point isn’t that there is a right way or wrong way to interpret the experience of a slacking coworker, but rather that every interpretation (reaction) is a personal one. There isn’t an inevitable response that every person would have if they experienced the same situation.

In this example, does a seemingly lazy coworker remind you of someone from your past who acted the same way and caused you problems? Has your work ethic been questioned at some point in your life, and so you have an old fear of being seen as less-than-hardworking, and your coworker’s behavior reminds you at some level of that old experience of your own? Perhaps someone in your past accused you of not working hard, yet was a hypocrite by being the lazy one. The hope is that identifying the root cause of our interpretations of other people’s behavior allows for the possibility of letting it go. How? By talking to ourselves to arrive at a more rational, or less personally harmful, conclusion.

To continue with this example, if your anger or upset does not result in your coworker changing their behavior, what is the purpose? We know the result—raised blood pressure and decreased satisfaction with your job. Who gets hurt by your interpretation (reaction) to your coworker?

Sometimes our emotional triggers at work can point us toward steps we can take to potentially improve the situation. If feeling upset with a slacking coworker motivates you to have a calm, candid conversation with the coworker or their supervisor, then the coworker may start working harder, and everyone on the team may benefit. Too often, though, our triggers result in complaining, gossip, and decreased satisfaction at work.

So, the overarching goal is to use our triggers as information in the pursuit of resolving them, one way or another. There is additional benefit in explicitly sharing your triggers with your coworkers. The rationale you present might be that you want your colleagues to better understand your negative emotions when they witness them. In actuality, if your coworkers know your triggers, they may be less likely to behave in ways that activate your triggers.

Ideally, work teams would invest a little time in candidly sharing what each person’s triggers are. This team-building activity brings people closer together (as most forms of self-disclosure tend to do), and informs everyone of how best to work with each other. It can also be a revelation to realize that the people around you do not necessarily share the same emotional triggers (although we tend to assume that other people share ours).
