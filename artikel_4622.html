Few watch complications have as much character as the chronograph. Originally developed as a tool for measuring elapsed time, chronos became an essential piece of equipment in aviation, motorsport, and professional timing.

Today, the chronograph remains one of watchmaking’s most recognizable designs, with twin pushers, layered subdials, and tachymeter scales giving even a simple wristwatch a distinctly technical look. It’s also one of the few complications where the mechanical function is just as satisfying to operate as it is to look at.

For anyone getting into watches, chronographs offer an especially interesting entry point. The category spans everything from affordable Japanese quartz movements to sophisticated Swiss automatics, with plenty of variation in case architecture, dial layout, and movement design along the way.

There’s a lot to learn too, such as the difference between a standard chronograph and a flyback, the appeal of a column-wheel movement, or why certain vintage racing references have become so collectible. You can spend a little or a lot and still come away with a watch that has a story behind it.

We put together this selection with both new buyers and seasoned enthusiasts in mind. Some are modern interpretations of historically important designs, others have direct ties to motorsport, and a few offer interesting movements or complications that make them stand out from the usual three-hand watch.

The primary distinction between a regular watch and a chronograph lies in a chronograph’s stopwatch functions. A chronograph enables its users to measure the elapsed time of an event, like one lap of a racetrack or how long you’ve had your steaks on the grill. All the while, your watch is keeping track of the current time.

In addition to basic timekeeping and stopwatch capabilities, many chronograph watches come equipped with a tachymeter, a scale used to measure speed based on elapsed time over a specific distance (usually one mile), and a telemeter, which measures distance based on the speed of sound and elapsed time. Water resistance is another common feature, protecting the watch from damage due to moisture or immersion in water.

Chronographs come in various types, each with its own characteristics:

A chronograph combines the functionality of a traditional watch with a built-in stopwatch. This allows users to measure elapsed time and split times, which makes it ideal for driving.

To use the stopwatch functions on a chronograph timepiece, you press the corresponding pushers (button) on the side of the watch to start, stop, and reset the stopwatch. Certain chronograph watches may have additional pushers for more advanced functions like split or lap times. Due to the extra mechanical complexity required, watches with more pushers will typically cost more than monopusher (single-pusher) timepieces.

A tachymeter is a scale on the bezel or dial of certain chronograph watches. Its purpose is to measure the speed of an object using the watch’s built-in stopwatch, based on the time passed. To use the tachymeter, start the chronograph function when something starts (like a car crossing a start line) and stop it when it’s finished (when the car hits the finish line). The tachymeter scale will then display the car’s average speed, based on the time it took to get from A to B.

Proper maintenance of your chronograph depends on its type. Mechanical chronograph watches require regular servicing (roughly every 5–7 years) to ensure optimal performance. Quartz chronograph watches may need battery replacements, and solar-powered watches should be exposed to light regularly to maintain their charge. Be sure to always follow the manufacturer’s maintenance guidelines.
