Winners of the Banff Mountain Film Festival Announced

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

The annual Banff Centre Mountain Festival gathers mountain luminaries from across the world at the Banff Centre for Arts and Creativity in Alberta. There, a celebration of mountain culture recognizes the best outdoor films and books of the past year. The climax of the festival took place this past weekend, and the winners of the 51st annual Banff Mountain Film Competition have now been announced.

The Grand Prize went to Listen, directed by Taliesin Black-Brown. In this short documentary, sound engineer Hank Lentfer records the sounds of nature around his Alaska home, while caring for his mother with dementia. As Lentfer documents the degradation of the natural world, we witness the slow loss of his mother. Drawing together these two threads, the film explores connection despite, and through, loss, using sound.

Rose Bush, an Oscar Award-winning director of photography serving on the competition’s jury, called Listen “a film that engages human-caused environmental devastation — the existential dilemma of our time — at its nucleus with overwhelming love and hope.”

The film also won the Grand Jury Prize at the Tribeca Film Festival.

In addition to a grand prize, the festival honors films in several categories. The winning film in the Adventure category was The Navigator, a feature-length work from director Jordan Manley. The film follows Nainoa Thompson, who was the first Native Hawaiian in six centuries to make the journey from Hawaii to Tahiti in a traditional voyaging canoe. Now, almost five decades later, Thompson and his crew make the journey again, using only traditional navigation techniques.

The best film in the Mountain Culture category was Land of Canaan, produced and directed by Maggie Lemere. Lemere’s film follows a group of Palestinian olive farmers, who work to protect their ancient olive groves amid the destruction by Israel. The film “illuminated the vital relationship of courage, vision, hope, and traditional ecological knowledge,” juror Rose Bush said, “in service of the essential value of sovereignty via mountain culture.”

In the Environment category, director James Dawson’s winning comedic documentary Derek vs Derek follows two Devon farmers, both named Derek. One Derek is an ardent dairy farmer while his environmentalist neighbor begins rewilding his land. A feud naturally ensues.

The Arc’teryx-sponsored Best Climbing Film was Zindabad, directed by Tom Fish. Fish follows Pakistani climber Mueez Ud Din into the Karakoram, alongside his uncle Javed Ali and French climber Mathieu Maynadier. Ud Din explores a remote, unknown glacier system, while the film examines what it means to be a Pakistani climber among peaks long claimed by international outsiders.

Directed by Alexis Berg, the Mountain Sports winner, Roots, follows trail runner Ruth Croft as she examines her future as an elite ultra-trail runner after an injury. Roots is “about an inner journey of healing and acceptance, rooted in the essential joy of running outdoors,” explained juror David Arnaud.

The Snow Sports winner centers on American skier Jessie Diggins. Diggins is the most decorated American cross-country skier ever, but Threshold, directed by Torsten Brinkema, reveals the deep personal struggles behind that success. Juror Beki Henderson noted that “the jury particularly commends Jessie Diggins for her extraordinary courage and honesty in sharing her story.”

There were more mountain films this year than you can even imagine. With 610 film submissions and 83 finalists, it’s no wonder that there are still more category winners.

Best short film winner Hello, Beautiful follows climber Alannah Yip as she struggles with identity, training for the Olympics while enduring medical hair loss. Director Darcy Hennessey created a film which “reminds us that personal transformation does not always come from pushing harder,” juror Kerry Stauffer explained.

For best feature film, the competition announced Ceremony, directed and produced by Banchi Hanuse. The film tells the story of the disappearance of the ooligan, a small fish, from British Columbia’s Bella Coola River. Jurors praised the film’s use of watercolor animation, personal stories, and community broadcasts to explore the importance of a seemingly unimportant little fish.

Director Marco Tribelhorn’s 75 seconds won the Creative Excellence Award. The film documents free-skier Jacob Wester’s 75-second-long battle for survival while trapped in an avalanche.

“A compelling visual treatment brings to life an experience that is impossible to imagine,” said Kerry Stauffer.

Last but not least, the Audience Choice Award went to Only Music // Snake River. Director Justin Fountain’s documentary follows a band that ditches the tour bus for a journey down the Snake River, seeking connection with strangers.
