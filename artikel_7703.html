How to Polish Wood Furniture

If you want to maintain the beautiful, natural look of your wood furniture, take the time to polish it. A good polishing restores the wood’s appearance, bringing out the beauty of the wood grain while helping to conceal minor scratches. Wood polish can also create a protective barrier that repels dust while shielding the finish and wood from spills. Of course, polishing only protects and prolongs the life of your wood furniture if you use the right type of polish and application technique. Use the wrong polish or apply it incorrectly and you can end up with unsightly buildup, streaks, and even permanent damage. We sought expert guidance from Saadet Turlu, founder of Wow by AlpC, a Sheridan, Wyoming-based company that specializes in high-end solid wood furniture.

There are several options when it comes to choosing a polish—or you can make your own furniture polish. Though liquid varieties are easy to apply, wax-based products offer more staying power. While the right polish is key to getting good results when polishing wood furniture, so too is using the right technique.

Remove all objects from the furniture, including lamps, objects and any other decor, so you can apply the polish evenly over the entire piece. You also don’t want these objects to end up with polish residue on them.

Wipe the area down with a dry microfiber cloth or tack cloth to remove any loose dust. Use a Q-tip to clean dust out of crevices around trim and furniture legs. If there is grime or oily fingerprints, use a barely damp cloth to clean thoroughly. If the piece is especially dirty, you may need to conduct a more thorough cleaning before proceeding.

Not all polish is safe for all wood finishes, and you can’t always trust the list of compatible finishes listed on the bottle. Before diving in, test the polish on an inconspicuous spot to make sure it reacts well with the wood finish. An underside area of the furniture or a corner works well.

Never spray wood polish directly onto the wood. Doing so can create streaks or puddling on the furniture surface while also creating overspray that can land on the floor or surrounding furnishings. Instead, apply a small amount of polish to a soft, lint-free microfiber towel. “Apply the polish very sparingly,” Turlu says. “Always wipe in the direction of the wood grain to ensure the product penetrates the pores evenly.” Apply sparingly. Too much polish can leave a sticky film on the wood surface.

Buffing the piece is key to getting an even, attractive finish. Switch to a separate clean dry microfiber cloth and rub the surface of the wood in the direction of the grain using light pressure. “Apply light, consistent pressure and use sweeping motions, following the direction of the wood grain,” Turlu says. “If streaks appear on the surface, it means you are either applying too much pressure or you used an excessive amount of product initially.”

While furniture polish will help to enhance the shine on your furniture and hide minor scratches, it won’t do anything for stains. To remove water stains from wood, start by identifying the type of stain your dealing with:

White water rings: Water rings are often a result of damage to the finish rather than the wood itself. The best way to deal with rings is to use a commercial water marker removes. Make sure the remover is compatible with the type of finish on the wood. If you’re not sure, test the product on a hidden part of the furniture first.

Dark water stains: Dark stains are usually an indication that the stain has reached into the wood fibers. Removing these stains requires a more extensive repair that’s better left to the pros, especially if the furniture is an antique or a family heirloom.

Aim for a polishing schedule of one to three months depending on how heavily the piece of furniture is used. You might polish a dining table or desk once a month, while you may wait three months for less heavily used pieces, like a china cabinet.

Turlu advises specifically against any products containing silicone. “While silicone may provide an immediate, artificial sheen, it penetrates permanently into the wood fibers,” he says. “If that piece of furniture ever needs to be professionally refinished, the silicone will cause the new finish to separate and fail (a nightmare for refinishers).”

A good polishing wax is almost always a better choice than a polishing oil for most furniture. Wax-based polishes create a barrier that not only enhances the look of the wood but also creates a protective barrier against water and scratches that lasts for months. A polishing oil might be easier to apply, but it remains on the surface of the wood and can attract airborne dust.
