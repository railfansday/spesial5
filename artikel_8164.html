Sweeping New BLM Proposal Would Limit Public Comment and Weaken Environmental Protections for American West

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Following a year of attempted sweeping changes to US public land management, the Bureau of Land Management (BLM) has proposed a rewrite of some of the most foundational regulations for managing more than 245 million acres of public land across the American West that would limit public comment and reduce environmental reviews.

Published on November 10 in the Federal Register, the proposal would overhaul how the agency plans projects, seeks public input, and adheres to environmental rules. Officially titled Revision of Regulations for Planning, Programming, Budgeting (43 CFR Part 1600), the BLM’s proposed changes would arguably be the largest overhaul of these rules since their adoption in 1979 under the Federal Land Policy and Management Act.

Officials from the Department of the Interior claim these revisions will accelerate project approvals, while local communities and conservation groups argue that the proposal would strip environmental safeguards to fast-track oil and gas drilling, mining, and commercial development.

One of the most important changes in the BLM’s proposal is the removal of the requirement that the agency prepare a comprehensive Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) each time it writes or revises a Resource Management Plan. These resource plans determine where extraction, grazing, recreation, and conservation can occur on public lands, and an EIS evaluates them for potential harm to ecosystems and surrounding communities.

Doug Burgum has served as the United States Secretary of the Interior since February 1, 2025. Photo via Doug Burgum

In the proposal, the regulatory section previously titled “Environmental Impact Statement” would be renamed “Environmental Compliance,” and the automatic EIS requirement would be removed, instead allowing the BLM “to determine how best to comply with NEPA during land use planning.” The agency claims that the current requirement duplicates procedures already governed by the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA).

Alongside the changes to environmental reviews, the proposal substantially limits the ability of the public and partner governments to weigh in. The rule eliminates the mandatory 90-day public comment period for draft land-use plans and EISs and replaces it with a 30-day window that can be extended by no more than 30 additional days.

It also removes requirements to publish a Federal Register notice when launching a planning process and drops the 30-day comment period previously required when the agency makes major last-minute changes to a proposed plan. Notably, the proposal would also enable BLM to replace written public comment periods with public meetings “when permissible under other authorities — such as NEPA — and when adequate notice to the public is provided.”

At the intergovernmental level, the proposal ends the requirement that BLM land-use plans maintain consistency with Tribal governments and other federal agency plans, while cutting a state governor’s final consistency review window from 60 days to 30 days and eliminating the governor’s right to appeal a rejection to the BLM Director. These changes strip away the legal leverage that Tribal nations, neighboring federal agencies, and state governors currently use to work with the BLM to align on land-use decisions.

The proposed rule would create a new regulatory mechanism entitled “Amending Land Use Plans To Address Emergencies” that would authorize a Senate-confirmed official in the Department of the Interior to bypass standard consultation procedures when amending a plan to support projects responding to “emergency circumstances.” However, the proposed text neither defines what constitutes an “emergency circumstance” nor limits the types of commercial or infrastructure projects that could qualify for the expedited track.

A map showing the land currently managed by the BLM. Photo via BLM

The proposal also overhauls how the BLM designates and manages Areas of Critical Environmental Concern (ACECs), which are landscapes recognized for sensitive fish and wildlife habitat, cultural sites, or natural hazards.

The new proposal removes requirements for a separate 60-day public comment period and Federal Register notice for proposed ACECs. Interior officials stated the revisions will give the agency greater flexibility to “right-size” designations rather than manage what the department described as “oversized buffer zones.”

“The boundaries of designated areas of critical environmental concern shall encompass the smallest area of public lands necessary to protect and prevent irreparable damage of the relevant and important values,” reads the proposal.

In a statement announcing the revisions, the BLM said the rule “would update planning terminology, eliminate duplicate requirements and simplify parts of the planning process that have become unnecessarily burdensome over time,” while ensuring public input occurs at “the most meaningful stages.”

Environmental advocacy organizations, including Earthjustice and the Sierra Club, condemned the proposal immediately following its release, arguing that eliminating mandatory EIS reviews and 90-day comment periods will allow the administration to make sweeping land-use decisions behind closed doors while sidelining local communities, Tribes, scientists, conservationists, and those who recreate on public lands.

The 60-day public comment period on the proposed rule (Docket No. BLM-2026-0298) is currently open through January 11, 2027. You can submit a public comment here. The BLM has also scheduled a virtual public information session for December 3, 2026.
