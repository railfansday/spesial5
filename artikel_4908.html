WHEN YOUR HOME gym is short on square footage like mine, you need to carefully think about every piece of equipment before it earns its rightful spot. That’s exactly why kettlebells are a staple in my setup. Over time, I’ve built a small but mighty collection—and every single one gets regular use.

A huge perk of kettlebells is that they don’t hog a lot of space (even in a compact home gym) and are relatively inexpensive, says Tyneka Pack, CPT. These are versatile strength-training tools, too, allowing you to get in a full-body workout, along with explosive power movements, says kettlebell specialist Taylor Beebe, CPT.

As with any fitness equipment, improper use of kettlebells can lead to injury. That’s why most trainers, including Beebe, recommend getting coaching on how to use kettlebells before diving in (peep our how to get started with kettlebell exercises guide).

Like dumbbells, kettlebells come in a wide range of weights, materials, and grips, so it’s important to determine which type you’re in the market for before you buy.

“When choosing the correct weight for your kettlebell, it is important to consider your exercise,” says Almoni Ellis, CSCS, CPT. She says that people generally are able to use heavier weights when doing purely lower-body exercises, but that those weights may be too heavy for a person when they’re doing a move like an overhead press, for example.

That said, there’s no one-size-fits-all starting point. Your experience level and current strength come into play, so if you’re unsure where to begin, try testing different weights at a local gym or consider ordering two sizes to see what feels best. As your strength improves, you can gradually increase the weight, adds Pack.

The most common option for general strength training is the cast-iron kettlebell, which works well for everything from goblet squats to cleans to swings. These are the kettlebells you’ll most often see in home gyms and fitness classes.

Ellis and Pack recommend choosing a cast iron or steel kettlebell because they’re the most durable and long-lasting materials. “Kettlebells [that are] made up of plastic[...] don’t last as long,” Pack says.

However, most adjustable kettlebells are made with some plastic components, which is necessary for the design. As for vinyl-coated kettlebells, they’re usually made of cast iron with a vinyl coating to prevent damage to floors.

Handle thickness plays a role in both comfort and performance. “Thicker handles can help build grip strength, but they may be more challenging for beginners to control,” says Pack.

The handle’s coating and texture matter just as much. A slightly textured surface can help you maintain a secure grip, especially during dynamic or high-rep movements. Slick handles, on the other hand, can become slippery as your hands sweat—raising the risk of losing your grip, Pack adds. If you’re training with kettlebell that has a slick grip, consider using workout chalk or liquid chalk during moves like swings.

To choose the best kettlebells, I consulted three personal trainers who regularly work with kettlebells: Taylor Beebe, CPT, Tyneka Pack, CPT, and Almoni Ellis, CSCS. I also got feedback from Women’s Health fitness and reviews editor Natascha Grief, NASM-CPT, who is a certified trainer.
