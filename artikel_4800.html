What the Recent Tech Outage Can Teach Us About Balance

As I write this, parts of North America and Europe are experiencing a Microsoft outage that is affecting certain parts of the cloud infrastructure, particularly office communication services such as Outlook, Teams, and Microsoft 360. While this is certainly causing disruption and inconvenience in many corporate and business settings, it may also be reminding us of an important reality that we often talk about but rarely implement: work-life balance.

In the United States, it has long been reported that workers struggle with creating healthy boundaries between their lives and their jobs, and a 2025 study found that a staggering 66 percent of American workers reported having experienced “some degree of burnout” in the past year (Robinson, 2025). Additionally, 2024 research indicated that 44 percent of employees and 53 percent of supervisors experienced burnout at their jobs (Malinsky, 2022). It is no secret that many of us struggle to find the “sweet spot” between work and the rest of our lives. Increasingly, this disparity between work and life is being seen as a larger problem that, according to a 2021 study, is more than “an individual-level phenomenon,” and is “a societal concern that necessitates systematic support from a wider range of stakeholders” (Fan, Yuyang, et al., 2021).

Along with the statistical evidence, we can find plenty of anecdotal proof that this is a widespread problem: you would be hard-pressed to find a person who does not report some level of exhaustion or burnout related to their career, myself and those I know being no exception. But if it is such an issue, why are we unable to see something like this Microsoft outage as a gift rather than an inconvenience? I would argue that it has to do with our longstanding relationship with work and our misprioritization of its importance. American attitudes about work have long been centered around productivity, busyness, results, and multitasking. Words like “balance” are simply not part of our career-related vernacular.

When something like a technology outage forces us to let go of our usual outcome-orientation towards our jobs, it can feel odd and somehow “wrong.” Looking at social media comments today, I am seeing more that speak to how annoying and inconvenient this outage is and less that speak to it allowing for a slower workday or an (albeit forced) opportunity to not work as hard. But perhaps we can learn from today’s widespread inconvenience. In some workplaces, you are unable to send or answer emails, communicate as usual, or access cloud-related platforms. In one sense, this is frustrating. In another, it is permission not to do these things that, let’s be honest, are often done from home, from the dinner table, at unhealthy hours, in the midst of other life activities, and at the cost of mindfulness and presence in our lives. I am not suggesting that tech outages are good for business, but rather that they can remind us that creating space between work and life is necessary for our overall well-being.

Sometimes inconveniences can be opportunities. In Buddhist philosophy, the concept that the “obstacle is the path” is applicable to today’s inconvenience. The obstacle (not being able to access the usual work platforms) is the path to allowing ourselves to slow down and create some space between work and life—at least for a day or two.
