The Inner Voice Isn't Always the Inner Enemy

Updated November 8, 2026 | Reviewed by Lybi Ma

For a long time, I felt like an imposter. Although I had graduated from medical school, passed the internal medicine boards, and completed a residency, part of me still grappled with imposter syndrome.

Imposter syndrome (also called the impostor phenomenon) is when you persistently doubt your abilities, feel like a fraud, and fear others will expose you, even when you have clear proof of your success.

I suppose it had to do with my early childhood. I was molested from the ages of 11 to 13. I told my parents, but when they confronted the senior Boy Scout leaders, they were shamed into silence. I was left with an undying feeling that it was all my fault. I internalized this, and that gave birth to a voice inside my head that wasn’t my own. It belonged to my perpetrator, who installed it in me when I didn’t have the language to refute it.

While I engaged with several therapists over decades, I foolishly believed that the shame I was attempting to overcome was something I could outrun, suppress, or avoid. And for a while, I believed I had. The proof was clear: I became a married physician. I was successful, and yet when the COVID-19 pandemic hit, all the pillars that had quietly moored me to my external life disappeared. In that isolation, shame came roaring back.

I, like millions of Americans, ran to alcohol to cope.

Throughout this period, the voice inside my head, the one I dubbed the unreliable narrator, played on an endless loop. He told me that I was too much, or not enough, or fundamentally broken. I believed him because, well, he was the voice in my head. I was ill-equipped to challenge this voice or ask whether these were facts or simply thoughts. It never occurred to me because that voice lived so clearly inside me that challenging him meant challenging the very framework that had made me a success.

This conflict became apparent when my beloved manager, an Ivy League professor, called me idiotic. No, he didn’t call me an idiot, though he might as well have. He said "idiotic," and I internalized that word and shut down. I couldn’t speak. I couldn't breathe. It was a truly embarrassing experience.

Eventually, I recovered, but I sat with that moment for a long time. What I came to understand much later was that, by using the term idiotic, my beloved manager activated an old trauma I had locked away in my subconscious. My former child molester often called me an idiot. When my manager used that word in a conversation with my adult self, it triggered a trauma response in my younger self—fight, flight, or freeze. My body chose freeze.

What I learned from this experience is the power of the pause—pausing is your superpower. Far too many of us respond quickly, often talking before the other person has finished their sentence. But I took it a step further and developed the four P’s: pause, present, ponder, and possibilities.

The trick is you have to practice when you’re not activated. Next time you’re speaking to your friend, when they stop talking, don’t answer. Pause. Count to three, check in with yourself, be present, ponder the situation, and then, and only then, can you see the possibilities.

Internal self: “What’s going on with you now?”

Activated self: “I'm feeling my face flush. My heart is racing.”

Internal self: “Okay, you’re experiencing shame. Do you have any idea why?"

Activated self: “My child molester used to call me an idiot.”

Internal self: “Okay, but you’re not an idiot. In fact, you’re not 11 years old. You’re an adult doctor, and this situation has nothing to do with what happened to you decades ago."

Internal self: “Now that you understand your manager activated some old trauma, how would you like to respond?"

Activated self: “You asked a good question. It deserves some thought. Let me get back to you.”

Remember: You can't think your way out of shame that lives in your nervous system.

Shame is a well-intentioned guard dog with terrible judgment about what constitutes actual danger.
