Data sovereignty takes more than keeping data close to home

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

SPONSORED POST: Knowing where your data lives is only part of the challenge. Who controls it, who can access it and how quickly you can recover when things go wrong matter just as much.

Keeping sensitive data within national borders might satisfy a residency requirement. But does it mean your organization is genuinely in control?

Not necessarily. Data could be stored locally while remaining subject to another jurisdiction's laws, dependent on overseas service providers or exposed to disruption beyond your control.

For enterprise IT teams, that distinction is becoming harder to ignore. Geopolitical tensions, evolving regulations and the growing use of AI are forcing organizations to reconsider what data sovereignty means in practice. Yet translating those concerns into a workable strategy is another matter.

And simply throwing more money at the problem won't necessarily solve it.

In The Register's on-demand webinar, Data Sovereignty By Design, moderator Tim Phillips speaks with Patrick Smith, Field CTO for EMEA at Everpure, formerly Pure Storage, about moving beyond compliance checklists and taking a more strategic approach to data control.

Drawing on findings from Everpure's research, Smith explores a curious disconnect: organizations are increasing their investment in sovereignty initiatives, yet many still lack a coordinated strategy. Some of the risks attracting the most attention aren't necessarily those receiving the greatest investment.

The discussion examines why sovereignty shouldn't be treated as an IT-only responsibility. Decisions about which data needs protection, which services must remain operational and what level of risk is acceptable require input from business leaders, security, procurement and legal teams.

There's also the question of priorities. Not every workload needs the same sovereign controls, and attempting to protect everything equally can make an already complicated challenge considerably harder.

Smith explains the concept of the "minimum viable company," identifying the critical services, applications and data an organization needs to keep operating through disruption. He also considers why visibility into data access, supplier dependencies and potential exit strategies should influence technology decisions from the outset.

For IT leaders trying to turn sovereignty from an abstract boardroom concern into something actionable, the conversation offers useful questions to take back to their organizations.

Because knowing where your data sits is one thing. Knowing whether you can maintain control when circumstances change is quite another.
