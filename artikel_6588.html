“Focus on Learning”: Career Lessons With Burberry’s Daniel Lee and Anna Wintour

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

The morning after Vogue World 2026: Milano, all anyone online wanted to talk about was “the robots”. The moment became a lightning rod for memes, TikTok reactions, and questions about the state of creativity today. It turns out that was sort of the point.

A couple of months after the event, Anna Wintour, chief content officer at Condé Nast and global editorial director of Vogue, invited Burberry’s chief creative officer Daniel Lee to New York for a fireside chat at the Vogue Business Global Summit. Lee also wanted to talk about “the robots.”

A robot walks the runway during Vogue World: Milano 2026.

Of course, the two discussed much more than that, taking turns interviewing each other about Lee’s journey at the British brand during a company-wide turnaround, Wintour’s first job in fashion, the US midterms that happened the day before, and success — spoiler: social media makes it look easy. Ahead of the official announcement, Wintour also teased the Met Costume Institute’s theme for 2027: extraORDINARY.

Anna Wintour: Daniel, good morning. Thank you for coming all the way from London. So Daniel, you chose a very quiet news day here in the US. Have you been following the election news?

Daniel Lee: Yeah, definitely. I was super jet-lagged, so I was watching the news all night.

AW: I have to ask you, how do you think [Senator-elect] James Talarico would look in a Burberry suit?

DL: Well, I’d be happy to see anyone looking great in a Burberry suit.

Anna and Daniel on stage at the Vogue Business Global Summit.

AW: Very diplomatic. I guess you’re a Burberry ambassador. I read that you first thought you wanted to be a designer when your aunt took you to see the windows at Harvey Nichols in Leeds as a boy. I guess the equivalent over here would be Saks. Do you agree?

DL: Yes, I’m afraid that is true. You know, I didn’t fall into fashion by looking through pages of Vogue. That’s probably the story I should start saying. I mean, I grew up in the north of England in an area called Yorkshire, which is very beautiful — it’s in the countryside and very green. It rains a lot.

DL: David Hockney is from my hometown. Also, Christopher Bailey is from the same area [Yorkshire]. The Burberry trench coats are made in that area, so I guess Burberry was somehow ingrained in me since I was born. Clothes were really practical in my childhood. Fashion wasn’t something that was really part of my family or my upbringing. We wore clothes to stay warm and dry.

DL: Very Burberry. As kids, we liked to play outside. We would spend our childhood riding bikes in the rain, in Wellington boots. When Harvey Nichols opened in Leeds, I remember going past that store and seeing these incredible windows, and I guess I realized clothes could be so much more. It was like seeing a fantasyland. I guess that’s when I started to think this maybe could be a career for me.

DL: Eventually, yes. As a child, I think my first instinct was to get out of Yorkshire. As much as I love the countryside, I knew there was more. I was curious.

AW: Well, you did just buy a house there, so you must like it?

DL: You stay connected to your roots. But I think, as a 12-year-old, I went to London for the first time, and I remember seeing the variety of people in the streets, seeing the theater, so many things going on. I really wanted to be part of that culture. I think that’s what initially drew me to London and eventually to art school. I went to [Central] Saint Martins. I did an art foundation year, into fashion qualifications later on.

AW: OK. You’re meant to ask me a question now.

DL: Anna, what was your first job in fashion? And what did you learn from it?

AW: I’m going to talk about the first job that I actually enjoyed in fashion, which was working at Harper’s & Queen (now Harper’s Bazaar UK) in London, where there were two to three people — on a good day — in the fashion department. So I went in there, and I was immediately an editor, even though I had zero experience. I was always so very grateful for that training because there were no budgets. I do remember my boss once giving me maybe £10 or £20 and saying, ‘Anna, go to Morocco or go south. Find a sheikh, find a maharaja, and do a shoot.’ And somehow you did. It was just a different attitude. You had to be so creative and make things out of nothing. Because there were no teams at Harper’s & Queen, I learned how to do everything: you covered the market, you did the shoots, you wrote the captions.

When I came to New York, I went to work at Harper’s Bazaar [US]. I got fired fairly immediately because they told me I would never understand the American market, which is probably true. I then landed on my feet at New York Magazine, which was unbelievable training because I was working in a newsroom with guys like Nick Pileggi and Tom Wolfe — all the great writers of that time. They didn’t give a damn about what I did. They put me next to the bathrooms, so I got to see everybody coming in and out. Because nobody cared about fashion or style, they just left me alone. Eventually, I got a call from Condé Nast. It’s interesting when you talk to young people today how they’re focused on success, and they’re not focused on learning. Without the base, you can't succeed.

I’m meant to ask you a question now. So, congratulations on your very successful show in September. I think it was the first time you collaborated with anyone creatively, and the person that you chose to collaborate with is Celia Birtwell — she’s an 85-year-old legend in textile design, a partner to the great Ossie Clark. Did you have to do much convincing to get her involved? Maybe explain to our audience a little bit more about who she is.

DL: I would say Celia is probably mostly known as Ossie Clark’s collaborator in all those years of incredible dresses and work that I think is on every designer’s moodboard. And she was also very, very close friends with David Hockney and is a subject in many beautiful pieces of his work. We were celebrating 170 years of Burberry this year. We wanted to do the brand justice, and we were thinking of ways to do new things. I met with Celia, actually, just for a conversation. You never really know if someone’s going to be interested in working together. People are terrified by our timelines.

Textile designer Celia Birtwell with her partner and collaborator Ossie Clark in July 1971. Birtwell’s prints for Clark’s dresses are, in Daniel Lee’s words, “on every designer's moodboard.”

She came to the meeting saying, ‘You know, I’m really inspired by the check. I love the way Hockney paints ginghams on a tablecloth, the perspective that he gives to that.’ I said, ‘Well, would you be up for making some new work?’ And she did. She started with the checks, then the ginghams, and then the beautiful kind of English florals. Hockney gives me so much joy, emotion and warmth.

Birtwell’s new take on the brand’s check was inspired by the way David Hockney paints gingham on a tablecloth.

AW: Yeah, and he was so prolific, painting right up until the end, using an iPad, using different ways to make art. I’m sure you saw that last incredible show that was on at the Louis Vuitton Foundation in Paris. What a life. [Now] you’re meant to ask me…

DL: Congratulations on Vogue World: Milan. Would you like to talk about the robots?

AW: I’d love to. So the subject of Vogue World: Milan was our belief in the art of the human hand, the creativity of the hand, and the human mind in the age of technology. And we felt that to add something that represented technology, in a way that was — in my view — faintly ridiculous, reinforced the idea behind the theme of the exhibition. As is often in my case, not everyone agreed with me, but it certainly caused a lot of conversation.

Gigi Hadid at Vogue World: Milano. The show celebrated, in Anna Wintour’s words, “the art of the human hand” in the age of technology.

We’re moving on now to San Francisco in September, and we’re really going to be addressing the diversity and the incredible communities that live in San Francisco, and how it’s a city that has stood up for so much. The symphony and the museums there are extraordinary.

Talking about San Francisco, let’s talk about tech. Your last collection seemed to us that you were really advocating the celebration of imperfection and the romance of things made by hand. Is that true? And what do you feel about AI in terms of your creative work?

DL: As a child, I would spend my time making birthday cards, making cakes with my mom and making dance costumes for my sister. I’m a maker. Obviously I have a mobile phone, but tech is not a huge part of my life per se. I’m not a big computer gamer; I never even check email. I only really use WhatsApp.

DL: I think that’s what I love about luxury fashion. I really think luxury is about preservation of this savoir-faire, this craftsmanship. One of the main roles of the creative directors and companies today is to preserve that know-how and make it relevant, so that customers today can appreciate it.

OK, Anna, could you tell us more about next year’s Costume Institute show?

AW: Absolutely. It’s going to be extraordinary.

Daniel, I think it’s five years that you’ve been at Burberry?

AW: You went into a company that was in disarray, and looking at it from the outside, maybe not very well run. Two years ago, CEO Joshua Schulman came in. That must have been very welcome to have a good partner. He’s been integral — led by your creativity — to Burberry’s unbelievable turnaround. What is it like to work with Josh?

DL: Josh brought an incredible energy, passion, and belief in Burberry. He loves the brand, and his energy really reverberates in the company. I think that’s the role of a great leader. Josh brought a lot of discipline to the brand. He really grew up in American department stores; he intimately understands wholesalers and has very personal relationships with them.

Daniel Lee, Anna Wintour and Burberry CEO Joshua Schulman. Lee credits Schulman, who joined two years ago, with bringing “discipline” and a clear pricing strategy to the brand’s turnaround.

AW: I remember being in your store on a Saturday morning looking for something for a small child, and he was there, folding the scarves.

DL: We laugh, but Josh spends his vacations in malls. We had an executive dinner and were asked about our favorite places, and I’m sure he mentioned a mall in California. When he arrived, he brought a very clear idea around price architecture.

Anna, what do you think the British fashion industry needs at the moment?

AW: I think they need to really focus on who they are. We all look to London for creativity and individuality — and a degree of eccentricity. There’s a freedom to the way you see the kids dressed in the streets there that is the antithesis of American or cool Italian, more luxury dressing. It’s a little bit closer to what I was saying when I first was working in London, when you had to do everything.

I think that the British Fashion Council really needs to focus on that and make more noise about what you can see in London that you cannot see anywhere else. They have a great brand like Burberry, and it was so wonderful to see McQueen back in London and Christopher Kane at Mulberry.

They have so many creative kids, students, who can’t get jobs there, so they need to make sure that London becomes more of a global fashion mecca — that the retailers, the CEOs, and the journalists go. [Prime Minister] Andy Burnham seems like he’s going to be much more open to business in the UK, and they need to lean on what they own.

Burberry Spring/Summer 2027, a collection that celebrates handcraft and the brand’s 170th year.

So, the last shows were followed by quite a few creative director departures and appointments. How do you see this constant creative shuffle shaping the industry?

DL: My experience is that it takes time to understand and have a dialogue with the company that you work for, understand the archive, the culture, the people. It also takes time to build a team, and people need stability. I advocate for continuity. Also, when there are constant aesthetic shifts, it’s hard for the customer to understand that. I think there are moments where things do need to be revolutionized, and change is needed. Change is good. But too much change is discombobulating. It’s incredibly hard when you join a brand.

What do you think is the future of what we used to call magazines?

AW: I was doing an interview with Grace Coddington, who’s worked at Vogue for so many years, and we were reminiscing about when we were only working on print. She was able to go off to these extraordinary locations for what seems like an unthinkable amount of time — probably only with £20 in her pocket. She would go to Scotland for 10 days; she would go to China for two weeks. There was a great luxury in being able to have time to plan and think about what an issue of Vogue should look like. But it was a very narrow audience. When I first arrived at Vogue, people would disappear in the afternoons because they were going to Jones Beach — there just wasn’t that much to do.

Anna Wintour and Grace Coddington. Recalling their print days, Wintour remembers when Coddington could spend 10 days shooting in Scotland or two weeks in China. That was “a great luxury,” she says, but for a much narrower audience.

How lucky are we now to have all these amazing platforms to talk to our audiences? Our audiences have grown to unthinkable numbers. One of the great experiences and joys of what I do now is helping Adam [Baidawi, GQ’s global editorial director], Chloe [Malle, American Vogue’s head of editorial content], and Chioma [Nnadi, British Vogue’s head of editorial content], and all the editors that I work with over the world.

This afternoon, I’m leaving for Asia to work with our editors in Japan, China, Taiwan, and beyond. And I learn a lot from all of them. You have to be on the ground. I really feel that it’s so important to understand different cultures and how they think and work. I remember years and years ago, when I was first starting out, a great friend of mine said, ‘Anna, wherever you are, you have to walk in the street, climb a high point, visit a museum, and have a cup of coffee.’ I always remember that. I think sometimes when people get successful, they don’t walk in the street. When you walk in the street, that’s when you see the world.

What advice do you give those who want to become creative directors? Do you think social media has distorted the way young creatives see their trajectory by making them think they can be their own boss, or do you think it’s a good thing?

DL: I think social media makes things look very easy. People don’t always see the journey: the highs, the lows, the learnings. I don’t think you should set out to be an overnight success or to have instant gratification on social media. I’ve been every level, every part of a studio. I’ve been the intern, the pattern maker, the design director. I’ve lived all those jobs, and I’ve had those experiences, and that helps me do the job I do today.

AW: This is an audience of mostly executives. What’s one piece of advice you would give to them when it comes to working with creative teams?

DL: I think empathy is very important, respect is very important, understanding is important, and humility. No one’s right all the time. My favorite kind of working relationship is when it feels collaborative. I think as a creative leader, I try to create a space and framework to allow people to feel safe. I think this is something I once heard you say: ‘You hire great people, and you let them work.’ Trust is important.
