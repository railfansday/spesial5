Celebrating Cultures And Equity On Thanksgiving Day

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

There is an important subtext to this Thanksgiving Day in the United States. A “blue tsunami” has occurred, so, come January, the Democratic party will control the House of Representatives and the Senate. While there are many important elements to this upheaval, one thread connects them all: Democrats can reimagine both how they govern and how they communicate.

Former White House communication director Dan Pfeiffer argues this is not only possible, it is necessary. The Democrats, he says, must “make sure voters know that we tried like hell and that Republicans are to blame for the lack of progress.” Part of a successful communication strategy would involve embracing others across the political aisle. And what better time is there than Thanksgiving Day and the long weekend that follows?

Thanksgiving has long been celebrated as a way to assimilate immigrants into our national melting pot. Why not use the US holiday as a jumping off point to acknowledge the various celebrations that draw upon commonality of white American traditions, like turkey and stuffing — but also invite individual expressions of food and traditions from the diaspora? If we all would be more open to new ideas and their origins, wouldn’t the next steps be to open up conversations and insights into sustainability and equity?

We use labels because there’s so much information in the world that we can’t process each piece of it individually. The tendency to resort to labels is part of the psychological shortcuts we take to make sense of the world. So we group things and people together, and we attach labels to them. Labels help us to limit our time and attention instead of analyzing small details.

Nonetheless, when we label a person, we simplify the way we represent them in our brains. Labeling can create misunderstandings and stereotypes. On this Thanksgiving Day, let’s consider how the labels we place easily and quickly on others diminish our potential for social awareness and awakenings. Instead of succumbing to labels, let’s give thanks for the vibrant palette of people and cultures that surround us. In doing so, we enrich our lives and, by extension, the lives of those around us.

In fact, disparate cultures with differing politics often find commonality when they sit down to eat. Food scholar and Williams College professor Darra Goldstein says, “Quite apart from sustaining us, food has the ability to unite us.” She spoke at gastronomical lectures hosted earlier this autumn at the University of Massachusetts Amherst.

And there’s photographer Jeremy Dennis’s exhibition, “The Course of Liberty,” at a new alternative space in Salem, Massachusetts called Airtight Garage. The exhibition provides a corrective to years of simplified myth-making, and his staged photographic compositions feature Native Americans in traditional clothing and unexpected settings. It is as if they have broken through a time-space continuum.

Think back to the fabled tale of scraggly European Pilgrim outcasts arriving on the eastern Atlantic shore. As Florida columnist JC Bruce laughs, “If you were an indigenous person living on the North American continent in 1621 and a bunch of religious zealots showed up with pasty skin and ridiculous clothing, would you think they were any odder than beings from the stars?”

“The Course of Liberty” questions what happens when two people claim the same space. As Cate McQuaid describes in the Boston Globe, the artist points to ideas of inherited history and reconciliation as Native people attempt to recover Native land.

Yes, we may need a lot more communication breakthroughs and backdoor discussions of sustainability and equity in the lead up to the January shift of Congress. But it’s not the first time such a moment in time has occurred in the US.

In 1864 President Abraham Lincoln reflected on the civil war. He saw a nation now dedicated “to the cause of freedom and humanity, and to afford to us reasonable hopes of an ultimate and happy deliverance from all our dangers and afflictions.”

So let’s celebrate on this Thanksgiving Day 2026 the birthday of cartoonist Charles Shultz, who said, “The best theology is probably no theology; just love one another.” Let’s do our best to live up to his ideal; good things will surely result if we do.
