3 ways to calm your critical inner voice

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

My ride to work used to be a big red London bus. I’d hop on and head to Broadcasting House, the BBC headquarters near Oxford Circus. Along for the ride was my invisible companion: Negative Nancy.

She would pipe up: “Oh, off to Broadcasting House, are you? What are you going in there for? You don’t belong there.”

By this point I’d worked my way from a secretarial job to producer on a national personal finance show. I had every right to be proud of my success, but Nancy wouldn’t shut up.

She followed me into the office: “You look like you know what you’re doing, but you don’t.”

Then into the studio, where I’d be interviewing a politician: “Your questions aren’t very good.”

Thanks, Nancy. Any other helpful observations?

I was doing the job, but that commentary made it hard to enjoy anything I’d achieved.

We all have an inner voice. It’s the running conversation we have with ourselves, helping us make sense of the world without saying everything out loud. 

“It looks cold. Better take a coat.” Useful.

“You’re going to make a fool of yourself in that meeting.” Less useful.

When that voice turns negative, it can take up enormous headspace. Mine invalidated my hard work and success. It also thwarted my thoughts about career progression. At work, it can persuade perfectly capable people to keep an idea to themselves, avoid a difficult conversation, or let an opportunity pass. Self-doubt becomes the name of the game.

If you lead or support colleagues, you’ll recognize some of those behaviors. You may recognize the voice yourself.

ACE your self talk 

I use a simple acronym to help: ACE. Acknowledge, Choose, and Evidence. Three ways to turn down that negative commentary and make room for confident supporting voices.

1. Acknowledge the voice

First, I had to acknowledge there was in fact a voice inside my head. And acknowledge that the commentary, although familiar, wasn’t always factually accurate. 

Giving it a name helped me catch it in the act. “Oh, hello, Negative Nancy. You again.” You might have a Bring-Me-Down Brian or a Run-Me-Around Rita. Name yours whatever you like. 

The point is to recognize when that voice starts chipping away at your confidence.

Notice when it gets chatty, too. Before a meeting? When you’re trying something new? When an email from your boss arrives and you immediately think, “What have I done wrong?”

Once you are aware of it, you can start questioning it.

2. Choose not to listen

Nancy had plenty of opinions about my career, but I didn’t have to let her make the decisions.

“Thanks, Nancy, but I’m going into work now. I’ve got a show to produce. Get off the bus!”

It sounds a little ridiculous, having an argument with an imaginary passenger. But answering back helped me interrupt the commentary rather than let it run all day.

You can hear a doubtful thought and still choose to speak in the meeting, put yourself forward, or have that conversation. You don’t have to wait for Nancy’s permission.

And if the voice pops back up? Recognize it and choose to ignore it again. This takes practice.

3. Evidence: Remind yourself of the facts

Negative Nancy has a very selective memory. That awkward comment from five years ago? Perfect recall. All the good feedback since? Apparently, the files have gone missing.

So gather some evidence. What have you achieved? What have people trusted you to do? What experience are you bringing to this situation?

In my case, I’d worked my way up to producing a national radio show. I hadn’t wandered into Broadcasting House and found an unattended desk. People had employed me to do that job.

Keep a “brag book,” a notebook or folder where you save positive feedback, successful projects, and things you’re proud of. When your inner voice starts dismissing your ability, you’ll have something concrete to refer to.

You can help colleagues do this too. Remind them specifically what they did well and the difference it made. Give them something their Nancy will struggle to argue with.

Keep fighting the voice

I’m sorry to tell you this, but there’s no way to completely get rid of your mean inner monologue. Negative Nancy’s voice still occasionally pipes up for me—and I coach people on workplace confidence!

It’s in our nature. It kept us safe when we were cave dwellers (“Don’t leave the cave! There’s a saber-toothed tiger outside, you idiot!”) so it’s always going to be ready to pop up when we least want it to. 

But the more conscious we are of our pesky critical inner voice, the more we talk openly about it with our colleagues, the easier it becomes to question and quiet it.
