Zion Draws The Crowds—These 3 Nearby Parks Are Where to Escape Them

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Just beyond Zion lie some truly incredible desert landscapes to wander without the crowds. (Photo: Arrterra via Getty)

Entry queues and crowded trails come with the territory at bucket-list national parks like Zion. With nearly 5 million visitors in 2025, it ranked as the second-most visited national park in the U.S. Once you make it through the entry gate, though, the payoff is real. Visitors walk beneath towering Navajo sandstone cliffs and wade the Virgin River deep into The Narrows. If luck’s on your side, you might even win the permit lottery to hike to Angels Landing.

But I’ll be honest: On my recent trip to Zion, the trails felt more like a conga line than a wilderness escape. I was more paranoid than peaceful, constantly stepping aside and watching my footing as I squeezed past other hikers. The incredible beauty of the park draws equally incredible crowds.

The good news is you don’t have to fight for elbow room to soak in Zion’s beauty. Its neighboring state parks, wildernesses, and national forests get a fraction of the attention, but are just as jaw-dropping. Explore the surrounding Greater Zion region, and you’ll unlock hikes through red rock without the gridlock.

Head beyond Zion for these underrated hiking spots instead.

Distance from Zion: 1 hour (West) Entrance fee: $15 per vehicle Best for: Hikers seeking desert variety without a grueling trek. Petrified dunes, sandstone cliffs, slot canyons, and hikeable lava tubes, all in one park.

If Snow Canyon State Park were a DJ, it’d spin a killer geological mashup of 180-million-year-old petrified dunes, Jurassic-period sandstone escarpments, and ancient river-carved slot canyons. The drop: cavernous, jet-black lava tubes you can hike into (headlamp recommended). The park packs a huge punch of diverse terrain you rarely see all in one place. It’s especially family-friendly and a good fit for more casual hikers who want photo-worthy views without a grueling trek.

Catch the park’s greatest hits in one go by linking up the Butterfly Trail, Lava Flow Overlook, West Canyon Overlook, and the Petrified Dunes Loop. The roughly 3-mile loop connects the park’s most notable features. Hikers will see ancient lava flows, winding slickrock passages, and sweeping views down into West Canyon. The petrified dunes along the route steal the show. Hikers walk over rolling hills frozen in time while swirling stripes of orange, cream, and crimson dance underfoot.

Looking for more technical hiking? Tackle the trek to Deidre Peak. The 7.4-mile out-and-back climbs nearly 1,500 feet in elevation and requires a Class 3-4 scramble over rugged sandstone outcrops. The trail crosses into the larger Red Cliffs Desert Reserve, a conservation area protected as a sanctuary for the native Mojave desert tortoise.

Distance from Zion: 1 hour (West) Entrance fee: $5 per vehicle Best for: Hikers and BLM land lovers seeking seclusion and solitude.

Beyond Snow Canyon sprawls Red Cliffs National Conservation Area. The nearly 45,000 acres of BLM land sits within the larger Red Cliffs Desert Reserve at the crossroads of the Mojave Desert, Great Basin, and Colorado Plateau. Here you’ll find a mix of plants and wildlife from all three regions. There are two endangered wildflowers that grow only in this corner of Utah: the dwarf bear poppy and Shivwits milkvetch. Both bloom in spring, with peak overlap in late April and early May. Then there’s southern Utah’s classic red sandstone stretching as far as the eye can see.

Popular out-and-back hikes like the 3.5-mile Elephant Arch Trail and 1.6-mile Red Reef Trail show how water and wind erosion turn sandstone landscapes into works of art. Elephant Arch earned its name from a distinct sandstone outcropping that looks uncannily like a trunk, head, and ears. Along the Red Reef Trail, hikers skirt massive, smooth sandstone bowls. These turn into swimming holes during spring rain and monsoon season, which runds July through September.

The 5-mile Black Gulch and Yellow Knolls Loop drops through a boulder-strewn gulch of dark volcanic rock, then rolls over yellow sandstone knolls. Hikers can also link the Red Reef and Prospector Trails to create a 10.5-mile loop requiring some scrambling and route-finding through remote backcountry canyons. On the return leg, wide views open up to the Pine Valley Mountains in the north, often capped with snow in winter and early spring.

Distance from Zion: 1 hour 15 minutes (Northeast) Entrance fee: None Best for: Hikers looking for trail variety that showcases Utah’s finest terrain

The state’s largest national forest shows off a completely different side of southern Utah. Dixie National Forest sprawls across nearly 2 million acres, rising from the desert valley around St. George to peaks above 11,000 feet. Sagebrush, pinyon, and juniper give way to aspen groves and conifer forests as you climb. The higher and farther you hike, the more surprises you’ll find: trout-filled mountain lakes, towering hoodoo spires, and the chance to spot Utah prairie dogs in the wild.

Dixie gives hikers a taste of the same pink-and-orange Claron limestone that made Bryce Canyon famous. The difference? Here, thick stands of ponderosa pine grow right up against the hoodoos. Plus, the trails see a fraction of the foot traffic, so you’ll have the views practically to yourself.

If you want more hoodoo action, check out Thunder Mountain Trail. It winds through what feels like a bite-sized Bryce Canyon. The out-and-back trail climbs more than 1,700 feet over 8.6 miles. You’re rewarded with an unforgettable trek through a maze of spires. For an even more awe-inspiring experience, hike early or late in the day, when low sun turns the Claron limestone a vibrant orange.

Looking for a more Zion-esque experience? Ashdown Gorge Trail echoes the Narrows, minus the permits, rental gear, and crowds. It travels 10 miles from the alpine rim of Cedar Breaks to a creek-carved canyon where red rock walls close in overhead. Heads up: There are two popular ways to hike Ashdown Gorge. You can do an out-and-back that starts at the bottom and heads up into the narrows (recommended), or a point-to-point that requires a shuttle.

For a longer, relatively flat dayhike, opt for the 11-mile Navajo Lake Loop. The trail circles its namesake, peeking in and out of dense pine, fir, and aspen forest, with shoreline views that make it easy to forget you’re in a red rock wonderland
