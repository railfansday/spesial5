Google's Gmail MCP server can read everything and send nothing

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Posted on Nov 27 • Originally published at kireev.us

If you run product at a SaaS company that touches Gmail or Drive, there is a date on your calendar that you didn't put there: 1 February 2027. After it, anything that "is or is driven by an AI Agent" has to reach Google Workspace through Google's own MCP servers.

Then you open the Gmail server and find it can search your inbox and write drafts, but it cannot send, reply or forward. This letter is about why that's not a bug.

Here is what Google did on 24 November, as reported by TechStackups. It made its eight Workspace MCP servers generally available (Gmail, Drive, Docs, Sheets, Slides, Calendar, Chat, Tasks) and added a clause to its API terms. Software that is an agent, or is driven by one, goes through MCP from 1 February. Agents that keep calling the REST APIs get dropped to Standard-tier quotas.

A backup job or a CRM sync on its own OAuth client is untouched. The line is drawn around the word "agent," and the developer decides which side of it they stand on by ticking a box in the Google Cloud console: "Use this client for an AI-powered agent."

That sounds administrative. It is a product decision made for you. Since 11 November the MCP servers reject clients that haven't ticked the box, and clients created earlier and left unmarked stay non-agentic for good. If your product started life as a boring integration and grew an AI feature last spring, you may be running an agent on a client that Google has classified as something else.

Google isn't being sneaky about this. It said it out loud.

The interesting part is what it said, and what it left out.

The Gmail MCP server can search and read threads, create and list drafts, manage labels, and move things to trash or spam. It cannot send, reply, forward, or edit a draft after it has been created. Google's quota page says those tools "require partner access and aren't generally available". Drive is similar: no moving, renaming or sharing files.

Now run the thought experiment you'd run on your own roadmap. You are building the email assistant everyone wants. It triages, summarizes, drafts the reply. And then a human has to open Gmail, find your draft, and press send, because the one action that closes the loop is behind a door marked partners only.

Google hasn't said who gets partner access or when. TechStackups notes that Anthropic's own Claude connector already advertises sending and replying, which tells you the door opens for some people.

So the question isn't whether the restriction is reasonable. It might be. The question is who decides, and on what schedule, that your product is allowed to finish the job.

Google's case, per the Cloud blog post it cites, is that OAuth was built for human-paced apps and "a valid token is no longer enough" for something autonomous. A single gateway means admins get one place for policy, approval, auditing and data-loss prevention. Prompt injection is the nightmare scenario for an inbox agent, and a send button is exactly where it hurts. I'll concede all of that.

Here's the part the safety story doesn't cover. The same terms say Workspace data can only be used for active, user-authorized tasks and must be deleted when the user disconnects. Google can cap how long you keep embeddings, memory indexes and user profiles at 30 days. Training, fine-tuning, evals and distillation on the data are banned, and so is building a competing Workspace product with it.

Read that list again as a roadmap for a startup. Memory that persists across sessions is the feature that makes an assistant feel like it knows you. A 30-day cap on the index behind it is a design constraint you didn't choose. Then, from 1 April 2027, TechStackups says paid MCP usage starts billing from the first request, while REST keeps big free daily allowances. A Gmail search_threads call counts as ten of your 5,000 daily requests, so a project gets about 500 searches a day before it pays.

Together they say that the company that owns the inbox also owns the speed limit, the memory limit and the price of an assistant that lives in it. Safety can be true and also convenient.

Slack ran the earlier cut. In May 2025 it moved conversations.history for commercially distributed apps outside its Marketplace from Tier 3 to Tier 1: one request a minute, 15 messages per request. Marketplace apps kept their old limits. The pitch was protecting workspace data from unvetted apps, and the practical effect was that every serious vendor applied to the Marketplace.

Slack drew its line by distribution channel. Google is drawing it by what the software is. That is a harder line to game, and a harder one to appeal, because "is this an agent?" is a question that gets blurrier every quarter. Is a feature that auto-suggests a reply an agent? Is a rules engine with a language model inside it?

A product that sits on Gmail should stop assuming the answer will go its way. Ask Google in writing which side your client is on, while there are still ten weeks of runway.

The other side of this is the operating system, and it showed up the same week. On 20 November Meredith Whittaker published Signal's response to AI assistants built into phones: on iOS 27 the app blocks screenshots and adds a "Block Siri AI" setting, and on Android it detects an assistant trying to read the screen and switches on its screen-security mode by itself. Signal admits the fix is partial and doesn't cover the filesystem, and it asks Apple, Google and Microsoft for a documented way to opt an app out of AI access.

Put the two stories next to each other. Google tells apps that agents may only come through the front door it built. Signal tells Google and Apple that agents shouldn't get through the front door at all unless the app agrees. Both are right about their own data.

Both are arguing about who holds the key.

Your product is on the receiving end of one of those arguments, probably both. You are somebody's app, so an OS agent can read your screen. You are also somebody's agent, so a platform can decide what you may touch.

I'd build for the world where the send button stays locked longer than you want. That doesn't mean giving up. It means treating the human handoff as the product instead of the apology.

A draft that opens in one tap with the right thread, the right recipient and a diff of what changed beats a draft you have to go hunting for. A queue of five ready replies that takes ten seconds to clear is a real workflow, even when a person presses the final key. I'd also keep your own record of what the assistant knows, outside anything Google may make you delete in 30 days, and be honest in your UI about what gets forgotten and why.

And I'd decide now, as a product call and not an engineering afterthought, whether your assistant is an agent by Google's definition. Marking it as one buys you a supported path and a quota clock. Leaving it off buys you a clean conscience until a reviewer disagrees.

My bet is that by summer the question every Workspace-adjacent roadmap meeting asks isn't "what can the model do?" but "what is the platform going to let it finish?" If your Q1 plan has no answer to that, it's a plan for someone else's product.
