8 Best Hallway Simulator JRPGs

To those unaware, the term “Hallway Sim” for JRPGs was derived from Final Fantasy 13, one of the black sheep of the series. It describes games where exploration takes a backseat to linear level design as you spend most of your time running down narrow corridors, fighting enemies, and watching the occasional cutscene before moving on to the next hallway.

And while that might sound like a recipe for disaster to anyone who enjoys getting lost in sprawling worlds, there’s something oddly comforting about a JRPG that knows exactly where it wants you to go. Of course, FF13 isn't the only game guilty of this design philosophy, nor is it necessarily the worst offender. Many JRPGs have traded sprawling overworlds for a more focused, linear experience, and some are all the better for it if you ask me.

If you love JRPGs but get bogged down by open world activities, check out the these JRPGs for players tired of open world.

So, if you're tired of open-world RPGs that seem to eat up every waking hour of your life, these hallway-simulator JRPGs might be exactly what you need, so why not set the term's derogatory meaning aside for just this once, pretty please?

We’re at the racelines, so I figured we’d open with the Tales of series, a line of games where I’ll gladly rise from my comatose grave to proudly defend Berseria. But see, you’re lucky because we’re talking about Tales of Arise today. What makes Arise stand out as a hallway simulator is how much it accomplishes with a largely linear structure.

The journey with Dahna’s rebellious gang of misfits is filled with a multitude of hub areas across various regions, and although they feature a bit of verticality here and there, the hallway bit quite literally shows up in the final act of the game, as hilarious as that may sound to someone who played it fully.

But hey, at least the real-time combat adds plenty of excitement to these otherwise straightforward maps and progression routes. The game is still an absolute blast to play, so don’t let anyone convince you otherwise, because Alphen and Shionne's budding growth certainly deserve your attention.

Despite its modern-day Tokyo setting, Tokyo Xanadu eX+ is a great example of a hallway simulator JRPG because its world is much more linear than its urban backdrop initially suggests. Don’t get me wrong, the game still has plenty to enjoy in its social-sim aspect that’ll strongly remind you of the Persona series' DNA.

But the cracks start to show once you dive into the actual gameplay, partly because the dungeons are (mostly) long corridors with small rooms in between, packed with enemies, your usual (and basic) environmental puzzles, and a boss waiting at the end. That doesn't make Tokyo Xanadu eX+ a bad JRPG, though, but I can see the repetition setting in for anyone else when the game railroads you along on this high-school high-jinks adventure.

Now, I know Soul Hackers 2 got a hefty amount of slack upon release and well past the honeymoon period, to the point where it had a divided audience, but hey, at least I’m in the minority who loved what I've played so far. However, I’d be lying to you if I didn’t admit that its actual world exploration feels surprisingly restrictive. All the way down to the dungeon design.

And the biggest offender is its Soul Matrix, where almost every character-specific dungeon is essentially a collection of long, repetitive corridors with branching paths that occasionally lead to treasure chests, enemies, or locked doors.

Even the main story dungeons aren't particularly special; if I’m being blunt, they often feel more like elaborate indoor mazes than places you'd genuinely want to explore, which is unfortunate because, aside from that, Soul Hackers 2 is a very decent JRPG if given the proper time and attention, with an adoring cast of characters at the helm.

Not every JRPG received the spotlight it deserved, as demonstrated by these obscure games from famous developers.

I’ll admit up front here: the Wild Arms series has a serious cult following. The first three games gave you these sprawling overworlds, goofy puzzle dungeons, and a Wild West-meets-fantasy vibe that nothing else quite matched. Then Wild Arms 4 came along and decided the best way to tell a story was to put you in a corridor… and keep you there.

Oh right, it does have one thing doing a bit of the heavy lifting: the hex-grid battle system. It’s a genuinely interesting twist on the combat system of other JRPGs at that time and keeps regular fights from feeling like standard menu-fests. But even that can’t fully hide how little there is to do between battles, since the game largely funnels you from one encounter to the next, with little opportunity to wander off the beaten path.

Scarlet Nexus has one of the coolest combat systems I’ve played in an action RPG; quite underrated if you ask me, but I have to be honest: you spend most of your time walking down corridors for the gameplay portion at least. And by the time you reach the endgame, it honestly feels like one long walk between fights every single time.

Don’t get me wrong; it’s a pretty good game, but it's built almost entirely out of hallways, and every main mission follows the same pattern. You push forward through a narrow, linear path, hit a small arena where a few Others drop in, and clear it out with your melee might and psychokinesis (PK).

The Brain Map and Bond Episodes add some welcome character progression and downtime, but they don't change the fact that Scarlet Nexus is a remarkably linear experience. But hey, as long as you try both of its two main characters’ routes, I promise Scarlet Nexus will be worth your time and investment, given how much dumb fun it offers.

I thought we’d dabble into the retro scene and talk about Grandia 2, one of the most beloved JRPGs on the Dreamcast. The combat is some of the best the genre has ever produced, and Ryudo is a genuinely fun protagonist, a grumpy mercenary who’d rather be anywhere else. But if you ask me what the game feels like from hour to hour, I’d say it’s like walking through a very pretty, very long hallway with a really good fight system attached.

Let’s talk about the dungeons because the game is built almost entirely out of narrow, winding paths that lead from Point A to Point B, with the occasional fork that gives you a treasure chest as a consolation prize for turning left. And to be fair, this is a very common JRPG design philosophy, especially from that era, since plenty of games from that generation are built around a story-first, linear structure.

In the most complimentary way I can put it, Grandia 2 is a hallway simulator with a killer combat system, a charming cast, and one of the most memorable villains of its time, but… just don’t go in expecting a world to explore.

I might be bending the rules here, but forgive me because I needed a way to compartmentalize this list without having one less entry. But hey, I guess that means this is a safe space to rave about Stranger of Paradise. Let’s get one thing straight, obviously. This is technically a loot-driven action RPG with a job system, which is about as JRPG as it gets, but the world barely exists, so there’s no cozy little town for you to wander, no overworld to explore, no little NPCs telling you about their lost cat or dad that ran out to get groceries.

Instead, you pick a mission from a menu, stare at a loading screen, and get tossed into a hallway-esque dungeon. Not that it’s a killing point, because this is, on all fronts, another fantastic Team Ninja game that gives you plenty of build options to take down its trash-fodder foes and hard-hitting boss fights. And if you’ve got the complete edition, the DLCs are a worthwhile plus for anyone who wants second helpings out of the game’s combat system.

As much as I’d want to make you roll your eyes by going with FF13, I decided the less obvious top pick to sign off with here would be Final Fantasy VII Remake. With Revelation coming in the first half of 2027, it just felt right.

The first part of this remake trilogy takes the opening few hours of the original, the bit in Midgar that you used to blow through in an afternoon, and turns it into a full-length game. That’s an ambitious swing, but despite what some of the divided fanbase may tell you, it works splendidly to give its charming cast of characters a whole new sense of direction and relationship-building moments.

What's particularly noticeable is the contrast between Midgar's enormous scale and how little of it you're actually allowed to explore. Even when the game introduces side quests and optional activities in areas like the Sector 7 Slums or Wall Market, these locations are relatively small hubs connected by straightforward routes, not fully explorable districts.

It’s not a true hallway-sim JRPG, as the term suggests for its sister game in the series, but these corridor-esque maps leave little room for meaningful exploration or player-driven detours. Again, not a diss at the game since it’s a lovely time for both hardcore fans and newcomers.
