The Best Fall TV Shows Hurt So Good

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Dead wives, school shootings, hate crimes: Television over the past few months piled on the misery. The best series found a way through it.

By Daniel Fienberg, Angie Han November 19, 2026 6:45am

ANGIE HAN How has your fall been, Dan? Mine has been perfectly fine — good, even, considering the recent turn of the political tide to add to the seasonal pleasures of Halloween and pumpkin-spice novelty items.

The characters I’ve been watching, on the other hand? Oof. TV’s always had sad sacks, of course, but more than once in the past few months I’ve found myself more annoyed than moved by the sheer surfeit of trauma — not least because it so often feels like a shortcut to attempted profundity.

In lieu of a personality, Apple’s Nocturne gives its hero ex-cop (Liev Schreiber) a dead wife, a dead kid and a dead partner. Peacock’s The Good Daughter opens with a murder, then a school shooting, then continues piling on stabbings and sexual assaults like it’s trying to win the Trauma Olympics. Abuse justifies Lizzie Borden’s murders in Netflix’s Monster. Bullying catalyzes Carrie White’s powers in Carrie. Not even robots are immune. What is Disney+’s VisionQuest if not a really long series of therapy sessions unpacking grief? I’m not suggesting TV shy away from tragedy. One of the fall’s best shows, Starz’s British import Tip Toe, centers on a horrific anti-gay hate crime. But creator Russell T Davies also loves his characters enough to make them more than the sum of the terrible events that befall them, which makes the series both less dreary than you’d expect and even more gutting.

It’s just that at a certain point, the misery starts to feel less affecting and more … cheap. Or maybe I’m a Chris Hargensen.

DANIEL FIENBERG It’s like I say: “Save the drama for your mama, but save the trauma for fall TV.”

Yes, trauma has abounded on small screens of late. Childhood sexual assault motivated Cathy’s (Florence Pugh) ruthless rise to independence and power in Netflix’s East of Eden; negligent counselors motivated doting mom Pamela Voorhees’ (Linda Cardellini) rise to homicidal mania in Peacock’s Crystal Lake; a car accident leaves the protagonist (Sam Nivola) in Hulu’s Phony certain that his mom (Connie Britton) has been replaced by an impostor. Somehow even the absence of trauma could cause trauma — not that I want to spoil what shaped Maria Sten’s Frances Neagley into a law enforcement badass in Amazon’s eponymous Reacher spinoff.

Sometimes trauma is a slick shorthand to justify rage — usually female — and it can feel, as you note, cheap. But it doesn’t need to. Tip Toe tells you immediately where the story is going, and it’s a five-hour descent into tragedy and horror, but there are moments of joy along the way — Davies’ It’s a Sin worked similarly — and the performances by Alan Cumming and David Morrissey are sure to rank among the year’s best. East of Eden lets Zoe Kazan restore the biblical sprawl that her grandfather Elia trimmed from his 1955 feature adaptation of John Steinbeck’s novel while giving Pugh and Hoon Lee, as Chinese American manservant Lee, well-deserved showcases.

An interesting case study in how to use trauma as an instigating event without wallowing in misery is Apple’s Small Prophets, from writer-director Mackenzie Crook. The hero, played by a revelatory Pearce Quigley, has been lost since his girlfriend vanished seven years ago, but the show is about friendship (Lauren Patel plays the other half of a quirky duo), healing and the alchemic process necessary to birth the supernatural beings known as homunculi. The show never goes where you expect, casting a spell of droll oddness that won’t work for everyone but did for me.

HAN I think “wallowing” is key here. There’s a difference between a story that grapples with tragedy to expand our understanding of its characters and the world they inhabit and one that thinks a character’s sadness makes them inherently interesting; one that uses trauma as the start of a bigger, more nuanced conversation and one that treats it like the final word.

If you, like me, have spent too much time recently watching shows that do the latter, the good news is that the antidote is readily available. I’m talking about the second season of Netflix’s Inuit comedy North of North, which finds its deeper purpose in the sense of communion and culture that binds its characters. Lead actress — and ray of sunshine in human form — Anna Lambe sets the tone. Or Paramount+’s It Gets Worse, a British show that nails both the giddy delight and agonizing mortification of standing on the precipice of real adulthood. And while I don’t know if things will indeed get worse for those characters, the fantastic HBO comedy Youth suggests, with creator/star Sharon Horgan’s trademark honesty and hilarity, that the chaotic process of growing into oneself continues well into midlife.

It’s possible for a show to speak meaningfully to our upsetting times without descending into sorrow. Robert and Michelle King’s CBS procedural Cupertino runs on ripped-from-the-headlines scandals, but it feels good to watch the charming and competent cast of lawyers hold fast-moving tech companies accountable for all the stuff they’re breaking along the way.

FIENBERG Youth and It Gets Worse fall into the category of feel-bad feel-good TV because the mortification factor in both is so very high. I tried watching all of It Gets Worse in one sitting and, much as I appreciate Leo Reich’s off-kilter, upbeat sensibility, I needed a break from the cringe. Youth could almost be a counterpoint to It Gets Worse titled See, We Told You. Fortunately, Horgan is one of those people whom I would watch in anything — even if she inherently renders the show’s “How could anybody still find me attractive?” premise a bit disingenuous. Fortunately, there’s more to it than that.

Cupertino is almost the opposite. It’s feel-good feel-bad TV. It’s about the impending techno-apocalypse and encroaching horrors of AI, but unlike Robert and Michelle King’s angry The Good Fight and terror-filled Evil, Cupertino is sunny and funny. As pessimistic as those earlier Kings shows were, Cupertino is also semi-optimistic in its insistence that there are people out there (played, in this case, by Mike Colter and Rachel Keller) fighting, well, the good fight.

(Relative) optimism is one way a show with ostensibly bleak subject matter can avoid being a downer. You know what else works? Aggressive plot churn. Two of my favorite returning shows this fall, Netflix’s The Diplomat and Black Doves, are packed with murder and geopolitical disaster — but they’re so packed that no cataclysm feels permanent because the next one is just around the corner. That’s a heck of a way not to wallow.

But the best way to beat the blahs is sheer, unabashed joy, and you mentioned my favorite warm-and-fuzzy show, North of North. It’s so full of heart and awash in sentiment that you could imagine it wallowing in that, which is even worse than wallowing in torment. But the show is never treacly. It’s culturally rich and deliriously hopeful, and Lambe is the purest of pure TV stars. Other things making me smile this fall: the rapid-fire absurdity of NBC’s The Fall and Rise of Reggie Dinkins, the telegenic grouchiness of Paramount+’s Bill Murray reality show A Course Called Ireland and the goofy accents and familial bonds in Netflix’s squid-tastic Below. What’s helped you break out of traumatic ruts this fall?

HAN Ironically, HBO’s War! Forget the title; it’s a perfectly juicy legal drama from George Kay, the man who made a plane hijacking fun in Apple TV’s Hijack. It follows the battling sides of a high-profile divorce involving a billionaire tech mogul (Dominic West) who’s actually sane — which makes the series a fantasy on par with Amazon’s dutifully ongoing Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power or Paramount+’s lovely Avatar: Seven Havens.

I too have been giggling at the second season of Rise and Fall of Reggie Dinkins, which is coming into its own after a shaky series premiere in January. And I treasure the time I spend each week with the harried medical professionals and equally harried teachers of NBC’s St. Denis Medical and ABC’s Abbott Elementary, respectively; there’s something so comforting about a well-oiled comic ensemble.

FIENBERG I haven’t yet mentioned my favorite show of the fall: the second season of Sterlin Harjo’s The Lowdown, FX’s genre-defying Oklahoma-set shaggy dog mystery/comedy/slice-of-life. Emmy voters ignored the first season, so in addition to Ethan Hawke at his scruffy best, the series has added a rarely better Tommy Lee Jones, a perfectly cast Betty Gilpin, and more twists, turns and philosophical detours than ever. Critics received six episodes before the season started, and I’ve been hoarding the last two because in today’s TV landscape, you never know when it’s going to take three years to get a new season — or whether you’ll get a new season at all.

So leaving aside the winter TV that’s on the horizon — Amy Poehler’s series return! Another Pride and Prejudice with zombies, only this time without zombies! The end of Emily in Paris! — what I’m both looking forward to and dreading the most is the conclusion of a great season of a show I love. That’s my trauma, and it feels good.
