Golshifteh Farahani and Director Chabname Zariab on Anti-Taliban Film ‘When She Hears the Bells,’ Sold by Le Pacte

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

TALLINN, Estonia — The striking images of Kabul international airport stormed by thousands of Afghans and foreigners desperately trying to be evacuated by U.S. planes as Kabul fell into the hands of the Taliban, have stayed etched in many people’s minds.

For Afghan-born Paris-based writer and director Chabname Zariad, long-listed for an Oscar with her short film “Hizia,” the takeover of Afghanistan from the hard-line Islamist group, was a gut-wrenching experience that immediately triggered her desire to spotlight in a movie their authoritarian regime and especially crackdown on women. That film -“When She Hears the Bells” (“Les clochettes de Kaboul”) is now an intense thriller drama shepherded by France’s Alta Rocca Films and repped by Le Pacte, with star-actress Goldshifteh Farahani (“Pirates of the Caribbean: Dead Men Tell No Tales,” “Alpha”) in the title role. Co-producing partners take in Belgium’s Versus Production, Germany’s Starhaus Filmproduktion and Arte France Cinéma.Both Zariab and Farahani are in Tallinn for the film’s world premiere on Nov. 18 at the Black Nights Film Festival’s first feature competition.

The film, penned by the filmmaker with her sister Parande Zariab, is set in the Summer of 2021, as Kabul is on the verge of falling into the hands of the Taliban. We follow the young widow Leila (Farahani), who arrives in Kabul and starts working in a kitchen, set in a “Bacha Bazi” house.

The term (literally meaning playing with boys in Persian) refers to a controversial yet still practiced tradition in Central Asia, “particularly in conservative and religious countries,” notes Zariab, whereby young boys, dressed as women, are forced to dance in front of men, and are subject to all kind of abuses. In the film, Leila decides to help a group of Bacha Bazi boys flee their horrendous fate, as Kabul edges closer to collapse.

Speaking with Variety on the near eve of the film’s world premiere, the director says she returned to the topic of the Bacha Bazi – depicted in her 2015 short film “When You Hear the Bells” – not to further dig into the subject but to use it as a symbol of the state of a complex society under Taliban rule. “In reality, when you remove women from the public sphere, you also repress the desire for them which leaves room for all types of abuses. It becomes a sick society,” she notes.

To raise awareness on the topic and attract the largest audience possible, Zariab and her sister borrowed from genre codes. The helmer who referenced Tarik Saleh’s films and Saeed Roustayi’s Cannes-selected “Leila’s Brothers,” said once she had built the form, the substance fell into place.

Hiring French-based Iranian actress Farahani for the title role was “an obvious choice.”

“Ever since I had seen Golshifteh in Asghar Farhadi’s “About Elly,” I had followed her career and in Atiq Rahimi’s “The Patience Stone,” she spoke an impeccable authentic Afghan Persian. Then her activism for women’s rights also played a major part for me. Not only did she deliver an exceptional performance, but her professionalism on set was astonishing,” says the helmer who cast her against mostly Afghan theatre actors and non-professionals to keep it authentic.

Farahani on Her MotivationSharing with Variety why she boarded the project, Farahani says: “First of course, it was the powerful, well-written script and the humanitarian message behind it. The story needed to be told. Second, it was the incredible perseverance of Chabname who never gave up on me. And finally, it was that inner feeling telling me there was no way I could skip this one. As if it were simply part of what was written.”

About her character Leila, the actress, who was bestowed Locarno Festival’s prestigious Excellence Award, Davide Campari in 2025, says she was compelled by “her ability to adapt and change. It reflects something you see so much in this region: how most people specially women can evolve in ways you might never imagine when they are forced to survive.

“It also reveals the potential within every human being to be pushed to the edge and find absolute courage, driven by dignity and human values. From a frightened, lonely woman to a lioness who decides to change the destiny of these [Bacha Bazi] children, even at the risk of her own life.”

Farahani says working with children and first-time actors was both a challenge and a blessing in disguise. “When it works at its best, it becomes one of the most pleasant and rewarding parts of the job. They can become the most wonderful acting partners,” she says, citing in particular the impeccable performance from Fatima Tawakkuli who plays Leila’s co-worker and friend Mari, and newcomer Mojtaba Ahmadi, aka the elder Basha Bazi dancer Tamin.

Zariab says the initial plan was to shoot in Iraqi Kurdistan which bears resemblance to Afghanistan, but when bombing between Israel and Iran started in 2024, the movie eventually rolled in Morocco. To capture wide shots of Kabul, she asked a friend cinematographer in Afghanistan to fill in.

For her feature debut, the helmer is grateful for the support she got from her entire team, especially Farahani, cinematographer Pierre Cottereau (“Café de Flore”), and her first AD Benjamin Blanc.

On the production front, she cites French producers Lou Chicoteaux and Jean-Etienne Brat of Alta Rocca Films (behind her 2020 short “Hizia”), Arte Cinéma and Le Pacte –~in charge of sales and French distribution – as “truly exceptional partners who understood the urgency of this film and the significance of its subject matter.”“I’m well aware that I was fortunate to have such a substantial budget [€3.5 million or $3.8 million) for my debut, especially at a time when funding is tightening across the board,” concedes Zariab.

Chicoteaux and Brat also admit that “getting people on board this wild adventure” was not an easy ride. “Yet we had a director whose legitimacy was beyond question as she herself had fled her home country because of the Taliban. From the West, she watched her nation suffer and sink into despair and had to make this film,” Brat explains.

Ultimately for him, “beyond the Bacha Bazi phenomenon which serves as potent symbol for the deviations that can emerge in societies undergoing intense radicalisation,” “When She Hears the Bells “is at its core “the story of a woman who risks her own life to save children; She emerges as a true heroine.”Commenting on the pic’s resistance statement, Farahani adds: “Artists tell stories about these horrors and make people pay attention to what is happening in our world. I feel their work is more important than what politicians do.

“Artists create bridges, while politicians and extremists create separation and duality. Artists try to make people identify with one another, despite geographical and cultural differences, through universal human values: love, loyalty, integrity, etc.,” she says.

“People may be poor in regions and countries like Afghanistan, but that doesn’t mean they lack dignity or wisdom. Art helps polish people’s judgements, often shaped by propaganda, into something more human, nuanced and real,” she concludes.Following its run in Tallinn, “When She Hears the Bell” will compete at the Torino Film Festival.
