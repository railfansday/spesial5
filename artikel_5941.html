Follow the Leader: Democrats face party battle as divisions deepen

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

'Saturday in America' host Kayleigh McEnany joins 'Fox & Friends Weekend' to discuss rising Democratic animosity toward House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, the left's ideological divide after the midterm elections and more.

You can’t play follow the leader until you decide who you want to follow.

That’s the conundrum facing House and Senate Democrats. Not all House Democrats are on board with promoting House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries (D-N.Y.) to Speaker. And Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-Md.) is challenging Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer (D-N.Y.) for Majority Leader.

"As of today, my position is no because that's what my constituents and my community is saying," said Rep.-elect Angela Gonzales-Torres (D-Calif.) when asked if she backed Jeffries, "It's really about the process. We want a democratic process. And that's what we're really fighting for. It's not about one person."

It may not be about one person. But no one is running against Jeffries. That’s why some Jeffries loyalists believe that House Democrats should endorse him for the Speakership by acclimation on Wednesday.

House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries speaks during a news conference at the U.S. Capitol in Washington, D.C., US, on Friday, Oct. 3, 2025. (Graeme Sloan/Bloomberg via Getty Images)

Rep.-elect Jessica Killin (D-Colo.) flipped a district from red to blue and endorses Jeffries.

"He hasn't had the opportunity to work in the majority as a leader of our party yet and I want to give him that opportunity," said Killin.

Yet nearby in in the Centennial State, progressive Rep.-elect Melat Kiros (D-Colo.) isn’t in Jeffries camp.

"Not yet," said Kiros when asked by yours truly if she would vote for Jeffries. "Ultimately this is about making sure that there is a democratic process in lieu of there not being multiple candidates."

But reservations about Jeffries aren’t just coming from the left. Try the center. Rep.-elect Sam Forstag (D-MT) won a district in western Montana which President Trump carried by 11 points two years ago. Forstag.

"I haven't committed my vote to anybody," said Forstag. "I'll be voting for whoever's going to actually help us modernize the way this place works."

HAKEEM JEFFRIES FACES PROGRESSIVE HOLDOUTS BUT NO RIVAL AS HOUSE SPEAKER RACE TAKES SHAPE

Before voters elected her to Congress, Killin toiled for multiple House Democrats as a chief of staff. She understands what Members must synthesize before supporting someone for leadership.

"We have a wild and unwieldy freshman class. It's over 100 people. This is part of process," said Killin. "Each person needs to follow their own process on how we're electing leadership."

House Republicans settled their leadership team last week, tapping Rep. Jim Jordan (R-Ohio) to serve as Minority Leader next year over current House Majority Leader Steve Scalise (R-La.). Rep.-elect Chris Stigall (R-Mo.) may consider investing in tubs of popcorn as he watches leadership machinations across the aisle.

"They're going to have one heck of a fight on their hands deciding who the voice of their party going forward is going to be," predicted Stigall. "If Team Jeffries can somehow manage to wrangle and herd those cats, it'll be a miracle, frankly."

In the Senate, Van Hollen says midterm wins by Democrats in states which haven’t been in the Democratic column in years spurred his candidacy for Majority Leader.

"We understand that this will be a competition," said Van Hollen. "I think it's important to give Senate Democrats a choice. That's what we just gave voters all over the country. A choice. They chose change."

Both Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, D-N.Y., and Sen. Chris Van Hollen, D-Md., are confident they have the votes to lead Democrats, but Schumer, so far, has the lead in public endorsements.  (Finn Gomez/Getty Images ; Alex Brandon-Pool/Getty Images)

Schumer has led Senate Democrats for a decade. Van Hollen is nearly nine years younger than the 75-year-old Schumer — who turns 76 next week. Van Hollen has at least five senators or senators-elect who caucus with the Democrats who will support him. There are three senators who associate with the Democrats who won’t back Schumer as the next Majority Leader.

"I've been open that I'm not voting for Sen. Schumer," said Sen. Elissa Slotkin (D-Mich.).

The revolt over Jeffries and Schumer reveals fractures between moderates and progressives. Plus old and new. Even Schumer’s hometown mayor sidestepped an opportunity to endorse the Brooklyn Democrat.

"I care less about who it is and I care more about what they do with that power," said New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani (D).

That says, Schumer has vocal supporters — including an ally from the Empire State.

"This caucus is grateful for the hard work of Sen. Schumer. No one has put in the time and effort that anyone has put into this cycle than he did," said Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand (D-N.Y.). "That is why you will see, despite all the grumblings, enormous gratitude for the hard work he has done to get us to this position."

"Chris Van Hollen is a great colleague," said Sen. Richard Blumenthal (D-Conn.). "But I'm supporting Chuck Schumer."

But even one Democrat aligned with Schumer says what gets done outweighs who does it.

"I think no matter who is Leader, we're going to deliver on this agenda," said Sen. Ruben Gallego (D-Ariz.).

Arizona Sen. Ruben Gallego defended Democrats' efforts to extend Obamacare subsidies. (Go Nakamura/Reuters)

A small group of demonstrators cursed at Jeffries when he spoke at a Democrats’ conference in Colorado late last week. The protesters hollered about impeachment and called him a "f****** liar." Frication like that threatens to sidetrack Democrats.

"There’s really a fight for the soul of the Democratic Party. Are they going to embrace these radical ideas?" asked Sen. Dave McCormick (R-Pa.) on Fox.

Some incoming progressives contend there’s no internal strife.

"People try to say we're we're fractured as a party. In reality, we're all working on the same message," said Rep.-elect Donavan McKinney (D-Mich.).

One Democrat said the internecine arguments are natural at this stage with Democrats just capturing big majorities.

"I think a little bit of scrumming around inside the party is a good thing," said Rep.-elect Fred Wellman (D-Mo.) who defeated Rep. Ann Wagner (R-MO) in what had been an historically red district. "The wonderful thing about my party is we're more than happy to duke it out."

Rep.-elect Eric Jones (D-Calif.) quibbles with the terminology characterizing Democratic disagreements.

"I wouldn't go so far as to say ‘duke it out’ because those are fighting words," said Jones. "But there are definitely open conversations. They're pretty collegial. Pretty collaborative."

But real differences exist. Sen. John Fetterman (D-Pa.) says he opposes what he termed Van Hollen’s "strong, anti-Israel kinds of views."

"I can't really think of what Van Hollen is known for. But he became like a progressive darling. But I don't see that he has a path to be leader," said Fetterman on Fox.

Liberal Democrats believe Van Hollen is willing to enact progressive priorities – which may be controversial among centrist Democrats.

"Sen. Van Hollen has been a long-time supporter of DC and DC statehood," said Washington, DC’s new non-voting delegate to the House, Del.-elect Robert White (D-DC). "I would get rid of the filibuster in order to get DC statehood."

On Fox, Van Hollen said he wouldn’t end the filibuster "right out of the gate." But the Maryland Democrat added that the filibuster was an "undemocratic provision."

Specifics matter in politics. They’ll matter if there’s a move to change the filibuster. Obliterating the filibuster might excite some progressives. But other Democrats may recoil. The same with Medicare for all. Progressives may embrace such a vote. But moderate Democrats who flipped red districts could abhor that prospect.

Those are the things which Democratic leaders must figure out. And when they do, only then will we know who is willing to follow them.
