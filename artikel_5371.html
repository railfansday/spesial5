10 stock market debuts that rose, closed flat or fell on their first day

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 10 IPO first-day pops and flops, from VA Linux and Netscape to Facebook and Peloton, show how far a first trading day can move from the IPO price

An initial public offering has two prices that matter on day one. The first is the IPO price, which the company and its underwriting banks set shortly before trading begins. The second is the closing price at the end of the first session. That close comes after a full day in which public investors could trade the shares freely for the first time. The distance between those two numbers is what traders call a pop or a flop.

A large pop looks like a win, and for investors who received shares at the IPO price, it is. For the company, it can mean something else. Every share sold below what the market proved willing to pay is money the company did not collect. A flop sends the opposite signal. The price was set higher than public demand could support on the first day.

This list of IPO first-day pops and flops gathers 10 stock market debuts that show both outcomes. It is a selection, not a complete ranking. Every percentage is rounded and measured the same way, from the IPO price to the first-day close. Opening trades and intraday highs are left aside. Each debut is judged on that single measure.

The first six slides cover gains, ordered from the largest percentage move to the smallest within this group. They run from VA Linux Systems, whose December 1999 debut opens the list, to Netscape, which went public in August 1995. Between them sit The Globe.com, Beyond Meat, Airbnb $ABNB and Snowflake $SNOW.

The last four slides cover debuts that disappointed. Facebook $META comes first, because its shares closed almost exactly at the $38 IPO price in May 2012. That was a flat result rather than a loss. It still fell short of the expectations that surrounded the offering. Uber $UBER, Robinhood and Peloton follow, and each closed its first session below its IPO price.

The dates cluster in two periods. Three of the gains came between 1995 and 1999, during the run-up in internet stocks. Six of the remaining seven debuts took place between 2019 and 2021, with Facebook's 2012 offering the exception. Each slide stands on its own figures: the IPO price, the first-day close and the percent move between them.

VA Linux Systems priced its IPO at $30 a share on December 9, 1999, and closed its first day of trading at $239.25. That rise of about 698% is widely cited as the largest first-day gain for a U.S. IPO.

In dollar terms, each share added $209.25 in a single session. An investor who received stock at the offer price held shares worth nearly eight times their cost by the closing bell.

The company did not create Linux. It built and sold computer hardware, chiefly servers and workstations, configured to run the Linux operating system. Linux is open-source software, meaning its code is free to use, copy and modify.

Finnish programmer Linus Torvalds released the first Linux kernel in 1991. By the late 1990s, the system had become a common choice for running web servers. VA Linux sold machines built for that kind of work.

VA Linux was not the first Linux-focused company to go public that year. Red Hat, which distributed a commercial version of the Linux software, held its IPO in August 1999. VA Linux offered public investors a hardware route into the same open-source trend four months later.

The company traced its roots to VA Research, which Larry Augustin co-founded in 1993 while a graduate student at Stanford University. The business adopted the VA Linux Systems name before going public.

VA Linux listed on Nasdaq $NDAQ under the ticker LNUX. Nasdaq was the main listing venue for technology and internet companies of the era.

The debut came near the height of dot-com enthusiasm. The offering priced about three months before the Nasdaq Composite index reached its peak in March 2000.

The company later moved away from the hardware business that defined its IPO. It became VA Software in 2001 and SourceForge in 2007, taking the second name from the software development website it operated.

The Globe.com priced its initial public offering at $9 a share on November 13, 1998. The stock closed its first trading day at $63.50, a gain of about 606% over that offering price.

That close left each share $54.50 above the price set for the offering. Put another way, the stock ended its first session worth a little more than seven times its IPO price.

The company began on a college campus. Stephan Paternot and Todd Krizelman founded the site while they were students at Cornell University in Ithaca, New York.

The Globe.com was an online community. Members could build personal home pages, talk in chat rooms and post on message boards. Much of what appeared on the site came from its own users. That model was an early version of what later became known as social networking.

The business sold no physical product and no packaged software. What it offered advertisers and investors was an audience, gathered in one place by the content its members made themselves. Like many websites of that period, it earned revenue by selling advertising on the pages those members visited.

The 606% move is the second-largest percentage gain among the six pops in this selection. Only VA Linux Systems, which listed a little more than a year later, closed its first day further above its IPO price. Netscape, the oldest debut in the group, gained about 108% in August 1995. Netscape sold browser software, and its first-day move was a fraction of The Globe.com's.

The listing came in late 1998. That was more than a year before the Nasdaq $NDAQ Composite index reached its dot-com peak in March 2000. The Globe.com debut arrived while investor appetite for internet shares was still building toward that high point. In that market, a site whose main asset was user traffic closed its first day far above the price its own offering had set.

Beyond Meat priced its initial public offering at $25 a share on May 2, 2019, above its marketed range, and the stock closed its first trading day at $65.75. That was a gain of about 163% over the IPO price.

The plant-based meat maker listed on Nasdaq $NDAQ under the ticker BYND. An investor who received shares at the offering price held a $40.75 per-share gain by the close.

The company and its underwriters had marketed the offering to investors at a lower range. Setting the final price above it showed that orders exceeded what the original range had assumed. The first-day climb meant buyers who missed an allocation paid well above even that higher price in the open market.

Beyond Meat makes meat substitutes from plant ingredients rather than animal protein. Its best-known product is the Beyond Burger, a patty made mainly from pea protein and designed to cook and look like ground beef. The company sold it in the meat case of grocery stores, alongside conventional beef, rather than in a separate vegetarian aisle. It also sold plant-based links under the Beyond Sausage name.

Restaurant chains had added the product before the listing. Carl's Jr. began offering a burger made with a Beyond Meat patty in early 2019.

Ethan Brown founded the company in 2009, and it was based in El Segundo, California. Its private backers before the IPO included Bill Gates and Leonardo DiCaprio. Tyson Foods, one of the largest U.S. meat processors, had also invested and then sold its stake before the offering.

At the time of the debut, Beyond Meat's best-known rival, Impossible Foods, was privately held. Public stock investors could not buy shares in Impossible Foods directly.

Both companies were pushing into fast food that spring. In April 2019, Burger King began testing the Impossible Whopper in St. Louis, built on an Impossible Foods patty. Plant-based burgers from both companies were on mainstream chain menus in the weeks before the BYND shares began trading.

Airbnb $ABNB priced its IPO at $68 a share on December 10, 2020, and the stock closed its first trading day at $144.71. That was a gain of about 113%, more than double the offering price.

The $68 price came after Airbnb raised its target range for the offering. The shares listed on Nasdaq $NDAQ under the ticker ABNB.

Airbnb went public during the COVID-19 pandemic, at a time when travel demand had collapsed. Border closures, lockdowns and quarantine rules cut into the short-term stays at the center of its business.

The company made cuts of its own that year. In May 2020, co-founder and CEO Brian Chesky announced that Airbnb would lay off about 1,900 employees, roughly a quarter of its staff. In the same message, Chesky said Airbnb expected its 2020 revenue to come in at less than half of its 2019 total.

The listing plan predated the crisis. Airbnb said in September 2019 that it expected to become a public company in 2020. It filed its registration statement publicly with the Securities and Exchange Commission in November 2020, weeks before the shares began trading.

Airbnb was founded in San Francisco in 2008 by Brian Chesky, Joe Gebbia and Nathan Blecharczyk. The idea began in 2007, when Chesky and Gebbia rented air mattresses in their apartment to visitors in town for a design conference. The early name, AirBed & Breakfast, came from that setup. The startup went through the Y Combinator accelerator in early 2009.

The company runs an online marketplace rather than a hotel chain. Hosts list homes, apartments and private rooms, and guests book them through the platform. Airbnb earns revenue from service fees on those bookings. It does not own the properties listed on its site.

Airbnb's debut came one day after another large consumer-tech listing. DoorDash, the food delivery company, began trading on the New York Stock Exchange on December 9, 2020.

Snowflake $SNOW priced its IPO at $120 a share on September 16, 2020, and closed its first trading day at $253.93. That was a gain of about 112%, or $133.93 per share above the offering price.

The offering raised about $3.4 billion. At the time, that made it the largest software IPO. The shares listed on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker SNOW.

Snowflake sells a cloud-based data platform. Companies use it to store large volumes of business data and to run queries and analysis on that data. The service runs on the major public clouds, including Amazon $AMZN Web Services, Microsoft $MSFT Azure and Google $GOOGL Cloud, rather than on hardware Snowflake owns.

That setup also puts Snowflake in competition with its hosts. Amazon, Microsoft and Google each sell their own data warehouse services, such as Amazon Redshift and Google BigQuery.

A core part of Snowflake's design separates data storage from computing power. Customers can scale each one up or down on its own. Snowflake charges largely by consumption. Customers pay for the computing they use instead of buying a fixed software license.

The company was founded in 2012 by Benoit Dageville, Thierry Cruanes and Marcin Żukowski. Dageville and Cruanes had previously worked at Oracle $ORCL, the database company. Frank Slootman was chief executive at the time of the listing. He had earlier run Data Domain, a data storage company, and ServiceNow $NOW, a maker of IT workflow software. Both of those companies went public while he was in charge, Data Domain in 2007 and ServiceNow in 2012.

Two outside investors were tied to the offering. Berkshire Hathaway $BRK.B, the conglomerate run by Warren Buffett, bought shares in a private placement at the IPO price. That meant Berkshire paid $120 a share, the same price set for the public offering, rather than the first-day closing price.

Berkshire seldom invests in newly public companies, and its purchase drew wide attention for that reason. Salesforce $CRM, the customer relationship management software company, also invested in Snowflake.

Netscape Communications priced its shares at $28 on August 9, 1995, and closed its first trading day at $58.25. That close left the stock about 108% above its offering price.

The $28 price was a revision. The company had first planned to sell shares at $14, then doubled the price before the stock began trading. Netscape listed on Nasdaq $NDAQ under the ticker NSCP.

Netscape made Navigator, a web browser. Navigator gave ordinary computer users a point-and-click way to view pages on the World Wide Web. In 1995, it was the product most people linked to the company's name.

Marc Andreessen was a co-founder. Before Netscape, he co-wrote Mosaic, an early graphical browser built at the National Center for Supercomputing Applications at the University of Illinois. His co-founder was Jim Clark, who had earlier founded Silicon Graphics.

Netscape was founded in 1994 under the name Mosaic Communications Corp. It took the Netscape name later that year after a dispute with the University of Illinois over the Mosaic name. The company was based in Mountain View, California.

Netscape had not turned a profit when it went public. Investors were buying into a young company whose value rested on the growth of web use rather than on earnings.

The Netscape IPO is often cited as the start of the dot-com boom.

Microsoft $MSFT released the first version of Internet Explorer later in August 1995. The rivalry between Navigator and Internet Explorer became known as the browser wars. In 1998, the U.S. Justice Department filed an antitrust case against Microsoft. The case focused in part on how Microsoft bundled Internet Explorer with Windows.

Netscape released Navigator's source code in 1998, and that project became Mozilla. The Mozilla project later produced the Firefox browser. America Online agreed to buy Netscape in November 1998. The deal closed in 1999, ending Netscape's run as an independent public company.

Facebook $META priced its IPO at $38 a share on May 18, 2012, and closed its first trading day at $38.23. That was a gain of about 0.6%, or 23 cents a share above the offering price.

The offering raised about $16 billion. At $38 a share, the social network carried a valuation of roughly $104 billion. Morgan Stanley $MS led the group of banks that underwrote the deal.

Mark Zuckerberg started Facebook in 2004 with several of his Harvard classmates. The company filed for its IPO in February 2012. It went public eight years after its founding. Its shares listed on Nasdaq $NDAQ under the ticker FB.

Zuckerberg rang Nasdaq's opening bell from Facebook's headquarters in Menlo Park, California, rather than from the exchange in New York. The ceremony went ahead on schedule. The trading did not.

Trading in Facebook shares had been scheduled to begin at 11 a.m. Eastern time. Technical problems at Nasdaq delayed the start by about half an hour. The same problems left some brokers and investors waiting to learn whether their orders had been filled, and at what price.

The episode drew a regulatory response. In May 2013, the Securities and Exchange Commission charged Nasdaq over its handling of the Facebook offering. The exchange agreed to pay a $10 million penalty to settle the charges.

Buyers who received shares at $38 did not lose money on the first day. The debut is still described as a flop because the shares delivered almost none of the first-day gain that investors had expected.

For Facebook, the flat close meant it sold its shares at close to the price public buyers were willing to pay that day. For investors who bought at the offering price, it meant almost no immediate return.

The company has since changed its name. Facebook's parent became Meta Platforms in October 2021. Its stock ticker switched from FB to META in 2022.

Uber $UBER Technologies priced its IPO at $45 a share on May 10, 2019, and closed its first trading day at $41.57. That left the stock down about 7.6% from its offering price.

The gap worked out to $3.43 a share. An investor who bought at the IPO price and held through the close had a paper loss on each share.

The offering raised about $8.1 billion. The shares trade on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker UBER.

Garrett Camp and Travis Kalanick founded Uber in San Francisco in 2009. It began as a service for booking black cars through a smartphone app. It later added lower-cost ride-hailing, food delivery through Uber Eats and freight brokerage through Uber Freight. By the time of the IPO, it operated in dozens of countries.

The company stayed private for about a decade before listing. In that time it raised money through a long run of private funding rounds. Its backers included the venture firm Benchmark, Japan's SoftBank and Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund.

Many early investors had bought shares years before the listing. The IPO gave them a public market in which those shares could eventually be sold. Public buyers on May 10 came in at a price set after years of private fundraising.

Leadership had changed before the listing. Kalanick resigned as chief executive in 2017. Dara Khosrowshahi, who had run Expedia $EXPE, became CEO that year and led Uber through the IPO.

Ride-hailing already had a public reference point. Lyft $LYFT, Uber's main U.S. rival, went public on Nasdaq $NDAQ in March 2019, weeks before Uber. Uber's own debut gave investors a second listed ride-hailing stock to weigh.

Drivers also drew attention to the offering. On May 8, 2019, two days before the IPO, drivers in several U.S. cities and other countries staged protests over pay and working conditions.

Robinhood Markets priced its IPO at $38 a share on July 29, 2021, and closed its first trading day at $34.82. That left the stock down about 8.4% from its offering price.

Each share finished $3.18 below where it started. Robinhood had marketed the deal to investors in a range of $38 to $42 a share. The final price landed at the bottom of that range.

Robinhood listed on Nasdaq $NDAQ under the ticker HOOD. The company runs a brokerage app built around commission-free trading of stocks, options and cryptocurrency. Vlad Tenev and Baiju Bhatt co-founded the company, which is based in Menlo Park, California.

The offering departed from a common IPO practice. Most new shares in a typical deal go to institutional investors such as mutual funds and hedge funds. Robinhood set aside part of its offering for its own retail customers. They could request shares through the app before trading began.

That structure matched the company's pitch to small investors. It also meant that some of the buyers at the offering price were individual users of the app. Those who bought at $38 and held through the close ended the session with a paper loss.

The listing came about six months after the January 2021 surge in GameStop $GME and other meme stocks. During that episode, Robinhood temporarily restricted purchases of several heavily traded stocks. The move drew complaints from users. Tenev testified about it before the House Financial Services Committee on February 18, 2021.

Robinhood's revenue model also faced scrutiny before the IPO. Much of its income came from payment for order flow. Under that arrangement, market makers pay a broker to route its customers' trades to them. In December 2020, Robinhood agreed to pay $65 million to settle Securities and Exchange Commission charges. The charges centered on how Robinhood had described those payments to customers.

Peloton priced its IPO at $29 a share on September 26, 2019, and closed its first day of trading at $25.76. That put the stock down about 11% from its offering price in its Nasdaq $NDAQ debut.

The shares finished the session $3.24 below the price set for the offering. Investors who bought at $29 ended the day holding stock worth less than they paid.

On Wall Street, a debut that closes under its offering price is known as a broken IPO. Peloton's listing fit that description at the end of its first session.

The company traded under the ticker PTON. Its business combined two things: connected exercise bikes and subscription classes streamed to those machines. That mix made Peloton part equipment maker and part media company.

Each bike carried a touchscreen that played instructor-led cycling sessions. Riders could join a class live or choose from a library of recorded workouts. An on-screen leaderboard let riders see how their output compared with others in the same class.

Owners paid a monthly fee for access to that class library, on top of the upfront cost of the equipment. The fee gave Peloton recurring revenue alongside its hardware sales.

John Foley, a former Barnes & Noble executive, co-founded Peloton in 2012. The company was based in New York City and filmed many of its classes in its own studios there. Before it had a product on the market, Peloton raised early money for its first bike through a Kickstarter crowdfunding campaign in 2013.

By the time of the IPO, the product line reached beyond cycling to a treadmill called the Tread. It used the same streamed-class format as the bike.

Peloton also sold a digital membership through its app for people who did not own its equipment. App users could stream classes such as strength training, yoga and meditation on phones and other devices.
