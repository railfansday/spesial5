Top Sports Ranked By Average Daily Calorie Intake

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Every sport requires a different level of maintenance in terms of nutrition; some are endurance sports, some are skill-based and others combine some combination of the two which ensures calorie intake varies widely.

At the top end of elite sport, the finest details can have the biggest impacts and what goes into an elite athlete's diet has developed into an extreme science the most demanding sports require an exceptional amount of calories per-day for maintenance and training and for match/tournament days.

However, there is a clear trend as to the higher forms on this list — it's centered around power and stamina, which should give away clues as to what will feature. Other factors come into play, such as refuelling mid-race/competition, sodium levels and much more.

Therefore, we've scouted and collated the top 10 calorific-heavy sports, ranked by daily calories consumed which, when compared to the calorie count of the average individual (2,500 kcal for men, 2,000 for women), goes to show what the body needs to perform at the elite level.

Six Premier League first-team players were tracked over a week of doubly labelled water by Liverpool John Moores researchers. They burned 3,566 calories a day and ate 3,186–3,789 on match days, 2,956 on training days.

It all hinges on match schedules, body makeup, position and load. Typically, footballers remain lean and light, boasting few bulging muscles in favour of agility, speed and constant running, which ensures they eat good carbohydrates, proteins and healthy fats — something which is attainable or even managed by healthy, regular gym-going individuals.

Lionel Messi is indisputably the GOAT when it comes to football, winning titles and breaking records wherever he goes, but how does he do it?

An Ironman race (a long-distance triathlon) is the most extreme mismatch in sport. A study of 18 Ironman athletes measured men burning 10,036 calories over the race and taking on 3,940 — finishing roughly 7,000 in the red on the day.

Since the gut cannot absorb anywhere near as much as the legs expend, it is an incredibly difficult task to manage especially since they also have to balance sodium intake too. Kristian Blummenfelt, the Olympic and Ironman champion, burns 7,000–8,500 a day in training and eats 4,900–6,400.

Thirty-one national-team skiers, weighed and recorded under supervision, ate 4,862 calories on a training day and 4,304 on a sprint competition day — the men's figures, from a 2019 study in the International Journal of Sport Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism.

Per hour really puts the sport into perspective, as skiers have to eat between 500 and 1,000+ calories per hour. It also varies across the type of flat terrain (500 – 650 calories per hour), hill/skate skiing (700 – 900 calories and racing (1,000–1,300).

Jordan Mazur, the 49ers' coordinator of nutrition, puts lineman requirements at 3,500 to 6,000 calories a day depending on whether a player is holding weight or building it. It varies due to a player's position, body size, and whether they need to build, lose, or maintain mass, with Joe Thomas revealing the amount of calories he had to eat as an offensive lineman.

Requirements are different for NFL skill players (wide receivers, defensive backs, quarterbacks), big skill players (linebackers, tight ends) and linemen (offensive and defensive linemen), with a high focus on carbohydrates and high protein.

This is the highest genuinely measured single-day intake on the list. During the 2025 Western States 100, a world-class runner finishing third in 14 hours 19 minutes took on 6,720 calories mid-race — while burning 16,104, confirmed by and published in the Journal of Applied Physiology.

He finished nearly 10,000 calories down. Whilst it is less dramatic than others on this list, the constant flow of movement over a long period of time drives up the calorie figures. Elite runners target 60g to 90g (and up to 120g with advanced gut training) of carbohydrates per hour using gels, chews, or sports drinks, as well as sodium-focused supplements and drinks to avoid hyponatremia, which leads to illness, passing out and long-term organ issues.

Heavyweight rowing has the highest routine training demand of any Olympic sport. A 2022 study of 14 elite open-class men put their requirement at 6,775 calories a day in a high-volume week, dropping to 4,626 in a taper.

Intake reportedly tracks it — around 6,970 a day for a national squad at peak volume, per two national-team dietitians writing in Current Sports Medicine Reports, though they label that data unpublished. Individual elite training sessions, roughly twice a day of intense on-water rowing, running, and heavy weightlifting, can burn up to 5,000 calories per session block alone.

The foundational study is still Wim Saris's 1989 work at Maastricht, which followed five riders through the Tour de France using doubly labelled water. They averaged 24.7 MJ eaten against 25.4 MJ burned — roughly 5,900 and 6,070 calories a day, sustained for three weeks. Mountain stages push the peak towards 9,000.

It also varies across different parts of races. There's flat/sprint stages (estimated 4,000 to 5,000 calories burned), mountain/hard stages (7,000 to 9,000+) and in-race fueling to consider as well, that amount to 400–500 calories/hour via gels, carb-mix drinks and rice cakes.

Professional sumo wrestlers are famously extremely powerful, and large. The amount of mass and force needed to succeed at the top level is remarkable, and the bulk comes from structure, not volume — and a consistent dose of the famous Japanese stew, chankonabe.

Reports describe them as being in a place of healthy obesity, with caloric intake ranging from approximately 5,000 to 8,000 kcal—and oftentimes exceeding 10,000. They burn incredible amounts due to their intense keiko (training), which can last up to six hours and begins early in the morning, and they typically skip breakfast, train in a fasted state, and then consuming a massive, nutrient-dense meal at midday—allowing for a metabolic cycle that promotes efficient weight gain while supporting muscle development.

Michael Phelps is the reason everyone thinks they know this number, and the number everyone knows is wrong. The 12,000-calorie breakfast that circulated during Beijing was never his figure. He corrected it himself in an interview with Olympics media: "It's just not true. Maybe eight to ten thousand calories per day."

Even corrected, it is enormous — the product of five to six hours in the water, six days a week, in a medium that strips body heat faster than air. Swimming's demand is relentless rather than explosive, and it runs year-round, ensuring that calories are high but the metabolic and muscular workrate ensure they are one of the most muscular-bound athletes out there.

Usain Bolt was in no doubt when naming the true Olympic GOAT between himself and legendary swimmer Michael Phelps.

The number at the top of this list is also the softest. Brian Shaw, four-time World's Strongest Man, walked Men's Journal through a seven-meal day totalling 12,019 calories — 705g of protein, 1,402g of carbohydrate — eaten across roughly 13 hours. "It's constant," he said. "All day long I have to eat, but it's part of the job."

Everything here is self-reported by men with an interest in the legend. Treat it as the ceiling of what's claimed, not what's proven. Eddie Hall, the former World's Strongest Man, claimed he would devour over '12,000 calories a day, sometimes up to 15,000', which perfectly rounds off this list.
