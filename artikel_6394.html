The mystery of long COVID: What lasting health problems does the virus cause?

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

A massive study shows how comparison groups shape findings about long COVID and which lasting health problems are linked to the virus.

Long COVID can leave people feeling unwell long after an infection. But figuring out which health problems the virus caused depends partly on who researchers compare those patients with.

A Stanford University team found just how much that choice matters.

When the researchers repeated an approach used in earlier studies, it linked COVID to unlikely problems, including injuries from lightning, drowning, and electrocution.

Changing the comparison group produced a much shorter list of lasting effects. By the second year, none met the study’s cutoff, though that does not mean every patient had recovered.

The team drew on 14.4 billion insurance claims from nearly 245 million US patients.

These are bills from doctors, hospitals, and pharmacies, so they record medical care rather than how someone feels each day.

Between about four months and a year after infection, the revised approach linked 98% fewer health problems to COVID than the older method did using the same records.

Jure Leskovec, a Stanford computer science professor, was one of the study’s senior authors.

“This doesn’t mean long COVID is 98 percent less common. It means that how we design these studies fundamentally changes which health effects we can reliably attribute to COVID,” Leskovec said.

Many earlier studies compared people who tested positive with people who had no recorded COVID diagnosis. That second group included people who tested negative and people who never took a test.

Those groups may differ before COVID enters the picture. Someone might get tested during a visit for diabetes or another illness, or because they see doctors more often.

“If you compare people who tested positive for COVID with people who were never tested, you risk attributing differences in their underlying health and healthcare-seeking behavior to the virus itself,” Leskovec said.

The concern is that researchers could end up blaming COVID for problems linked to someone’s existing health or reasons for seeking care.

The team compared people who tested negative and never had COVID recorded with untested people who had similar medical histories.

Between one and four months later, 82.5% of the health problems tracked were at least 10% more common in the group that tested negative.

A negative test could not explain those extra problems; the researchers pointed to other factors associated with getting tested.

For the main analysis, follow-up began with each patient’s first PCR test between March 1, 2020, and March 1, 2021.

The team compared positive results only with negative results, so everyone had a reason to get tested.

The researchers then adjusted the comparison group to match the positive group’s medical history over the previous four years.

They also matched age, sex, and neighborhood measures such as income.

This matching approach is called a synthetic control. Co-senior author Stefan Wager, an associate professor at Stanford Graduate School of Business, said economists commonly use it.

The records came from Komodo Health, which gathers claims from insurers, pharmacies, and programs including Medicare and Medicaid.

The researchers followed patients for up to two years and examined 614 health outcomes.

“Even with 244 million patients, you can get highly precise answers to the wrong question. Study design matters as much as data size,” Leskovec said.

As an initial check, the revised method picked up familiar COVID symptoms during the first month of infection.

Hamed Nilforoshan, a Stanford computer science doctoral student and one of three first authors, also recreated the older approach, closely following a 2021 Nature study. “Something wasn’t adding up,” Nilforoshan said.

The team checked 49 outcomes that COVID could not plausibly cause, including firearm injuries and birth defects recorded in adults. The older approach linked COVID to more than half.

It suggested a 76% increase in a category of injuries that included lightning, drowning, and electrocution. It also suggested an 18.6% increase in adults receiving diagnoses for birth defects of the eye, ear, face, or neck.

The authors said chance or flaws in study design presumably explained those unlikely links.

The revised approach flagged just two of the 49 outcomes, both showing lower risk after a positive test, which suggests it may slightly underestimate some real effects.

For the main results, a health problem had to increase by at least 10% and pass a strict statistical check that accounted for hundreds of comparisons.

From day 30 to day 119, the revised method linked 43 health problems to COVID, compared with 262 under the older method.

From about four months to a year, the revised method found five, compared with 286 under the older method. In the second year, those counts were zero and 369.

On average, recorded health problems in the positive group fell to roughly the comparison group’s level within a year. Earlier US veterans studies had found harmful effects after two or three years.

During the first months, COVID remained linked to problems involving the lungs, heart and blood vessels, kidneys, immune system, muscles, gut, and nervous system. Hair loss also appeared.

The biggest relative increase involved myopathy, a muscle disease, with related insurance claims about three times as common after a positive test.

Hair loss claims were nearly twice as common, while palpitations, malnutrition, and fatigue were 12% to 21% more common.

Between about four months and a year, five problems still met the cutoff: myopathy, hair loss, two categories of blood clots in the lungs, and respiratory failure.

Pneumonia, shortness of breath, chest pain, and fatigue increased by less than 10%. Even small increases in such common problems can affect many people.

Steven Goodman, a professor in Stanford Medicine’s Department of Epidemiology and Population Health, compared the findings with illnesses that linger after other infections.

“These findings indicate that long COVID may be more similar to other extended post-viral syndromes than many have thought,” Goodman said.

Still, billing records can miss symptoms such as fatigue and brain fog.

The study also covered people who sought PCR testing through healthcare, leaving out infections identified only through home tests.

About 20% of the comparison group caught COVID within two years, which could make the virus’s effects look smaller. The study could not establish what caused symptoms that lasted beyond a year.

The authors stressed that lasting symptoms are real. Other illnesses that prompted the original test, or other factors, could help explain them, but these records cannot settle that question.

“These results should complement physicians’ clinical assessments, not replace them,” Leskovec said.

The team called for research into the biology of long COVID and biomarkers, measurable signs in the body that could help identify it.

They also suggested repeating earlier studies with comparison groups based on testing to see how much their results change.

The study is published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
