Fossils found in Thailand reveal a giant rodent that weighed about 66 pounds

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Two fossil teeth from Thailand reveal an ancient rodent estimated to weigh 66 pounds, offering new evidence of how early rodents grew large.

Most rodents are small or medium-sized, but fossils reveal that some ancient species grew much larger.

Scientists found a fossil tooth in a coal mine in southern Thailand that belonged to an unusually large rodent.

The researchers believe it weighed about 30 kilograms (66 pounds). They named it Yakamys sirindhornae.

Sixty-six pounds is about what a large North American beaver weighs. Yakamys lived about 34 million years ago. The giant rodents of South America are the largest ever found but they came long after it.

The find suggests that rodents grew large earlier than fossils had shown. It also suggests they did so in more parts of the world. For now, the evidence is two teeth.

Both teeth were found in a layer of clay inside the main coal seam of a mine in Krabi Province. They were collected by a Thai-French fossil expedition.

The project was designed by Yaowalak Chaimanee of the University of Poitiers in France.

The co-authors work in France and Canada and at Thailand’s Department of Mineral Resources. The fossils are now held by that department.

One tooth is a lower molar, which is a grinding tooth from the back of the jaw. The other is a broken piece of a lower incisor. Incisors are the long front teeth a rodent gnaws with.

The clay is roughly 35.1 million to 33.7 million years old. That age is based on other mammals found at the site and on magnetism preserved in the rock.

In the name Yakamys, the word yak is Thai for giant and mys is Greek for mouse or rat.

The species name honors Princess Maha Chakri Sirindhorn for her long support of science and fossil research in Thailand.

The molar is 0.46 inch (11.56 millimeters) long and almost as wide. That sounds small. It isn’t small next to the teeth of its relatives.

Yakamys belongs to Ischyromyidae, one of the earliest major families of rodents. Its molar is the largest yet described among early members of that family.

Until now, an animal called Hulgana was regarded as the largest Asian member of the family. Its molars are less than half as long and less than half as wide.

The closest in size is Manitsha tanka. This rodent was described from South Dakota in 1941. Its lower second molar measures about 0.32 by 0.35 inch (8.2 by 8.9 millimeters).

The incisor was cut lengthwise by the team to place Yakamys in that family. Its enamel was then examined under an electron microscope.

The banding inside the enamel is consistent with the pattern reported for other members of the family.

No bones of Yakamys have been found and so its weight had to be estimated from tooth measurements.

Bigger teeth go with heavier bodies in living rodents and this relationship has been turned into equations.

Virginie Millien is a biologist at McGill University in Canada. She applied seven published equations to the two teeth. The answers ranged from 47 to 104 pounds (21.4 to 47.3 kilograms).

The seven answers averaged 70 pounds (31.6 kilograms) and the middle value was 63 pounds (28.6 kilograms).

The team took the midpoint of those two figures and rounded it to 30 kilograms, or 66 pounds.

The weight reported by the team is therefore a rounded summary of answers that disagree. Millien called it “only a ballpark figure.”

Estimates of this kind are uncertain because the link between tooth size and body weight varies among living rodents. The estimates span roughly 24 to 152 pounds (10.8 to 69 kilograms) when each margin of error is included.

The weights of giant rodents have been revised sharply before. One South American giant was first estimated at more than 2,200 pounds (1,000 kilograms).

Later studies that included one by Millien in 2008 estimated about 770 pounds (350 kilograms).

Rodents include more species than any other group of living mammals. Their oldest fossils come from North America. Very large body size has evolved in only a few branches of the group.

The best-known giants lived in South America long after Yakamys. Large species were rarely documented among early rodents and Manitsha was one of the few examples.

The fossil record of the family in Asia was sparse. Most of it came from small animals in China and India.

Yakamys extends the known range of the family into Southeast Asia. It is also evidence that one early rodent line had already grown large by about 34 million years ago.

Yakamys and Manitsha are similar in estimated weight but their molars are built differently. To the authors, that suggests large size in this family wasn’t tied to one tooth design.

Giant rodents are commonly linked to life on islands. That explanation is hard to apply to Yakamys.

The authors wrote that the ancient geography of the Krabi area doesn’t point to an island. Neither do the other mammals found there.

The clay that held the teeth formed in a lake. Many other fossil mammals have been found in the coal around it.

One possibility was raised by the team. Yakamys may have lived along lake edges much as capybaras live near water today. Food there may have been plentiful.

But lake clay alone doesn’t show that an animal spent time in the water.

More complete fossils that include skull and skeleton bones would be needed to test the idea. More evidence from the rocks and the other animals at the site would be needed too.

Two teeth aren’t enough to establish exactly where Yakamys belongs within its family. The researchers could not run a formal analysis to identify its closest relatives.

Even the pairing of the teeth is tentative. The molar and the incisor were found close together in the same layer. The team assigned both to one species because both are unusually large.

A 3D model of the molar is available through MorphoMuseuM, an open online collection of 3D specimens. Other researchers can examine the tooth there.

Yakamys may not have been the first large rodent in the region.

An unpublished rodent incisor from older rocks in Myanmar is held by the same team. It is considerably larger than the incisors of other rodents described from the region.

The Myanmar incisor hasn’t been formally described or identified. Its size alone can’t show what kind of rodent it came from.

One question remains open. Was large body size a local event or a more widespread part of early rodent evolution?

More fossils from Myanmar and elsewhere in the region will be needed to answer it.

The full study was published in the journal Scientific Reports.
