Brilliant cosmic afterglow may reveal a monster explosion in hiding

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

A modest gamma-ray burst left an unusually bright afterglow. Astronomers suggest that a jet angled away from Earth could explain the mismatch.

In gamma-ray bursts, a stronger flash tends to leave a brighter glow behind. GRB 260310A didn’t follow that pattern.

Its flash was modest, yet the light that came after it was one of the brightest of its kind astronomers have ever recorded.

A new study suggests that its nearby location made the afterglow look unusually bright, while our viewing angle may explain the weak initial flash.

NASA’s Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope detected the burst’s 53-second flash of gamma rays on March 10, 2026.

A team led by Yu-Han Yang, a postdoctoral scholar at the University of Rome Tor Vergata, tracked what followed for about two months.

Co-author Roberto Ricci led the team’s radio observations at the Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array in New Mexico.

“We see hundreds of GRBs every year, but the oddballs are the most informative ones,” Ricci said.

“We could explain individual features of this burst, but fitting them all into the same picture proved much harder.”

Gamma rays are the most energetic form of light. Many gamma-ray bursts begin when a massive star collapses and launches jets of matter outward at nearly the speed of light.

When a jet runs into the gas around the star, it produces a longer-lasting light called an afterglow, which telescopes can follow in X-rays, visible light, and radio waves.

GRB 260310A came from that kind of star. A spectrum taken at Calar Alto Observatory in Spain on March 13 showed only the afterglow.

By April 4, the light carried the broad features of a supernova, confirming that a massive star had died.

Seen from Earth, the afterglow was extreme. Compared with earlier bursts, it ranked among the brightest 1% in X-rays and visible light, and the brightest 5% in radio waves.

Most of that brightness is due to distance. GRB 260310A went off closer to us than about 97% of bursts with known distances.

Once the researchers accounted for distance, the afterglow’s intrinsic brightness was ordinary, and in some measurements relatively faint.

Its peak in visible light was fainter than about 80% of the other bursts, and its X-ray output fell in the bottom 15%.

So the puzzle isn’t really how bright the afterglow was. It’s how weak the gamma rays were next to it.

Astronomers find these explosions most easily when a jet points straight at Earth.

“Much like a flashlight, the jet appears brightest when seen head-on. Most of our models are calibrated using events seen from that same perspective. This burst gave us a chance to look beyond that glare,” Yang explained.

To fit the usual link between gamma rays and afterglows, GRB 260310A would have needed roughly 30 times more gamma-ray energy than it gave off.

A jet aimed away from Earth would dim the gamma rays in just that way.

The visible light fit a side view too. When a jet faces Earth, its afterglow is expected to peak within minutes. This one was still getting brighter hours after the burst.

“We looked at it from a side angle,” Yang said. “As the jet slowed down, its light became visible over a wider range of angles, allowing us to probe the jet’s shape, from its outer edges toward its inner core.”

The X-ray glow faded slowly for about nine days, then dropped quickly. Around 18 days after the burst, it started climbing again, and it didn’t fade until about day 30.

Most of the X-ray tracking came from the Einstein Probe mission, which can’t separate the burst from other sources close by in the sky. NASA’s Chandra X-ray Observatory can, and it found two fainter sources near the burst.

“That was unexpected. At first, I thought another X-ray source was contaminating our observations. But the brightening really came from the afterglow,” said Yang, who coordinated the X-ray monitoring.

“This prompted us to explore a more complex jet’s shape.”

One idea is a jet with two parts: a narrow, fast, energetic core surrounded by a broader, slower flow.

In the team’s model, the core reaches only about 0.8 degrees out from the jet’s center line, and the outer flow reaches about 16 degrees. Earth would be about 10 degrees off center.

Early on, Earth would have seen light mostly from the broader flow. As the core slowed, its light would have spread wide enough to reach Earth, producing the second X-ray peak.

“What appeared to us as an unremarkable GRB might have looked like a monster explosion to an observer looking straight down the jet’s core,” Yang said.

“Our models suggest that the explosion packed roughly a billion years of the Sun’s energy output into a pencil-thin beam.”

The other idea is that extra energy reached the expanding blast weeks after the burst and made it brighten again. The researchers haven’t ruled out either one.

Most of these jets point away from Earth, and their gamma rays are often too faint for detectors to notice, so most of these explosions go unseen.

Late X-ray rises may also be more common than the records show. In NASA’s Swift X-ray archive, only 13 of more than 1,300 long bursts were tracked for more than 15 days.

None of the team’s standard models could match all of this burst’s light, at every wavelength, across the whole two months.

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile began a 10-year survey of the southern sky on June 30, 2026.

Graduate student Muskan Yadav contributed to the study. “Gamma rays are not the most efficient route to spot these hidden jets: we find them once in a blue moon,” Yadav said.

“Upcoming surveys will pick up hundreds of them. Identifying them among millions of variable sources won’t be easy, but it’s a challenge I’m excited to take on.”

With more of them, astronomers could learn how often dying stars launch jets like this one. They could also learn how those jets break through a star’s outer layers.

The full study was published in the journal The Astrophysical Journal Letters.
