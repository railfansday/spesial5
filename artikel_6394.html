A fossil pygmy sperm whale discovered in Peru might hold the record for the most uneven, lopsided face of any mammal ever recorded

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

A 6-million-year-old pygmy sperm whale from Peru had both nostrils on the left, a snout angled 31 degrees down and a face unlike any other whale.

In 2015, Aldo Benites-Palomino was working on his bachelor’s thesis at the Museo de Historia Natural in Lima, Peru, when he examined a fossil sperm whale skull that looked twisted.

The skull had been found in the mid-1990s in the Pisco Formation of southern Peru. It stayed unnamed until this month.

Benites-Palomino and 10 colleagues have now described it as Scaphokogia huachafo, a pygmy sperm whale about 9 feet (2.7 meters) long.

According to the University of Zurich, it shows the most extreme facial asymmetry known in any cetacean and possibly in any mammal, though that claim rests on a single skull.

The holotype, the specimen that defines a species, carries the catalog number MUSM 972. It came from Aguada de Lomas in the Sacaco basin of Arequipa, from rock laid down between 6.3 and 5.6 million years ago.

Toothed whales already have uneven skulls, because the fatty organs they use to make sound sit in the forehead. In this animal, both bony nostrils lie left of the midline and the left one is much larger than the right.

The right premaxilla, a bone of the upper face, grew thick and heavy, while the two sides around the eyes differ in shape and reach.

Benites-Palomino, who studied the fossil for his doctorate at the University of Zurich and now teaches at Friedrich-Alexander-Universität Erlangen-Nürnberg, gave the university a blunt summary. “The skull looks completely crooked,” he said.

The first worry was damage. Sediment can flatten or twist a skull over millions of years, so the team checked the bones for signs of it.

The sutures were fully closed, and the paper reports no deformation elsewhere on the skull. Benites-Palomino called the shape “genuine biology, not a result of poor preservation.”

The drawing of the fossil in the paper also marks shark bite marks along the snout. Sharks left similar traces on other Pisco fossils, and a separate study found that megalodons went after the fatty noses of sperm whales in the same formation.

The team placed 37 points on the facial bones of 3D skull models with a program called MorphoDig, then ran the statistics in R.

The sample held 31 taxa, among them 11 sperm whales, 4 early toothed whales and 16 members of the group that includes modern dolphins.

Francesco Nobile produced the 3D scans of MUSM 171, according to the acknowledgements, which describe the paper as the end of nearly a decade of work on Peruvian fossil sperm whales.

S. huachafo sat at the extreme of the pygmy sperm whales in the first whole-face analysis and again in a second one that corrected for family relationships.

Other strange skulls sat near the edges too, including the walrus-like dolphin Odobenocetops, also known from Peru. The analysis placed Pebanista, the giant freshwater dolphin from the Peruvian Amazon described by Zurich paleontologists, close to some sperm whales.

The authors listed flatfish, with both eyes on one side, and owls, with offset ears, as the better-known cases of uneven heads in vertebrates.

The species name comes from a term that Peruvians use for something flashy, bizarre or tacky. Benites-Palomino said the proportions of the whale “broke every rule of symmetry and elegance seen in living cetaceans.”

“Its anatomy is so extravagant and odd that huachafo seemed the perfect name for it,” he said.

The snout of S. huachafo tilted 31 degrees below the main axis of the skull. Its relatives had less of a bend, with about 16 to 21 degrees in S. cochlearis and 27 degrees in S. totajpe.

Dense bone and ossified cartilage filled out the snout, which would have moved the center of gravity forward. Heavy bone often goes with bottom feeding in sea mammals, as in sea cows and walruses, and the dense skeleton of Perucetus colossus is another Peruvian example of a whale built with extra bone.

A second skull, MUSM 171, kept the atlas, the first bone of the neck. With the neck raised as far as it could go, the snout still pointed 22 degrees downward. With the neck lowered fully, the angle reached 55 degrees.

Gabriel Aguirre-Fernández, a co-author and doctoral advisor at the University of Zurich, said the whole arrangement served one purpose. “The entire cranio-cervical complex is modified to point the snout directly toward the bottom,” he said.

Living sperm whales hunt squid in deep ocean water, the university said. The paper describes S. huachafo as a bottom feeder in the shallow coastal seas of the late Miocene.

Most bottom-feeding marine mammals find food by sight or with specialized structures around the mouth, the authors wrote. Scaphokogia appears to lack those structures, and they proposed that it used echolocation to find prey in soft sediment.

Eight large tooth sockets mark the underside of the upper jaw, and spongy bone had filled them in. The authors suggested that the animal may have had teeth earlier in life and lost them.

The paper states that the size of the nasal organs in Scaphokogia cannot be assessed, because soft tissue rarely fossilizes. The authors based the sonar idea on the attachment sites those organs left on the bone.

MUSM 171 is identified only as Scaphokogia sp., and it is the one specimen of the genus with an atlas. The range of motion given for S. huachafo is therefore a reconstruction from a related animal.

When the team compared only the left and right premaxillae, the most extreme animal was S. totajpe, a species Benites-Palomino helped name in 2020, and not S. huachafo.

That test ranked Scaphokogia as the most asymmetric whale genus in the sample. A separate comparison between groups gave a p-value of 0.076, which the authors called marginal.

The university said this way of feeding disappeared as ocean conditions changed at the end of the Miocene. Surface scans of the skull are public through Morphomuseum. The catalog number MUSM 972 still ties the original to the Lima museum where Benites-Palomino first saw it in 2015.

The full study was published in the journal Communications Biology.
