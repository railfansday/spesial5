Elizabeth Debicki Is the Heart and Soul of 'Cliff Booth'

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

The following article contains minor spoilers for The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth.

It might seem strange, the notion that people have been surprised to find that a David Fincher film, written by Quentin Tarantino and starring Brad Pitt, is “actually good.” But moviegoers had their fair share of reasons to be skeptical of The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth: chief among them, that Tarantino was forcing a mind-melding of the auteurs that could prove disastrously incongruous simply because he didn’t want to blow his long-awaited 10th and final film on a sequel. The 10-film rule is, of course, completely self-imposed, a mandate Tarantino is stubbornly sticking to instead of realizing he can still employ strict curation and quality control without limiting his filmography to a perfect even number. But now that he’s actually, finally, on what will be his last movie (according to his rules), there’s this notion that he’s afraid to pull the trigger—which some believe he all but confirmed when, in 2023, he announced his 10th film would be called The Movie Critic, only to completely retract that a month later.

Two years later, Tarantino sat down as a guest on (what else?) The Church of Tarantino podcast, hosted by two QT superfans, which is actually a pretty fun listen in between all the excessive stanning. So quickly do they bring up QT10 that it’s obvious the director was anxious to set the record straight. Tarantino tells the hosts that TMC started as an exercise in attempting to write a compelling story about “the most boring” profession imaginable. Despite succeeding, he realized he wasn’t excited by it once the film entered pre-production. There was also a version of the story that he adapted into an eight-hour miniseries, until he realized he had no interest in the process of selling and making TV either.

It’s similar to the way he’s talked about what became Cliff Booth, which started as a novel before morphing into an unwieldy feature script until Pitt convinced him to let someone else take a crack at directing it, lest these titular misadventures go to waste. Watching the film last week, my ears perked up when, in the midst of being courted by Cliff, high-powered Hollywood agent Karen Crosby reveals she got her start in the industry as a… movie critic. She reflects back on her time writing about film derisively, describing the profession as one with a low barrier for entry.

Karen is instantly one of the most beguiling characters in a movie filled with colorful performances both big and small. Played by Elizabeth Debicki with an icy, over-it resolve, Karen’s nagging fascination with Cliff makes for what one critic aptly described as Tarantino’s best-written romance since Jackie Brown. Despite being the closest thing to a co-lead, you walk away from the film feeling like you haven’t seen nearly enough of her.

Later in the film, Debicki gets a showy, highlight reel-worthy monologue where Karen, instead of extolling the virtues of cinema as a former critic might, recites what’s apparently an infamous credo among her close friends: that film, while entertaining, hardly rises to the level of being true art. (This is much to the delight of one of her clients, A-list TV star Charlie Peters, played by Scott Caan.)

It was at this point that I was certain we were watching remixed and repurposed scenes from The Movie Critic—and that if Karen Crosby wasn’t the eponymous character, she was at the very least a gender-flipped version. In one of the only times Tarantino talked openly about his erstwhile film, he said it would be “based on a guy who really lived but was never really famous, and he used to write movie reviews for a porno rag.” Meanwhile, the internet ran with the idea that the film would actually be a not-so-thinly-veiled tribute to Pauline Kael. Karen, for what it’s worth, name-drops Kael when describing her past life.

That this is among the best scenes in the otherwise thoroughly enjoyable Further Mis-Adventures only underscores how silly Tarantino’s 10-film imposition is. Look, l I agree with him: as a logline, it hardly sounds exciting enough to be a career finale, but a Tarantino-directed version of the movie probably would’ve been a good time given how much the director clearly enjoys luxuriating in this world. He can write odes to ‘60s and ‘70s cinema in his sleep. But because it would be a retread with no 11th script to follow, it’ll never see the light of day.

Or will it? I hate to enable weird science, but The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth doesn’t simply work; it’s one of the best movies of the year. Can another director submit himself to Tarantino’s tone and vision the way Fincher does so gamely here? Would Fincher even want to do that again? Hard to say on both fronts, but if Tarantino is going to keep being precious about his next (last?) directorial outing, the least he could do is let someone else execute his other ideas before they start collecting dust. Even Tarantino’s B-Tier is still a great time at the movies.
