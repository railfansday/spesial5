Relationships Are Becoming Web3's Most Valuable Infrastructure

The past decade and a half has seen the crypto sector build systems designed to take trust out of the equation entirely, so much so that once two parties agree to a transaction, a smart contract settles the deal, and neither side needs to know, or even like, the other.

However, what none of that code can do is decide who belongs in the deal to begin with, i.e. which founder gets funded, which project gets integrated and which partner gets the first call. All of these decisions are still made by people, and people tend to back those they know or those someone they trust has vouched for.

Ask almost any founder as to how their last round came together, and the answer usually starts with someone who made an introduction, took a call they didn't have to take, or remembered a favor from two cycles ago.

In effect, while the technology was built so strangers could transact without trusting each other, yet the business of building on it still runs on relationships, arguably more now than at any point before.

For a brief moment during the 2021 boom, it looked as though the aforementioned status quo might have changed, given that capital was so plentiful. As a result, a half decent deck and a busy Discord was all that was needed to close a seed round.

Introductions mattered less because hardly anyone was saying no; however, over the last couple of years, that window has firmly shut. Galaxy Research counted only five new crypto venture funds closing in the second quarter of 2026, the fewest since 2019, while roughly 78% of the quarter's capital went to later-stage companies with track records.

Fewer investors are writing checks, and they are writing them to teams they already know or to teams that someone they trust has vouched for. Consequently, the founders who are struggling the most in this market are those who only started networking once they needed something.

The clearest sign that relationships carry real economic value came when someone finally put a price on them. To this point, last October, Coinbase paid roughly $375 million for Echo, the platform Cobie Fish built to let communities invest together in private rounds. By the time of the sale, participating projects had already raised more than $200 million via it (spread across about 300 deals).

From the outside looking in, one can see that by stripping away Echo’s onchain plumbing, its core asset was simply ‘trust.’ People put money into deals because someone they respected was already in them and that is the idea that will likely define the industry’s near to mid term future.

The trouble, as things stand, is that a large part of Web3 still treats relationships as transactions with a handshake attached. Partnerships get announced on X with two logos and a fire emoji, then quietly forgotten within a month.

Anyone who has worked in crypto communications has watched this happen from the inside where the announcement becomes the deliverable, and nobody checks whether the two teams actually spoke again after the press release went out.

But it is worth remembering that memories in this industry are long and the cast of characters in this space is far smaller than it looks from the outside. The same faces turn up at every conference from New York to Dubai to Singapore, and reputations travel faster than any token.

That is what earns relationships the label of infrastructure because the physical ones can take years to build and only get noticed when they fail.

In closing, while the protocols themselves may be trustless, the people building them never will be, and the teams that accept that early will be the ones still standing (and forging long term deals) when the next cycle does eventually come along.
