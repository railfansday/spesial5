‘Inexcusable’ stadium sagas provide NWSL with unwelcome reality check

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Problems faced by Gotham FC and Washington Spirit over staging of their playoff semi-finals shows the league still has work to do

In so many ways, Icahn Stadium, tucked away on Randall’s Island in the Harlem River, is a fantastic place to watch soccer.

The 5,000-seat facility was thrown together in the early 2000s, constructed to replace Downing Stadium, an aging relic that hosted Pelé’s first match with the New York Cosmos 51 years ago. It sits in the shadow of the Triborough Bridge and in the late afternoon fans get one of the more spectacular backdrops in American soccer: the silhouette of the Manhattan skyline framed against the warm orange and red hues of the sunset. The playing surface, replaced in 2024 by Major League Soccer’s NYC FC, is immaculate.

So, yes, the stadium has its charms. But it is no place to host an NWSL match, let alone one in the postseason.

Gotham FC were forced to host their semi-final against the Kansas City Current at Icahn on Sunday because the club deemed the pitch at their usual home – Sports Illustrated Stadium in Harrison, New Jersey – to be unsafe. The playing surface was widely and deservedly derided after the club’s quarter-final win over Seattle last weekend, with players from both teams calling it “atrocious” and “so bad.” Reign coach Laura Harvey put it even more bluntly, saying that there is “no way a playoff game should be played on that shit.”

Just down I-95 in Washington DC, the Washington Spirit faced their own issues when hosting the other NWSL semi-final on Saturday. For a moment, it looked as if they’d have to play at cavernous Northwest Stadium, the home of the NFL’s Washington Commanders, an almost universally despised venue. The Spirit share Audi Field with MLS’s DC United, who are effectively their landlords; United had already arranged to host a Division III college football game there on Saturday – only last-minute bargaining allowed the Spirit to use the stadium.

The two matches – one at a stadium fit for lower-division soccer and the other played on a field with American football markings – painted a stark picture of where the NWSL, despite its explosive growth in recent years, stands in the pecking order of professional sports in the United States.

On Sunday it may have cost Gotham, who were defeated by the Current. Gotham were well-supported at Icahn, where a near-capacity, partisan crowd filled the venue with noise throughout the evening. But it only helped so much: for the second match in a row, Gotham did not register a single shot on goal.

Midfielder Midge Purce, whose second-half cross created the home side’s only goal of the evening – an own goal – was not in any hurry to peg the club’s performance on the venue switch, saying that such things are “never an excuse” for poor performances. Still, the situation felt undeniably consequential for a club who were nearly unplayable at home this year.

“I would say it’s inexcusable,” Purce said. “It’s not what you want in a professional league, for women who are progressing in the United States, we’re supposed to be one of the best leagues in the entire world. I think it’s a bad look for everyone involved. We always want to be at Sports Illustrated [Stadium] in front of our fans and we earned that right throughout the season.”

Purce went on to absolve Gotham’s ownership of responsibility, saying they had done “absolutely everything they can do to make the best situation for us”. She added: “If I knew who to blame, I would ‘at’ them. I don’t have the names.”

Purce was not arguing, of course, that the game should have been played on an inferior playing surface at Sports Illustrated, and neither was her head coach, Juan Carlos Amorós, who said prioritizing player safety was the “right decision.” Still, the Spaniard lamented the move, speaking wistfully about how it may have affected the outcome.

“I think it is part of the sadness,” Amorós told the media. “I think we will never know what would have happened if we had played [at Sports Illustrated Stadium] on a good pitch … It has been a sad weekend. I also think about the situation in Washington yesterday. You know this league deserves better. The two arguably best teams, most consistent, having to deal with these situations. And then yesterday, you know, you sit down to watch that game around the world and you’re seeing those American football lines ... we couldn’t play in our own ground where we could have had more people, where we are unbeaten in 13 games.”

The Current do not generally deal with this issue, as they are one of a handful of NWSL teams who own and operate their own stadium. The club’s head coach, Chris Armas, was quick to point out that he wasn’t entirely familiar with the intricacies of what led to Sunday’s game ending up at Icahn.

“There seemed to be some noise around it,” Armas said. “And rightfully so, but listen – it was a change for them and a change for us. Where the league is at, and how we’re coming along, our league is moving fast. Expansion, the quality of play, the players, the coaches. Things can’t be perfect yet. That stuff will come. Stadiums are going to come, facilities are going to be built. But everything has to catch up – and we’re not there. We can be critical for sure. But the future is bright for our league.”

Gotham won’t have to deal with playing their matches in New Jersey for much longer as they’ve long-planned to move into Etihad Park, NYC FC’s future home ground, in 2028. Yet even then they’ll be playing in a facility they don’t own. And while the future of the NWSL as a whole may look bright, this past weekend’s semi-finals offer a needed – yet unwelcome – reality check.
