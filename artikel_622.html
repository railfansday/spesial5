How ‘The Lowdown’ Season 2 Finale Sets Up a (Hoped for) Third Season

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Creator Sterlin Harjo and Ethan Hawke break down Lee's growth as a person and the loose threads still left to explore.

[This story contains spoilers for season two of The Lowdown and “The Tower,” the Nov. 25 season finale.]

The first season of The Lowdown had a pretty clear resolution: The story that “truthstorian” Lee Raybon (Ethan Hawke) had been chasing throughout the season — about the death of the brother (Tim Blake Nelson) of Oklahoma’s likely next governor (Kyle Maclachlan) and their family’s corrupt history — was printed (if not in the form he initially thought) and seemingly put to rest.

Season two, however, concludes with multiple threads still untied — and some even circling back to season one. Lee, with help from some of the journalists he disparaged at the beginning of the season, gets an exposé on conditions at Judge Sallsman’s (John Hawkes) Fresh Start meatpacking operations published, but he’s frustrated that he can’t tie the judge himself to the story. As he continues chasing leads — including getting Martha Plimpton’s Sheriff Thorne to go on record about the judge — Marty (Keith David) saves Lee from walking into a confrontation with some of the people behind last season’s One Well conspiracy. They realize that Lee’s story about Fresh Start, the One Well people and Marty’s investigation of the illicit sale of indigenous artifacts are all part of a bigger picture. Ginger (Betty Gilpin), meanwhile, leaves Tulsa after realizing that, for several reasons, she can’t continue her life there.

In other words, there is a lot left to explore in a potential third season — which FX hasn’t decided on yet.

“I’m trying to twist their arm,” The Lowdown creator and showrunner Sterlin Harjo told The Hollywood Reporter about the very open-ended finale. “We left it open and pushed forward to a season three because I like three-season shows. I don’t know if we’re going to be doing it, but I hope that we are.”

The finale, titled “The Tower,” did offer closure on one front: Lee, his daughter Francis (Ryan Kiera Armstrong) and their family and friends lay Tommy Lee Jones’ Lee Sr., aka Frog, to rest at a somewhat chaotic funeral, which features the return of Peter Dinklage as Lee’s friend Wendell, who eulogizes Frog with a reading of Dylan Thomas’ “Do Not Go Gentle Into That Good Night.” That’s followed by a more relaxed, and touching, gathering at Sweet Emily’s afterward, where Francis reads a journal entry of Frog’s detailing the apprehension he felt about being a father when Lee was a baby.

“He’s going through the stages of grief. How he understands and reinterprets he and his father’s relationship now that his father’s gone, it’s going to be an ever-changing thing,” Hawke tells THR. “But I feel like something positive and profound happens to Lee when his daughter is reading to him.”

Harjo and Hawke talked with THR about the loose plot threads, Lee’s ongoing work of becoming a better person, and how a conversation between Harjo and Jones led to the finale’s prologue. (Separate interviews are combined and edited below.)

Before we talk about the end of the season, I want to go back to episode six — where Frog dies at the end of a father-son road trip — because the scenes with Ethan and Tommy are just so, so good. It seems like a huge theme of the season of Lee coming to terms with his dad. He’s kind of starting to get there throughout that episode, but there’s still a lot left for him to process.

STERLIN HARJO I would say so. You have this like these two guys that don’t communicate well. I kind of like compare it to the Tim and Dawn love story in the English Office. They have to hide all of their affection, so a touch on the shoulder becomes like a kiss. It’s similar with Lee and Frog because they don’t communicate. These small gestures end up feeling really big. I think them connecting and going home, they don’t say ‘I love you’ or anything like that. But he touches his son on the back. He tells some stories about [Lee’s] mother. All of these things are really big, because they don’t communicate that well, and they have a hard time meeting each other in the middle.

I think that that last scene is beautiful where Frog says to Lee, you know, and it almost escapes out of his mouth, “It wasn’t so bad in there, the hospital, and I might have needed a rest.” That’s the apology. Lee is kind of shocked and can’t say anything, and he just nods, which is also his apology and acknowledgement of it. It’s really beautiful moment. And those are my kids that are playing with the bubbles [as Frog watches Lee walk into the convenience store]. I’m really proud of all that.

ETHAN HAWKE What I like about the writing of the show is it’s never one thing. It’s like more like real life. There is the possibility of healing. It kind of dawns on him at the end of episode six that his father wants to make up with him. And if his father wants to do that, then his father is a different man than he had assumed he was. That opportunity for healing existing is a great benefit to Lee. But of course, they don’t really get to resolve it, and that’s the beauty of episode eight when his daughter is reading from [Frog’s] journal to him.

Would the Lee of season one have been able to hand off the Fresh Start story?

HAWKE No. Those are the small growths that you get to show in a TV show. What I love about it, having never made a TV show before, the canvas size is just so big that you can really get intimate with a character and show those little adjustments. I don’t think at the beginning of season one that he would have been able to go to his ex-wife’s wedding and not make it about himself. That was a small journey. This journey of showing respect to other journalists was [another].

HARJO It’s such a big deal for me. I wanted that to be a big moment. I opened the season with him calling everyone sellouts and assholes, and then he has to humble himself and go to all of them, hat in hand, and say I need your help. I think it’s also a part of the growth of his personal life and the relationship with his father — him having to realize that he’s not the only one. The story isn’t just about him.

There are a lot more loose ends at the end of this season than there were at the end of season one.

HARJO We’ve got to twist FX and Disney’s arm for season three. I don’t know how the powers that be work that out. It sure would help if we had awards. Somehow, the Hollywood awards have always been elusive to me and my projects, even though they get reviewed so well. We’ll see what happens, but hopefully something like that happens and that helps us a little bit. I don’t know where we stand right now. I hope to do a season three.

Assuming you do go get to go forward — I hope so too — would those loose ends be the primary plot driver in a third season, or more secondary?

HARJO It will be the plot of season three, that bigger conspiracy. Obviously there will be some surprises, but what I wanted to do was the idea that season one, you feel like it’s one and done. This is the conspiracy. Then we’re moving on to another conspiracy in season two. One of the things I keep saying in season one is everything’s connected. And actually in Reservation Dogs, we said that a lot too. But the idea is that we were kind of pretending it was going to be these two separate things, but actually they’re all connected.

HAWKE The setup is pretty clear. At the end of season two, we see that there’s a connection between the crime of season one and the crime of season two. There’s a connection between this judge and One Well. I think season three would be about bringing the whole tower down.

From a character perspective, what do you want to keep exploring with Lee?

HAWKE We’ve always kind of dreamed of this as a trilogy. Put simply, season one is about him learning how to be a father, and season two is learning about how to be a son, and a third season would be about him learning to live with himself, and completing the hero’s journey.

HARJO I’m really interested in season three being about, What is it like watching your daughter become the next stage of all of that? And also, what does it mean to slow down? What does it mean to look at your life and go, OK, I don’t always have to chase the truth at the detriment of my health, I can also have balance. How do I balance my life, find happiness and find love, and I’m able to be a father and a son and also someone that fights for the truth. How do we become a whole human being and be more responsible? I think that is in the cards for Lee.

There weren’t any obvious markers for where Ginger has ended up in her final scene. Where is she as she tries to get away from her former life?

HARJO She’s in Eureka Springs [Arkansas], which is a couple hours away from Tulsa going east, and it’s just an interesting old mining and brothel town. It’s really beautiful, in the Ozarks, and a great place to go to get away. We broke off with Betty and took a small group to Eureka Springs and filmed that.

I also wanted to ask about the prologue scene in the finale, showing Frog at the fireworks stand in Texas and watching Paris, Texas, which the opening scene of the season pays homage to. Did you just want to give people a sense of where he started this season?

HARJO Tommy Lee Jones would call me up now and again. “Hey, I want to talk about the script. You want to come over?” I’d go to his house, and his wife Dawn was awesome, and we would talk about polo or whatever for a little bit. Then he’d say, “All right, come back here,” and we’d go into his office and start talking about the script. At one point, he goes, “Would you like to know where Frog came from?” And I was like, “What do you mean?” He’s like, “Well, the backstory that I came up with.” I was like, “Yeah, of course.” He just told me that story, and I was amazed. It was just the backstory that he had invented — Frog was in Palestine, Texas, working at this fireworks stand. He told me this story, and I was like, “Well, I’ve got to do that. I have to put that in the finale.” So we did.
