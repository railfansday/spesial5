Unocoin Estimates $200,000 Loss as Backup-Key Finding Explains Wallet Transfers

Unocoin has revised its account of a security incident that disrupted the Indian exchange, putting estimated cryptocurrency losses at about $200,000. The more significant technical finding is how transactions could proceed after the exchange asked its wallet providers to freeze activity.

Unocoin’s November 14 update identifies compromised backup keys as part of the explanation. The incident illustrates a distinction that can be easy to miss: suspending access to a service and removing the ability to authorize a blockchain transaction are separate things.

Unocoin said three operational hot wallets were compromised and promised to fund the identified loss from its own resources. Its earlier assessment had not identified missing cryptocurrency. The company also disclosed possible exposure of customer information, while saying it had found no evidence that KYC documents were accessed.

Those findings remain subject to investigation. An initial loss estimate is useful for assessing scale, but it is not the same as a final reconciliation of every asset and transaction. Potential information exposure also needs to be assessed separately from the amount of cryptocurrency taken.

That distinction matters for customers. Replacing coins can restore an account balance; it cannot make exposed contact or account information private again. A financial remedy and a data-security response address different consequences.

BitGo’s November 13 account says the affected Ethereum wallet used a two-of-three multisignature arrangement. Unocoin controlled the user and backup keys, while BitGo controlled the third. BitGo says the reviewed transfers used the two Unocoin keys and were broadcast outside its platform.

The design permits an owner to recover assets without depending on the service provider. That is valuable when a provider is unavailable, but the same independence means that the provider’s refusal to sign cannot prevent two other valid keys from acting together.

BitGo’s wallet documentation distinguishes self-custody arrangements from its custody products, including differences in who controls keys. A provider’s name therefore does not, by itself, tell a reader who had final authority over a particular wallet.

The broader control question is whether emergency credentials are separated effectively from everyday systems. A recovery mechanism has to remain available to its legitimate owner while being difficult for an intruder to assemble with other signing material.

NewsBTC’s coverage of business Bitcoin accounts with team approvals examined another part of that problem: permission to request or approve a transfer is not necessarily identical to possession of the cryptographic keys.

Combining several safeguards can help, but their independence matters. Two approvals provide less protection if one compromised system can obtain both. This is an architectural lesson from the reported signing arrangement, not a determination of the still-unknown route into Unocoin’s backup storage.

Unocoin says it is rebuilding infrastructure and investigating how the backup material was compromised. A convincing follow-up would connect that forensic explanation with a complete accounting of losses, restored services and the handling of potentially exposed information.

BitGo’s wider commercial growth, covered in NewsBTC’s report on its quarterly results, shows how large the custody and infrastructure business has become. Scale makes clear descriptions of individual service arrangements more valuable, particularly when several companies participate in one transaction process.

The available disclosures narrow the explanation considerably. They identify which keys authorized the reviewed Ethereum transfers. They do not yet provide a complete account of how those keys were obtained or establish that every part of the incident has been resolved.
