Alix Earle Bares All: How the TikToker Is Becoming Beauty’s Next Billion-Dollar Mogul

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

She showed 8 million followers her flaws, then sold them the fix. Inside the 25-year-old’s sold-out, no-days-off skin care business.

Keeping up with Alix Earle is beyond the capacity of most mere mortals. Over the past year alone, Earle could be found delivering a perfect foxtrot on Dancing With the Stars weeks before shimmying alongside Pedro Pascal and Cardi B on Bad Bunny’s Super Bowl Halftime Show; vibing to Lady Gaga’s Coachella set and hours later lecturing a Harvard Business School class about the creator economy; falling in sky-high platforms at Kendall Jenner’s L.A. Halloween party, then strolling New York’s CFDA Awards red carpet (bruises hidden) in a gown styled by Law Roach. For weeks this summer, she cruised in a yacht along the Sardinia coast with Kylie Jenner BFF Stassie Karanikolaou, flying to Los Angeles for the Sept. 1 premiere of her Netflix reality series Earle Meets World (a global top 10 debut just renewed for a second season), all while weathering headlines about a rumored fling with Tom Brady and the messy fallout of a business rift with Call Her Daddy‘s Alex Cooper. At the moment, though, seated in a sunny conference room inside Reale Actives’ Beverly Hills office, she’s taking her magnetic energy and putting it to work on keeping her winning streak alive.

Less than eight months ago, on March 31, Earle launched Reale Actives, a skin care brand formulated in collaboration with dermatologist Kiran Mian and tailored for sensitive and acne-prone skin. Developed in secret for two years, the four-product launch drew a record-breaking response from Earle’s online community — a fiercely supportive tribe, particularly after she shared a years-long, psychologically debilitating struggle with cystic acne. It generated $1 million in sales in less than five minutes. By midday, sales hit $5 million, and by 4 p.m., all products and sets had sold out completely.

It’s nearly lunchtime on a late-October day, and Earle, at the head of the table, is introducing five members of her team: CEO Andrea Blieden, director of influencer marketing and partnerships Katie Clementi, brand director Brianna Ellis-Mitchell, social director Logan Reynolds and, on Zoom, chief marketing officer Amanda Goetz. By year’s end, there will be 23 full-time employees across two office floors.

The brain trust is gathered to evaluate a top-secret item that will hit Reale Actives’ site in March to mark the brand’s one-year anniversary. Will it fit in with the rest of the product line, including the recently launched Go Calm serum? Is it giving REI? Does it need more branding? Maybe a free gift with purchase or a fun slogan? (“Zit girl” could work, but men buy the products, too, so maybe “dirty thoughts, clean skin”?) Do we like the shape?

Everyone weighs in. “Yes, I have my own opinions,” Earle says after the meeting ends, “but I trust their points of view. We all want the same thing: Everything we put our name on and ask our community to purchase with their hard-earned money must be the best product with the best quality.”

Blieden, whose résumé includes executive posts at Kiehl’s and The Body Shop, says Earle is totally dialed in and personally tests samples nonstop. “We have nine different text channels that include Alix,” she says. “She’s not like other celebrity or influencer founders. She is truly involved.” True to form, Earle connects directly with her consumers whenever she can. “I’ve seen her in every situation when people approach her, and she always stops to talk, introduce herself and get names. She gets addresses and sends product,” says the CEO. Once, when a user was having a hard time with breakouts, Earle had the company fly the fan to New York for a consultation with the Reale Actives dermatologist. All this while assiduously keeping up her prolific content creation, often with multiple posts daily. The day I visited, Earle posted a GRWM video that featured her putting on makeup and selecting an office fit for our interview: a gray cashmere sweater and pants, Saint Laurent Brigitte patent pumps and a Mombasa purse. It has since been viewed 1.3 million times.

“If you were to ask me, at any point before I was doing anything on social media, what I would want my career or end goal to be, I would have said having a brand of my own,” says Earle. “My dad and his brothers have a construction company together, and seeing how passionate they are about it has always been inspiring. I didn’t know exactly what kind of company I wanted or that it would come so early for me. The second I got this massive opportunity online, it was great — but I also understood that it can be fleeting. You have relevancy one moment, and it’s gone the next.”

Earle’s father, Thomas “TJ” Earle, says he never doubted his daughter’s ability to achieve a goal. “In her youth, she was a terrible basketball player, but she didn’t care. She was really small, but she went out there and gave it 100 percent. It didn’t matter that people thought she wasn’t good at it,” says TJ, who heads up Earle construction while managing the careers of Alix and her influencer sister, Ashtin. (Alix is repped by a powerful team that also includes senior agents at UTA and attorney Kevin Yorn.) “She did the same on TikTok. She was always giving the camera to somebody and asking them to take videos of her wherever we went. We all made fun of her and gave her the nickname ‘TikTok.’ She knew she wanted to do it and just kept powering through.”

TJ recalls Alix, while still in college, calling home excitedly to say that brands were offering her $250 for sponsored posts. These days, she reportedly commands between $250,000 and $500,000 per post for a one-off brand collab. While TJ won’t confirm exact figures, he says all her deals have one common denominator: “It always comes down to whether or not she truly likes it.” If she really likes it, he says she may “even do it for free.” Recently, Earle agreed to a deal to promote what she later said on TikTok was a foundation, but after trying the product, she realized she couldn’t in good faith endorse it. She told her agent to cancel the contract. “In some way, it feels so silly to be throwing away insane amounts of money,” she says, “but I really do just believe in longevity and maintaining that trust with my audience.” (Earle endorses and backs Cymbiotika supplements, GORGIE energy drink, SipMargs margaritas and was an early investor in Poppi, which sold to PepsiCo for $1.95 billion.)

Before launching Reale Actives, Earle fielded offers to become the face of several brands, deals that came with the freedom to claim she “did everything” to build it but meant she didn’t have to lift a finger beyond signing her checks. It never interested her. “This started as a concept in my head, and I want to be a part of every single little decision and detail,” Earle says. “The team does a great job of taking some of the front load off so that when we get in here, there are already different directions we can choose to go in.”

Earle does the hiring interviews herself. “The first two roles I interviewed were for a CEO and dermatologist. I’d just graduated college, so I was trying to find people who would share the same passion for the brand because I didn’t know how to rate their experience since I didn’t have any.” Blieden says she interviewed with Earle four times, put together a project presentation and underwent a surprise test. “It had come down to me and another candidate, and on a Saturday, Alix sent us each a text message that said, ‘Hey, I’ve been thinking about some stuff about the brand. Would love to discuss it with you,’ ” recalls Blieden. “I responded in 1.2 seconds because I was so excited. I guess the other person responded Monday.”

The hustle culture may not be for everyone, but there are no days off on social media. “She is lovely to work with, but she’s a beast and doesn’t mess around,” says the CEO. Adds TJ: “If you look at her calendar, it’s filled seven days a week, crisscrossing the country, meetings, events, photo shoots. Nobody could keep up with her pace. She’s just an animal.”

Earle describes scrapping the launch video they’d “spent so much time and budget on.” The clip was just “vibes,” nothing memorable. They jettisoned it for a nearly nude video featuring Earle dripping wet, unretouched — as all Reale Actives imagery has been since — to normalize imperfect skin. “I wanted people to think that two things can coexist: The line can be efficacious for your skin, but it can also be daring, fun and sexy. Skin care doesn’t have to be clinical and boring.”

The rollout ran on Earle’s knack for turning fans into co-conspirators, beginning with an Instagram account, @wtfisalixdoing, that dropped hints about the launch and continuing with influencers receiving locked suitcases containing a single puzzle piece, and a blank billboard in New York’s SoHo that put the pieces together one by one until the puzzle was completed, at which time creators received codes for unlocking the Reale Actives swag inside their suitcases. The award-winning campaign spanned a week, after having been fueled for weeks prior by Easter eggs on Earle’s social accounts.

Nick Brown, co-founder of venture capital firm Imaginary, which backs the brand and has also invested in Kim Kardashian’s Skims, Glossier, Farfetch and Tom Holland’s Bero, explains what has become known as “the Alix Earle Effect.” “You can count on a very small number of hands the number of times a creator has been able to move product at such a force that you start to realize it as an investor,” he says. “It’s seismic and not one-off. I kept seeing her do this — for mass brands, masstige brands, luxury brands, in beauty and in apparel. She really has a deep understanding for the brands she connects with and, more specifically, the connection between her own passion and what her audience will respond to. That’s why it’s organic and works so well.” Brands that enjoyed the boom included Giorgio Armani Beauty, Drunk Elephant, Patrick Ta Beauty, Charlotte Tilbury, Mielle Organics and NYX Makeup. Now that she’s applied her magic touch to Reale Actives, Brown confirms the business is already “very profitable” less than a year in and, he forecasts, is well on its way to becoming “a multi-hundred-million-dollar net revenue business.” Likening Reale Actives to Hailey Bieber’s Rhode and Selena Gomez’s Rare Beauty, he says, “She is constantly thinking about how to turn left, create an element of surprise and do something that’s a slightly different riff on what’s been done, enough to make people stop and say, ‘That was really cool.’ She is otherworldly when it comes to that.”

Despite what may seem like a charmed path to a reported $20 million net worth (so far), Earle has hit some speed bumps, facing criticism that it was her several rounds of Accutane, long considered a silver bullet, and not Reale Actives that cleared her skin. “I knew that was coming,” she says matter-of-factly. “But [the criticism] was not something I was afraid of because the truth is the truth. After I finished Accutane for the third time, my skin went back to being a mess and was probably worse than ever before. The moment that people started talking about it, I made a video and I showed my skin journey and was like, ‘Here’s before I did Accutane, here’s after I did Accutane, here’s before I started the products and here’s after I started the products.’ I have nothing to hide. I’m not lying. I actually use these products and nothing else.”

Earle also bore the brunt of the “negativity bandwagon” when she surfaced videos of her long (by American standards) European summer holiday. “Things got so negative this summer that it really helped me thicken my skin and gain a little bit more confidence in myself, knowing that you’re not going to be able to correct every narrative out there,” she says. “As a female founder, people want to put you into this box of ‘you can’t have.’ But you can let loose and have fun and share the human moments that we all have.”

Jumping from the phone screen to the small screen for Earle Meets World was also an adjustment. The series featured her divorced parents, TJ and Alisa Earle; her stepmom, Ashley (as in Dupré, the woman at the center of a 2008 sex scandal involving former New York Gov. Eliot Spitzer); four siblings and mom Alisa’s boyfriend, Todd. “I was worried going into it because I wasn’t sure if I would be able to connect with people in a different way than on socials,” she says. “But it gave people a completely different perspective on my life. As scary and embarrassing as those moments are, I really, really loved doing it.” Earle, who works with an acting coach, has passed on all scripts that have come her way, more out of fear of getting it wrong than anything else: “I only want to do something if it’s the right fit or I could be good at embodying that character.”

The season ended with Earle’s first on-camera comments about the falling-out with Cooper. Back in 2023, Earle and her podcast, Hot Mess, became the first creators signed to Cooper’s media network, Unwell. The deal lasted 18 months, at which point Earle found out she’d been dropped from Unwell when it broke in the media. “I constantly felt taken advantage of and used and mistreated and manipulated,” Earle said on her reality show, with her father adding that the podcast wasn’t given basic resources like video crews. Cooper has yet to comment on the drama.

Earle also recently faced a wave of criticism when she told a magazine that she’s never read a book. “Let’s clear the air: I have definitely read a book,” Earle tells me with a smile. “But no, I don’t actively read. I’m not reading anything right now.” (Other than — wink, wink — Daddy Issues, by Sofia Franklyn, who also had beef with Cooper; Earle posted a photo of herself perusing the title on TikTok overlayed with audio from Barstool Sports podcast hosts Dan Katz and Eric Sollenberger criticizing her admission. The clip had drawn 9.3 million views as of press time.)

A big teachable moment came in the form of her evolving relationship with her father as manager (dad-ager?): “She would not respond to emails, answer my calls or would roll her eyes at me in a meeting, which is typical stuff for a 20-something to do to their dad, but not someone you work with. Finally, I said, ‘Alix, listen, I’ve been doing this a long time with family, and here’s how it needs to work. There’s Dad, and then when it’s business, you treat me no differently than you would treat anybody else on the team.’ She’s super respectful to everyone, so that was easy for her.”

Running a business with multiple revenue streams and being smart about money were modeled by her dad, after all. She says, “I’m saving a lot, so some goes into a retirement fund, and I’ve invested in different stocks and bonds beyond the company investments. I always have the understanding that this can go away at any time, so I always keep that at the back of my mind.”
