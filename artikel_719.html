Documentary ‘Porno Para Ricardo,’ About Cuban Punk Musician Gorki, Shows ‘the Struggle of a Free Spirit Trapped in a Cage’

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

When Czech documentarian and photographer Hana Jakrlová encountered the crumbling streets of Cuba after meeting the dissident punk musician Gorki there, she had a strangely familiar sensation.

“I was living in New York at the time,” she recalls, “and Obama had just begun the process of warming relations with Cuba. All my friends in New York were worried about Cuba changing and McDonald’s popping up on every corner.”

“So I went to photograph Cuba – and experienced the strangest sense of déjà vu. I had spent the first 20 years of my life in communist Czechoslovakia, and being in Cuba felt like stepping back into my youth, only in a Caribbean setting. Everything was familiar: the hopelessness, the absurdity, the propaganda, the misery – both economic and, above all, spiritual.”

Then, she says, she met a Cuban punk guitar player and leader of the banned group Porno para Ricardo, leading to a 12-year production journey that culminated this week in her feature docu debut, “Porno Para Ricardo,” which screened recently at the Ji.hlava International Documentary Film Festival.

“After talking to him and attending a Porno para Ricardo rehearsal (at Gorki’s house – the band had been completely driven underground by the Cuban regime and could no longer perform in public), I realized I wanted to make a project about this guy.”

At first as part of Jakrlová’s photography project on Cuban anti-regime artists, she soon realized she needed to concentrate on Gorki and his band, she says.

“I also came to understand that something was missing from my photographs of a punk band. So I started filming, initially with my own camera and later with a director of photography.”

That was in 2014, the director says, adding she’s now been to Cuba 10 times and spent almost a year there in total. “One thing that has always remained a mystery to me is how the establishment kept letting me back in.”

Jakrlová sometimes filmed herself and also used archival material “shot by the brilliant Cuban documentary filmmaker and dissident Claudio Fuentes.”

But she brought in respected Czech cinematographer Adam Ol’ha to help capture the remarkably striking visuals of Gorki’s life and struggles.

“We also worked with local talent – sound people and others – and some of them faced serious repercussions for their courage in working with us.”

“I wanted to show the struggle of a free spirit trapped in a cage,” Jakrlová says. “The life of an artist and dissident who has dedicated his entire life to fighting a totalitarian regime, spent three years in prison and faces persecution and oppression on a daily basis. But I also wanted to show a man torn between his love for his country and his love for his family. Gorki has a five-year-old grandson who lives in exile with his daughter, and Gorki has never been able to see him.”

Then, things changed, thanks to Cuban authorities, she says. “The regime gave him a clear ultimatum: Either he leaves the country or he goes to prison.”

“So, after filming Gorki’s dramatic departure into forced exile, the film became not only a punk essay on freedom, but also a film about exile.”

Filming her subject in Cuba involved daily harassment and threats from police, Jakrlová says. They also worried about another possibility: That he could die in a mysterious car accident, as has happened to several Cuban dissidents.”

The regime worries about its image abroad, says Jakrlová. “This helps explain why our crew wasn’t arrested during the shoot. However, we were followed constantly, as became apparent during Gorki’s subsequent arrest.”

As for why Gorki has always remained so passionate about his message (“This is for the bitch Raul Castro,” he yells while introducing one anti-regime song) Jakrlová says she came to understand they share some core beliefs.

“We are both freedom-loving people. Unlike Gorki, I was lucky enough to see communism fall in my country towards the end of my adolescence, so I never had to find out whether I had it in me to fight against it. What motivates Gorki is his love of freedom, his need for freedom, and also his sense of responsibility.”

“And that’s what connects Gorki with people like Václav Havel, Oswaldo Payá or Nelson Mandela. These were courageous and truly inspirational people. I feel incredibly lucky to have met two of them: Gorki Águila and Václav Havel.”

Jakrlová’s greatest challenge in completing “Porno Para Ricardo,” she says, was the deep daily dysfunction of Cuban society.

“Being a director is challenging, especially in a country where nothing works and nobody expects anything to work.”

“The only thing that worked 100% was Gorki. He is the most responsible person I know, always ready to work. He even risked his life by waiting for the film crew to arrive in Cuba after the regime had threatened him and given him an ultimatum: Leave his country or go to prison.”

Gorki now resides in Mexico, where he is awaiting a decision on a U.S. visa that would reunite him with his wife, daughter and grandson in Florida, Jakrlová says.
