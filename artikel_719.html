‘The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping’ Review: A Worthier Prequel to the Dystopian Battle Royale, Powered by Joseph Zada’s Star-Making Turn

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

The ensemble of Francis Lawrence’s saga of fascism and rebellion also features Jesse Plemons, Ralph Fiennes, Glenn Close, Kieran Culkin and Elle Fanning.

The sixth movie in the $3.3 billion Hunger Games franchise contends, like many series entries before it, with a somewhat jarring tonal discordancy between the glitzy spectacle and grotesque political satire of the Capitol and the emotionally loaded brutality of the Arena, where children and teens from each of the 12 districts of Panem are forced to fight for their lives until only one is standing. But Sunrise on the Reaping overcomes that imbalance because the horror of the survival battle and the sorrow of the losses sustained are rendered with as much scalding pathos as suspense and excitement. Francis Lawrence, who has directed all but the first of the features adapted from Suzanne Collins’ hugely popular YA novels, knows what he’s doing. But the surprise asset here is Australian actor Joseph Zada in a breakout performance as 16-year-old Haymitch Abernathy, the rebellious victor of the 50th Hunger Games. Played as an adult by Woody Harrelson in the first four films, Haymitch was subsequently forced to serve as a mentor to contestants every year but remained furtively on the side of the resistance. Zada (seen recently as Cal on Netflix’s East of Eden) doesn’t have much physical resemblance to Harrelson, but the voice is convincingly that of the Haymitch we know, coming into manhood with firm convictions born out of injustice and providing a resonant link to the earlier films.

Invested with tenacity, kindness and heartrending vulnerability by Zada, Haymitch provides a much more poignant and involving origin story than that of Coriolanus Snow in the lumbering first prequel, The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes. The autocratic Capitol leader would go on to consolidate his power by using the televised Games to keep the Panem population scared and under control.

Set 40 years after Songbirds and Snakes and 24 years before the first Hunger Games movie, Sunrise on the Reaping opens on Haymitch’s 16th birthday, which coincides with the second Quarter Quell, when the names of “tributes” from each district are drawn to enter the Arena. Being a special edition that happens just once every 25 years, the traditional choice of one male and one female has been doubled to two of each.

Haymitch initially is not among the four selected tributes for District 12, the grimly industrial coal mining sector where everyone appears to be living on or below the poverty line. But when one of the chosen boys tries to make a run for it and is shot by the Stormtrooper-like “Peacekeepers,” Haymitch is drawn into a skirmish and dropped into the vacant slot as punishment.

The showbiz cynicism of the operation is illustrated by Plutarch Heavensbee — Jesse Plemons in the role previously played by Philip Seymour Hoffman as an older man, following his promotion to Head Gamekeeper. Designated by the Capitol to oversee the nationwide broadcast, Plutarch dashes about giving camera directions and insisting on do-overs to maximize the drama of tributes being painfully separated from their loved ones and ushered off to almost certain death. As in the propaganda films of most dictatorial regimes, the heavily edited televised content removes all signs of messy rebellion and anti-Capitol sentiment.

The separation is especially wrenching for Haymitch from his widowed mother and adoring younger brother, but equally or perhaps even more so from his girlfriend Lenore Dove Baird (Whitney Peak), who tends geese on her father’s farm. (She’s a descendant of Lucy Gray Baird, the winner of the 10th Hunger Games, played by Rachel Zegler in Songbirds and Snakes.)

The sobriety of the farewells, however, is undercut by the unrestrained caricatures of some high-ranking Capitol officials. Every time the movie lurches into high-camp excess it lessens the emotional weight. The most intrusive example is Glenn Close, exploring new frontiers for over-the-top as Drusilla Sickle, her contemptuous cruelty fueled by her indignation over being assigned to chaperone the District 12 tributes, whom she regards as grimy subhumans.

Wheeled out onstage like Hannibal Lecter to draw the lottery, Drusilla is trussed up in what looks like a leather bumblebee costume, with a red Aunt Gladys wig topped by a raven fascinator, crazy-lady makeup and a mouthful of prosthetic dentures that even Tilda Swinton might balk at. All the better to chomp on the scenery. Shrieking like a pantomime dame, Close goes full Norma Desmond and then flips to Joan Crawford mode, losing her shit with a riding crop (see 1955’s Queen Bee) when she’s sassed by Haymitch’s smart-mouthed fellow tribute Maysilee (Mckenna Grace), a shopkeeper’s daughter.

Even higher on the shrill scale is Drusilla’s husband, Capitol stylist Magno Stift (Billy Porter), sashaying around in extravagant finery with tiny live golden snakes as earrings. He declines to dress the District 12 unfortunates, sharing his lunatic wife’s opinion that they are not fit for couture. Mercifully, Magno is not around for long, making way for the familiar figure of Effie Trinket (Elle Fanning, amusingly playing a younger version of the character originated onscreen by Elizabeth Banks). The future chaperone briskly takes charge of 12’s tributes, giving their outfits an elegant upgrade before the opening ceremony chariot parade.

While Fanning gives Effie a shot of humanity beneath her giddy glam manner, the same cannot be said of Kieran Culkin as preening TV broadcast host Caesar Flickerman. “Look alive, children, while you still can!” he tells the tributes in a bit of gallows humor that might have been funny had he not been so obnoxiously glib. Where Stanley Tucci offset Caesar’s callous showmanship with mischievous charisma in the first films, Culkin just plays sarcastic mockery. His Caesar is a charmless creep.

While the sinister combination of razzle-dazzle and fascist barbarity has always been essential to the Hunger Games world, it’s a relief here when the Capitol monsters take a backseat to the young people whose lives hang in the balance. From the very beginning, we see what a beloved figure Haymitch is in the village, how pure is the love he shares with Lenore and how tender and reassuring he is with the youngest of the district’s tributes, Louella (Molly McCann).

They make a pact to be allies, extending also to Maysilee and fourth District 12 tribute Wyatt (Ben Wang), even if his sideline as an oddsmaker on the Games earns him some initial distrust. But when tragedy strikes early on during the chariot parade, Haymitch stares down Coriolanus Snow, his eyes expressing fury, disgust and defiance. While that uneasy moment will be cut for the broadcast, it was witnessed by the public at the opening ceremony, an embarassment to Snow that puts a target on Haymitch’s back.

Played as a youth by Tom Blyth in Songbirds and Snakes and by Donald Sutherland as an older man in the original Hunger Games quadrilogy, Snow here has the soft-spoken menace and steely authority of Ralph Fiennes. His intelligence is unquestionable but he’s also paranoid and gullible about his consigliere Plutarch and given to fits of petulant rage.

The certainty that Snow will ensure he dies an agonizing death in the Arena makes Haymitch rethink his alliances, telling the other District 12 tributes that it’s not safe to be around him. But he does enter cautiously into a pact with Ampert (Percy Daggs IV) from District 3, who informs him that other districts are open to collaboration in order to prevail over the elite contestants from Districts 1 and 2. Known as Career tributes, these are the most frequent victors in the Games, due to their wealth, privilege and extensive combat and weaponry training.

Ampert was entered into the games by Snow in retaliation for the attempt to sabotage Capitol communications by the boy’s father, Beetee (Kelvin Harrison Jr.), now forced to serve as a mentor but still secretly plotting. The mentors specifically assigned to District 12 are past victors Wiress (Maya Hawke) and Mags (Lili Taylor); neither actor is given a lot to do but they are a welcome presence — warm, supportive and candid with the tributes.

Lawrence and screenwriters Billy Ray and Michael Lesslie have relatively little time and breathing room to give the more ruthless Arena opponents much character definition. But two District 1 Careers are memorably lethal — Silka (Laura Marcus) with her double-headed axes and muscle-bound Panache (Jhaleil Swaby) with his ferocious anger and strength.

The nail-biting Arena action is vicious and bloody, sustaining high stakes as Haymitch attempts to break the Capitol’s brain hub, which sends in “mutts” — genetically modified animals, from electrified butterflies to killer squirrels and birds — to overwhelm the tributes earmarked for death.

Zada is the beating heart of the film, mutinous but neither cocky nor fearless, and he has stirring scenes with Ampert and Maysilee. The hair from one of Britney Spears’ blonde bangs periods is a distraction, but Grace is fiery in the latter role — gutsy, determined and a loyal friend to Haymitch once their allegiance is cemented. She’s also handy with a poisoned blow dart. Iona Bell as a young substitute tribute drugged into compliance by the Capitol also has touching moments.

Other cast standouts are Peak, lovely as Haymitch’s sweetheart; and the always dependable Plemons, who deftly navigates Plutarch’s double duty as a Capitol official playing for the other side.

Bolstered by DP Jo Willems’ muscular camerawork, James Newton Howard’s soaring score and Uli Hanisch’s imaginative production design, Sunrise on the Reaping is a return, after the disappointing Songbirds and Snakes, to the spirit of the first movies. There’s even a brief appearance by baby Katniss Everdeen to complete the circle. It’s impossible not to feel sympathy when young people are being sacrificed in horrific ways for the ritualistic entertainment of the masses and the power-flexing of a pitiless overlord. A moving coda set many years later re-introduces familiar faces that will add to the satisfaction for longtime franchise fans.
