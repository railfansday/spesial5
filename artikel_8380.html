I don't follow your math. Why are you taxing the traditional withdrawal at 24%? Presumably you're assuming that waiting to withdraw from the IRA means you will be in a higher tax bracket (24%) than you are if you Roth-convert years earlier (20% - a bracket that doesn't exist)?

If that's your point - that IRA withdrawals will be taxed at a higher rate if your IRA investments do really well (compared to Roth conversions of smaller amounts years earlier) - that can be mitigated by tax-efficient allocation of investments to taxable and tax-deferred accounts. Our tax-deferred accounts are 100% in bond investments. If you assume the same tax bracket for Roth conversions and traditional withdrawals, the numbers are pretty close to a wash even with your 100% rate of return.

That said, of course RMDs go up every year and eventually even with tax-efficient allocation, RMDs can be taxed at a higher rate than Roth conversions years earlier, if your tax-deferred accounts are large enough. And that's even worse after one member of the couple dies. And Roth accounts are better for heirs to inherit than even taxable accounts because of the 10 years of tax-free gains they get. And finally, given the high federal debt, it seems reasonable to assume that tax rates will go up in the future (although perhaps that will have already happened by the time we retire and are ready to consider Roth conversions seriously). So I think there are good reasons to do Roth conversions for a few years after we retire, but maybe not 100% of our tax-deferred accounts should be converted even if that were possible while remaining in a low-ish tax bracket. That's because of the medical expense deduction: as we start needing expensive long-term care, funds in a tax-deferred account can be withdrawn at low tax rates.
