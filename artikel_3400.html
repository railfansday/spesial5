3 Reasons Why Console Prices Aren’t Going Down Anytime Soon

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Between chip shortages and tariffs, gaming will stay expensive for a while

At this point in a console generation, system prices would have usually come down significantly, broadening install bases and making gaming more accessible. That isn’t what happened this time around. The base PlayStation 5 costs $649.99, $150 more than at launch, while the Xbox Series X is $799.99, $300 more than when it came out six years ago.

Unfortunately, that situation is unlikely to change anytime soon.

One of the main reasons consoles are so expensive right now is an ongoing shortage of hardware components needed to build these systems, specifically DRAM (memory). This shortage has largely been caused by increased construction of AI data centers, which require vast quantities of memory. As per GameIndustry.Biz, an analysis by S&P Global Market Intelligence Kagan claims it will take until at least 2028 for console manufacturing prices to start coming down as DRAM production increases.

Many major manufacturers have shifted from producing consumer-focused DRAM to the type of memory used in AI data centers, reducing the supply of consumer DRAM and causing prices to skyrocket. While manufacturers are looking to increase DRAM production to meet current consumer demand, it will reportedly take until 2028, at the earliest, for them to scale up. Beyond affecting the current generation of consoles, this will impact future consoles as well, meaning the next-gen Xbox Project Helix and PlayStation 6 would likely be exceedingly expensive if they were to launch next year.

While current estimates indicate that prices for consumer DRAM could start coming down by 2028 due to increased production, much of this extra supply could end up going straight to AI data centers. “If data center demand grows, then it could quickly eat into the extra supply,” S&P analyst Neil Barbour said to GamesIndustry.biz. “The memory [fabrication facilities] are not expanding with an eye specifically toward the consumer market. They’re racking up record revenue and margins on the data center build-out, so these supply expansions are primarily meant to feed that demand.”

“However, if the build-out plateaus for regulatory reasons or simply because the demand isn’t as strong as they had anticipated, then more of that capacity could become available for traditional DRAM. What this data suggests is that the conversation doesn’t really even start until 2028.” If there’s a minor silver lining, Barbour adds, “I wouldn’t say further price increases are guaranteed in this environment.”

On top of the ongoing DRAM shortage, the Trump administration proposed a new slate of tariffs on Wednesday, which will hit imports from dozens of countries, including China, Japan, South Korea, and members of the European Union. Many of the duties go up to 20 percent, and the administration threatened to raise tariffs on imports from China up to as high as 40 percent if Beijing doesn’t comply with certain demands.

A large share of the world’s RAM supply is produced by the South Korean companies Samsung and SK Hynix, as well as the Chinese company CXMT. Samsung and SK Hynix primarily produce their RAM in China and South Korea. As for consoles, PlayStation 5s are manufactured in Japan, China, and other countries affected by U.S. tariffs. The Xbox Series consoles are also at least partially manufactured in China. Although it's hard to predict how long these duties will remain in effect, even if the Trump tariffs were lifted overnight, game console prices are likely to stay high for years to come.
