Could Your GLP-1s Make You Blind? Here’s What to Make of the FDA’s Latest Notice.

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

For years, there have been rumblings and even research about a rare but serious condition that’s been linked to taking a GLP-1 medication. Now, the Food and Drug Administration is doing something about it.

The FDA just released a drug safety communication to warn patients and healthcare providers about the potential increased risk of developing non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) when you take a GLP-1 medication. NAION is a rare condition that can lead to vision loss in one eye, and it can be permanent.

Again, this isn't the first time concerns have been raised about the risk of NAION. But it is the first time the FDA has issued a warning about it.

Given that up to 11 percent of Americans currently take a GLP-1 for weight loss, this news stands to impact a lot of people. How concerned should you be if you're one of them? Eye experts and a doctor who prescribes GLP-1s break it down.

Meet the experts: Joseph Rizzo, MD, director of the Neuro-Ophthalmology Service at Mass Eye and Ear, a member of the Mass General Brigham healthcare system; Bavand Youssefzadeh, DO, an ophthalmologist with Cedars-Sinai in Los Angeles and a cofounder of the Global LASIK and Cataract Institute; and Mir Ali, MD, the medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, California.

The FDA's notice is designed to flag the potential risk of NAION to people who use GLP-1s. The FDA also notes that the agency will require all manufacturers of GLP-1 medications to add information about the risk of NAION to the Warnings and Precautions section of their labels.

"Patients and caregivers should be aware of a potential increased risk of NAION, a rare condition that can result in acute, painless, decreased vision in one eye, including permanent vision loss, with GLP-1 RA use," the notice reads.

The agency cited a 2024 study published in JAMA Ophthalmology as well as its own meta-analyses and retrospective cohort study in the notice. They also noted that “NAION cases have been reported for all currently marketed GLP-1 RA products.”

However, the complication remains rare: incidents from the FDA’s meta-analysis were just 6.4 events per 100,000 per year for patients using the medications.

The warning is just that—a warning to patients and physicians that this could happen, explains Joseph Rizzo, MD, director of the Neuro-Ophthalmology Service at Mass Eye and Ear, a member of the Mass General Brigham healthcare system. "Most outcome-related issues in medicine should be thought of as being probabilistic—there is a certain chance that events can occur," he says. "This warning indicates that the FDA has reason to believe, at least at this time, that the probability of NAION occurring is higher in patients who take GLP-1 RA drugs."

But Dr. Rizzo stresses that there's no evidence that these drugs cause NAION—just that there's a link.

So…why issue a warning if it's not proven that GLP-1s cause NAION and it's so rare? "The severity of a complication matters alongside its frequency," says Bavand Youssefzadeh, DO, an ophthalmologist with Cedars-Sinai in Los Angeles and a cofounder of the Global LASIK and Cataract Institute. "Even a rare risk deserves attention when it can permanently affect vision." The warning is likely designed to give healthcare providers a heads-up that it could be NAION if a patient has symptoms, he explains.

"It's their duty to report any adverse effects, just so people are aware of them," says Mir Ali, MD, the medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, California.

While NAION is scary, doctors say it's important to put the data in perspective. "It's extremely rare, but it can happen," Dr. Ali says. Given how much GLP-1s can help with blood sugar management, weight loss, and downstream health effects, Dr. Ali says that the risk of NAION is "not a reason not to seek these medications."

Dr. Rizzo agrees. "I essentially never discourage the general use of GLP-1 RA drugs," he says. "Ultimately, the risk-to-benefit assessment must be decided by the patient, but physicians should provide sufficient information to the patients to better inform their decisions."

People should stay aware of the risk without panicking, Dr. Youssefzadeh says. "It's also worth remembering that the people most likely to be prescribed these drugs—people with diabetes, high blood pressure, sleep apnea—already carry a higher baseline risk for NAION, so the risk isn't starting from zero," he adds.
