No more permanent coalitions: What the midterm results reveal

ANALYSIS -- “Life moves pretty fast,” that famed 20th-century philosopher Ferris Bueller once said. “If you don't stop and look around once in a while, you could miss it.”

In 2024, Democrats were consigned to the political wilderness. Republicans had captured the White House, flipped the Senate and held the House. President Donald Trump had completely altered the party coalitions as he led the ascendant GOP to victory.

Now, just two years later, Democrats are on the rise. A historic midterm tsunami has swept them into such commanding Senate and House majorities that some are questioning whether Republicans will ever be in charge again. And if Texas is blue, how does the GOP have any chance in the Electoral College again?

A big takeaway from the midterm results should be that there’s very little permanence in our politics.

What may have looked like a new GOP coalition was really more of a Trump coalition, and it crumbled in the face of economic frustrations, affordability concerns and the president’s unpopular policies.

With Trump’s negative job approval rating weighing them down, Republican candidates suffered losses among virtually all demographic groups – including by wide margins among constituencies thought to make up the GOP’s core.

Trump or the Republicans finished ahead among male voters by 4 points in 2018, 8 points in 2020, 14 points in 2022 and 12 points in 2024, according to the exit polls. Men went for Democratic candidates by 4 points (51 percent to 47 percent) this cycle. Meanwhile, Republicans lost even more ground among women. While Kamala Harris finished ahead of Trump by 8 points in 2024, that margin grew to 21 points for Democratic candidates this year.

Trump closed the gap among Hispanic voters to just 5 points behind Harris two years ago. This year, Hispanic voters nationwide supported Democratic candidates by 32 points (65 percent to 33 percent). Even white voters shifted significantly. They backed Trump or  Republican candidates by 10 points in 2018, 17 points in 2020, 18 points in 2022 and 15 points in 2024. And yet Democrats edged out Republicans by a point (50 percent to 49 percent) on Nov. 3.

Voters without a college degree were supposed to be a key cog in Republicans’ new populist coalition. But they went from +13 (56 percent to 43 percent) for Trump in 2024 to backing Republican candidates by just 1 point this year. Meanwhile, the gap among voters with a college degree went from 14 points for Harris in 2024 to 30 points for Democrats this cycle.

Republicans had closed the gap among young voters aged 18 to 29 from 35 points in 2018 to just 11 points in 2024. But younger voters supported Democratic candidates this year by 50 points (74 percent to 24 percent). Not to mention Democrats flipped voters aged 45 to 64 for the first time in nearly two decades.

White evangelicals appear to be one of the only groups that didn’t shift toward Democrats. They supported GOP candidates 82 percent to 17 percent. But they only made up 17 percent of the electorate, according to the exit polls, which is down from a very consistent 25 percent for more than a decade. So there was still a problem with that group of voters, but it was more about turnout than persuasion.

While there was an unmistakable leftward shift this year, the results should not be considered a permanent party realignment any more than 2024 was. While Democrats did extremely well in Texas this year, they still haven’t won the Lone Star State in a presidential race in 50 years. So maybe it’s wise for everyone to take a deep breath.

If you take this year’s election results and match them with the Senate seats on the ballot in 2028, there doesn’t appear to be a viable path for Republicans back to the majority. But there wasn’t a particularly viable Democratic path to the Senate majority at this point two years ago. And if the Alaska election goes their way, they will end up winning Senate races in seven states that were initially rated Solid Republican.

If one party starts to get too comfortable, voters have a way of bringing politicians back down to earth. If Democrats come to Congress and push too far to the left or become too defined by their most extreme members, they will squander the opportunity they earned this year.

The dramatic demographic swings should be evidence that many voters are not beholden to either party and are open to switching allegiances, particularly in a time of economic uncertainty.

At the same time, if Republicans don’t go through some time of reflection and instead triple down on the policies that led to the blue wave, they risk cementing some of the shifts that started this cycle. It just won’t be thick cement.
