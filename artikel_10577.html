Why Engagement Beats Intelligence: Rethinking Success Metrics In Education

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Geoffrey Alphonso, CEO of Alef Education.

In traditional classroom settings, students who respond first or grasp ideas quickly often receive the most praise. This dynamic creates a skewed learning environment that favors speed over in-depth understanding and intelligence over effort.

When a teacher consistently praises the first student to raise their hand, it triggers a powerful feedback loop. The quick responder builds academic self-efficacy and participates even more, while students who need more time to process feel their input isn’t valuable, fostering learned helplessness and lower engagement over time.

However, academic ability alone doesn’t determine a learner’s potential. A highly capable student can become disconnected from learning, withdraw effort when work becomes difficult or stop challenging themselves. Another student may require more time to grasp a concept but continue participating, questioning, persevering and steadily improving.

This highlights a largely overlooked aspect of the learning process: Intelligence can indicate what a learner may be capable of, but engagement ultimately determines academic success.

The potential of a student is often assessed solely through their academic ability. However, the learning process itself unfolds over several years, during which students face increasingly complex materials, changing environments and periods where their progress might be slow or inconsistent.

A broader view of student potential should focus on engagement, sustained efforts and their ability to challenge themselves. The highly intelligent student who grasped concepts quickly during the initial stages may not be the one who remains most engaged as learning complexity progressively increases.

Despite this, engagement isn’t student-dependent alone. In a 2024 study involving 3,643 adolescents across several stages of development, positive teacher-student relationships and a stronger sense of belonging in school were found to be key factors driving engagement.​

The focus, therefore, needs to shift from “Why isn’t this student engaged?” to “What conditions enable students to remain engaged?”

As lessons become increasingly complex and students encounter hard-to-grasp concepts, their long-standing learning approaches might not be adequate. When progress necessitates sustained concentration and repeated efforts, intelligence alone doesn’t determine the final outcome.

At this juncture, a learner must remain sufficiently invested to keep up their progress. Through meaningful engagement characterized by sustained concentration, intellectual effort, persistence and willingness to explore unfamiliar ideas, students can master difficult concepts without losing the motivation to keep learning.

Engagement and lifelong learning are mutually dependent. Higher engagement supports ongoing learning, while the pursuit of knowledge keeps students deeply immersed in the learning process.

By prioritizing this, the UAE’s national vision for education extends beyond academic attainment, with a focus on developing adaptable, resilient and lifelong learners. The future skills expected of UAE learners, as defined by the UAE National Education Charter, include resilience and adaptability, initiative and perseverance, self-management and discipline, self-confidence and commitment to lifelong learning, among others.​

Students who develop these critical competencies in the classroom go on to become lifelong learners even after formal education ends, assuming responsibility for their own development through self-management. The objective isn’t just to train students to retain more information but also to nurture capable learners who possess the capacity and willingness to continue progressing.

For edtech leaders, the shift of focus from intelligence to engagement poses an important question: Do digital learning environments support sustained participation? At this stage, the challenge is no longer merely digitizing content, but ensuring that students are able to push themselves and achieve self-efficacy, becoming responsible for their own development.

Although the latest technological innovations can enhance learning access, increased platform usage alone may not convert to sustained learner engagement. For both education and edtech leaders, the objective should ultimately be engagement with learning itself, rather than engagement with a particular technology.

Passive engagement is one of the biggest pitfalls in digital learning. When edtech platforms prioritize screen time over active processing, it can lead to poor retention and less than desirable pedagogical outcomes.

The problem isn’t the learning module, course structure or the content itself, but the way they’re presented. To tackle this, edtech platforms should be made highly immersive and interactive with minimal distractions.

For instance, rather than playing a long video without breaks, quizzes and other creative tools can be added in between to ensure maximum retention. Students can be encouraged to actively refresh their learning through real-world applications, ensuring that their learning continues even when the module ends.

Moreover, digital learning doesn’t need to happen in isolation. Edtech platforms can encourage social engagement through collaborative challenges and shared learning goals. Personal progress trackers and gamified achievements can recognize improvement over time, sustained effort and the application of feedback, rather than how quickly students complete a task or outperform their peers. Interactive polls and open-ended response tools can give students opportunities to learn from one another, while teacher-guided feedback can help each learner recognize their progress and understand what to work on next.

A holistic learning environment helps ensure that students with varying abilities and talents have equal opportunity to develop, learn and progress. Whether it’s a quick learner who grasps concepts easily and answers questions first, or someone who studies and progresses at their own pace—both should be given positive reinforcement and support.

Although intelligence, academic capability and achievements matter, a student’s current progress shouldn’t be viewed as a reflection of their potential. Potential isn’t something that’s innate and fixed; it can be developed by confronting difficult concepts and remaining engaged with one’s own development. This is particularly relevant as education systems, including the UAE’s, place increasing emphasis on resilience, adaptability and lifelong learning.

It’s high time we redefine academic potential from what a learner can do today to their capacity for sustained engagement and lifelong development.​
