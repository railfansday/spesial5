What we know about the global Microsoft outage

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Watch: What we know about the Microsoft global outage overnight

A major global Microsoft outage has caused problems, or in some cases disabled entire services, including emails and artificial intelligence products.

Microsoft has said the outage was not caused by a cyber-security incident, but efforts to fix the issues have so far been largely unsuccessful.

Here's what we know about the services affected and what went wrong.

Schools, GPs and businesses hit by global Microsoft outage as company scrambles to solve issue

Microsoft 365 Services, including Teams, Outlook and Microsoft's artificial intelligence software Copilot, have been affected by "connectivity problems".

Customers are reporting varying levels of access - many have not been able to send or receive emails, view certain files or details on their calendar apps.

Some users have been able to use Teams and are using messaging and online meeting functions without issues.

More than 430 million people use Microsoft 365 apps according to Microsoft's website.

The full scale of the outage has not been confirmed, but monitoring website Downdetector had 140,000 people from 74 countries report issues with Outlook on Thursday evening.

The real figure affected is likely much higher because Downdetector relies primarily on users self-reporting.

Microsoft 365 is also used by companies worldwide and is expected to have a knock-on effect to other services.

Tech giants OpenAI and Anthropic have both reported problems as a result of the outage.

ChatGPT-maker OpenAI said it was investigating an issue affecting its connections to Microsoft's services, which has caused some tools to not work as expected.

From city governments in the US to Australian spy agencies, disruption across the globe has been widespread.

Courts from Los Angeles to Texas warned that communications might be delayed.

In Canada, the public broadcaster CBC reported that a healthcare network running several hospitals in Toronto was experiencing email issues, but that patient care was not affected.

The government of Japan also said its shared IT platform had been disrupted.

Across the UK, GP surgeries, pharmacies, police and schools have been affected by the outage.

Some GP surgeries have urged patients not to submit messages via their website while the problem remains, while several large police forces are urging members of the public to call if they want to make a report.

Microsoft has ruled out a cyber-attack and attributed the ongoing outage to "elevated load" on its infrastructure.

The term refers to when servers are handling a much higher volume of stress than they usually do.

In the case of the Microsoft outage, a large number of servers became "unexpectedly unavailable", the company said, which sent added traffic to remaining servers, straining these.

On Friday afternoon, Microsoft said it had seen "encouraging recovery progress" in Europe and improvement in North America.

The company added it is focused on "addressing the remaining areas of disruption" and warned "some users may still experience intermittent issues as recovery actions continue".

In an earlier statement, the tech giant had said that its attempted fixes were "not taking as expected".

Although email service Outlook appears to be resuming for some, the outage has so far lasted for more than 20 hours - in outage terms that is considered a long time.

Prof James Ferryman from the University of Reading's department of computer science said the details the company has disclosed provide some clues.

"What Microsoft has told us is that the part of its servers that sets up secure connections is getting swamped, and once that happens the server just stops answering," he said.

"The trouble is that this kind of failure snowballs.

"One server falls over, its work gets passed to the next one, and meanwhile huge numbers of phones and laptops keep trying to get back in.

"You can't just turn it all back on at once. You have to bring it back bit by bit."

The challenge is therefore not just fixing the fault, but managing the huge surge of devices trying to reconnect once services start coming back online.

Gergely Orosz, a former leading software engineer at several major tech companies including Skype and Uber, said this could be the "most disruptive" business email outage "of all time".

He said it was still difficult to know just how many people have been affected by the disruption, but added he "cannot recall" an outage over business email ever taking this long to return to normal.

Australian cyber-security expert Troy Hunt told the BBC the outage could have far-reaching consequences.

"Microsoft is a massive part of the internet backbone and reminds us how critical email still is.

"Not only does this outage affect many people's ability to work but also things like the ability to communicate about appointments, purchase things that need verification, create or access accounts and services," he said.
