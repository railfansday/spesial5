Houthi rebel advance in Yemen may further destabilise global economy

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

What happens next in Yemen may send fresh shockwaves to the world economy

The Iran-backed Houthi rebels who seized much of Yemen in 2014 have now forced the army of the internationally recognised government to withdraw from the Red Sea coast in order to defend their interim capital, Aden.

Their advance bodes ill for Yemen's legitimate, internationally recognised government, its Saudi backers and, by extension, much of the rest of the world.

Yemen, a mountainous, heavily armed, intensely tribal and largely impoverished nation, sits at the far south-west corner of the Arabian Peninsula.

The country has two coasts: One faces the Indian Ocean on which sits the once-prosperous trading port of Aden, the other faces the Red Sea and, most importantly, the narrow chokepoint at the southern exit of that waterway - the so-called Bab al-Mandab Strait or, in English, The Gate of Tears.

Whoever controls that strait can threaten any or all maritime traffic passing through from both the Mediterranean and the Red Sea to the Indian Ocean.

In normal times, it accounts for around 12% of global trade, with anywhere between 70 to 150 ships a day making the transit.

But not any more. Although the Houthis say they are no threat to maritime traffic, they are currently at war with Saudi Arabia, making it extremely hazardous for the Saudis to send their oil tankers past the now rebel-controlled coast.

When the US and Israel started a war with Iran in February, Tehran responded by choking off much of the oil trade flowing out of the Gulf through another narrow chokepoint - the Strait of Hormuz. This sent oil prices soaring, boosted inflation and hurt economies everywhere.

The Saudis had a Plan B. Instead of exporting their oil past the dangerous coast of Iran, where their tankers risked being droned or mined by Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps, they started pumping it overland through their East-West Pipeline to an export terminal on the Red Sea at Yanbu. This worked for a while.

But then in July a cold truce between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis broke down, that pipeline got hit by drones and the rebels started firing missiles and drones at targets all over Saudi Arabia.

On land, the Houthis seized the initiative and made a lightning dash down the Red Sea coast, sending the Yemeni army into headlong retreat. A brief counter-offensive appears to have failed and the Houthis are now reported to be in full control of the Bab al-Mandab Strait and the nearby offshore islands.

Nicholas Hopton, a former British Ambassador to both Yemen and Iran and now director-general of the UK-based Middle East Association, says: "The decision by the legitimate government of Yemen to prioritise the military defence of Aden at the expense of withdrawing from other areas suggests that the war is not going well for the Saudi-backed [Yemeni] government. And the rest of the world should be worried."

But Mohammed al-Basha, the Yemeni founder of the US-based risk advisory firm Basha Report, believes the Houthis would struggle to capture the heavily fortified city of Aden.

"Any attempt to seize a densely populated city like Aden would come at a significant human cost," Basha said. "Advancing across the flat coastal plains would also expose Houthi forces to air power."

"For now, the Houthis appear to be seeking a negotiated surrender, hoping to exploit infighting and divisions among southern factions to achieve their objectives without a costly urban offensive."

It is conceivable that the Yemen war will settle into a less dramatic but deeply unsatisfactory stasis - a situation where casualty figures subside yet the country remains split between areas controlled by the Houthis - Iran's Shia allies - and the disparate and often poorly motivated forces of the Saudi-backed Yemeni army.

He also warns this could have a knock-on effect to security well beyond Yemen's borders.

"By extending their control over the western coastline, the Houthis will increase their ability to smuggle weapons and ammunition from Iran, the Horn of Africa and elsewhere, posing additional threats to the region," Col Smailes said.

"This control will enable both Iran and the Houthis to extend their influence in East Africa and beyond."
