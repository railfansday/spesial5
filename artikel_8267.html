Do Skiers Really Need Another Outerwear Brand? Atomic Thinks So.

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Atomic's outerwear features clean lines, minimal logos, and ski-specific details, including articulated construction designed to allow freedom of movement. (Photo: Cam McLeod/Atomic)

The ski world probably doesn’t need another outerwear brand. Dozens of other companies already make quality ski jackets and pants; we’re not exactly short on options.

So when Atomic, the Austrian brand best known for its skis, boots, and bindings, announced it was getting into apparel, I had a pretty obvious question: Why?

At a recent North American launch event I attended at Sundance Mountain Resort in Utah, Atomic acknowledged the challenge of entering an already crowded market. But their answer was simple: After more than 70 years developing ski equipment, who better to make clothing for skiers than a company that lives and breathes skiing?

Fair enough. Though plenty of established apparel companies could make the same argument.

After handling and trying on several pieces from the new collection, including some Gore-Tex Pro outerwear coming next year, I came away impressed. The clothing has clean lines, thoughtful colors, familiar technical materials, and small design details that suggest someone actually thought about what it’s like to spend a day skiing in this stuff.

I haven’t skied in it yet, so the real performance test is still to come. But there’s enough here to make Atomic’s latest expansion worth a closer look. It is a significant expansion, including a complete skiwear collection, from insulated resort jackets and technical shells to bibs, midlayers, base layers, and lifestyle pieces.

Of course, Atomic isn’t the first ski equipment company to see an opportunity in apparel. Salomon, Armada, and Rossignol have all made the move, while Black Diamond, whose roots are in climbing and backcountry skiing, did the same more than a decade ago. Atomic itself has steadily expanded beyond skis, boots, and bindings over the years, adding helmets and goggles to its lineup.

So the move into apparel isn’t entirely surprising. But what does Atomic bring to an already crowded category?

For starters, Atomic isn’t entering the apparel business entirely on its own. The company is part of Amer Sports, which also owns Salomon and Arc’teryx, two brands with decades of experience making technical outerwear. Atomic’s designers explained that they’ve been able to draw on existing fabric research, development resources, and manufacturing expertise within the group.

Atomic also brings its own experience developing ski equipment since 1955. I’ve visited the company’s headquarters in Altenmarkt, Austria and seen firsthand how its engineers develop skis, boots, and bindings, taking prototypes from the office to the snow in minutes and bringing that feedback back into development. Now, outerwear designers follow a similar process, with access to on-snow testing close to headquarters.

Then there’s Atomic’s reputation. In SKI’s 2026 ski test results, Atomic was among the top three most-recognized brands, earning distinctions in categories ranging from backcountry and powder to frontside carving. In my view, that’s a track record worth protecting, and one that gives skiers a reason to take their clothing seriously.

The materials are reassuringly familiar, too. Atomic uses established insulation and fabric technologies from PrimaLoft and Pertex, alongside its own waterproof fabrics. Gore-Tex Pro will join the collection in fall 2027.

For the current lineup, the differences are fairly straightforward. The Revent 2L Insulated Jacket and Pants use Atomic’s 2LAYR Move, a two-layer fabric rated at 20,000 mm waterproofing, with PrimaLoft Silver synthetic insulation for warmth. They’re designed for skiers who prefer an insulated jacket and pants rather than managing multiple layers.

The Revent 3L Jacket and Bib Pants, meanwhile, use Atomic’s 3LAYR Protect, a three-layer fabric also rated at 20,000 mm waterproofing. They’re uninsulated shells, giving skiers more flexibility to add or remove layers as temperatures and conditions change.

Of course, familiar materials and impressive specifications are only a starting point. How a jacket is cut, how the seams are constructed, and how everything fits and moves together can make the difference between loving your gear and being annoyed by it before lunchtime.

And that’s where some of Atomic’s smaller design choices caught my attention.

Atomic describes its approach as performance-first rather than fashion-first, and some of the most interesting details are the ones you might not notice until you’re actually wearing the clothing.

The women’s Snowcloud Puff Jacket is a good example. It has a slightly cropped silhouette, an insulated powder skirt, and a removable hood.

Take the hood off, and there aren’t exposed snaps or awkward attachment points that make it look like something is missing. It’s still a finished-looking jacket.

That matters when you’re using a puffy in different ways. A hood is nice around town or when the jacket is your outermost layer on a dry day. But under a shell that already has a hood, it can feel bulky and unnecessary.

The Revent 2L Shell Anorak, a relaxed, unisex shell, also caught my eye. It has the oversized silhouette and kangaroo pocket you’d expect, but the concealed chest pocket inside the collar was an added touch you don’t often find in anorak styles. As someone who finds it incredibly annoying to put a phone or sunglasses in a kangaroo pocket and have everything bouncing around, I appreciated that.

Other ski-specific details include lift-pass pockets on the left sleeves of most jackets (not just outer shells), helmet-compatible hoods, well-placed ventilation, reinforced pant cuffs, and articulated construction, including pre-shaped elbows and knees designed to accommodate how skiers actually move.

None of these features is revolutionary, of course. But they’re familiar details, executed with the kind of practical attention that matters when you’re spending a full day in ski gear.

As for fit, the pieces I tried generally ran true to size, with a slightly longer, freeride-inspired cut that felt relaxed without being baggy. That said, at six feet tall, I found both pairs of softshell and hardshell pants a little shorter than I’d like.

Atomic’s 2026–’27 apparel collection is available now at Atomic.com. Looking ahead to fall 2027, skiers can expect to find Atomic apparel at select retailers and ski shops as well.

Atomic clearly understands that skiers care about style, too. The aesthetic is clean and understated without being boring. Muted but thoughtful colors, cropped puffies, relaxed anoraks, and monochromatic outfits give the collection plenty of visual appeal. The silhouettes and design details are consistent across categories, so the pieces look like they belong together without requiring a fully matching kit.

But one of my favorite design details is something you might never notice unless you’re a skier, like me, who rolls up your pant cuffs when you’re out of your boots.

On the inside of the right pant cuff, Atomic has hidden its name. That’s the only time the wordmark appears, which feels decidedly cool.

It’s also a pretty good introduction to Atomic’s approach to branding. You won’t find its name prominently displayed across the collection. Instead, the Atomic star appears subtly printed on sleeves or worked into seam placements and color blocking.

The design is recognizable once you know what to look for, but it doesn’t announce itself from across the lift line.

Like Wild Rye and a handful of other ski apparel brands of late, Atomic seems comfortable letting the design speak for itself. There’s something confident about that, especially in a sport where logos can be as loud as the colors they’re printed on.

After all, is there a better compliment than someone saying, “That’s a sick jacket. Who makes it?”

With skis, boots, bindings, helmets, goggles, and now clothing, Atomic can outfit skiers almost completely under one brand.

That doesn’t mean there’s any reason to buy everything from Atomic. Most of us mix and match equipment, developing relationships with different brands for different reasons. Maybe you love your Atomic skis but swear by another company’s boots. Your favorite jacket might come from a brand that doesn’t make hardgoods, and your base layers from somewhere else entirely.

Still, there’s something to be said for gear designed to work together.

Think about wearing a helmet and goggles from the same company. When the fit is seamless, it’s one less thing to worry about.

Atomic seems to be taking a similar approach to its apparel, developing shells, insulation, and base layers with complementary fits, colors, and layering systems. Whether that delivers a meaningful performance advantage remains to be seen, but I understand the intent.

Ultimately, that’s the appeal of well-designed ski equipment, regardless of whose name is on it. We spend enough time and money figuring out what to wear and what to ski. Once we’re on the mountain, we shouldn’t have to think about any of it.
