They're more complicated and more expensive than LCD screens, but your next gaming monitor should still be an OLED

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Your next gaming monitor should be an OLED display. There, I've said it. And I know the notion won't please everyone. After all, a large grey proboscidean lumbers into the room whenever OLED technology is mentioned. Put simply, almost any risk of the dreaded burn-in is an immediate deal-breaker for some. I do get that.

Likewise, despite my musings just the other day on how affordable gaming monitors have generally become and the fact you can now snag an OLED panel, specifically, for sub £300 / $300, it's a stretch to say OLED gaming comes cheap. And yet here I am arguing firmly in favour of OLED.

The point is that OLED is fundamentally superior to LCD, the latter being the dominant display tech on the PC for decades and also, as it happens, an unambiguous technological kludge. Understanding why is the relatively easy bit. Where things get extraordinarily, even belligerently, complicated concerns the current OLED gaming monitor market and the baffling array of panel types and monitor models on offer.

We'll get to all that. But let's start with the core appeal of OLED, which comes down to two factors: lighting control and speed. In both regards, OLED technology completely annihilates LCD. The thing about OLED panels and lighting is that each pixel is its own individually controlled light source. Actually, each subpixel is.

That means literally perfect lighting control. There's no leakage, no need for bodges like local dimming. Just the ability to have immediately adjacent pixels doing absolutely opposite things, one fully lit up, the other totally off. The impact of that on contrast is epic. In short, if you want a true HDR experience that combines sizzling bright highlights with properly controlled black levels, only OLED can do that.

The problem with LCD, you see, is that it's a transmissive technology, so light is pushed through the pixels from a big, dumb backlight behind. The liquid crystals in each subpixel are then oriented by an electric charge, controlling the quantity and colour of the light output. In practice, there are oh so many limitations to that approach.

Chief among these problems is that the backlight leaks through in all kinds of unintended ways. And let's be clear, technologies like mini-LED backlights with full-array local dimming don't get anywhere near entirely compensating for LCD's shortcomings. For starters, even a mini-LED backlight with 2,000 zones is very, very low resolution in terms of lighting.

With that kind of zone count, you're looking at a backlight resolution of roughly 55 zones by 35 zones in a 16:9 monitor. Imagine if the actual image resolution was 55 by 35 pixels. Yuck. In the context of a 4K monitor, then, each zone is shared across approximately 4,000 pixels. That's a lot and it translates into visible blooming, weird brightness gradients, dimming zones noticeably popping on and off and all that messy, ugly stuff.

While we're talking liquid crystals twisting in the electrical wind, that's also the reason why LCD is so much slower than OLED. It involves the physical rotation of those rod-shaped crystals to update the image. Like I said, the pixels in an OLED panel are their own light-emitting diode sources, and very rapidly reacting ones at that. So, OLED response times are essentially instantaneous from a human perception perspective.

I will admit, it's still impressive how good modern LCD panels can look, despite their numerous baked-in limitations. But in lots of important ways, OLED is inherently more suited to gaming displays than LCD. The catch is that it, too, is imperfect, ye olde burn-in being the most obvious shortcoming.

The "O" in OLED refers to "organic", as in Organic Light Emitting Diode. The "organic" bit translates into carbon-based molecules that degrade with use. In short, the harder you drive an OLED pixel, the more its ability to output light is worn out. Over time, the whole panel will wear to some degree, degrading maximum light output. But the real fear is differential wear that leads to unevenness across the screen. The classic scenario is static on-screen interface furniture, perhaps the Windows task bar or persistent parts of a game interface like a health bar or minimap. Eventually, those elements will lead to uneven panel wear.

Pretty much all current OLED gaming monitors have various burn-in mitigation technologies, such as pixel shift, refresh cycles, edge dimming and so on. But none entirely solve the problem. As for real-world expectations of just how bad burn-in can be, there are several independent assessments of OLED monitor burn-in. For instance, Monitors Unboxed has been running one that's now at the 30-month mark, testing absolute worst-case usage on the MSI MPG 321URX, which is from the first generation of 4K, Samsung-made QD-OLED panels.

For what it's worth, my overall view - based on both personal experience with numerous OLED panels over the last few years, and what you might call the emerging consensus - is that burn-in is a real problem, but shouldn't stop most of us buying an OLED. The aforementioned worst-case scenario usage includes driving the panel hard in terms of brightness settings, routine use of persistent interface elements and even failing to allow burn-in mitigation measures to run optimally. Basically, doing all the wrong things. And the evidence is that even this doesn't lead to truly catastrophic burn-in.

After a year of really heavy daily use, you'll probably see some very slight burn-in that's only visible if you go out of your way to look for it. In the second and third years, the burn-in will become more apparent, but only gradually, and even then it will likely still only be visible in certain situations, like an application window with a uniform grey background. The panel will also experience a touch of overall brightness drop off, but not enough to be subjectively noticeable.

All of that applies to early OLED monitors released three to four years ago, starting with the Alienware 34 AW3423DW. By definition, it hasn't been possible to test recently released panel generations over several years. But each new generation has arrived with promises of improved burn-in resistance.

If that's an overview of generic OLED tech, what about the specifics of the stuff available to buy? It turns out all OLED gaming monitors use panels from just two sources, LG and Samsung. Strictly, there is a new player just arriving on the scene, TCL CSOT, and with it a novel so-called inkjet OLED tech. But there's little to nothing you can actually buy right now with that technology in the gaming space.

So, LG and Samsung it is. LG's tech has broadly been branded "WOLED", where the "W" stands for "white", while Samsung's panels have been branded "QD-OLED", with the "QD" referring to quantum dot technology. Whether you buy an OLED monitor from Asus, MSI, Alienware or anywhere else, it'll use one of those two basic panel types.

Indeed, until recently, it's been relatively simple to generalise about the two options. LG's WOLED panels used an additional white subpixel to boost peak brightness. Samsung's QD-OLED, meanwhile, had the edge for colour volume and vibrancy, thanks to its strategy of sticking with standard red, green and blue subpixels. Quantum dot tech also more efficiently converts the blue light generated by Samsung's emissive OLED layers into coloured light, compared to the simple filters LG uses to convert white light into coloured light.

Both shared essentially the same near-instantaneous 0.03ms pixel response. Combine that with high refresh rates and the result is razor-sharp motion clarity. However, there were and remain some further detailed differences. Samsung QD-OLED suffered from so-called purple tinting in bright ambient light due to the structure of the panel and the use of quantum dots, which can be activated by ambient light. Basically, stick one of the first three generations of QD-OLED panel next to a window on a bright day and it turns a bit purpley-grey, compromising black levels and overall contrast. On the other hand, the colours in LG's WOLEDs could look a little washed out, due to the use of that white subpixel to boost brightness.

Over those early panel generations, LG also had the edge when it came to image sharpness. Computer OSes like Windows function under the assumption that the subpixels in a digital display are arranged in a vertical RGB-stripe arrangement. LG's "RGWB" stripe, or red, green, white and blue subpixels arranged in vertical stripes, was much closer to that ideal RGB-stripe than Samsung's QD-OLED panels, which had an oddly triangular RGB subpixel structure. The upshot was that those triangular subpixel QD-OLED panels suffered from text fringing and a very slight softness in image quality, comparatively.

However, much of that has changed for the most recent generations of panels released in the last six to 12 months. The hot new thing, at least in terms of branding, is "tandem" OLED technology. Thus, LG has its new "Tandem WOLED" panels and Samsung has "Penta Tandem QD-OLED" tech.

In both cases, you might imagine the "tandem" bit implies fully dual-layer OLED panels, like those used by Apple in its iPad Pro tablets and the new PROLED MacBook Pro display. But actually, it refers to added emissive layers within the subpixels. LG's latest panels actually now have four layers, while Samsung's have five, hence the latter's pointed "Penta" branding flex. Either way, the added layers are claimed to increase brightness and improve durability.

Early monitor models based on either OLED panel type (like the aforementioned OG Alienware) tended to top out at around 250 nits of full-screen brightness, which I'd argue wasn't really good enough. The newer "tandem" generations have upped that to 300 and 335 nits for Samsung and LG respectively, which is closing in on the 400 nits I personally think is required to consider the full-screen brightness problem solved. Just note those figures are for pure white brightness, a metric that naturally favours LG's panels with their added white subpixel.

When it comes to colour brightness - and, in practice, most images you look at in-game are coloured, not pure white - Samsung's QD-OLED tech is often brighter. Don't, then, assume a higher outright brightness rating means brighter games. It's more complicated than that. The arrival of the Samsung Penta Tandem QD-OLED generation also brought with it an improvement, but not a total eradication, of the purple-tint issue in bright ambient light.

If you've followed all that. and honestly, props if you have, I'm afraid to say this is where things get really baffling. Both LG and Samsung have also released pure RGB-stripe subpixel variants of their newest "tandem" OLED panel tech. For LG, that means dropping the white subpixel for the sake of both better colour volume and more precise rendering, but slightly to the detriment of peak brightness. For Samsung, that means a switch from the triangular subpixel arrangement to vertical RGB-stripe and mostly just an improvement in pixel precision and text quality.

Not all of these panel types are available in all shapes, sizes and resolutions. But it gets confusing really fast. Right now, for instance, there are no fewer than six different 32-inch 4K OLED panel types to choose from. From LG you can have its original RGWB WOLED in, say, the Asus ROG Swift PG32UCDP, the new RGWB Tandem WOLED (for instance, the Asus ROG Strix XG32UQWMS) and then finally the RGB-stripe variation on Tandem WOLED ( see the Asus ROG Swift PG32UCWM). From Samsung, meanwhile, you can choose between its original triangular subpixel QD-OLED (like on the MSI MAG 321UP), the Penta Tandem follow up (of the MSI MPG 321UP X24) and then the all-singing RGB-stripe Penta Tandem (of the MSI MPG OLED 322URDX36, which is just about to be released).

Just to complicate things even further, LG's RGB-stripe "tandem" panels are still branded "WOLED", because the "W" in WOLED doesn't and has never referred to the white subpixels LG had hitherto used, but instead the white colour of the light produced by the emissive layers within each subpixel before conversion by the RGB filters. Got that? Phew.

The long and short of all this is that if you're comparing the very latest "tandem RGB-stripe" panels from LG and Samsung, they are closer now in terms of the subjective experience than ever before. Up until this generation, I always favoured QD-OLED for its superior colour vibrancy while accepting the purple tint issue. But as I peck out these words on the new 39-inch 5K2K Alienware AW3926QW and its Tandem WOLED RGB-stripe panel, I think I might just lean LG right now for its superior contrast in bright ambient light conditions, as well as this slightly more neutral subjective colour balance.

Anyway, as much as all that may feel like an exhaustive (and exhausting) dissertation, there is actually a fair bit I don't have space to fully cover. There are slight variations in the panel types, for instance, in terms of issues like uniformity and graininess, colour shift as opposed to outright burn-in, and also OLED flicker. But I wouldn't consider any of those even near to being deal breakers.

Really, it's just the long-term outlook that would have me thinking twice. First, that involves burn-in. I'd be pretty confident of getting at least three years out of an OLED. The big brands all have three year burn-in cover in any case (which is a very good reason to favour a big, familiar brand that you can be fairly certain will honour that warranty down the road). And it wouldn't surprise me if five years was very doable with the latest panels. The catch is that you can't, currently, be entirely sure about that.

Second, OLED technology is developing fast. It's come a long way in the last few years and I suspect that the next few will see comparable progress. Two or three years from now, I think full-screen brightness will likely be a totally solved problem, for instance, burn-in will be barely any concern at all, and prices will have come down even further. Ultimately, however, I wouldn't let that expectation stop me buying an OLED. It may not be entirely without flaws. But OLED is still by far the best PC display tech available today.
