Screen Tenants by a Standard You Wrote Before Listing – Not by First Impressions

Before you list a rental, write down the income, credit, landlord references, and eviction history an applicant needs. Then verify each one for every applicant instead of taking the application’s word for it. One written standard helps you pick tenants who pay and keeps your decisions within fair housing law.

Turning away the wrong applicant costs a little more time with the unit empty. Removing the wrong tenant can take a court case and months of lost rent.

A standard set before you meet anyone keeps a good first impression from outweighing a bad rental history. Say an applicant charms you at the showing, but their last landlord reports two months of unpaid rent. A standard that asks for on-time rent at the last two rentals rules them out, and the unit goes to the next complete application.

The Fair Housing Act bars most landlords from turning anyone away because of race, color, religion, sex, national origin, disability, or having children under 18. A standard applied the same way to everyone is your record of why you turned someone down. In many states and cities, source-of-income laws also bar refusing an applicant whose housing voucher pays part of the rent.

If you check criminal records, write down which convictions count and how far back you’ll look, within your state’s and city’s rules. New Jersey and New York City make most landlords wait to run a criminal check until they’ve offered the unit, pending its result, to an applicant who meets the rest of the standard.

Use one written application for everyone, and keep its fee within what your state allows.

Hand every applicant the standard in writing before you take a fee or run a check. Washington requires landlords to share their criteria, in writing or posted, before any screening, and elsewhere it spares an applicant who can’t qualify a wasted fee.
