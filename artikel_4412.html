Save Now, Pay Later: Why It Makes Sense to Buy a Less Expensive Old Car

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Life is too short to wait for the perfect vehicle.

I have a bit of automotive ADHD. I can’t stick with a single car for too long. I get bored, reach my wit’s end with throwing money into it, or simply come across a good deal through my endless scouring of Facebook Marketplace and Craigslist. That means I’m constantly switching cars, and because of my limited wealth, those cars generally fall into the category I’d affectionately describe as shitboxes.

This story originally appeared in Volume 38 of Road & Track.

Look, I don’t want to buy shitboxes. It’s just the best course of action if I want to experience as many cars as possible for as reasonable a price as possible. Buying shitboxes is not for the faint of heart. Here’s how I do it and why.

When I’m searching for my next deal, I look for clean-title cars, ideally without fatal rust or debilitating powertrain issues. Pretty much every other problem—trivialities such as cosmetics and air-­conditioning—is acceptable. That mind-set is how I’ve proudly managed to own three different generations of BMW M5 and a 270,000-mile Lexus GX470 that I drove from New York to Alaska and back without issue. The shitbox mentality gets the job done, especially with Aughts enthusiast icons, which still offer modern convenience and potential reliability at the tail end of the depreciation curve.

Most recently­, I landed myself a 2009 Mercedes-­Benz G550 for about half off market value.

That story began on a slow Thursday afternoon, while I was letting the Marketplace algorithm feed me its best. The G550 popped up, and I offered the seller $3500 under asking. Instead of telling me to kick rocks, he wrote, “If you are serious huyer [sic] bring me cash today and it’s yours.” Guess what I did.

With payment cobbled together from my checking account and, to avoid the delay of a bank transfer, cash loans from a few friends who share my affliction, I drove five hours from New York through torrential rain and handed over the money in a dark lot in Northern Virginia late at night. It was sketchy and awesome. Now I own a G550 that’s stuck in second gear and has a sunroof flanked by worrisome rust holes.

Aside from paying less up front, if you buy a car at its floor, you’re only going to sell it for more. Probably. And when it comes to maintenance costs, I would rather cheap out on the purchase price, knowing what’s already broken, than drop double that on a better example and wait for something to inevitably break. Also, I could pay cash in full for the vehicle and then fix the issues I want to fix over time while enjoying what I’m able to enjoy about the car along the way. All with the assurance that whatever had to be fixed was completed under my supervision and done correctly. According to my shitbox math, it makes sense.

I turned 30 this summer. By a conservative estimate, I’ve owned at least 30 cars. But I’m not in the game of flipping. For me, it’s all about having as many adventures as I can behind the wheel of the greatest cars that have graced this world’s roads before they explode in value on Bring a Trailer’s auction pages. Holding out for perfection is not an option.

Road & Track’s digital director is often surrounded by beat-up project cars. His fleet currently consists of a problematic manual-swapped 1991 BMW 325i sedan and a Mercedes G550. Before R&T, Brown worked at Jalopnik, The Drive, and Business Insider.
