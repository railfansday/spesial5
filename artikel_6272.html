Climate change is unfolding very differently just inches above the ground

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Microclimate research finds climate change can warm one slope while cooling another nearby, and most wildlife lives in that near-ground air.

Climate change usually arrives as one number for a whole region: so many degrees warmer than it used to be.

A regional average may reveal little about the conditions a beetle, seedling or toad experiences near the ground.

New research finds that air near the ground can warm much faster than the regional average, barely at all, or even cool while the region heats up.

The clearest example in the review is a patch of coast in southwest England about 1.2 miles (2 kilometers) on a side.

A computer reconstruction of its hottest days from 1983 to 2015 put the regional warming at about half a degree Fahrenheit (0.3°C). Inside that patch, one spot warmed by 5.2°F (2.9°C) and another cooled by 2.3°F (1.3°C).

Same coast, same three decades, opposite directions.

Caroline Greiser, a researcher at Stockholm University and its Bolin Centre for Climate Research, led the review with 20 colleagues from Europe, South Africa and the United States.

They call this near-ground climate the microclimate, and it doesn’t change at the same rate as the climate around it.

“Climate change is often described as a single number for a region. But organisms live in specific places – in a particular patch of forest or on a particular slope. The climate they experience can change at a very different rate from the regional average,” Greiser said.

The regional number comes from weather stations, which are deliberately placed in open spots away from trees and slopes.

It also comes from climate models that work on grid squares 6 to 60 miles (10 to 100 kilometers) across. Neither can see the shade under a shrub.

The review doesn’t report new field measurements.

It pulls together published work from atmospheric science and ecology and adds three computer simulations of real landscapes, run with models built by Ilya Maclean of the University of Exeter.

The simulations track the air 2 inches (5 centimeters) above the ground, where many small land animals and seedlings live.

The physics is simple to state. Sunlight that reaches the ground either warms the surface and the air above it, evaporates water, or gets stored in soil and plants.

Anything that shifts that split shifts the temperature a small creature feels.

A 2022 study found that daytime cloud cover over Europe fell by roughly 1.5% to 2% between the periods 1980 to 1995 and 2000 to 2015.

Extra sun on a clear day lands far harder on a south-facing slope than on a forest floor, so an uneven landscape gets more uneven as the clouds thin out.

At night, clouds catch heat rising off the ground and send part of it back down, which can keep near-ground temperatures warmer than on a clear night.

On a clear, calm night, cold air drains downhill and collects in hollows, and a valley bottom can frost while the slope above it doesn’t.

The team’s simulation of a landscape in the Spanish Pyrenees put numbers on this.

Doubling the cloud cover instead of halving it lowered near-ground temperatures on the hottest days of the year by about 22°F (12.4°C) on average, with the strongest cooling on open ground.

The same change in the sky cooled some patches far more than others.

Snow mattered most on the coldest nights. A snow blanket raised minimum near-ground temperatures by about 5.4°F (3°C) on average, roughly ten times the effect of wind or air humidity.

Where winter snow is disappearing, that insulation goes with it.

Warming is pushing shrubs and trees into grasslands and tundra in many parts of the world.

The review’s third simulation took a 0.4-square-mile (1-square-kilometer) patch of the Anatolian steppe in Turkey and filled it in with the shrubs and trees growing at a nearby site.

On the hottest days of the year, near-ground temperatures fell by up to 30°F (16.6°C) under tall, dense shrubs and trees, while open patches where the model made the grass shorter warmed by up to 11°F (6°C).

The same landscape, under the same sky, went two directions at once.

“Vegetation is not simply responding to climate change – it can also modify the climate experienced by organisms living under canopies,” Greiser said.

“When vegetation changes, it changes the local energy balance, and this can lead to very different temperature trends within the same landscape.”

Smaller shifts are already under way. In a mixed forest in New Hampshire, spring leaves came out earlier between 1982 and 2019.

The review’s second simulation found that the extra April leaf cover changed the forest floor’s minimum and maximum temperatures by only about a third of a degree Fahrenheit (0.2°C) each.

The shade can also vanish fast. Droughts, insect outbreaks, fires and storms strip canopies, and a single disturbance can expose a forest floor to conditions equal to several decades of regional warming at once.

Earth.com has reported the same pattern after bark beetle outbreaks and in fragmented forests.

Which plants take over matters too. In a large tree-planting experiment in China, plots with 24 tree species grew denser canopies than single-species plots, and the ground beneath them was up to 7.9°F (4.4°C) cooler.

In the Arctic, shrubs replacing pale lichen can reflect only half as much sunlight, because dark vegetation absorbs far more than lichen or snow.

Jonas Lembrechts of the University of Antwerp is a senior author on the review.

He and his co-authors point to the growing number of long-term near-ground temperature records as the way to check simulations like these against measurements taken on the ground.

Cool pockets, such as forested slopes and basins where cold air pools, might warm more slowly than the open ground beside them. The review finds the evidence for that mixed.

Forty-five years of records from a forested mountain watershed showed little difference in long-term warming between sites, despite frequent cold-air pooling.

Across Austria, by contrast, winter temperature inversions became 11% less frequent between 1961 and 2017, and low-lying sites warmed faster as a result.

The mixed evidence is the practical problem. Predictions of where species can live are mostly built from regional climate data.

The authors argue those predictions will keep misjudging which populations are safe until they account for what happens at ground level.

“If we want to understand where species will persist and where they will be most vulnerable, we need to know not only how the regional climate is changing, but also how that change translates into the microclimates organisms actually experience,” Greiser said.

The simulations changed one thing at a time, clouds or wind or snow, while holding everything else steady.

Real weather changes several of them at once, so the team describes the runs as illustrations of single mechanisms rather than forecasts.

The landscape model also leaves out cold air draining downhill, so real patterns are probably patchier than the maps show.

The review also deals mostly in averages, which the authors wrote can hide the heat waves and frosts that actually push a species out of a place.

The hardest open problem they name is physical.

How heat and moisture move through a tangle of branches and leaves is still hard to quantify, so how strongly vegetation shields or amplifies warming remains uncertain.

Long-term records that track vegetation and near-ground temperature together, in the same places, are what would settle it. Those paired records remain rare.

“The processes that determine microclimate are complex and involve both atmospheric and biological systems. Understanding them therefore requires collaboration between atmospheric scientists and ecologists,” Greiser said.

The full study was published in the journal Global Change Biology.
