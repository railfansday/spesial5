Bitcoin could face a cash squeeze without another Fed rate hike

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

The November 12 update uses Q2 2026 data, pointing to a potential financing channel that could matter before another Fed hike.

A New York Fed study published Nov. 12 puts modeled US bank liquidity stress at a level last seen in 2014, highlighting a funding vulnerability that could curb Bitcoin risk-taking even without another Fed hike.

The research update uses bank holding company data through the second quarter of 2026. Its liquidity stress ratio remained moderate by historical standards, at slightly above half its third-quarter 2007 peak.

The ratio compares liabilities weighted for their likelihood of withdrawal, plus off-balance-sheet exposures, with assets weighted for liquidity. Its rise since early 2022 reflects a decline in liquid assets alongside more unstable funding and off-balance-sheet exposures. That leaves banks more exposed if funding becomes harder to retain.

A separate capital measure tests a repeat of the 2022 rate shock. The modeled capital gap was $151 billion in Q2 2026, down from $161 billion a year earlier. It measures capital needed after the hypothetical shock to bring each bank's Tier 1 common equity to at least 10% of risk-weighted assets, fully reflecting unrealized securities gains and losses. That is a model benchmark, not evidence of current realized losses or a regulatory breach.

The potential transmission runs through financing and risk budgets. Banks facing more expensive or less dependable funding could become more selective about lending. Financial intermediaries dependent on that credit could then have less capacity or willingness to hold risky assets, including Bitcoin.

The Q2 2026 model shows a funding mismatch. Whether it becomes a Bitcoin squeeze depends on financing conditions.

Illustrative condition: wider funding and credit spreads alongside weaker Bitcoin financing would support the transmission case.

Illustrative condition: stable funding and financing would weaken the case that modeled bank vulnerability is squeezing Bitcoin.

A separate Fed funding-shock study published in February explains how weaker capital measured at market values and reliance on uninsured deposits can raise banks' funding costs. Its results depend on the scenario: the added shock hurts profitability and capital under stagflation, but has minimal effects in a severe recession with sharply falling rates.

There is already evidence of selective credit restraint. The Fed's October lending survey, covering conditions generally in the third quarter, reported tighter approval standards on net across every queried nonbank financial borrower category. Large and middle-market business-loan standards were basically unchanged. CryptoSlate previously examined the possible implications for Bitcoin.

The risk-appetite link has historical support. An August 2023 IMF working paper found that US monetary tightening reduced a common crypto price factor through a risk-taking channel similar to equities. Applying that channel to the new bank-vulnerability findings remains an inference.

The Nov. 12 H.15 release lists effective federal funds at 3.88% for Nov. 11 and the 10-year Treasury yield at 4.98% for Nov. 10. Treasury markets were closed Nov. 11. Those benchmark rates are only part of financing conditions: banks can also restrict access to credit.

For Bitcoin, the next test is whether the modeled vulnerability becomes tighter financing. Widening funding and credit spreads alongside weaker Bitcoin financing would strengthen that case; stable funding conditions would weaken it.
