Xreal Aura Review: Stunning Screens, Frustrating Controls

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Smart glasses company Xreal has released its magnum opus—the Xreal Aura, a $1,279 pair of extended-reality glasses that runs on Google’s Android XR platform. The Aura glasses can project virtual screens into the wearer’s field of vision and include augmented-reality elements for use in the real world.

The Aura glasses arrive just before the first wave of Android XR glasses from Google and Samsung, in partnership with glasses manufacturers Warby Parker and Gentle Monster. Those devices aim squarely at Meta’s smart-glasses empire. But the Aura glasses are more like virtual reality headsets, now in a more comfortable, glasses-like design. They go head-to-head with Meta’s VR Glasses, slated for release in early 2027.

The first big caveat about the Aura glasses is that they’re wired. A long cord hooks onto the end of one of the glasses’ arms via a USB-C cable. That cord connects to an attached compute puck, about the size of a smartphone and weighing a little more than a half-pound. It comes in 12 or 16 GB storage options and has a battery that powers the glasses for roughly four to five hours with constant use.

When the Aura glasses work, they’re wonderful. The glasses are pretty lightweight, at 95 grams. A four-mic array sits in the arms with Bose EQ tuning that sounds great. (You can also connect wireless headphones.) The display screen has a 1920 x 1200-pixel-per-eye resolution that is noticeably better than other Xreal glasses I’ve worn. A 70-degree field of view means the screens and virtual elements pop up in a wide frame of the wearer’s vision. That means the outer edges of your view will still be the real-life room around you. That can help if you’re trying not to fall over, but it can also be noticeable when trying to feel immersed in a movie.

The big thing about Aura is that you can, in theory, run every app that already exists on Android. Xreal has a few primary categories for how it expects people to use its Aura glasses. The big one is entertainment. Like other Xreal glasses, the Aura can project a screen right in your field of vision. You can watch movies, TV shows, or YouTube videos, right in your field of view. The virtual screen stays fixed, or it can follow you around as you turn your head. You can also unplug the glasses from the compute puck and use them as display glasses to extend your Steam Deck’s screen, for example, though I had issues with that when playing more graphics-intensive games like Cyberpunk 2077.

The Aura glasses attach to a compute puck for processing, which also doubles as a battery.

The other big use case is productivity. The Aura glasses can host virtual workstations that let you swap between tabs and keep multiple tasks running. So you can have a Google Doc right in front of you, Spotify to the side, and those Slack messages you keep ignoring in the back. If you’ve ever wished you had even more space to stash all your browser tabs, this is the device for you. Pair it with a Bluetooth keyboard, and you’ll have a whole workstation on your face, even on a plane, without having to crane your neck awkwardly at a laptop. I wrote almost half this review with the glasses as my screen, but eventually swapped back to a regular desktop to give my eyes a rest.

There are also augmented reality experiences, like using Google Earth to turn a room into a 3D model of a city that you can stomp around in like Godzilla, plus immersive virtual tours so you can see exotic places like the Galapagos.

Two lenses are set in the frame, one smaller lens behind the other. The smaller lens carries the display and AR elements, popping them up within a 70-degree field of view. The outer lens can go from perfectly see-through to very dark. Turning up the darkness makes it easier to see the projected virtual screens. Turning the darkness down enables a much clearer pass-through, which can help you avoid tripping over things while walking around.

That said, these glasses aren’t really meant to be worn while walking around. Even with no AR elements enabled, the double-lens layout feels distracting. There is also no way around the fact that these glasses are still pretty chunky. If you can rock the thicc fit, then great, but I’ve gotten some pointed stares when I walk around with them.

The compute puck can be used as a trackpad to navigate the interface, and it also has a fingerprint sensor.

The glasses do feel lightweight, but after a few minutes of use, they start to heat up. When I take them off, they leave red divots on the bridge of my nose. It’s a small price to pay, though, and it doesn’t ruin the experience. Compare that to something like a VR headset, which does offer better immersion but at the cost of being heavy and uncomfortable to wear for long stretches.

The compute puck can be an annoyance. It’s light enough, but using the Aura glasses means you have to carry yet another thing that is strapped to the device on your face. There were some smart choices made on the puck, like a fingerprint sensor for biometric authentication, and the puck’s surface works like a trackpad to move the onscreen cursor. That seems much smoother than reaching out to control the device with hand movements, but the trackpad wasn't very precise either. Which is a shame, as it would be ideal for someone on an airplane, say, who wants to control the device without punching the seat in front of them.

Controls are the biggest problem I’ve had with the Aura. The front cameras track your hand movements. Hold out your hand and pinch two fingers together to open up an app or move a window. It works most of the time, but some gestures don’t always register. I find myself trying to open Spotify or switch between titles on Prime Video and have to repeatedly pinch the air in front of me for anything to happen. When people have been around to see me, they’ve told me I look like I’ve gone bonkers, just grunting and wildly pinching thin air.

The Xreal Aura glasses are a stunning display of how far smart-glasses technology has come. But they still feel like a novelty that most people won’t want or need. They haven’t quite solved the core issues facing the product category—looking like a dork or being effortlessly comfortable to wear. But they do feel like a big step in the right direction. If only they could get a pinch more usable.
