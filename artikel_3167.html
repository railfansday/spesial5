How Often Do You Need To Scrub The Do Not Call List? The 31-Day Rule Explained

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Phil Portman is a serial entrepreneur and the Founder & CEO of Textdrip — a small business SMS marketing tool to automate SMS campaigns.

​I see businesses repeatedly making the same mistake: They scrub their contact list once and then keep calling and texting from that list for months. That’s a risky habit, because every day new numbers join the Do Not Call Registry. A list that was clean in January can be full of problems by March.

After years of experience working in a regulated industry, I’m sure about one thing: Businesses rarely fail because the rules are unclear. They fail because their processes break down in practice. The Do Not Call rules are a crystal-clear example of this.

How often do you have to scrub the Do Not Call (DNC) list? Every 31 days. To keep safe harbor protection, you need to scrub against a version of the registry that is no more than 31 days old. In practice, that means every month.

The National Do Not Call Registry is run by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC). It lets people add their personal phone numbers to a list that tells telemarketers to stop contacting them. It exists to protect consumers who don’t want sales calls. It covers personal numbers, such as home and mobile lines. It doesn’t cover business-to-business calls to a company line. Knowing this difference helps you decide which numbers need a check.

Many teams assume it only covers voice calls, but it doesn’t. The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) treats text messages as calls, so the same protections apply to SMS marketing. If you text prospects, those numbers also need a DNC check.

State lists exist as well. Some states keep their own Do Not Call lists, and some, such as Florida and Texas, have stricter rules than the federal registry. If you contact people in several states, you need to check each state’s rules before you launch.

Some calls are exempt, such as those to people who gave written permission or have an established business relationship with you. Even then, you must honor opt-out requests.

To qualify for safe harbor under FTC and FCC regulations, telemarketers must scrub their list against the National DNC Registry. Every call must be made using a registry version downloaded no more than 31 days earlier. Simply put, you must rescrub every month. If it’s day 32, you’re already at risk.

Suppose you scrubbed a list on the first of the month. On the 20th, a prospect joins the registry. Your team keeps working that same list. Any call made after day 31 relies on an outdated version. Many teams scrub only when they upload new leads. This sounds fine, but a campaign can run for weeks, and a number can join the registry halfway through. If you keep contacting that number, you’re working from outdated data.

Safe harbor is a defense that protects a business when a call goes out by mistake. But it only applies if you had a working process in place before the mistake happened. A written policy that nobody reads or follows won’t help. Training without a current list won’t help either.

The FCC rule lists what a DNC safe harbor process needs:

• An internal Do Not Call list that is kept up to date.

• Registry access used to scrub within the 31-day window.

• Logs (records) showing when each scrub happened.

Record-keeping is a common gap. Without records, you have no safe harbor, even if the scrub did happen. If a regulator or a lawyer asks for proof, saying “we did it” won’t be enough. You need proof (dates and logs).

Do Not Call violations get expensive quickly. Under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), a consumer can sue for $500 per violation. If the violation was willful or knowing, a court can raise that to $1,500 per violation. A consumer generally must receive more than one call from the same business within 12 months to bring a DNC claim.

Some of these claims are filed as class actions. One outdated list can hold thousands of people, and each one may have a claim. A small gap in your process can turn into a very large bill. Legal fees and settlements add to that, along with damage to your business reputation.

I lead a company in a regulated industry, so I think about this often. Compliance works best when it is practiced as a routine instead of an event. We built Landline Remover to help teams make scrubbing a routine.

1. Scrub every time you upload leads, not just on launch day.

2. Rescrub active lists monthly, ideally a few days before the 31-day window closes.

3. Check your internal list and the national list together. Your internal do-not-call list holds people who told you directly to stop. The registry doesn’t know about them. Both lists matter.

4. Check reassigned numbers. Numbers change owners. The FCC’s reassigned numbers database can tell you whether a number has changed hands since you got consent.

5. Log every scrub with the date. That log is your proof if a court or regulator asks.

6. Automate the scrubbing process so it doesn’t depend on memory.

Do Not Call compliance is a monthly habit, not a one-time task. The rule is simple: scrub within 31 days, every time.

DNC scrubbing alone can’t protect a business. You need to keep proof that you followed the rule. Scrubbing only protects you if you can prove it. So build a regular scrubbing habit and keep the records. Remember that regulators don’t just look at your efforts—they ask for evidence.
