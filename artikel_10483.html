Without your audience in the room, your brand is guesswork

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

A group of experienced executives is discussing how the brand can “connect with Gen Z.” Everyone has a deck and an opinion. But nobody in the room is Gen Z. I’ve sat in this type of meeting more times than I’d like to admit.

We collect demographic reports, analyze trends, and increasingly ask AI to tell us who our audiences are and what they want. But this goes wrong when we allow it to replace conversations.

A generation is not a community. A community is not an individual. People born in the same years can have almost nothing in common beyond their age. Demographics describe groups, not the individuals within them.

I’m more interested in what they choose and why. What do they dedicate their time to? Where do they gather? Why do they feel like they belong there? You cannot design your way into cultural relevance without first understanding the culture.

FEELINGS INSTEAD OF DATA

Pep Salazar has spent more than two decades learning this lesson from another perspective. As cofounder and director of OFFF, the international creativity and design festival that began in Barcelona over 25 years ago, he has helped turn a gathering of digital creatives into an enduring international community.

When we spoke recently, Pep described the tension between knowing an audience through metrics and understanding it as people. Sponsors want attendance numbers, engagement reports, and evidence of reach. Those figures do offer important insights, but they don’t explain why someone travels to Barcelona to spend three days among fellow creatives.

For him, it’s more interesting to understand feelings rather than data. A dashboard can tell you what people clicked. A conversation can tell you why they care.

Pep wants people to spend time together and talk. But his approach goes further. He also learns by taking risks. Pep deliberately introduces new perspectives and ideas that challenge what his audience already knows and expects. Not everything resonates immediately, but that’s part of the process. As he puts it, “I understand by doing.”

UNDERSTAND BY DOING

That’s an important distinction for brands. Understanding your audience doesn’t mean always giving people more of what they already know and love. It means knowing when to experiment and introducing something unexpected. And learning from the response.

When inviting a target audience into the process, you don’t have to outsource every decision to them. You can develop the judgment to understand when to respond to a community, and when to challenge it with something unfamiliar.

Pep also points to another common mistake brands make when trying to connect with creative communities: Don’t show up only to sell. He has watched brands arrive at OFFF with polished corporate messaging, while missing the social dynamics of the event. His advice is simple: “Come here to understand the people and to talk to them.”

LANGUAGE AND SOUL

He also worries that generative AI is making corporate language more interchangeable. “When you speak similar to your neighbor, then you don’t have a soul,” he told me.

I recognize that problem from my own work. Too many brands begin by studying the competition and end up borrowing its language, aesthetics, and assumptions. The result may look professional, but it rarely feels personal and unique. People want brands with personality that speak the language of their community.

So the next time people in a boardroom start debating how to reach a particular audience, ask who is missing in the room. Invite your audience in before the strategy is set in stone. Spend time where they gather. Make room for opinions and responses that might contradict your brief and give those perspectives the power to change the outcome.

Otherwise, the empty chair at the table says more about your brand than the strategy deck ever will.

Erik Herrström is founder and creative director of STUDIO HERRSTRÖM.
