Spain’s election could pull Madrid closer to Trump’s Washington

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Spanish voters head to the polls on Sunday for a snap election that could bring down socialist Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez, who has repeatedly clashed with U.S. President Donald Trump.

It could also put the far right in government for the first time in the country's modern democratic era.

The center-right Popular Party is the frontrunner, and it could win a majority with far-right Vox, according to El País's final polling averages. A government led by the PP could shift Spain's approach to Washington, Beijing and NATO.

It's the first test of whether Europe's mainstream conservatives are willing to bring the far right into government, ahead of elections in France and Poland next year.

Despite Spain's economy growing 2.7% year over year in the third quarter, more than twice the euro zone's 1.3%, the campaign has been dominated by housing and cost-of-living crises.

Inflation soared to 4.9% in the year to October, above a euro zone average of 4%. November's flash estimate published Friday showed Spanish inflation cooling to 4.1%, still well above the 2.3% reading at the start of the year. In an October YouGov survey, 53% of respondents named housing one of the country's two most important issues.

Home purchase prices nearly doubled in the decade to the second quarter of 2026, according to Eurostat. In 2025, house prices rose nearly three times as fast as household incomes, the European Commission said.

Nationwide protests began after an 87-year-old woman died two weeks after being evicted from her Madrid apartment in September.

Sánchez called the snap election after lawmakers rejected two government decrees designed to broaden housing protections, including limiting rent increases and blocking certain evictions and automatic lease renewals. Congress later ratified a revised version of one decree, limiting rent increases and evictions in some circumstances.

Sánchez has made a name for himself internationally after clashing with Trump over NATO spending, the war in Gaza and Spain's refusal to let U.S. forces use its bases for operations against Iran. Trump even threatened to cut off "all trade" with Spain during the dispute over the bases.

At a Nov. 21 rally in Tenerife, Sánchez asked: "What good has President Trump brought to the United States and, by extension, to the world?"

He has also told voters that the PP and Vox would roll back government cost-of-living assistance. Days before the election, Sánchez's government approved 1.21 billion euros ($1.33 billion) to extend public transport discounts through 2027 and approved a general pay increase of up to 4.5% for 2027.

But in a final pre-election survey for El Español on Nov. 23, 66% of respondents gave Sánchez a failing grade and 29.5% a passing one. His government has faced scrutiny over its handling of the Ceuta migrant crisis, two deadly train collisions this year and corruption cases involving people close to the prime minister.

PP leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo, the current favorite to replace Sánchez, has made alleged government corruption and inflation central to his campaign. At a Nov. 22 rally in Murcia, he said the government wanted voters to forget "that inflation is eating into your pay check, that you can't afford a home, that you're paying more than ever for everything."

The PP led the final El País polling average, published Nov. 24, with 31.4%, followed by Sánchez's Socialists at 29.1% and Vox at 17.9%. Several seat projections put the PP and Vox above the 176 seats needed for a majority in the 350-seat lower house. If Vox joins a government, it would be the first far-right party to do so since Spain returned to democracy after Francisco Franco, the dictator who ruled for 40 years, died in 1975.

The PP wants to repeal the 2023 housing law and scrap rent caps in designated high-pressure areas. It proposes speeding up construction permits and lowering taxes on first homes for buyers under 40. Vox also wants to repeal the housing law and would prioritize Spanish citizens for subsidized housing and public services.

Sánchez's Socialists want to extend renter protections and make at least a quarter of new homes public or permanently price-restricted. It would use proceeds from a bank levy to support housing, which it says will raise a further $1.5 billion. The PP has pledged to scrap this.

The PP also favors easing climate rules, extending the life of nuclear plants and replacing a decree that sets tight new renewable-power requirements on data centers with large new grid connections.

A PP-Vox coalition would have its tensions, with immigration likely to be a major pressure point as Vox has an increasingly hard line on deportation and the militarization of some borders. The parties clashed in regional governments over the distribution of unaccompanied migrant children, leading Vox to quit several coalitions in 2024.

Immigration has since gained more prominence amid the government's handling of reports that more than 70,000 people crossed from Morocco into the Spanish enclave of Ceuta on July 30 and 31.

"Immigration has played a significant role in supporting Spain's recent growth, a strong role for Vox could complicate the government's ability to sustain that," said Jess Middleton, senior Europe analyst at risk intelligence company Verisk Maplecroft.

The parties also differ on defense commitments: both support more investment, but neither has committed to NATO's target of spending 5% of GDP on defense. The PP pledges closer NATO cooperation, while Vox puts greater emphasis on border security.

The PP favors closer integration with the European Union, while Vox wants more independence from it. The success or failure of a PP-Vox partnership will also have wider lessons for Europe "where long-standing firewalls against far-right parties are coming under strain," added Middleton, pointing to the rise of Germany's AfD and the prospect of Marine Le Pen potentially winning next year's election in France.

A shift to the right could improve relations between the U.S. and Spain. Sánchez used his tenure to strengthen ties with Beijing, the country's largest goods trading partner outside the EU.

The U.S. remained Spain's largest source of foreign direct investment. Flows more than doubled to 11.6 billion euros in the 12 months through June 2026, from 5.1 billion euros in the previous 12 months, according to Spain's Economy Ministry.

Carla Hobbs, head of the European Council on Foreign Relations' Madrid office, expects a PP-led government to seek closer ties with Washington and bring its China policy more in line with the EU's. Vox could complicate that. The first test for Washington would be whether a new government changes Spain's position on defense spending or the use of its bases.
