Client insists on lying in loan application? Do ‘noisy withdrawal,’ new ABA ethics opinion says

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

A recent ABA ethics opinion addresses the intersection of client confidentiality and direct knowledge that a client is about to commit crime or fraud.

Formal Opinion 526, published Nov. 18, focuses on ABA Model Rules 4.1(b) and 1.6(b). Rule 4.1(b) prohibits a lawyer, in representing a client, from failing to disclose a material fact when it is necessary to avoid assisting a client’s criminal or fraudulent act. However, the rule says there can be no disclosure if the “disclosure is prohibited by Rule 1.6.”

In turn, Rule 1.6 generally prohibits a lawyer from disclosing client information unless the lawyer believes that the disclosure will prevent a client from committing crime or fraud that will cause substantial injury to a third party. If this scenario is met, Rule 1.6(b) permits disclosure.

The opinion cites the example of a lawyer who represents a borrower in a lending transaction. The borrower asks the lawyer to provide the lender an evaluation about whether the borrower had adequate collateral for a loan. The lawyer does so, but then later learns that the borrower has no such collateral.

In this situation, the lawyer should first try to convince his client, the borrower, to come clean and rectify the situation. If the client will not, then the lawyer is required to disclose that material fact under Rule 4.1(b). This often requires the lawyer to withdraw under Rule 1.16(a). The withdrawal often must be a so-called noisy withdrawal, which notifies the lender or defrauded party that there is a problem.

The opinion further explains that “the lawyer’s disclosure obligation will generally be satisfied by withdrawing or disavowing the lawyer’s previous evaluation that served to further the client’s criminal or fraudulent activity.” This is because disclosure under Rule 4.1(b) “is limited to the amount of information necessary to avoid ‘assisting’ in a client’s crime or fraud.”
