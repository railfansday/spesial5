Law firms are susceptible to insider theft but can take steps to protect themselves

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

The Georgia-based Dozier Law Firm was a victim of ongoing internal theft that went undetected for years.

Investigators in July uncovered evidence of forgery and theft at the firm, and police later charged employee Nadine Brooks with more than 100 felonies and misdemeanors. She is suspected of stealing $190,000 from the firm’s Macon, Georgia, location through fraudulent credit card payments over a five-year period.

In Louisiana, Sarah Steiner, an employee of the Trey Morris Law Firm, pleaded guilty to one count of felony theft of $25,000 or more and was ordered to pay $147,000 in restitution to the firm. She was sentenced to 10 years in prison last month.

And three years ago in Texas, Christiane Kathleen Irwin, a law firm controller, admitted to embezzling $1.48 million by inflating her payroll check over three years. She was sentenced to 50 months in prison and ordered to pay the embezzled amount in restitution.

The amount of money law firms lose to occupational fraud is unknown, but experts believe the figure is significant. Because firms are busy trying to protect themselves from wire fraud, phishing scams and ransomware attacks, they often neglect to arm themselves against inside threats. Fraud frequently occurs through a firm’s routine operations: managing trust accounts, payroll, credit card and expense reports, billings and collections.

Insider thefts cost U.S. businesses between $20 billion and $50 billion per year, according to some estimates. In May, the Association of Certified Fraud Examiners published Occupational Fraud 2026: A Report to the Nations which examined 2,402 cases that took place around the world and reports organizations lose an estimated 5% of revenue to fraud each year. The study’s Fraud Tree lists three main classifications: asset misappropriation, corruption (conflicts of interest, bribery, illegal gratuities, economic extortion) and financial statement fraud.

Attorneys are accountable when a nonlawyer employee steals from a client. For example, if a firm’s receptionist stole money from a client’s prepayments, the lawyer is still liable and legally responsible for the money, according to Emil J. Ali, an attorney who specializes in ethics and discipline matters. He recommends steps such as regularly reviewing a firm’s check log and examining records overseen by the receptionist.

Attorneys often prioritize working with clients and leave other professionals to handle administrative tasks. “However, even a 20-year veteran in accounting needs supervision, whether for intentional or unintentional acts. The buck stops with the lawyer,” says Ali, a partner at McCabe Ali in Los Angeles and president-elect of the Association of Professional Responsibility Lawyers.

Philadelphia-based ethics attorney Ellen Brotman, who formerly practiced criminal defense, now represents lawyers and judges who need help with defending their licenses, reinstatements and bar admissions. Brotman, a member of the Pennsylvania Bar Association Legal Ethics and Professional Responsibility Committee and the Philadelphia Bar Association Professional Guidance Committee, also advises judges and lawyers about conflicts rules, termination, handling money and defending disciplinary prosecutions.

“We get a lot of calls about money,” Brotman says. “It’s shocking to me how good lawyers don’t understand the rules about money.”

Lawyers should pay attention to keeping trust accounts separate from general accounts. “Your trust account is always other people’s money. Your operating account is always your money,” says Brotman, noting that attorneys may not oversee some processes. For example, if a bookkeeper is a long-term trusted employee or close relative, the theft may go unnoticed by the attorney. “It’s your obligation to keep that money safe.”

Employees who steal from law firms aren’t always easy to spot, but there may be signs, such as beginning to live extravagant lifestyles disproportionate to their incomes. Dedicated employees who insist on working alone, stay late and come into the office at odd times also might be trying to conceal theft.

International Fraud Awareness Week, a public campaign beginning Nov. 15, encourages companies to learn about occupational fraud and share tips. It is sponsored by the ACFE, which published the Occupational Fraud 2026 study.

“There are things that organizations can do to limit the occurrence and impact of occupational fraud,” says Mason Wilder, research director for the ACFE study.

Wilder says the organization has seen losses decrease over the last decade. “We hope that some of that is because of some of the awareness we’ve raised with this report and the resources that we provide not only our members but the general public to help better detect, prevent and respond to fraud.”

For instance, lawyers can learn to better understand financial statements and look for questionable expenses and revenues, along with fake receipts or personal purchases disguised as business expenses, Wilder says.
