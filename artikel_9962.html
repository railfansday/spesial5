$1 of GPT-5.1 tokens finds new ways around China’s Great Firewall

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

PLUS: Lenovo's infra group finally makes big profit; Indonesia’s glaciers are gone; Australian bank releases its AI bug-finder; and more!

Researchers have found new ways to evade China’s Great Firewall after spending just $1 worth of tokens.

That result appeared last week in a paper titled “CircumVolve: Automated Discovery of Censorship Evasion Strategies Using Large Language Models” presented at the Conference on Computer and Communications Security run by the Association for Computing Machinery’s Special Interest Group on Security, Audit and Control.

Penned by six researchers from the University of Massachusetts Amherst, University of Colorado Boulder, and activist group the Great Firewall Report, the paper points out that finding holes in the Great Firewall is a laborious process – so just the kind of task worth handing to AI.

The authors came up with a tool they call “CircumVolve” and describe as “a system for automated discovery of censorship circumvention strategies across the full network stack.”

“CircumVolve combines LLM code generation with iterative feedback-driven evaluation to produce verifiable and deployable evasion techniques,” they wrote. “CircumVolve models different censorship techniques as discrete scenarios, each capturing the specific context, protocol constraints, and blocking mechanisms of real-world censors. Within each scenario, evasion strategies are represented as executable Python programs that implement packet and protocol manipulations — from rewriting TCP/IP header fields, to crafting DNS queries that exploit parser ambiguities, to modifying the internal structure of encrypted QUIC Initial packets — while maintaining protocol correctness within tolerable bounds or strategically violating it when beneficial.”

The authors tested CircumVolve on simulated censorship tools used by China, Iran, Pakistan, and Kazakhstan, and on virtual private servers deployed on clouds in those countries. Those tests found the median API cost to the first successful evasion was less than $1 in token expenditure costs when using GPT-5.1.

The authors acknowledge that operators of censorship infrastructure might be able to observe CircumVolve at work, but feel its relatively small payloads – 80 KB of outgoing and 150 KB of incoming traffic – and 25-45-second pauses between tests might see it fly under the radar.

“CircumVolve represents a first step in leveraging LLMs to accelerate the censorship-circumvention lifecycle,” the authors wrote. “By automating the labor-intensive tasks of protocol analysis, implementation, and testing, AI-driven approaches make studying circumvention capabilities far more accessible to the comparatively under-resourced Internet freedom community [and] demonstrates how AI-assisted systems can narrow the resource gap between Internet freedom advocates and well-funded censoring states.”

Ever since acquiring IBM’s x86 server business in 2014, Chinese hardware giant Lenovo has struggled to turn a profit from datacenter sales, even as it expanded its business into storage, networking, and selling to hyperscalers.

The AI boom has finally done what various strategy shifts and waves of hype could not: propel the company’s Infrastructure Solutions Group (ISG) to a $1 billion quarterly profit.

In its Q2 results announcement [PDF] last week, the company revealed ISG revenue hit a quarterly record of $11.3 billion, up 178 percent year-over-year, and that lovely big profit. At a time when enterprise hardware supply chains are tight, Lenovo says it was able to increase shipments of x86 servers and even emerge as the largest vendor of such systems with 16 percent market share.

Lenovo also claimed it’s moved further ahead as the planet’s premier PC provider, by pointing to 15 percent year-on-year growth for its intelligent devices group where quarterly revenue reached $17.4 billion. The company claims it has 23.8 percent of the PC market and now has seven percent more market share than its closest rival, Apple. The company said premium devices account for 63 percent of its PC business

Overall revenue rose 48 percent year over year to $30.24 billion for the quarter.

It may surprise readers to know that Indonesia, the nation that spreads across 17,000 islands in the tropical latitudes of 11°S to 6°N, is home to alpine regions sufficiently high that glaciers can form.

Indonesia’s glaciers clung to the top of Puncak Jaya, aka Carstenz Pyramid, a 4,884-meter peak on New Guinea at 4°S.

Sadly, an assessment published last week by NASA says the glaciers have probably melted to the point at which not much is left other than a few patches of ice.

NASA last week posted images that show glaciers covering 4.31 km2 atop Puncak Jaya in 1988, and another from this year that shows most of that ice is gone. Images from September 2025 apparently showed just .09km2 of ice remained.

“To qualify as a glacier, a body of ice generally has to be thick enough to flow downhill under its own weight, and it needs an area where snow builds up faster than it melts,” NASA’s Kathryn Hansen wrote. “Puncak Jaya’s remaining ice has thinned so much and broken into such small, stagnant patches that it no longer flows.”

For what it’s worth, commercial operators run expeditions to Puncak Jaya. Peak-baggers seek it out because some consider it the highest mountain in Australasia/Oceania and therefore one of the “Seven Summits” –  the highest point on each continent.

The Reg won’t get into the debate about the validity of including Puncak Jaya on that list, but if you fancy some equatorial alpine action your correspondent recently climbed Malaysia’s 4,095m Mount Kinabalu. It’s an overnight walk that requires no technical skills or gear, but definitely demands supple knees. The peak was clearly glaciated in the distant past but snow is now extremely rare on Kinabalu.

Here’s an odd one: Australia’s Commonwealth Bank (CBA) created an AI-powered tool to scan its bespoke code for bugs, and has now packaged it as a free release called “CodeCheck.”

The bank says analyzes the output of OpenAI’s Codex and Anthropic’s Claude Code with “a consistent process for analyzing code, investigating potential vulnerabilities and providing information to assist engineers.” Apparently, it is “ based on an approach used within CBA, but it is not the same as, and does not represent, the systems, tools or controls CBA uses internally.”

The bank posted CodeCheck to GitHub, where it warns the project is “a one-time open-source release for businesses to evaluate” and that it will not actively maintain, update, or support the code.

Japan’s Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) last week announced it has reached an agreement with Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (MHI) to take over responsibility for running launches of its flagship H3 rocket when used in certain configurations.

The deal means MHI will handle launches for H3s that use a pair of LE-9 liquid-fuel engines alongside two SRB-3 solid-fuel boosters.

JAXA will continue running launches that use different, or experimental, configurations.

Once JAXA is satisfied those new rigs are reliable, it plans to transition them to private operators, too. ®
