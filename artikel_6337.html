Wild baboons seem to plan breakfast around a favorite fruit

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Wild baboons stopped visiting their favorite fruit trees after a fire destroyed the crop, suggesting flexible food planning.

Wild Guinea baboons in West Africa travel to the same area each year to feed on a favored fruit. In 2019, this annual pattern was disrupted by a fire that swept through the feeding grounds.

The fire reached the area just as the fruit was beginning to ripen on the trees. Only a single visit to the fruit trees was recorded during February of that year.

The baboons resumed their usual visits the following year, when ripe Natal oranges were available again.

These observations suggest that the animals do more than simply recall where and when food can be found. Their foraging plans also appear to be revised when a familiar food source is lost.

The study was led by Lisa Ohrndorf of the German Primate Center in Göttingen, Germany.

“The year of the fire gave us a unique opportunity: We were able to observe how the animals react when a normally reliable food source suddenly becomes unavailable during their usual season,” said Ohrndorf.

Her co-authors were Roger Mundry, Dietmar Zinner and Julia Fischer. All three are affiliated with the center and with the University of Göttingen.

The baboons inhabit Niokolo-Koba National Park in southeastern Senegal, where several groups have been followed by researchers since 2007.

Earlier work carried out at the same center had shown that these baboons share meat with close companions.

A clear preference for the Natal orange is shown by the animals during the dry season. The fruit is roughly the size of a common orange and is protected by a hard outer shell.

Inside the shell the seeds are embedded in a layer of soft pulp that is eaten by the baboons.

Natal oranges ripen between January and March and reach their greatest abundance in February. Data from recent years indicate that nearly a fifth of February feeding time was devoted to this fruit.

Very few other plants in the park are known to produce fleshy fruit during the dry season. The long journey to the Natal orange trees is therefore likely to be well rewarded.

Most of the Natal orange trees are found in the northern parts of the home ranges of the baboons.

These trees cannot be seen from the riverside forest in which the animals usually spend the night.

In 2023 a series of evenly spaced survey routes was walked by the researchers through the area. A total of 5,847 Natal orange trees was counted along these routes.

The densest stand found in this survey was then used as the target area for the analysis of baboon movements.

Fires are a regular feature of the park and are recorded in every dry season. They normally burn between mid-December and early January and mainly consume the tall grass left after the rains.

The fires of 2019 were unusual because they arrived much later than in most years.

Satellite records indicate that most of the target area burned within a few days at the end of January. By that point the Natal oranges had already begun to ripen.

Nearly all of the ripe fruit was destroyed by the heat of the fire. The trees themselves suffered little lasting damage and plant growth in the following rainy season was close to normal.

Nine years of observation records for seven baboon groups were examined by the team. Each group contained an average of 29 animals.

The baboons were observed on 22 days in February 2019 but entered the target area on only one of them. In other years between eight and 29 visits were recorded in February with an average of about 16.

During the fire year the baboons were seen feeding on the fruit of fan palms. The fruit of a shrubby tree growing in other parts of their range was also eaten.

The baboons visited the area four times in March 2019, at the low end of the range of four to 22 visits recorded in other years.

The baboons may have returned in March to feed on fruit from taller trees, where it hangs higher and is less exposed to the heat of a fire.

By 2020 the Natal oranges had recovered and the usual pattern of visits to the area had resumed.

The researchers also analyzed 2,922 GPS tracks that had been collected over a period of nine years.

Of those tracks, 530 showed a group entering the area where the fruit trees grow.

When Natal oranges were in season the baboons were found to follow more direct routes to the trees. Their average travel speed on these trips was also about 14% higher than at other times of year.

The distance involved in these trips was considerable. From the known sleeping sites the trees lay roughly 1.4 miles (2.2 kilometers) away in a straight line.

Baboons are generally slow to leave their sleeping trees in the morning. More than an hour commonly passes between sunrise and their departure.

On trips to the feeding area, the baboons left their sleeping sites about 40 minutes earlier when the fruit was in season.

Cold weather normally delays departure because the animals tend to sun themselves before setting off.

Mornings in the fruit season were in fact colder on average. Since the baboons still left earlier, warmer temperatures cannot account for the change.

In studies of animal learning a distinction is drawn between habitual behavior and behavior driven by an expected reward. A habit may persist for some time after the associated reward is no longer available.

The direct and rapid trips to the trees could in principle have reflected nothing more than a fixed yearly routine. The response of the baboons to the fire argues against that interpretation.

If the visits had been governed by habit alone the empty trees would probably have continued to be visited. The baboons instead shifted their foraging to food sources located in other parts of their range.

The results suggest that the value of a journey is assessed by these baboons before the journey is undertaken.

Comparable behavior has been described in several other primates. Chacma baboons have been observed leaving their sleeping sites early in order to reach fig trees. Female chimpanzees leave their nests earlier when a preferred fruit is located far away.

Memory for food locations is not restricted to primates. Small birds such as chickadees are able to recall where large numbers of their food stores were hidden.

Responses to fire are also known to vary widely among animals. Some deer return to burned ground within days while certain lizards detect smoke and move away before the flames arrive.

The late fire occurred only once during the study period. Visit numbers could therefore be compared across years but full statistical testing of the fire year was not possible.

GPS positions were recorded at 30-minute intervals during follows that usually ended around 1 p.m. Some movement was missed as a result and the actual routes were probably longer and less direct.

The means by which the baboons navigate through their home range also remains unclear. Repeated use of the same trails and roads and stream crossings points to a reliance on learned routes.

A further open question concerns the way in which the baboons recognize that the Natal oranges are ready to eat.

Day length and temperature or the appearance of other seasonal foods may serve as cues for the animals. These possibilities were not tested in the present study.

The study offers a closer look at the way wild baboons make decisions about where to search for food.

Knowledge of where food grows is clearly useful but knowing when to stop searching may be equally important.

The full study was published in the journal Animal Cognition.
