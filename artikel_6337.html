Voyager 1 went silent in deep space right before reaching a historic milestone of one ‘light day’ away from Earth

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

NASA briefly lost contact with Voyager 1 in October 2026. The signal returned just weeks before the spacecraft reaches a historic milestone.

On Oct. 18, 2026, the antennas of NASA’s Deep Space Network tried to pick up Voyager 1 during a scheduled contact and found nothing. A second attempt 24 hours later also failed.

The signal came back on Oct. 20, and the team at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in Southern California has kept in touch with the probe since then.

NASA schedules Nov. 18 as the day Voyager 1, already the farthest human-made object, reaches one light-day from Earth, according to JPL. They had not previously said what caused the October problems.

The Deep Space Network has large antennas at Goldstone in California, near Madrid in Spain and near Canberra in Australia. The largest dish at each site measures about 230 feet (70 meters) across.

Those antennas handled the two contact attempts in October. Mission controllers logged no detectable signal on Oct. 18 or Oct. 19.

Kareem Badaruddin, the Voyager mission manager at JPL, said the episode was new to his team. “This is the first time we’ve seen the spacecraft do something like this,” he said in the JPL statement.

He also said the mission has reached a stage where the team has “to expect the unexpected.” The probe has surprised its operators before, including the time it began talking through a radio system not used since 1981.

After contact returned, the team worked out that one group of thrusters on the spacecraft had gone offline and that a backup group had turned on.

The onboard computers carry instructions to make that swap by themselves when they detect a problem with the active thrusters.

JPL calls this kind of automatic reaction fault protection. It puts the probe in a safe, stable state and pauses normal operations so that engineers on the ground can study the problem.

In March 2025, engineers revived a set of roll thrusters that had stopped working in 2004.

Early in September, about six weeks before the probe dropped out of contact, JPL engineers sent the commands that turned off three devices on Voyager 1 and turned on three lower-power substitutes.

Inside the team, the job goes by the name Big Bang. The devices double as a source of warmth for the spacecraft, so JPL could not do them one at a time and all three changed at once.

Voyager 2 got the treatment first, and NASA reported on Aug. 4, 2026 that the swap there should keep three instruments powered for at least one more year. An instrument on Voyager 2 would otherwise have gone dark before the end of 2026.

Nothing in the Oct. 29 note ties the September commands to the thrusters.

Both probes run on radioisotope generators, which turn the heat from decaying plutonium into electricity. The output on each probe falls by about 4 watts a year and, with margins now thin, JPL has been shutting off whatever is not essential.

Ten science instruments went up on Voyager 1 in 1977. A NASA status page updated in April lists only two still running on it, the magnetometer and the plasma wave subsystem.

Controllers switched off the cosmic ray subsystem on Feb. 25, 2025, in one of a series of shutdowns meant to stretch the remaining power. The low-energy charged particle instrument was next, on April 17, 2026.

Without the Big Bang swap, another Voyager 1 instrument would likely have been turned off as early as summer 2027, JPL said.

Voyager 1 launched on Sept. 5, 1977, two weeks after its twin, passed Jupiter and Saturn, and entered interstellar space in August 2012. It is moving at about 38,000 miles per hour (61,155 kilometers per hour).

NASA’s light-day page sets the crossing on Nov. 18, when the probe will sit about 16.09 billion miles (25.9 billion kilometers) from Earth.

Light covers the distance to the Moon in 1.3 seconds. Artemis II astronauts needed about three days for the same trip.

NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory explains how far away Voyager 1 is when it reaches one light-day from Earth. Credit: NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

Voyager 2 took a different route so it could visit Uranus and Neptune, and NASA expects it to reach the same distance in November 2035. The nearest star system, Proxima Centauri, lies about 4.24 light-years away.

Voyager project manager Suzanne Dodd described what the distance does to a conversation. “If you want to say ‘hello’ to Voyager, you send your message and then you go home and you wait two days before you finally get a response,” she said in the JPL statement.

JPL’s Oct. 29 note does not say what knocked the thrusters offline, and engineers are still investigating. It also does not confirm that the thruster switch was what interrupted the signal.

The note does not name the three devices replaced in September or give the amount of power saved. It also says nothing about when the mission might end, although the team expects more surprises before summer 2027.

The light-day crossing itself will change nothing on board or around the spacecraft, JPL said. Its value lies in showing the length of the mission and the size of the distances involved.

At one light-day, a command sent from Goldstone, Madrid or Canberra needs a full day to reach Voyager 1. The answer needs another.

The information was obtained from NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory.
