Flying squirrels have an unusual habit that may shape entire forests

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Flying squirrels in China may influence where young trees grow by storing nuts in branches instead of underground.

Squirrels help oak forests grow by burying acorns and forgetting about them. But two species of flying squirrel in southern China have a different way of storing food.

Instead of burying nuts, they tuck them between small branches. A new study suggests that this habit may affect where young trees grow.

Researchers found fewer seedlings a short distance from parent trees. But their numbers increased again about 66 feet (20 meters) away, where squirrels often store their nuts.

Scientists have usually linked this pattern to tree diseases. The new study suggests that squirrels may also play a role.

The work was carried out by Xuelian Tan, Han Xu and their colleagues at the Research Institute of Tropical Forestry. This institute is part of the Chinese Academy of Forestry in Guangzhou.

The study was a field survey of two tree species in a single forest. It therefore shows a match between stored nuts and seedlings rather than proof that the squirrels planted them.

The research took place in Jianfengling, a forest within the Hainan Tropical Rainforest National Park on Hainan Island.

The forest averages about 67°F (19.7°C), and about 97 inches (2,461 millimeters) of rain falls there each year. In total, 992 species of free-standing trees and shrubs have been recorded in the forest.

Two of these trees are Cyclobalanopsis edithiae and Cyclobalanopsis patelliformis, which are close relatives of oaks. Their nuts are smooth and are not covered by the small cup at their base.

Many animals feed on these nuts. Their slick surfaces make them difficult to hold in place on a branch, however.

The flying squirrels solve this problem by gnawing grooves into the nuts, allowing them to wedge their food between branches.

The grooves differ between the two kinds of nut. Oval C. edithiae nuts usually receive one zigzag groove around the middle. Flatter C. patelliformis nuts, on the other hand, may carry up to 20 grooves on the underside.

Most stored nuts sat 33 to 82 feet (10 to 25 meters) from the nearest possible parent tree. Photographs in the new study show squirrels returning to collect their nuts.

Another image shows a gnawed nut that had fallen to the forest floor and begun to sprout.

The importance of this behavior is linked to an older idea about how tropical forests work. During the early 1970s, the ecologists Daniel Janzen and Joseph Connell observed a pattern among tree seedlings.

Young trees, they noticed, were seldom found close to adults of their own species. The pair put this down to disease and pests.

Fungi and plant-eating insects that target one tree species spread from an adult tree to the seedlings around it.

Seedlings close to the parent are therefore more likely to die. Most seeds, however, land near the parent tree in the first place.

When both effects are combined, most surviving seedlings would be expected at a middle distance from the parent.

Ecologists refer to this process as conspecific negative density dependence. In simpler terms, plants tend to do poorly when they are surrounded by their own species.

This effect helps prevent any single species from dominating a forest. It is one reason why a rainforest can support so many different kinds of trees.

An earlier study in one of the Jianfengling plots detected the effect in 89% of tree species.

Where a seedling ends up hinges on two separate events. A seed has to land somewhere, and the young plant has to survive in that spot.

Seed-carrying animals can shift the first of these. Whether they could mimic the disease pattern on their own had gone largely untested.

Twenty parent trees anchored the survey. On ten of them, squirrels had lodged nuts in at least one direction.

The other ten were bare on all four sides. Each of the first ten trees got two survey lines.

One pointed toward the stashed nuts, and the second ran off where no nuts hung. A single line from each bare tree, comparable in trunk size, formed a second point of comparison.

Markers went down every 16 feet (5 meters), stopping at roughly 100 feet (30 meters). Beside each marker lay three plots about a yard (1 meter) square. Every seedling inside those plots was tallied.

Seedlings along the test lines were most plentiful beside the parent tree. Their numbers, however, fell away at 16 and 33 feet (5 and 10 meters).

At 49 and 66 feet (15 and 20 meters), on the other hand, the counts climbed once more. Farther out they declined again, and the comparison lines showed no second rise at all.

The 66-foot mark stood out in particular. There, the test lines held more seedlings than both comparison groups. The difference was statistically significant.

This is, notably, the very band of distances in which the squirrels hang their nuts. Beside the trunk itself, the test lines also held more seedlings, though only relative to the second group.

The authors offered a simple explanation, namely that trees ignored by squirrels may produce fewer nuts. Fewer nuts would mean fewer seedlings and, in turn, fewer squirrel visits.

Comparisons on two sides of the same tree therefore mattered, since seedling numbers vary widely from tree to tree.

Disease, for its part, ought to leave more damage on saplings close to the parent. Fine roots from 33 young trees were therefore examined, 15 of one species and 18 of the other.

The trees grew at different distances from adults. Each sapling’s root samples were inspected at 100 points under a microscope for lesions, which can indicate infection.

The level of damage, however, showed no clear trend with distance.

Fungi are not cleared by this result. Root lesions, the authors wrote, may not reflect the full pressure that pathogens place on seedlings.

They also wrote that “our results do not imply that pathogens are unimportant.”

Squirrels that move nuts are, in their account, one more possible influence on where seedlings take root.

Tan and Xu’s team has not confirmed whether squirrels directly help new trees grow. Other animals and local growing conditions may also play a role.

Changing how squirrels store nuts could provide stronger evidence, but such experiments are difficult in the wild.

The researchers suggest tracking seedlings over several years to see whether the nuts grow into healthy trees. Future studies could also examine other animals and forests.

The full study was published in the journal Forest Ecosystems.
