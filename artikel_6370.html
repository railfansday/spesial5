NASA captures two powerful Pacific storms with a surprising connection

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

NASA satellites captured a rare super typhoon with an enormous eye as another powerful Pacific storm transformed and moved toward Alaska.

In late October 2026, two powerful storms dominated opposite ends of the North Pacific Ocean.

One developed an enormous, nearly circular eye, while the other transformed into a sprawling weather system that swept toward Alaska.

Yet both began as tropical cyclones in the western Pacific, and both appeared in a remarkable satellite view captured by NASA on October 29, 2026.

Super Typhoon In-fa was still an intense tropical system near the Mariana Islands.

Far to the north, the remnants of Typhoon Champi had transformed into an entirely different kind of storm.

Together, they offered a striking demonstration of how dramatically storms can change as they travel across the planet.

Super Typhoon In-fa stood out for its unusual structure.

NASA’s Terra satellite captured a closer view on October 30, revealing a remarkably symmetrical storm just west of the Mariana Islands.

In-fa displayed characteristics of an annular typhoon, a rare type of tropical cyclone with a distinctive ring-shaped structure.

Most typhoons develop long, spiraling bands of thunderstorms that extend outward from their centers.

Annular storms typically have fewer prominent outer rainbands, giving them a smoother, more circular appearance.

Their unusual structure has earned them comparisons to doughnuts and truck tires.

Only about four percent of tropical cyclones in the western North Pacific develop annular characteristics.

These storms also tend to have larger eyes. While the eye of a typical tropical cyclone averages around 15 miles across, annular storms average nearly 30 miles.

Their structure can also help them maintain their strength longer when environmental conditions become less favorable.

While In-fa maintained its tropical identity, Champi was undergoing a dramatic transformation thousands of miles away.

Earlier in its journey, Champi had become one of the most powerful typhoons of 2026, with sustained winds approaching 185 miles per hour.

The storm brought damaging winds and heavy rainfall to several western Pacific locations, including Taiwan, Guam, China, and Japan.

But tropical cyclones depend on warm ocean water for energy. As Champi traveled northward, it encountered conditions that fundamentally changed how the storm operated.

Through a process known as extratropical transition, Champi’s remnants became incorporated into a larger weather system over the Bering Sea.

Instead of drawing energy primarily from warm ocean waters, the developing system was driven by temperature differences between air masses.

The change also transformed its appearance.

Tropical cyclones are generally organized around a central core, with powerful thunderstorms circling the eye.

Extratropical storms often look quite different from space. Their clouds can spread across enormous areas, forming sweeping bands that resemble giant commas.

These storms can also develop weather fronts, where contrasting air masses meet.

One particularly important change involves the area covered by strong winds.

NASA meteorologist Gary Partyka explained that a transitioning storm does not necessarily develop faster winds. Instead, its wind field can expand dramatically.

This means a storm can affect a much larger region even as its original tropical structure disappears.

Champi’s remnants became intertwined with an existing midlatitude storm over the Bering Sea, likely helping intensify the larger weather system.

The resulting weather brought strong winds, heavy rainfall, and snow to parts of Alaska.

Alaska might seem an unlikely destination for a storm that began in the tropical Pacific, but the connection is well established.

Former typhoons sometimes travel northward and become part of the large weather systems that move across the North Pacific.

Champi was already the fourth former typhoon to reach Alaskan waters in 2026.

The others were Bang-Lang in August, Dujuan in September, and Choi-wan in early October.

University of Alaska Fairbanks climatologist Rick Thoman noted that even the exceptionally strong El Niño years of 1997 and 2015 each brought only three former typhoons into the region during autumn.

Approximately 60 former typhoons have affected Alaska since 1970.

The unusually busy 2026 season raised questions about the broader atmospheric conditions helping steer these storms northward.

One possible contributor was the strong El Niño underway in the Pacific.

El Niño develops when surface waters across parts of the equatorial Pacific become warmer than usual, altering weather patterns across distant regions.

In the Northwest Pacific, these changes can influence where tropical cyclones form and how they travel.

Rather than moving steadily westward toward Asia, some storms are more likely to curve northward.

That shift can bring tropical cyclones closer to Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands, Japan, and the Korean Peninsula.

It can also increase the opportunities for former typhoons to reach higher latitudes and interact with powerful northern storm systems.

El Niño is not responsible for every individual storm, but its influence on atmospheric circulation may help explain why the Pacific experienced such an active period.

The 2026 Northwest Pacific season had already produced more than two dozen named storms by late October.

In-fa was the fifth typhoon to affect the Mariana Islands that year, while Champi added to an unusually long list of former tropical cyclones reaching Alaska.

NASA’s satellite imagery captured an extraordinary contrast between two powerful storms, revealing how tropical cyclones can evolve as they move across different parts of the Pacific.

While In-fa retained its remarkable circular structure, Champi had become part of a much larger weather system.

Together, the storms offered a striking glimpse into the powerful atmospheric forces shaping weather across the Pacific.
