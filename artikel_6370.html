Most of the deep ocean food supply in the Gulf of Mexico suddenly vanished, and most scientists blame the Deepwater Horizon oil spill

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Deep-sea fish, krill and squid in the Gulf of Mexico fell 60 to 85 percent after 2011, and the Deepwater Horizon oil spill is the leading suspect.

Marine biologists have always assumed the deep ocean is the safest and most stable ecosystem on the planet. Because it is so vast and isolated from human life, they believed it was practically impossible to destroy.

Unfortunately, a massive die-off in the Gulf of Mexico just proved that assumption is dangerously incorrect.

Between 2011 and 2015, the fish, shrimp, krill and squid living in the dark water below about 650 feet (200 meters) lost 60 to 85 percent of their numbers. Surveys through 2023 have not found them coming back.

The crash shows up in two records. One is the nets that scientists have towed through the same patch of ocean in the Gulf of Mexico for 13 years. The other is ship sonar, whose echoes come back weaker when fewer animals are swimming below.

Nothing in the water itself, not temperature, oxygen or food, changed in step with it. The one event that did line up in time was the Deepwater Horizon oil spill. The authors consider this the most likely explanation.

Tracey Sutton of Nova Southeastern University led the work, with colleagues at Florida International University, the University of South Florida and NOAA.

The paper is careful about what it can show. The spill’s role is inferred from timing and from the absence of any natural driver, not from an experiment.

The record begins in January 2011, three months after the well was sealed. A nine-month campaign towed a net through a grid of 46 stations across the northern Gulf of Mexico. That first campaign is the baseline against which everything since has been measured.

The same net, with six separate bags that open and close on command, was towed again on nine shorter cruises between 2015 and 2023.

Each tow sampled from the surface down to 4,900 feet (1,500 meters) in five depth bands, at noon and again at midnight.

Towing twice a day matters, because most of these animals rise thousands of feet toward the surface every night to feed and sink again at dawn.

Only samples from the Gulf of Mexico’s own water mass were compared, since the Loop Current, which enters from the south, carries fewer animals and would have muddied the count.

Fish numbers per unit of water dropped 63.5 percent between 2011 and the later cruises. Shrimp and krill together dropped 62 percent, and small squid and their relatives dropped 59.7 percent.

The groups that feed almost everything else fell hardest. Krill dropped 72.8 percent and lanternfish 62.9 percent. Bristlemouths, tiny fish that may be the most numerous vertebrates on Earth, dropped 69 percent.

The sonar agreed. Sound bouncing back from the top 650 feet (200 meters) at night, when the migrators are up feeding, was 40 percent weaker after 2011.

A drop that size over three and a half years is bigger than any change recorded over 60 years in the California Current. That is the only other long-running count of deep-sea fish.

In an interview with Earth.com, Sutton said the fall itself is not the part that surprised him most. “The slow recovery is the most surprising finding, with the realization that this downward shift may be irreversible,” he said.

And the dense swarms that predators depend on nearly disappeared. Across 134 full-column tows in the whole series, only one catch after the decline held more fish than the leanest catch of 2011.

A crowd that shrinks fast can lose genetic variety, so the team sequenced three common lanternfish species collected between 2011 and 2023, the longest such record for any deep-sea animal.

Two of the three showed it. In one species, a standard measure of genetic variety fell about 11 percent between 2011 and 2016, then recovered partway by 2023. A second species followed the same path a few years later. The third showed no clear trend.

The same tows kept adding to the list of what lives down there. Sutton told Earth.com that the deep interior holds more kinds of animals than any other part of the Gulf of Mexico, coral reefs included.

His team has recorded almost 200 fish species there for the first time since the work began, he said. That is about one in every nine fish species now known from the Gulf.

“Our knowledge of what lives in the deep ocean is incredibly poor,” he said, “even in the best-studied systems in the world, like the Gulf.”

The authors checked their own methods first. The same net, the same crew and the same analysis ran across every year, so a change in gear can’t explain the drop.

Water temperature in the twilight zone from 650 to 2,000 feet (200 to 600 meters) barely moved from year to year, and the deep Gulf stayed well supplied with oxygen. The plankton these animals eat didn’t thin out either; in 2013, mid-decline, it was above average.

So the authors turned to the spill. The Deepwater Horizon blowout on April 20, 2010 released up to 4.9 million barrels of oil over 87 days.

Crews pumped 770,000 gallons (2.9 million liters) of chemical dispersant into the plume at the wellhead, 4,900 feet down.

Oil residues turned up in the eggs of deep-sea fish and the tissues of squid at least six years later, at concentrations known to kill or weaken fish.

The paper does not test oil on these animals directly. It argues from the sequence of events and from what oil did elsewhere. For example, herring in Prince William Sound collapsed four years after the Exxon Valdez spill and have still not recovered.

The Gulf’s deep-feeding whales eat these animals, and their numbers have been falling too. Long-term counts show Gervais’ beaked whales down 83.4 percent since the spill, and sperm whales down 31 percent. This is more than the spill’s own mortality models predicted.

Tagged sperm whales are now roaming the Gulf instead of feeding in their usual predictable spots.

Endangered Rice’s whales have been recorded feeding near the bottom, often without success. In addition, researchers at sea report seeing thin whales, while stranded ones are often described as emaciated.

Sutton said 27 of the 28 whale and dolphin species known from the Gulf live in the open ocean, and nearly all of them eat the animals his team counted. Less prey means fewer marine mammals, he said, “and those that are there may be less fit.”

Bluefin and yellowfin tuna, swordfish, marlin, sailfish and sharks feed on the same animals, he said. So do seabirds that hunt lanternfish at night, including some endangered species.

These animals also run what scientists call the biological pump, the sinking of carbon from the surface into the deep ocean. “That function affects every person on the planet.”

One obvious objection is that the 2011 baseline was a single, nine-month campaign, run just after the spill. The team’s answer is that counts from the 1980s and 1990s at one eastern Gulf station look like 2011, not like anything since.

The authors wrote that the deep Gulf is now in “a sustained, low-abundance state” for which the recovery time, “if even possible, remains unknown.”

Asked by Earth.com what a recovery would take, Sutton used the researchers’ name for that dark, open water, the deep-pelagic ocean.

“We do not have the capacity to restore the deep-pelagic ocean directly, so the best we can do is give the system time and not make it worse with more pollution,” he said.

Whether the deep Gulf is coming back at all is the open question, and the same grid of stations is where an answer would first show up.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
