Russia-Ukraine war extends to Baltic, Mediterranean

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

By attacking Russian vessels around the world, Ukraine is trying to increase the pressure on Russia and also sending out signals to its Western allies. What is the impact on global maritime trade?

First there was an attack on a Russian cargo vessel, the Sparta II, off the Finnish coast, then on the Russian corvette Merkury and a dry cargo vessel that was allegedly carrying weapons off the coast of Libya. These spectacular Ukrainian operations in regions far from the Ukrainian capital Kyiv have caused a stir. They came shortly after several suspected Russian missiles crashed in Poland, not far from where an international military exercise was underway.

Intelligence agencies have long warned that Russia was preparing some form of provocation along NATO's eastern flank.

What are the risks of Russia's war in Ukraine escalating further and drawing in other regions?

It is no secret that Russia has long since expanded its operations beyond Ukraine: With military provocation, hybrid operations and acts of sabotage, Moscow has attempted to put pressure on the countries that openly support Ukraine.

And Ukraine has also targeted Russia's military and economy beyond its borders.

Since the summer, attacks by both Russia and Ukraine have increased in the Black Sea. Not only have Russian and Ukrainian cargo ships been targeted, but also vessels from other countries, particularly Turkey.

Furthermore, in the past few years, Ukraine has managed to deal significant blows to Russia, deep within Russian territory, thanks to aerial and maritime drones. It has targeted military airfields and other strategic targets such as oil refineries, including one in Omsk, Siberia, as well as military vessels in the eastern Black Sea.

Russia has access only to a very narrow strip of the Baltic Sea coast, which is flanked by Finland and Estonia. Even ahead of the attack on the Sparta II, Ukraine had struck in the area multiple times, targeting oil terminals in the region around St. Petersburg, in Ust-Luga and Primorsk.

Russia uses the Baltic Sea to transport supplies to its Kaliningrad exclave and to export oil and other goods to the rest of the world, with the help of its "shadow fleet."

Ukrainian special forces are also said to have carried out operations against Russian military facilities in Syria, well before the fall of the Assad regime in December 2024. They are also reported to have lent support to local Tuareg fighters in Mali who were able to inflict heavy losses on the Malian army and allied Russian mercenaries.

Kyiv is thus attempting to harm Russia's military and economy worldwide, even if in military terms this does not help Ukraine directly.

The Russian corvette that was sunk was part of Russia's Black Sea Fleet but because of Turkey's blockade of the Bosporus and the Dardanelles, it would not have been allowed to return to the Black Sea. Instead, it was escorting Russian cargo ships in the Mediterranean. Primarily, the attacks send a political message: Russian ships can no longer feel safe — anywhere in the world.

"We are not speaking about random shadow vessels which simply transport oil above the price cap and as a result bring revenues for the Russian treasury," security expert Elisabeth Braw from the Atlantic Council told DW. Sparta II was suspected of carrying military equipment, which Moscow denies.

"Ukraine is showing that it knows exactly, and secondly that it's able to hit them wherever and whatever waters they are in," Braw continued. "And this is something I don't think Ukraine has been able to do at the beginning of the war."

Officially, Kyiv's goal is to disrupt Russian logistics and supply routes, but military experts and politicians also see the attacks as a clear political signal to Western allies. They suspect that Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy is sending a clear message of strength with an eye toward possible future peace negotiations.

In an interview on the German news outlet Tagesschau, security expert Joachim Weber from the University of Bonn suggested that Zelenskyy was paying less attention to the US, out of a sense of disappointment. He said that Ukrainian attacks in distant maritime areas were a sign that there was less inhibition and that Ukraine was ready to expand the conflict geographically to increase pressure on Russia, despite the risks.

Weber warned that war could become omnipresent if there was an increase in attacks on civilian vessels or ships that were considered suspicious in international waters. He said that Russia was providing more military escorts for the ships of its shadow fleet. "The likelihood that an accidental exchange of fire between armed forces, coast guards or anyone could occur is rising."

Braw pointed out that violations of the Law of the Sea were not only rising with regard to the Russia-Ukraine war but also in relation to other conflicts and playing out in "the South China Sea, the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea."

She said about 90% of global trade today was conducted via the oceans and she feared that others might conclude it was not "really to their advantage" to follow the rules of international maritime law.

She added that they might find it "beneficial in the short term to violate maritime rules" for short-term gains. "That would make the world's oceans significantly less safe for everybody."

This article was originally written in German.
