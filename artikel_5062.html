Morning Minute: Ethereum Focuses on Security As AI Threats Rise

The Ethereum Foundation has doubled its protocol security team and made cryptography its top priority. The reason isn’t quantum computers.

Protocol lead Kev Wedderburn wrote this week that “recently AI emerged as a more urgent risk”, and the distinction matters. The quantum threat has always been a hardware problem, and hardware problems announce themselves years in advance because somebody has to build the machine. The AI version is a math problem. A sufficiently capable model could find a structural shortcut in the elliptic curve cryptography that secures Ethereum and Bitcoin, and it would need no special hardware to do it.

Researcher Justin Drake raised the scenario in October. In a worst case, he said, existing signatures could become vulnerable within months.

An Anthropic mathematician named Levent Alpöge posted a claimed proof of the Riemann hypothesis, produced with an unreleased version of Claude, along with a formalized proof archive. Nobody has verified it. It doesn’t break elliptic curve cryptography and nobody is claiming it does. What it does is demonstrate the capability class Drake was describing, roughly a month after he described it.

Axios reported Tuesday that more than two dozen people familiar with internal discussions at OpenAI and Anthropic say employees worry their most advanced unreleased models may never be safe enough to release. The concerns named include reward hacking, models that hide or misrepresent their reasoning, and systems capable enough to find weaknesses in the safety measures their own developers built. OpenAI’s chief scientist proposed Sunday that labs agree on shared limits on how far they train models.

The plan replaces elliptic curves with hash-based cryptography, which rests on fewer structural assumptions about mathematics. It touches how users authorize transactions, how validators secure the network, and how layer-2 data gets verified. Drake proposed pulling the deadline from December 2029 to December 2028. Ethlabs withdrew its faster-block-production proposal from the Hegotá upgrade to free up engineers. The Foundation has deployed AI agents to hunt bugs in its own infrastructure, which is a reasonable response and also an admission about where the advantage currently sits.

Wedderburn’s own framing: it’s harder than the Merge, and there’s less time. He also acknowledged that moving faster makes it easier to ship bugs.

Bitcoin is on the same road. Blockstream’s Jonas Nick published a security analysis of SHRINCS last week, the apparent frontrunner to replace Bitcoin’s signatures, with sizes running from 548 bytes to 5,777 against today’s 64 to 71.

So the AI threat is looming (and accelerating), but the two largest crypto assets have teams that are responding. Now we wait on action, and more assurances that crypto is safe from AI threats. Assurances that could ensure the bottom is in fact in, if and when they arrive…
