Nvidia has no chief compliance officer. Its board now faces an oversight test

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Good morning. Nvidia’s sudden leadership shake-up raises a bigger question than who will replace Jensen Huang: Did the company’s board do enough to ensure its export-control rules were working, and what should investors expect it to do now?

With CFO Colette Kress serving as interim CEO following Huang’s resignation last week, the board, now chaired by Harvey Jones, must choose a permanent leader and assess whether the company’s compliance safeguards worked.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent intensified scrutiny of Nvidia’s compliance practices Sunday, questioning whether potential violations of U.S. export restrictions were isolated or involved senior management. “Was it a low-level fail? Was it in the middle or was it up in the C-suite? And who knew?” he asked on Fox News’ Sunday Morning Futures.

The controversy is further complicated by reports of an affair between Huang and Alice Huang (no relation), a former executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based Nvidia business partner. Huang has acknowledged infidelity but has not publicly identified the woman.

The central export-control question is whether advanced Nvidia chips supplied through Megaspeed were diverted to China. U.S. rules have permitted certain data centers outside China to provide remote computing services to Chinese customers, but that is distinct from the unauthorized transfer of restricted physical chips, Fortune reported.

U.S. authorities have also reportedly examined Alice Huang’s possible connections to Chinese intelligence. Those allegations remain unproven, and investigations into Megaspeed’s activities are ongoing.

What the board’s oversight structure promises

Nvidia’s 2026 proxy statement says the company “does not have a chief compliance officer.” That does not mean it lacks a compliance structure, however.

The proxy describes an enterprise risk management process reviewed by the CEO, other named executive officers reporting directly to the CEO, including Kress, Debora Shoquist, EVP of operations, and Timothy S. Teter, EVP, general counsel and secretary, as well as senior management. These leaders manage risks within their respective areas.

However, Nvidia’s Nominating and Corporate Governance Committee has explicit board-level oversight responsibility for trade compliance, regulatory matters, and related risks—including those at the center of the Megaspeed controversy.

The five-member committee consists entirely of independent, non-executive directors, according to the proxy statement. Its members include board chair Jones and Stephen C. Neal, who chairs the committee itself.

Board oversight was already under scrutiny before Huang’s departure on Thursday. In June, Sen. Elizabeth Warren wrote to Teter and Audit Committee Chair Brooke Seawell, a former CFO at Synopsys, questioning Nvidia’s export-control compliance, board oversight, and response to reports of chip diversion.

For finance executives and directors, the questions concern accountability: Who owned the risk, what information reached the board, and who had the authority to stop a questionable transaction?

An independent review should establish whether commercial decisions faced sufficient scrutiny, who was responsible for compliance, and whether existing controls worked. It should also determine whether those responsible for financial reporting and public disclosures knew about potential compliance problems.

For investors, the question is no longer simply whether Nvidia’s safeguards worked, but whether the board can demonstrate that they did. Its response, including any changes to oversight, internal controls, and disclosure practices, will be an important test of its governance.
