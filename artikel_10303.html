Leaders: transformation should not be your focus

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

In his 1980 book Managing in Turbulent Times, management guru Peter Drucker wrote, “The greatest danger in times of turbulence is not the turbulence; it is to act with yesterday’s logic.” This seems obvious. But more often than not, businesses lean into safe, tried and true, old-school thinking as their life raft during uncertain times. Consider Nvidia, initially defending keeping Jensen Huang in place as CEO amidst accusations of major ethical missteps. 

It may be tempting to think that the other side of turbulence is overhauling the organization in a major transformation effort. But Geoff Tuff and Steve Goldbach, who are leaders in Deloitte’s sustainability practice, have a different take on moving through organizational turbulence. They believe that large-scale transformation is not the right focus. In fact, their research and experience show that organizations who focus on transformation tend to fail. 

What they offer instead is an alternative view that is compellingly explored in their book Hone: How Purposeful Leaders Defy Drift. Cumulatively, Geoff and Steve have decades of experience working with Fortune 500 companies that end up drifting in the process of trying to transform. When I recently spoke with them they shared that “drift” is the unintentional, imperceptible shift that leaves an organization uncompetitive because leaders fail to make micro-adjustments along the way. The solution? Hone: make small, intentional continuous adjustments to stay on course rather than wait until a massive disruption is necessary.

Here are my three biggest takeaways from our conversation.

1. Honing is the antidote to failed transformation. Broad-scale transformations carry a 70% failure rate because humans naturally resist drastic change. This leads to organizational “tissue rejection.” Instead replace massive transformation with continuous “honing” to prevent drift. Geoff’s pastime and love of sailing have clearly influenced their perspective. Because just like adjusting a boat’s autopilot to account for winds and currents, leaders should focus on honing (for example, micro-adjustments in leadership style, accountability, KPI’s, etc.)  to stay on course rather than wait until a massive, disruptive transformation is required.

2. AI can help identify where honing is needed. Invisible drift can be made visible because AI can detect subtle patterns and organizational drift that are otherwise invisible to the human eye, enabling managers to address issues proactively. An AI enabled organizational network analysis can track thousands of interactions over time (such as email and Slack messages about meetings). For example, it can spot if an engineering team’s contact with the product team has decreased over 30% over multiple quarters. A 2021 study published in Nature Human Behaviour showed that a 60,000 person Microsoft team’s collaboration efforts decreased when the team went remote. Employees engaged in more intra-team communication than inter-team communication. AI can serve as an early warning system, surface weak signals and alert leaders about a future they may not want so that they can course correct. 

3. Honing demands deep behavioral curiosity. The good news is that we can adopt a “honing mindset.” A successful “honer” walks through the world constructively dissatisfied with current processes. And not because they’re unhappy, but because they’re more critically curious and attuned to identify design flaws, eliminate waste, and continuously tweak systems. Organizations change when the individuals within them start behaving differently. To drive that change, leaders must practice self-awareness and empathy to understand what truly motivates their teams.

If you’re wondering how to make honing a regular behavioral practice on your own team, start by inviting someone in from the outside. It should be a person from a different team or even a different industry who can cast a bird’s eye view on your operations and ask the “naive questions.” In design thinking we call this “lateral thinking.” Then, collect three of their questions and keep them as regular trackers that you continuously go back to asking yourself and your team as navigational tools. Because just as it’s easier to spot a ship that’s going off course from the shoreline, an outsider can help spark the necessary micro-adjustments to ensure that you’re honing- not drifting.
