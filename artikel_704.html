NASA discovers Jupiter’s invisible shield is more complex than Earth’s

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

NASA's Juno spacecraft has uncovered remarkable new details about how Jupiter withstands the constant stream of high-energy particles flowing from the Sun. The findings reveal that the solar system's largest planet relies on a much more complex system of plasma waves and shock regions than Earth does.

Led by scientists at the University of Iowa, the research provides the most detailed observations yet of Jupiter's bow shock, an invisible boundary where particles from the Sun encounter the planet's magnetic environment. The discovery reveals important differences in how Jupiter and Earth respond to these incoming particles.

Beyond Jupiter, the results could also help scientists understand some of the most powerful events in the universe, including the extreme shock waves associated with exploding stars.

How Jupiter Protects Itself From the Solar Wind

The Sun continuously releases a stream of electrically charged particles called the solar wind. These particles travel through space at tremendous speeds, often faster than the speed at which sound waves move through the surrounding plasma. When the solar wind approaches a planet with a magnetic field, it encounters a boundary known as the bow shock.

At Earth, this boundary plays an important protective role. It slows and heats the incoming particles, helping redirect much of the solar wind around the planet. Without this process, more harmful particles could reach Earth's atmosphere, potentially making conditions less favorable for life.

Jupiter also has a bow shock, but its enormous size, powerful magnetic field, and gaseous composition make it a particularly interesting place to study how these boundaries behave. Scientists have long wanted to understand whether Jupiter handles the solar wind differently from Earth and what those differences might reveal about other objects throughout the universe.

Scientists Discover a More Complex Defense System

Using measurements from Juno, researchers discovered that Jupiter relies on plasma waves with multiple frequencies to slow and heat incoming solar particles. These different frequencies, known as "harmonics," create a more elaborate wave pattern than the one typically observed at Earth.

The process can be compared to music. Just as several notes played together produce a richer sound than a single note, plasma waves containing multiple frequencies can interact with incoming particles in more complex ways. This allows Jupiter's bow shock to transfer energy to more particles, heating them and reducing their speed.

Earth's bow shock, by comparison, primarily uses plasma waves concentrated around a single frequency. That simpler arrangement is generally sufficient for the conditions encountered near our planet.

"Jupiter has found its own way to deal with the solar wind, through plasma waves that are stronger and exhibit richer harmonic structures," says Jayasri Joseph, a postdoctoral researcher in the Department of Physics and Astronomy at Iowa and the study's corresponding author. "That's important because if you have multiple frequencies, you can heat more particles in the solar wind and slow them down."

The researchers identified another striking difference in how the two planets interact with the solar wind.

Before incoming particles reach Jupiter's main bow shock, they can encounter smaller shock structures called shocklets. These additional regions help slow the solar wind before it arrives at the primary shock boundary.

Earth's bow shock, in contrast, generally serves as the main region where the incoming solar wind is slowed and redirected. Jupiter's additional shock structures reveal a more complicated process for managing the energy carried by solar particles.

"Earth does not need to take these extra steps to handle the solar wind because the impact isn't as powerful," says Bill Kurth, a research scientist in Iowa's Department of Physics and Astronomy and a coauthor of the study.

Kurth has participated in NASA's Juno mission since the spacecraft launched in 2011.

What Jupiter Can Teach Us About Exploding Stars

Although these observations focus on Jupiter, their significance could extend far beyond our solar system.

Shock waves occur throughout the universe, particularly in environments where enormous amounts of energy are released. Some of the most extreme examples are found in supernova remnants, the expanding clouds of material left behind when certain dying stars explode.

These cosmic shock waves are vastly more powerful than those encountered near Jupiter. However, the physical processes that control how energy moves through plasma share important similarities. By examining Jupiter's bow shock in unprecedented detail, scientists can investigate mechanisms that would be much harder to measure directly around distant stars.

"Because even more powerful shocks occur around astrophysical objects such as supernova remnants, Jupiter's bow shock offers a nearby natural laboratory for exploring and for understanding how nature converts enormous amounts of flow energy into heat and energetic particles," Joseph says.

Understanding these processes could improve scientific models of how shock waves transfer energy, heat particles, and shape environments across the universe.

NASA's Juno Makes Unprecedented Measurements

The breakthrough was made possible by observations Juno collected in December 2024. The NASA spacecraft has been orbiting Jupiter since July 2016, providing researchers with an extraordinary opportunity to investigate the giant planet and its surrounding environment.

A key part of the discovery came from the Juno Waves instrument, which was designed and built by physicists at the University of Iowa. The instrument measures electric and magnetic wave activity, allowing scientists to study the behavior of plasma around Jupiter.

Earlier spacecraft had detected the presence of bow shocks at outer planets, but they lacked the measurements needed to reveal the detailed structure of the plasma waves involved. Juno's observations have now provided a much clearer picture of how these waves behave at Jupiter.

The findings were published online on July 31 in Nature Communications in a study titled "Plasma wave observations from Juno spacecraft at the Jovian bow shock."

Additional contributors from the University of Iowa included Allison Jaynes, an associate professor in the Department of Physics and Astronomy, along with Jeremy Faden and Chris Piker, who provide research support in the same department.

The international research team also included Lynn Wilson III of NASA Goddard Space Flight Center; John Connerney of NASA Goddard Space Flight Center and the Space Research Corporation; Frederic Allegrini of the Southwest Research Institute and the University of Texas-San Antonio; Rob Wilson of the University of Colorado-Boulder; Ali Sulaiman of the University of Minnesota; Scott Boltion and Robert Ebert of the Southwest Research Institute; and Barry Mauk of the Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics Laboratory.
