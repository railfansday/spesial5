12 inches of rushing water can carry away most cars, the NWS says

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

How much water it takes to sweep away a car, along with National Weather Service depth thresholds and road signs to know before approaching a flooded road

Flooding kills more people each year than any other thunderstorm-related hazard, according to the National Weather Service. That puts floodwater ahead of lightning, tornadoes and every other danger a thunderstorm can produce.

The National Weather Service is the U.S. federal agency responsible for weather forecasts and warnings. It sits within the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. Its flood safety guidance is built around a four-word phrase: Turn Around Don't Drown.

The phrase tells drivers to turn back rather than enter a flooded road. The agency backs that message with specific depths. None of them exceeds 2 feet.

The short answer to how much water it takes to sweep away a car is 12 inches of rushing water for most cars, according to the weather service.

This slideshow covers five pieces of that guidance. Three are depth thresholds. The first is the depth at which fast-moving water can knock over an adult. The second is the depth that can carry away most cars, and the third is the depth that can carry off SUVs and trucks. The thresholds climb from a person on foot to passenger cars to larger vehicles.

The other two items deal with the road itself. One explains why the weather service warns drivers never to go around barriers on a flooded road. The other describes the two road signs that carry the Turn Around Don't Drown message. One is a yellow permanent warning sign, and the other is a pink temporary incident sign.

One detail applies to every number in this piece. The weather service ties its depth thresholds to fast-moving or rushing water. It does not attach a specific current speed to them. The figures describe what moving water can do at those depths.

The guidance covers people on foot as well as people in vehicles. The first threshold below concerns a pedestrian rather than a car. The slides that follow move to passenger cars, then larger vehicles, then the barriers and signs placed on flooded roads.

Six inches of fast-moving flood water can knock an adult off their feet, according to the National Weather Service. The agency gives this depth for a person on foot, not for a vehicle.

Walking into or near flood waters accounts for the second-largest share of flood deaths, the weather service reports. Only deaths tied to driving into flood water make up a bigger share.

That category is broader than wading. It covers people who are near flood water as well as those who walk into it.

Just 12 inches of rushing water is enough to sweep away most cars, according to the National Weather Service. The figure applies to most cars, not to every vehicle on the road.

Larger vehicles have their own, higher threshold in the agency's guidance. The 12-inch mark is the one for ordinary cars.

The NWS says people underestimate the force of moving water. Its warning about cars is framed around water in motion, not around depth alone.

The agency cites the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention on the circumstances of flood drownings. The CDC reports that over half of flood-related drownings occur when someone drives a vehicle into dangerous flood water.

By that measure, entering flood water in a vehicle accounts for more flood-related drownings than all other circumstances combined.

Never drive around a barrier that closes off a flooded road, the National Weather Service warns. The road beneath the water may have collapsed.

That possible collapse is the reason the agency gives for the rule. Floodwater hides the surface underneath it. A stretch of road that looks intact from behind the barrier may no longer be there.

The NWS guidance also applies to roads without barriers. The agency states that driving into flood waters is never safe. That rule holds on any flooded road, whether or not a crew has closed it.

The warning covers people on foot as well. The NWS says walking into flood waters is never safe either. Leaving a car to cross a flooded stretch on foot falls under the same warning.

Read together, the two points link a depth to a behavior. The 12-inch threshold describes what rushing water can do to a car. The CDC figure shows how often a flood drowning involves a driver who entered dangerous water.

The National Weather Service says 2 feet of rushing water can carry off SUVs and trucks.

Many flood deaths involve vehicles that moving water carries downstream, according to the National Weather Service. The agency describes many of these drownings as preventable.

Guidance from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention warns that floodwater of sufficient depth can carry away sport utility vehicles and pickup trucks. The same guidance explains what happens to a vehicle after it drives into floodwater.

At first, the vehicle floats. Air trapped inside the cab holds it up. Water then comes in through the doors. The vehicle grows heavier as it fills, and then it sinks.

A sunken vehicle creates another problem for anyone still inside. Under water, the pressure outside the cab differs from the pressure inside it. That difference makes the doors impossible to open.

The agency treats a barrier on a flooded road as a place to stop, not an obstacle to steer around. Its message at that point matches its campaign name: turn around.

Two road sign designs carry the "Turn Around Don't Drown" message: a yellow permanent warning sign and a pink temporary incident sign. The National Weather Service describes where each one belongs and what it looks like.

The yellow sign reads "When Flooded, Turn Around Don't Drown." It meets Federal Highway Administration standards. It is designed to stay in place as a permanent road sign.

The NWS lists three kinds of locations where the yellow sign belongs. One is a spot where flooding happens often. Another is a spot where flooding begins quickly. The third is a place where crews cannot realistically put up incident signs in time.

The yellow sign has a black legend and a black border on a yellow background. The NWS specifies the yellow as RGB 255:208:69, or Pantone 116. Warning signs are covered in Chapter 2C of the Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices, known as the MUTCD.

The pink sign reads "Flooding Ahead Turn Around Don't Drown." Through a Letter of Support, the Federal Highway Administration has encouraged its use as an official incident management sign.

Incident management signs are one type of Temporary Traffic Control sign. They are used for short-term events. The NWS gives accidents, natural disasters and hazardous material spills as examples.

The pink sign is meant for places where stream water running across a road makes passage dangerous. One example is a road that dips down to the level of a stream channel. Another is a bridge or culvert that cannot handle high flows.

The pink sign goes on temporary sign holders. It is not meant to be mounted on construction barricades.

The two designs split the job by timing. The yellow sign stays up at locations with a frequent or fast-developing flood risk. The pink sign is a temporary tool, set up for a specific event where water is crossing the road.
