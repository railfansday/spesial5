Everyone wants a piece of creator IPs. Outshine Talent founder Barbara Jones is making that work in her clients’ favor.

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Creator IPs have been the talk of the town this year, and we don’t expect that to change as we head into 2027. As you’ll know if you’re a regular Tubefilter reader, there are many creator IP projects in the works–some with major Hollywood studios, some with Netflix and Disney, some with smaller independent studios, and some produced entirely by creators themselves (maybe with a little help from YouTube?).

While much of the attention around this new media “gold rush” has focused on mainstream entertainment companies (finally) recognizing creators’ value, we think it’s equally as important to spotlight development efforts happening within established creator-space companies.

That’s why we chatted with Barbara Jones, who founded Outshine Talent in 2019. Prior to launching Outshine, Jones founded influencer marketing agency Intuition Media Group, where she spent 12 years, and also served as a founding board member of the Influencer Marketing Association.

Jones recently expanded Outshine to a new arm, called Outshine Entertainment, that focuses on helping creators build original IPs across things like short films, microdramas, full-length films, and TV; it also produces live events. Outshine is encouraging its clients to take creative chances and make their dream projects, and is also encouraging studios and streamers that could offer financial and production support for those projects to get involved.

“It’s a natural evolution for a lot of these creators to say, ‘I want to do more,'” Jones says. “‘I’m a great writer, I’m a comedian, I’m an actor, I want to do more of these things.’ And I feel the industry is actually realizing the value and saying, ‘Yeah, I think you’re right. We need to start supporting these new, fresh faces, fresh ideas, things that are maybe a little more inexpensive and worth taking a shot on, seeing what we can do.'”

Jones says she feels the atmosphere right now is ripe for creators to experiment, and to license out their production ideas to places like Tubi that are willing to take the ride with them. She believes creators keeping a relatively low production cost will spur companies to support them.

“We’re filling the pipeline with originals and new ideas and things that don’t have to cost a fortune to try and test,” she says. “Creators have that ability to test things out, see where they go, and then expand upon them if something’s really, really going.”

She points to the Romano Brothers series as an example. Sahid, Maystrik, and Hernandez originally created the series as a parody of popular love sibling-rivalry love triangles in shows like The Summer I Turned Pretty. They weren’t sure it would take off, but they threw it up on TikTok–and after a very enthusiastic reception from viewers, they committed to the idea and turned it into a full-fledged original series with Tubi.

Successful creators tend to be inherently good at identifying what content resonates with their viewers, and how to build on that content’s themes and ideas to widen the scope and keep people coming back. That’s exactly what happened with Never Have I Ever Spent the Winter with the Romano Brothers.

“They had just done it for themselves,” Jones says. “But they realized it would turn into something because it had audience interest. And that’s the fun part about it. I always say to our talent: The gatekeepers are gone. You’ve just got to try it. Just make it. We can always find money. We can find resources. We can find those things, but everyone’s looking for fresh ideas, for ingenuity.”

She points to another plus of working with creators: They’re “speedboats” whereas traditional entertainment tends to be a “cruise ship.”

With creators, “You can be quicker to try things,” she says. “And, for the first time, I’m really feeling like there are outside forces that want to help creators fund some of these things.”

With that funding in hand, creators are “leveling up, because now these are productions versus being solo, making everything yourself,” Jones says.

Some funding can come from brands, Jones thinks. “I was just talking to a brand at one of our events, and I was saying, Why aren’t we just doing a microdrama? Let’s just do a microdrama that has product placement in it, but is actually a fun storyline,” she says. “That takes a normal sponsored post to the next level. We have to start thinking differently. I really believe creators are modern media, and I think people are catching up to that and realizing [a creator’s] audience is what they need to be focused on.”

We’ve already seen a few brands push in and build their own microdramas to advertise products. It’s not far-fetched at all to envision a world where Sahid, Maystrik, and Hernandez’s next parody project comes with a sponsor–maybe even one that’s willing to be a little self-aware and play along with the satire. Imagine a classic romcom “oh no, there’s only one bed and it’s snowing outside so we have to cuddle!” situation sponsored by a cozy sleepwear company, or a hot cocoa brand.

Jones says supporting creators’ original IPs is a top priority for Outshine going into 2027.

“It’s very exciting. I have a lot of young, fresh faces who are super creative and love to have ideas, and now I feel like I actually have places to bring them,” she says. “I have people who are really listening to what creators can do.”
