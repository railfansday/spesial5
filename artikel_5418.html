10 unusual artificial reefs, from subway cars and a tank to sculptures

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These unusual artificial reefs turn subway cars, an aircraft carrier, a tank, sculptures and concrete modules into habitat where corals, mussels and fish can settle

A retired New York City subway car, an American M42 Duster tank and a decommissioned U.S. Navy aircraft carrier now share the same job. Each was placed on the seafloor on purpose. Each now serves as a surface where corals, mussels, sponges and fish can settle.

An artificial reef is a human-made structure put underwater to give marine life something hard to grow on. Large stretches of ocean floor are flat mud or sand. The larvae of corals, oysters and sponges need a firm surface to attach to. Small fish need crevices where they can shelter from predators. A sunken hull, a concrete module or a cement sculpture can supply both. Once algae and invertebrates take hold, larger fish arrive to feed, and a bare patch of seabed becomes working habitat.

These projects serve people as well as wildlife. Wreck sites and sculpture parks attract recreational divers and snorkelers. Some are placed with the stated aim of drawing visitors away from fragile natural reefs. Retired ships also have to be cleared of toxic substances before they go into the water. The aircraft carrier on this list was checked for that by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency before it was sunk.

The 10 entries below, all unusual artificial reefs or reef-building methods, fall into three broad groups. Some are decommissioned machines. They include that aircraft carrier, a World War II transport ship and a military transport plane that once served in the Turkish Air Force. A tank in Jordan's Aqaba Marine Park and hundreds of subway cars off Delaware belong to the same group. Some are works of art, including two installations in an underwater museum near Cancun, Mexico. The rest are purpose-built structures. One is a reef off Key Biscayne, Florida, designed to evoke the mythical city of Atlantis. Others are hollow concrete modules used around the world and cement pyramids set beside natural coral in Indonesia.

The entries sit in waters ranging from the Gulf of Mexico and the U.S. Atlantic coast to the Caribbean, the Red Sea and Indonesia's Coral Triangle. Each offers a different answer to the same practical question: what can go on the ocean floor to give sea life a foothold?

An American M42 Duster tank rests on the seabed of the Aqaba Marine Park in Jordan. The Jordanian Royal Ecological Diving Society sank it on purpose in 1999 so that sea sponges and corals would have somewhere to settle.

The wreck lies about 15 feet below the surface, according to Daniel Keller, who compiled a list of unique artificial reefs on Medium.

At that depth, the tank sits within reach of snorkelers as well as scuba divers. Keller describes the site as a well-known spot for both activities.

The sinking was a deliberate ecological project. The diving society's stated aim was to give sponges and corals a foothold inside the marine park.

Keller writes that sea stars, lionfish and shrimp now live on and around the wreck. Those residents cover three distinct groups of sea life. Sea stars are echinoderms, lionfish are predatory fish and shrimp are crustaceans.

Neptune Memorial Reef is a 16-acre artificial reef off Key Biscayne, Florida, designed to resemble the mythical sunken city of Atlantis. Its metal and cement structures give algae and coral a hard surface to settle and grow on.

The reef takes its name from Neptune, the Roman god of the sea. Key Biscayne is a barrier island just off Miami, linked to the mainland by the Rickenbacker Causeway. The reef sits in the Atlantic Ocean off the island's shore.

Atlantis comes from Plato. He described the island civilization in two dialogues, Timaeus and Critias, written around 360 BCE. In Critias, Plato names Poseidon, the Greek counterpart of Neptune, as the god who founded Atlantis. He placed the island beyond the Pillars of Hercules. In his account, Atlantis was a powerful naval empire that was defeated by ancient Athens. He wrote that it sank into the sea in a single day and night.

The reef recreates that drowned city in architectural detail. Statues of lions, decorative gates and pillars stand across Neptune Memorial Reef. Marine growth now covers the pillars. Together they give the site the look of a city in ruins.

Arches and holes built into the structures give small fish a stable place to live. Reef-building corals lay down skeletons of calcium carbonate. Each colony that grows on the reef adds its own skeleton to the structure.

The reef also serves as an undersea burial ground. It was built so that the memorial would also support a healthy marine environment. Snorkelers and scuba divers are free to visit.

A 2012 study measured how much life the structures had drawn. It counted 195 coral colonies and 56 fish species at the site, according to Daniel Keller's list of unique artificial reefs.

Those counts come from one survey in one year. They show what lived on the reef at that point, not how its populations have changed since.

The USS Oriskany is a decommissioned aircraft carrier that was sunk in the Gulf of Mexico near Pensacola, Florida, in 2006 to serve as an artificial reef. Daniel Keller's Medium list of unique artificial reefs describes it as the world's largest artificial reef made from a sunken vessel.

Keller's post gives the carrier's weight as 30,800 tons and its length as 888 feet. Those two figures are the basis for the size claim. Most other entries on the list are vehicles, sculptures or concrete modules. The Oriskany was a warship built to launch and recover aircraft at sea.

That scale shapes what the reef offers. A single tank or sculpture gives marine life one compact patch of hard surface. A carrier hull of this length offers a long run of steel, with flat decks, vertical sides and enclosed compartments. Those surfaces sit at different depths and angles. That gives settling organisms a wider range of places to attach.

Before the sinking, the Environmental Protection Agency made sure the Oriskany was free of toxic substances, according to Keller's post. A hull still carrying toxic material would work against the purpose of an artificial reef, which is to give marine life a place to settle and grow.

Once the carrier settled on the bottom, its hull and deck became hard structure in open water. Algae, corals and mussels now grow on the ship, Keller writes. Each of those organisms attaches to a firm surface, and the wreck offers a large one. As that growth builds up, the steel outline of a warship turns into living habitat.

Divers call the wreck the Great Carrier Reef, according to Keller's post. The nickname plays on Australia's Great Barrier Reef and points back to the vessel's original role as an aircraft carrier. The name treats the wreck as a reef first and a ship second, which matches the reason it was sunk.

Redbird Reef, off the Delaware coast, includes 714 retired New York City subway cars. The reef lies 80 feet down and covers more than 1.3 square miles of seabed.

The subway cars share the site with other retired hardware. The reef also holds 86 armored personnel carriers and tanks, all retired from service. About 3,000 tons of truck tires went into the water as well. Eight barges and tugboats round out the list of materials.

That inventory draws on surplus from several different sectors. Urban transit supplied the rail cars, and the military supplied the armored vehicles. Trucking supplied the tires, and the barges and tugboats came from maritime work. None of those items was originally built with marine life in mind.

Redbird Reef is built from many objects rather than from a single wreck. Each one adds hard surfaces, edges and hollow spaces to the seafloor. That sets it apart from reefs made from one sunken ship, where a single hull supplies all the structure.

The surrounding Mid-Atlantic seabed is featureless mud and sand. The steel shells of the rail cars and military vehicles supply structure that the bottom there otherwise lacks.

Oysters grow on the sunken equipment. Blue mussels live there too, along with other invertebrates that attach to hard surfaces. Both oysters and blue mussels are filter feeders that strain plankton from the surrounding water.

The Medium post by Daniel Keller that profiles the reef also makes a claim about its output. The post says the reef has produced more than 400 times as much food for marine life as the plain seabed. It does not name a study or survey behind the comparison.

Cars that once carried commuters through New York now rest on the Atlantic floor off Delaware. They sit beside armored vehicles, truck tires and tugboats that were retired from very different jobs.

The Silent Evolution is an underwater art installation made up of 450 human figures, set inside a national marine park near Cancun, Mexico. It belongs to the Cancun Underwater Museum of Art, a collection created to improve marine ecosystems and draw more tourists to the area.

The sculptor behind it is Jason deCaires Taylor. Unlike a scuttled ship or a retired military vehicle, this reef was made for the seabed from the start. Its subject is people rather than machinery.

The installation lies on Manchones Reef in Mexico. It is one of the works in the MUSA Collection.

The figures are arranged in groups of standing people. Some tilt their faces up toward the sky. Others look down at the seafloor beneath them.

Taylor builds his sculptures from marine-grade cement. The material contains no metals or other substances that could harm marine life. He also leaves parts of each surface rough on purpose. Those textured patches give coral larvae a place to gain a foothold.

The figures do two jobs for the museum. Visitors come to see them as an exhibit. Reef organisms use the same surfaces as a place to settle and grow.

A C-47 Dakota military transport plane was sunk off the Karaada coast in 2008 to become an artificial reef. The wreck is now a dive site that supports a wide range of marine species, including giant groupers.

Before it reached the seabed, the aircraft flew as a transport plane in the Turkish Air Force.

The Dakota is an aircraft rather than a ship, a ground vehicle or a purpose-built concrete module.

Divers visit the site to see the plane itself and the sea life that now lives on and around it.

The USNS Hoyt S. Vandenberg is a World War II transport ship that was sunk on purpose off Key West, Florida, in 2009 to create an artificial reef. Daniel Keller's Medium list of unique artificial reefs calls it the world's second-largest artificial reef, after the USS Oriskany.

The ship is 522 feet long. That makes it 366 feet shorter than the Oriskany, the aircraft carrier that sits ahead of it in Keller's size comparison. The two Florida wrecks top that comparison, but the carrier is far larger.

The Vandenberg's resting place was chosen in advance. Keller writes that researchers spent months surveying the seabed before the ship went down. They were looking for a spot where the hull would not harm the natural reefs nearby.

That survey addresses a basic risk of ship reefs. A hull set down on top of living coral would destroy part of the habitat the project is meant to add to. Placing it on open seabed avoids that trade-off.

Keller describes the wreck as a popular recreational dive site.

Keller also writes that authorities hope the Vandenberg will ease the pressure heavy tourism puts on natural reefs. The aim is for some divers to choose the ship over living coral elsewhere. Keller presents this as a hope for the project, not as a measured result.

Urban Reef is a sculpture in the shape of a house, placed on the seafloor off the coast of Mexico as part of the Cancun Underwater Museum of Art. Open windows in its walls lead into enclosed spaces where fish and other marine animals can shelter.

The piece is by the sculptor Taylor, who also created The Silent Evolution in the same museum. He developed the design with marine biologists. The building is meant to be occupied by sea life rather than people.

The layout works as habitat. The open windows serve as entry points. Fish can swim from the surrounding water into the enclosed rooms and back out again. Those rooms give fish and other animals a protected place to stay.

Urban Reef shares the museum with sculpted human figures. The house takes architecture rather than the human body as its subject. It turns a familiar human shelter into one built for marine animals.

Taylor builds his sculptures from marine-grade cement. The material contains no metals or other additives that could harm sea creatures. He also leaves some surfaces rough so coral larvae can attach firmly. His forms include openings and gaps that let fish and other animals pass in and out.

Taylor placed his sculptures near the Mesoamerican Reef off Cancun. His aim was to draw visitors away from the natural reef and ease some of the pressure on it.

Taylor has said that the first layers of reef life, such as algae and mollusks, can start attaching to his sculptures within days or weeks.

Smithsonian magazine reports that scientists are divided over whether manmade sculptures on the seafloor help marine ecosystems. Some consider them too disruptive. Others expect coral to grow over the figures and hide them completely within 10 years. On that view, Taylor's sculptures would eventually disappear from sight beneath the coral growth they were made to host.

Reef Balls are a building method rather than a single reef: hollow concrete spheres with perforated surfaces, set on the seabed to give fish and corals somewhere to live. More than 500,000 of them have helped produce about 4,000 new coral colonies, according to a Medium post by Daniel Keller of Naples, Florida.

Keller places those colonies across Asia, Africa, the U.S. and the Caribbean. That geographic spread separates the modules from the other entries in this list, each of which sits at a single named location. Reef Balls were also built for no other purpose. Ships, tanks and subway cars become reefs at the end of a working life. These structures are cast from the start to serve as habitat.

Keller describes the modules as custom-designed, and the design centers on shape and material. The hollow interior and the holes in the shell are meant to attract fish. The concrete is part of the design too. Keller writes that it is environmentally safe and formulated to match the composition and pH of seawater.

Builders sometimes attach coral plugs directly to the modules, which gives coral a head start on the bare concrete. Keller says the practice speeds up the growth of habitat around the structures. Without plugs, a module depends on drifting coral larvae arriving and settling on their own.

The approach has also been used in lakes. For a project in Lake Murray, workers placed a large bladder inside each mold and filled the gap around it with concrete. The molds were bought from the Reef Ball Foundation. The finished modules are dome-shaped, with openings that let fish swim through in both directions.

In Nova Scotia, community members in Pictou Landing made the reef balls that were placed in Bras d'Or Lake. Openings in those modules give fish and lobster places to shelter and live.

At Jemeluk, Indonesia, cement pyramids were placed on the seabed on purpose to serve as an artificial reef. They stand beside natural coral at a popular dive site. Tropical fish and green sea turtles use the area.

Unlike a sunken tank or ship, the pyramids had no earlier life above water. They are cement forms built for one job: to give marine life a hard structure on the seafloor.

The pyramids sit right next to living coral reefs rather than on an open stretch of seabed. Divers at Jemeluk can see the built structures and the natural reef at the same site.

The Jemeluk pyramids are part of a national program. Indonesian authorities installed artificial reefs as part of a wider effort to protect the country's marine ecosystems, according to Daniel Keller.

Indonesia's coastline lies within the Coral Triangle. Keller writes that the region has recently faced illegal fishing and coral degradation. The cement pyramids at Jemeluk are one of the artificial reef projects placed under that national effort to protect Indonesian waters.
