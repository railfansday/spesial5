10 pharma deals worth $9.2 billion to $41 billion, and what each buyer got

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

From Sanofi's $41 billion Insmed deal to Merck's Cidara bet, 10 recent acquisitions show what drugmakers buy and what must go right.

Drugmakers are buying other companies again, and the largest of them are spending billions of dollars on a single purchase. Drug and biotech companies cut back on acquisitions to a low point in 2022, then bought at a surging pace in 2025 and faster still in 2026, according to CNBC. The buying goes far beyond a handful of famous names, because by early June 2026 companies had agreed to 201 biotech deals worth $106 billion worldwide. None of the purchases that follow is an isolated gamble. Together they show one industry moving in the same direction at the same time.

Many of these purchases are defensive. Every best-selling medicine eventually loses its patent protection, and a drugmaker that depends on one needs its next source of revenue in place before that drug's sales begin to fall. Much of what these companies are buying hasn't proven itself yet. Buyers are paying for experimental drugs and for analysts' sales forecasts far more often than for revenue a target already collects. A headline price in this wave therefore says little about what a company earns today. It says more about what its buyer expects the next decade to bring.

Reuters surveyed large drug and biotech takeovers since 2015, ranging from Sanofi's purchase of Insmed to Pfizer $PFE's bidding contest for Metsera. Several of them aren't for medicines at all. Merck $MRK KGaA is buying lab-supplies maker Bio-Techne $TECH, Boston Scientific $BSX is buying device maker Penumbra, and Abbott $ABT is buying cancer-test maker Exact Sciences. That spread shows that the buying reaches past drug pipelines into the tools researchers use, the devices doctors use and the tests they order.

Sanofi is paying to stop depending on one medicine. The drugmaker agreed to pay about $41 billion in cash for Insmed, according to Reuters, and with it Sanofi gains a source of growth separate from its blockbuster, Dupixent. Insmed already sells two respiratory drugs, Brinsupri and Arikayce. Sanofi is buying medicines that patients already take, not candidates still in the lab. Dupixent is expected to lose its exclusivity in 2031–32, according to a Jefferies estimate reported by CNBC. What Sanofi bought is time, with a second business running before its main one starts to fade.

The price makes sense only if Insmed's drugs grow as large as analysts expect. Jefferies analysts forecast that Brinsupri will bring in $6.8 billion in revenue in 2035. Sanofi is paying now for sales that are still about a decade away. That forecast also has to hold up against competition in the respiratory field where Insmed works, and AstraZeneca $AZN is studying its own drug, tozorakimab, for the lung disease bronchiectasis. A purchase like this does not repair the research gaps that left Sanofi relying on Dupixent. "The deal does not fix Sanofi's R&D/pipeline questions, but it buys time," Jefferies analysts said.

Not everyone sees the deal as a stopgap. Barclays analysts say the deal improves Sanofi's growth outlook and could make the company a more attractive investment. Investors agreed when Sanofi announced that it had agreed to buy Insmed, and they sent its shares up 8% in Paris. Their support matters because the bet belongs to Belen Garijo, who had been Sanofi's CEO only since May when she committed the company to the 2026 deal.

Whether Garijo bought a fix or only a reprieve will become clear when Dupixent loses its protection and Brinsupri's sales either meet the forecast or fall short.

Vertex Pharmaceuticals $VRTX is paying the kind of sum a major drugmaker spends on a broad business, but it is getting a small and specialized one. Vertex agreed to pay about $10 billion for Crinetics Pharmaceuticals, a deal that adds treatments for rare hormone disorders to its range, according to Reuters. Crinetics' business centers on PALSONIFY, a drug for acromegaly in adults. Few Americans have the condition, and Vertex and Crinetics estimate that only 20,000 of them have been diagnosed with it. With a patient pool that small, Vertex is not paying for the revenue Crinetics earns today.

What Vertex is paying for is a projection. The companies expect the merged business to bring in more than $5 billion a year at its peak, but they don't expect the purchase to lift Vertex's non-GAAP operating income until 2029. Until then, Vertex carries the cost of the deal before it adds to profit. If sales come in below that projection, Vertex will wait even longer for a return.

Vertex is not buying expertise in hormone disorders. It is betting that the expertise it already has will work in a new field. "We believe Vertex can build on the strong momentum of the PALSONIFY launch by applying our experience in commercializing medicines for rare genetic diseases," Reshma Kewalramani, Vertex's chief executive officer and president, said, as reported by Vertex and Crinetics. Vertex's experience is in rare genetic diseases, while Crinetics works in rare hormone disorders, which is a neighboring field rather than familiar ground. For the deal to pay off, the young launch has to keep growing, and the forecast has to turn into real sales by the end of the decade.

Vertex bought a forecast in a neighboring field, and it pays only if Vertex's rare-disease playbook works there too.

Merck $MRK KGaA, a separate company from Merck & Co, isn't buying a medicine. It is buying the equipment other drugmakers use to discover and produce theirs. Merck KGaA is paying $11.3 billion for Bio-Techne $TECH to widen its life-science business through lab and bioprocessing equipment. The company is paying for the tools behind drug research and production, not for a product that needs a trial win. This is a move into a neighboring field. Merck KGaA will profit if drug developers keep spending heavily on research and manufacturing, whether or not any single molecule works.

Merck KGaA has grown through large acquisitions before. It bought Sigma-Aldrich in 2014, so a purchase on this scale is a familiar move for the company. The Bio-Techne deal also comes under new leadership. Kai Beckmann took over as chief executive from Belén Garijo in May, which puts a commitment of this size among the first decisions of his tenure.

With no clinical gamble involved, the risk lies in the price. Merck KGaA agreed to pay a 24% premium to Bio-Techne's Wednesday closing share price. The company chose to pay well above what investors valued Bio-Techne at just before the offer in order to win it. Merck KGaA is also spending more than it holds. The company has about 2.74 billion euros ($3.11 billion) in cash and equivalents, far short of what it agreed to pay for Bio-Techne. It is funding the purchase with a mix of cash and debt. For the purchase to pay off, drug developers must keep buying Bio-Techne's tools in enough volume to cover both the premium and the debt.

Merck KGaA isn't betting on which drug will win. It is betting that whoever wins will need its equipment, and it is taking on debt to make that bet.

AbbVie spent its money to go deeper into the field it already depends on. The company agreed to pay $10.9 billion for Apogee Therapeutics to strengthen its pipeline of immune-disease drug candidates, according to Reuters. Immunology is already a major business for AbbVie, which reports $9.165 billion in net revenue from its immunology portfolio in the third quarter of 2026 alone. The purchase concentrates AbbVie's bets in one field rather than spreading them across several. That makes the deal a wager on staying ahead in immunology, where the company has the most to lose if it runs short of new drugs.

AbbVie isn't buying time to replace a fading drug, because its immunology business is growing even as its old best-seller declines. AbbVie reports that its Humira sales were down 31.2% in the third quarter of 2026, while its immunology sales were up 16.3% over the same period. With the wider portfolio climbing despite Humira's slide, Apogee is not a rescue.

What AbbVie bought is a set of drug candidates whose value depends on results that haven't come in yet. One of them, APG273, is a candidate aimed at both IL-13 and TSLP that AbbVie reports it is developing for asthma. It sells nothing today, and its worth lies in what it might eventually do for patients. Robert A. Michael, AbbVie's chairman and chief executive officer, counted the Apogee purchase among the steps by which the company "enhanced our long-term growth potential," Michael said. He tied the deal to AbbVie's future growth rather than to its current sales. For the bet to pay off, Apogee's candidates have to succeed in trials and win approval, and only then can they keep AbbVie's immunology business growing beyond the medicines it sells today.

GSK bought cancer drugs, but what it is really paying for is protection against its own patent calendar. The company agreed to pay $10.6 billion for Nuvalent's advanced-stage cancer drug candidates, according to Reuters. CNBC reports that GSK is counting on the purchase to lift its revenue growth starting in 2027, and that its leading HIV medicine will start losing market exclusivity in 2028. GSK wants to be selling the new medicines before that protection ends. That makes this a defensive purchase, with GSK buying its next source of income before the current one fades.

That plan still depends on regulators. "The acquisition provides GSK with immediate new sales growth opportunities," Luke Miels, GSK's CEO, said. Yet the FDA faced a Nov. 27 deadline to decide on neladalkib and had issued no decision when GSK signed. The growth Miels described becomes real only after regulators approve a drug they had not yet decided on. Until then, GSK owns a pending application, not a product it can sell.

Even if the FDA approves both drugs, GSK appears to have bought steady sales rather than a blockbuster. GSK could collect a combined $823 million from neladalkib and zidesamtinib in 2029 if both win approval, according to a January estimate from CGS International reported by CNBC. That return is modest next to the price, and Barclays analysts see a firm limit on how much the deal can deliver. GSK chose lower-risk drugs that could reach patients sooner over a shot at a giant. That choice makes sense for a company that needs a revenue gap filled more than it needs a big win.

GSK also broke a limit it had set for itself. Miels had said he would pursue deals of £2 billion to £4 billion, yet the Nuvalent purchase is the second-largest deal in GSK's history. A chief who promised modest purchases ended up making one of his company's biggest bets. That suggests even cautious drugmakers may be spending past their own limits during this boom.

GSK bought time, but whether that time is worth the price depends on FDA rulings that had not arrived when it signed.

Boston Scientific $BSX is paying for medical devices that doctors already use, not for a drug that still has to prove itself. The company agreed to pay $14.5 billion for Penumbra to build out its blood-vessel product line, according to Reuters. Penumbra makes thrombectomy and embolization devices, which are tools that pull clots out of blood vessels or deliberately block vessels off. The purchase is also a return to a field Boston Scientific once left: It sold its neurovascular business to Stryker $SYK in 2011, MedTech Dive reports, and buying a leader lets it come back near the front. "We've really been looking for a long time on how to become a scaled, market-leading neurovascular company, not one that's coming from a distance behind the pack and trying to roll up a bunch of companies," Boston Scientific CEO Mike Mahoney said. Boston Scientific bought a new field and, with it, time, skipping the years a slower build from behind would take.

No clinical trial decides this deal, but the deal has been announced and not yet completed. Boston Scientific has agreed to carry the risk that regulators block it. The company would owe Penumbra $900 million if regulatory approval fails while every other condition is met. Penumbra's own break-up fee is smaller, at $525 million.

Analysts see a sound fit between the two companies, but Boston Scientific is entering a crowded field. Needham analyst Mike Matson regards Boston Scientific as the most sensible buyer for Penumbra. Even if regulators approve the deal, Boston Scientific gains only the chance to compete in a market that Stifel analyst Rick Wise describes as growing fast and full of strong competitors.

Boston Scientific paid to stop trailing the pack, and now it has to outrun it.

Many of the biggest prices in this wave of deals pay for drugs that haven't yet proven themselves, but Abbott $ABT paid for a business that already sells. Abbott agreed to pay up to $23 billion for Exact Sciences, Reuters reports, and the company it is buying already has sizable sales. Abbott and Exact Sciences project that Exact's 2025 revenue will top $3 billion, so Abbott is paying for current sales as well as a promise. The deal also gives Abbott a full lineup of cancer tests rather than one famous brand. Along with Cologuard, the best-known screening kit, Exact sells Oncotype DX and the newer Oncodetect and Cancerguard tests. Of the three things buyers in this wave are paying for, this is the clearest case of a foothold in a neighboring field, and Abbott collects that field's revenue from the day the deal closes.

Existing sales don't make this a safe purchase at this price, and Abbott is betting on how fast those sales keep climbing. Abbott and Exact Sciences say that Exact's organic sales are growing in the high teens and that Abbott is aiming at a $60 billion U.S. segment made up of tests that screen for cancer or help guide its treatment. Abbott paid for a business that is expanding quickly on its own in a large field, not only for its current size. Selling more into that segment depends on the newer tests, not on Cologuard alone. Robert B. Ford $F, Abbott's chairman and chief executive officer, says Exact's strengths fit alongside what Abbott already does. The payoff therefore rests on combining the two businesses well, not on simply owning Exact's revenue.

For now, Abbott has agreed on a price but has not finished the purchase. The deal, struck in 2025, still needs approval from Exact's shareholders and from regulators before it can close. The first thing that has to go right is the closing itself. The second is turning Exact's newer tests into the next Cologuard.

Until both happen, Abbott's move into cancer testing remains a plan.

Merck $MRK paid for a promise, not for a business with sales. The company agreed to pay $9.2 billion for Cidara Therapeutics, according to Reuters, and what it gets is CD388, an experimental flu-prevention drug that Merck says is still in its Phase 3 ANCHOR study. Merck is paying for what the drug might do, not for anything it sells today.

Merck's own accounting treats the deal as an investment in research rather than as the purchase of a running business. Merck expects to account for the deal as an asset acquisition and to take a charge of approximately $9.0 billion to its 2026 research and development expense. On Merck's books, nearly the whole price counts as spending on research.

Merck is making a bet this large on a disease that causes a great many deaths worldwide, and CD388 is meant to prevent flu rather than treat it. An estimated 290,000 to 650,000 people worldwide die of flu every year, according to Merck. The company is aiming the drug first at adults and adolescents at higher risk of flu complications. Those are the patients for whom preventing flu matters most. If CD388 works, it would meet a need that returns every flu season. That market is the forecast Merck paid for, but it is not yet a fact.

The cost of the bet doesn't end with the purchase price. Merck expects its earnings to come in approximately 30 cents a share lower in the deal's first 12 months, from the cost of advancing CD388 and of financing the purchase. Robert M. Davis, Merck's chairman and chief executive officer, said the acquisition "exemplifies our business development strategy of investing where compelling science and value meet." The science he means has not yet passed its final test.

Until the Phase 3 trial succeeds, Merck owns a forecast, and it will see a return on the $9.2 billion only if CD388 wins approval and prevents flu in the patients it targets.

Pfizer $PFE chose to buy its way into weight-loss drugs rather than build a presence there on its own, and what it got for the money is a forecast. The $10 billion deal gives Pfizer an entry into the fast-growing market for weight-loss drugs, Reuters reports, but Metsera's drugs have yet to sell. Pfizer's former research and development chief, John LaMattina, believes the company must see Metsera's pipeline as central to where it goes next. The deal's terms also leave part of the price open. The $10 billion headline includes contingent payments, so the cash Pfizer is sure to hand over is lower.

A bidding war, more than a calm valuation, set the final price. Pfizer offered $7.3 billion in September and had to raise its offer to $10 billion while competing with Novo Nordisk for the company. The contest ended after the U.S. Federal Trade Commission sent Novo and Metsera a letter about antitrust risk, and Metsera's board then sided with Pfizer. Novo's offer carried "unacceptably high legal and regulatory risks," Metsera said. Pfizer paid what it did as much because of the fight and the regulator's warning as because of what Metsera is worth.

Pfizer won't know for many years whether the price was worth paying. Courtney Breen, an analyst at Bernstein, puts the revenue Pfizer would need by 2040 at $11 billion if the purchase is to earn back its cost. David Risinger of Leerink Partners expects Metsera's drugs, taken together, to reach $5 billion in sales at their height. Both estimates describe a business that Metsera's drugs would have to build over the coming decade and beyond. Pfizer will learn whether its bet works only as those drugs reach patients and their sales grow.

Pfizer bought a place in the weight-loss race, not a lead in it.

Merck $MRK & Co is buying Verona Pharma to prepare for the day its best-selling drug loses protection. Merck agreed to pay about $10 billion for Verona to strengthen its respiratory lineup as Keytruda patents near expiry, according to Reuters. Key Keytruda patents begin expiring in 2028, and that leaves Merck only a few years to line up new revenue. Verona can't fill that gap alone, given that Merck took in nearly $30 billion in Keytruda revenue in 2024. The purchase gives Merck one piece of a replacement rather than the replacement itself, and the company is buying time in installments.

Merck paid almost entirely for sales that don't exist yet. Verona sold just $42.3 million worth of its drug Ohtuvayre in 2024, a small sum next to a price of about $10 billion. What Merck bought is a forecast of what Ohtuvayre might become. For the deal to pay off, Ohtuvayre's sales have to grow very large, and they have to grow before Merck starts losing Keytruda revenue to patent expirations.

Merck's earlier purchases give some investors reason to trust this one. Merck bought Prometheus Biosciences in 2023. Its 2021 purchase of Acceleron Pharma brought it Winrevair. "Merck deal looks good at first glance. Given their home run with Prometheus and Winrevair, this (Ohtuvayre) looks like a potential complementary therapy," said Kevin Gade, chief operating officer at Bahl & Gaynor. Gade's confidence rests on Merck's past deals rather than on anything Ohtuvayre has sold.

Merck's record earns it some trust, but the deal pays off only if Ohtuvayre grows into the forecast Merck paid for.
