AI's gains concentrate at a few firms: 10 findings on pay, hiring and productivity

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Free daily briefing on global business news.

PwC's 2026 AI jobs barometer shows productivity, hiring and pay gains pooling at a few firms and workers. Here are 10 findings.

The argument over AI and work used to turn on whether the technology would ever reach ordinary jobs. That argument is settled. Employers and workers already use AI every day, in offices, call centers and finance departments. Engineers are no longer the only workers who feel the technology, since nearly 40% of jobs around the world are open to change from AI, according to the IMF. When that much work is open to a new technology, asking whether it will change jobs no longer tells anyone much. The questions worth asking are who collects its gains and who is left out.

Using AI at work has become common, but its payoff for employment is harder to find. About 43% of U.S. workers regularly use AI on the job, according to Indeed Hiring Lab. The spread of AI-related skills has done nothing so far to lift employment, according to the IMF. Wide use of a tool and broad benefit from it are not the same thing. That leaves open where AI's gains are going, and the more revealing story is which companies and which workers are capturing them.

PwC's 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer set out to measure how AI was reshaping jobs, skills, pay and company productivity. PwC looked at a small group of superstar companies, the pay premium for AI skills and junior jobs that now ask for senior abilities. Jobs, skills, pay and output are the four things workers and employers feel most directly, so they are the right places to look for where AI's gains are going. Some of those gains reach across sectors, while others collect at a handful of companies or among a narrow group of workers. A gap between leaders and laggards shows who is ahead, but it does not explain why. "Careful interpretation of this chart is necessary – this is not to say AI is causing these impacts," PwC said in a note on one of its charts. That caution applies to every gap that follows. Set beside independent data, each finding shows how far a given gain reaches.

The companies most exposed to AI are opening a wide productivity lead over the rest, and that lead is getting bigger fast. Since 2022, their advantage has tripled, and their productivity now grows 40% faster than that of the companies least exposed to AI, according to PwC. Those firms are not simply ahead of their peers. They are moving further away from them each year. For a company, heavy exposure to AI has gone together with pulling ahead.

Across whole industries, the gains look far calmer. The Federal Reserve Board reports that industries more exposed to AI do have higher productivity growth, but their trends have stayed steady over time instead of widening. Information, finance, and professional and business services rank among the industries most exposed to AI. AI tools are built for this desk-based work, so any broad gain from AI should show up there first. The two findings fit together. A sharply widening lead for some companies, alongside a steady trend for their industries, suggests the gains are concentrated in particular firms within those sectors rather than spread across them.

Economists studying the national data see the same split between company results and wider growth. Paul E. Soto, Mason Thieu and Jeffrey S. Allen, economists at the Federal Reserve Board, said the pattern is "suggestive of micro-level productivity gains not adding up in aggregate." Strong results at the leading companies are not yet visible as a broad rise in output. The productivity gap is best read as a measure of how far some companies have pulled ahead, not of how much AI has raised productivity across the economy.

If the gains sit with particular companies, the next question is whether every exposed firm shares in them or mainly those at the top of the group.

Companies that are heavily exposed to AI do not all benefit equally. Within that group, a handful of firms have pulled far ahead of everyone else. The top fifth of the most AI-exposed firms average 163% productivity growth, far above the rest of their own group, according to PwC. Many companies are exposed to AI, but the biggest productivity gains show up at the firms at the very top of that group. The 163% figure describes what a few leading companies achieved, not what exposed companies in general have managed.

Across the wider economy, firms that use AI more heavily see gains that are real but small. Economists Tina Highfill and Jon D. Samuels of the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, or BEA, estimate that each additional percentage point of workers who use AI frequently goes with only about 0.1% to 0.2% higher real output. Compared with that everyday range, the top firms' result is an extreme case, and other companies should not expect to match it. Even a more generous outside estimate stays in single digits. A study of European firms by Aldasoro and colleagues estimated a 4% rise in labor productivity, according to Aldasoro figures reported by BEA. That estimate falls far below the top tier's average. Managers planning their own AI spending should treat the 163% figure as an exceptional result that a few firms reached, not as a return they can count on.

The largest productivity gains so far appear at a small set of leading companies, and the next question is whether workers at AI-heavy employers share in them through hiring and pay.

Many people expect the companies that lean hardest on AI to cut jobs, but the most exposed firms are adding workers faster than anyone else. Those companies grew their staff by 52%, compared with 36% at less exposed firms, according to PwC. The employers with the most AI in their work are the ones hiring most quickly. They are also paying their people more, with pay rising 24% at the most exposed firms against 17% elsewhere. Staff at these companies are getting larger raises as well as more colleagues. At these employers, heavy exposure to AI has gone together with growth in both the size of the workforce and what it earns.

The upside is real, but it belongs to the workers at a particular set of employers. PwC takes a hopeful view of AI and jobs more generally. "Far from being a job killer, AI may actually be a job expander," PwC said. The word "may" marks that view as a possibility, not a finding about jobs across the economy. The faster hiring and bigger raises took place inside the exposed companies themselves. The figures show those firms growing faster than their peers, not the job market as a whole getting larger. For a worker, the lesson is that the stronger hiring and better pay are found at AI-heavy employers. That pattern gives one more sign that the rewards from AI are collecting at the companies where AI use is already concentrated.

Employers wanted AI specialists far more than they wanted workers in general in 2025. They posted 68.9% more openings for AI specialists that year, while they posted only 8.6% more openings across all jobs, according to PwC. That gap shows where companies are putting their money, which is into a narrow group of workers they consider worth competing for. Companies can post many openings for a skill without hiring many people for it, though. How many people end up holding these jobs is what matters to someone looking for work.

Data science, one of the occupations most closely tied to AI, is expected to keep growing, but it will supply a fairly small number of new positions. BLS projects about 82,500 more data scientist jobs in 2034 than in 2024. That is real growth for the field, yet it adds far fewer positions than the larger tech occupations will. Strong demand for a small pool of workers fits the larger pattern of AI's rewards collecting among a few. For most job seekers, the specialist boom is worth following, but it is not where their next job is likely to come from.

The largest projected gain in tech jobs goes to a mainstream role. BLS projects about 267,700 new software developer jobs over the same decade, the largest numerical gain among the occupations in its chart. The efficiency that companies want from AI is also expected to hold down routine work, with about 153,700 fewer customer service representative jobs projected. That decline reflects fewer future openings as companies use AI to handle the work more efficiently, not layoffs that have already happened.

The rush for AI specialists is the most visible part of a larger shift, and for most workers what matters is whether their own job is among those growing or those shrinking.

Employers outside engineering now ask for AI skills when they hire. "AI specialist hiring is rising in all sectors," PwC said. Marketing shows how far that demand has moved from software. Over 2025, employers went from mentioning AI in 8.4% of marketing job postings to 14.9%, according to Indeed Hiring Lab. For a worker in marketing or another business function, AI has become a subject employers raise in hiring, not a concern for the IT department alone.

Even in the field where employers mention AI most, they hire mainly for other work. In data and analytics, the leading field, employers mentioned AI in 45% of postings in December 2025, according to Indeed Hiring Lab. A high share in one field shows where employers want AI skills most. It does not mean the jobs in that field have become AI jobs.

In tech, employers mention AI in a large share of postings at a time when they advertise far fewer jobs. The number of tech openings employers post is 34% lower than before the pandemic, according to Indeed Hiring Lab. For someone looking for tech work, a high AI share describes what the remaining openings require, not a rise in hiring.

Across the whole U.S. job market, AI appears in only a small minority of job ads. Employers mentioned AI in 4.2% of all U.S. job postings in December 2025, the highest level on Indeed's AI tracker, according to Indeed Hiring Lab. The leading sectors show where employers are changing what they ask of new hires.

Most U.S. job ads still make no mention of AI.

Employers keep raising what they will pay for AI skills. PwC finds that employers advertise about 62% more pay on average for workers with AI skills, a bigger premium than a year earlier. A premium that grows from one year to the next shows that employers value these skills more now than they did before. For anyone deciding what to learn next, this rising pay is the clearest sign of where the money in AI sits today.

AI skills earn workers far more than the usual reward for learning something new. Employers in the U.K. and U.S. offer only about 3% more pay in job ads that ask for one new skill, according to the IMF. Workers who stack several new skills do better, and U.K. employers offer up to 15% more pay when they ask for four or more. Even that reward falls well short of what employers pay for AI skills. Employers appear to treat AI skill as a category of its own, apart from the general reward workers get for keeping up.

Few job ads ask for new skills of any kind. Employers in advanced economies ask for a new skill in only about 1 in 10 job ads, according to the IMF. Pay boosts tied to fresh skills therefore reach a limited part of the job market. A rising AI premium means workers who hold those skills are getting paid well. It does not show that AI is lifting pay for most people.

The premium may not last, either. The IMF warns that AI may automate many IT tasks. If that happens, employers' demand for tech skills need not turn into equal demand for the specialists who hold them.

The premium is a guide to where AI pays today, not a promise that the pay will last.

Employers are changing the skills they ask for much faster in some jobs than in others. In the jobs most exposed to AI, employers change their skill requirements 2.2 times as fast as in less exposed work, and that gap is 75% wider than it was a year earlier, according to PwC. For someone in one of those jobs, the knowledge that got them hired becomes outdated sooner, and they have less time to learn what replaces it. In a single year, exposed roles have moved further from other work in how quickly their skill demands shift. The pressure to keep learning is therefore uneven. Workers in the jobs most exposed to AI face most of it, while people in less exposed roles deal with a slower and more familiar rate of change.

The direction of that gap matters more than its size in any one year. A gap that widens from one year to the next points toward a job market that is splitting further apart, not one that is settling into a new balance. For workers in exposed roles, a single round of retraining is unlikely to be enough if employers there keep revising what they want. For workers elsewhere, the skills they hold today will stay useful for longer. Employers in exposed fields face a similar choice between training their staff more often and hiring new people whose skills are more current.

If the skills in exposed jobs keep changing this quickly, the next question is which abilities keep their value through all that change.

The common assumption about AI at work is that employers hand tasks to software and the job gets smaller. In the jobs most exposed to AI, the tasks employers are adding depend far more on human abilities than the tasks added in other work. Those new tasks rely on empathy, creativity and presence 2.5 times more than elsewhere, according to PwC. Workers in these roles are taking on more of the work that involves understanding other people, coming up with original ideas and being there in person. A job that is highly exposed to AI is therefore not simply a smaller job. These roles are gaining duties, and the duties they gain are ones that people perform better than software.

For someone in an exposed field, the finding shows where to put effort. Learning to use AI tools has value, but technical fluency alone does not match the kind of new work these jobs are adding. A worker who learns to read a client's needs, propose fresh approaches and handle difficult situations face to face is preparing for the tasks employers are actually assigning. Those abilities are also harder for software to take over than routine technical work.

Workers who build these abilities may find they hold a steadier place in fields that AI is reshaping quickly, because the work employers keep adding there is the work machines handle worst.

In the jobs most exposed to AI, employers now want junior hires who bring senior skills from their first day. PwC finds that junior roles in those jobs are seven times as likely to demand skills traditionally expected of senior staff, and that postings with these upgraded requirements have risen 35% since 2019. Employers have steadily made senior abilities a larger part of what they expect from a first hire. CNBC reports that junior staff under Orlando Bravo, founder of Thoma Bravo, now spend far less of their week on financial models and comparison work than they once did. "If you define the role of an associate as just doing a spreadsheet, you don't need that, but our associates are now calling on companies a lot more," Bravo said. In his account, junior staff at his firm gave up the most routine part of the job and kept a more demanding one.

A tougher job description shows what employers want from juniors, not that they want more of them. Employers raised those demands during a year of job cuts linked to AI, with over 50,000 U.S. layoffs in 2025 tied to the technology, according to CNBC. Bravo is hiring more people for the first time in his career, but he is describing his own firm rather than the wider market. He sees young workers taking on bigger work sooner. Given the layoffs, the same higher demands may also mean fewer junior jobs and a harder path into them.

Young workers who already have AI skills get paid for meeting those higher demands. Employers will pay young hires who bring AI skills as much as 25% more, according to Randstad figures reported by CNBC. That extra pay goes only to those who arrive with the skills. The British government treats the problem as one of job design. Liz Kendall, the U.K. technology secretary, says the government will train people and change how starting jobs are built. A government that plans to rework the first job confirms that the change reaches the starting role itself.

A first job now demands more than it used to, and who gets one depends more on the skills young workers bring on their first day.

The gaps between companies, skills and pay add up to a split that a worker can feel directly, in which jobs still offer a way up. That divide now runs between kinds of work, not only between firms. "Our analysis suggests opportunity is migrating from democratised roles toward professionalised and low AI exposure roles (with exceptions)," PwC said. The hedge in parentheses matters, because it marks the shift as a direction rather than a rule. Still, the track a worker lands on may depend more on the kind of job they hold than on the company that employs them.

U.S. government forecasters expect both tracks to take shape over the next decade. BLS projects that data scientist jobs will grow 34.6% from 2025 to 2035, a fast rise in exactly the kind of professionalized work that opportunity is moving toward. On the other side, it projects the loss of 752,100 office and administrative support jobs, the routine work that automation can take over. In these projections, fast growth comes in a specialized field and steep losses come in routine office work. That pattern points the same way as PwC's reading of where opportunity is heading.

The largest source of new U.S. jobs has little to do with AI. Private health care and social assistance will account for about 37.0% of projected new jobs, driven by an aging population and chronic illness, according to BLS. Exposure to AI matters for which track some jobs end up on, but a worker who weighs options by AI alone misses where much of the hiring will happen. Both tracks rest on forecasts, so the shrinking one is a projection to watch, not a settled outcome.
