It's capital gains distribution season, and the early estimates are in. After a strong year (broad U.S. benchmarks are up about 10%), outflows to ETFs are forcing many active mutual funds to sell winners and pass the gains to remaining shareholders, mostly between late November and year-end. Morningstar's early look found a handful of strategies distributing more than 30% of NAV, led by a 57% estimate. Share class matters, too: one Columbia fund shows roughly 42% for institutional shares and 56% for A shares. Estimates are preliminary and can still change.

This is a client communication opportunity hiding in plain sight, because distributions in taxable accounts are taxable even when reinvested. Clients in 401(k)s and IRAs can relax, a handy reminder of why asset location matters. Screen taxable holdings against the estimates and call clients with meaningful exposure to talk through harvesting losses to offset gains, waiting to buy until after the ex-date, and whether a lower-distribution ETF is worth the tax cost of switching. Try: "Before year-end, I checked which of your funds are expected to pay taxable gains. Here is what I found, and here is what we can do about it."

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang resigned Thursday morning after a week of reports about his personal conduct and questions about whether Nvidia chips reached China through a customer in Singapore. The first report involved an affair with an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based Nvidia customer, whom U.S. officials suspected of spying for China (China denies it). A second relationship, with one of Huang's direct reports, surfaced Wednesday, and the board met that night. CFO Colette Kress is now interim CEO while the board searches for a permanent replacement, and shares closed down about 4% after plunging as much as 14% early in the session.

Investors should take Huang's departure seriously, but we think the sell-off has gotten ahead of the business, pricing in more damage than the numbers show so far. Analyst Dan Ives estimates Nvidia is worth 25% to 30% less without him, and the market has already applied much of that. Demand is still strong (last quarter's revenue rose 106% to $96.2 billion), and we estimate that even if regulators cut off sales routed through Singapore, Nvidia would lose no more than about 5% of revenue. Export policy and customers' pricing leverage are the bigger risks. What should be done with your shares depends on position size and why you own the company: If it's a large position bought for Huang's judgment, reassess how much risk you want to carry; if it's a smaller position held for Nvidia's products and AI demand, that thesis is intact.

Between a sharp tech selloff, a hot inflation report, and Nvidia's CEO stepping down, clients have plenty to wonder about, which makes this a good week to reach out before they call you. Here is an email or call opener:

“Markets have been choppy this month, with a tech selloff, a hot inflation report, and some big headlines. Nothing about your plan has to change, but I'd like to take 15 minutes to check in on three things: how you're feeling about the swings, whether anything in your income, health, or cash needs has shifted, and a few year-end tax moves worth making before Dec. 31. Prioritize calls to clients nearing retirement, clients with concentrated stock positions, and clients holding mutual funds expected to make big year-end distributions.

On Nov. 5th, the IRS released Revenue Procedure 2026-35, which adjusts almost 70 tax provisions for 2027 (generally for returns filed in 2028). The standard deduction rises about 3%, to $33,200 for joint filers, $16,600 for single filers, and $24,925 for heads of household. The top 37% bracket still starts at $661,375 for single filers and $793,650 for joint filers, the child tax credit rises $100 to $2,300 (up to $1,800 refundable), and the health FSA limit rises $100 to $3,500.

For most clients, the standard deduction and bracket changes matter most, since they set the baseline for next year’s tax bill. On the estate and gift side, the basic estate tax exclusion rises to $15,480,000 per person (up from $15 million), and the annual gift exclusion rises to $20,000 per recipient (up from $19,000) The release does not cover 2027 retirement-plan contribution limits, which have historically been announced separately.

The tax side is murkier than the betting side: how prediction-market contracts are classified (gambling income, capital gains, or something else) remains unsettled. If winnings are treated as gambling income, a 2026 law change limits loss deductions to 90% of losses (and only for itemizers, against winnings), so a client who breaks even can still owe tax. Under capital treatment, losses can offset capital gains instead.

Why It Matters to Advisors: At 5.51%, I bonds top today’s short-Treasury ETF yields (SGOV’s 30-day SEC yield is 3.83%), which makes them worth a look for clients. There are caveats worth sharing: the 5.51% applies only for the first six months, after which the rate follows inflation (the fixed portion stays and the rest can fall), and the lockup and $10,000 cap limit how much of a reserve they can cover. Interest is also federally taxable (deferred until redemption) but exempt from state and local income tax, and I bonds can’t be held in an IRA. With September CPI at 3.7% and a December hike looming, it’s a good week to ask: “Let’s look at whether I bonds belong in your cash reserve. Do you want to run the numbers?”
