Transient nuclear softening for zygotic gene activation in mice

Nuclei in early mammalian embryos undergo epigenetic reprogramming that enables the zygote to develop into a complete organism. Whereas changes in chromatin state during this critical transition period have been extensively studied1,2,3, changes in the nucleus that houses chromatin remain poorly understood. Here, using direct organelle capture and biophysical manipulation methods, we find that nuclei attain profound softness and plasticity during the two-cell embryonic stage in mice, facilitating major zygotic gene activation. The prominent nuclear deformability is attributable to the rapid clearance of lamin B1 from the nuclear envelope by the autophagy factor LC3. Nuclear stiffening by lamin B1 retention or its overloading results in incomplete activation of zygotic genes and impaired development. However, embryos that maintain nuclear deformability escape from this transcriptional interference. High-resolution microscopy and sequential three-dimensional fluorescence in situ hybridization analyses revealed an accumulation of active RNA polymerase II and coordinated positioning of key genomic loci near the deformable nuclear envelope. An engineered molecular tether that stiffens the nuclei restricts these nuclear activities and suppresses expression of key zygotic gene activation genes. Together, our study identifies a distinct physical nuclear state that embryos acquire at the beginning of life that supports the initiation of the zygotic transcription program.

The physical characteristics of the cell nucleus are extraordinarily diverse, exhibiting distinct shape and mechanical phenotypes across tissue types, physiological states and disease conditions. Fragile or misshapen nuclei are hallmarks of cancer, cardiomyopathy and premature ageing4,5. Nuclei in stem cells gradually stiffen during differentiation4,5,6,7. While these changes are widely recognized as consequences of altered nuclear composition and chromatin dynamics, they also influence genome organization and transcription8,9,10. Despite the importance, the physical state of the nucleus at the earliest stages of mammalian development, when life begins, remains unclear.

During the first few cleavages, nuclei undergo extensive epigenetic reprogramming, enabling the zygote to initiate a new developmental program. In mice, this transition occurs within the first approximately 48 h after fertilization and is associated with a surge in new transcript production, known as major zygotic gene activation (ZGA)11,12,13. Major ZGA occurs during the mid-to-late two-cell stage, and embryos that impair ZGA fail in subsequent development. Around the time of ZGA, chromatin exhibits distinct structural and biochemical changes accompanied by histone modifiers and transcription factors14,15. By contrast, whether the nucleus organizing chromatin undergoes any crucial changes that drive ZGA is not known. In somatic cells, nuclear shape and mechanics depend on lamins, which line the inner nuclear membrane16,17. Lamins are long-lived, enriched in eggs and are crucial for mammalian development18,19,20. Previous studies have documented that, of the two major types, A-type lamins exhibit cell-lineage-specific differential expression patterns, whereas B-type lamins are constitutively expressed throughout development16. However, their dynamics and transiency, as well as the underlying core regulatory mechanism, during the earliest embryonic stages are poorly understood. Likewise, how the nuclear lamina, which forms a structural meshwork, controls nuclear mechanics and genome dynamics during this critical transition is unknown. Investigating physical nuclear characteristics necessitates direct quantification and manipulation of their mechanical properties. However, achieving this without compromising cell viability poses a challenge.

To investigate physical nuclear properties, we first performed high-resolution confocal scanning of fertilized mouse eggs during early cleavages (Fig. 1a). Our three-dimensional (3D) images of nuclei with H2B–mCherry, expressed without perturbing development (Extended Data Fig. 1a), revealed their distinct shape features, showing uneven local deformation at the two-cell stage in contrast to their predominantly spherical appearance at the one-cell and four-cell stages (Fig. 1b,c and Extended Data Fig. 1b). Timelapse imaging revealed that one-cell nuclei remained spherical during the cell cycle (Fig. 1d). Two-cell nuclei underwent slow, incremental deformation, which started at around 21 h post-insemination (h.p.i.), peaked at 24–26 h.p.i. and then gradually regressed by 32–36 h.p.i. (Fig. 1e). At the four-cell stage, nuclei were again spherical (Fig. 1f) (Extended Data Fig. 1c,d and Supplementary Videos 1−3). Before the onset of deformation, two-cell nuclei had fully swollen and reached steady sizes; once deformed, the altered nuclear shapes persisted for hours (Extended Data Fig. 1e,f). Similar deformation was observed using emerin–eGFP for the nuclear envelope (Fig. 1g) or mCherry–NLS for the nuclear interior (Extended Data Fig. 1g).

This finding prompted us to develop a biophysical setup that quantitatively characterizes the mechanical properties of early embryonic nuclei in situ (Fig. 1h). The setup equipped a pressure-controlled nanocapillary, of which the tip was inserted into a fertilized embryo to capture the nuclear surface. A weak, constant suction pressure (2.1 × 101 kPa) pinched the surface, which was then stretched through axial translocation of the nanocapillary. The nuclei could be gradually strained, resulting in tension, and the strain reached a maximum just before the detachment (Fig. 1i). The analysis could be performed from the one-cell to four-cell stages, over which the predominantly fluid-like cytoplasm minimally influenced the measured mechanics (Extended Data Fig. 2a–f). Measurements were also insensitive to nuclear markers and were comparable to label-free imaging results (Extended Data Fig. 2g,h).

Our analysis revealed that two-cell nuclei were significantly more deformable than one-cell and four-cell nuclei (Fig. 1j). The deformable nature persisted throughout the cell cycle (from 21 to 32 h.p.i.) even as DNA doubled at about 24 h.p.i. Nuclear stiffness, as estimated based on the nanocapillary pressure and the extent of nuclear deformation, transiently lowered at the two-cell stage (one-cell, 5.0 ± 1.3 nN µm−1; two-cell, 2.0 ± 0.5 nN µm−1; four-cell, 4.6 ± 1.9 nN µm−1; n = 18, 38 and 13 embryos, respectively) (Fig. 1k). The stiffness values varied between male and female pronuclei (30.7 ± 21.3%, n = 8 pairs) and between blastomeres of each two-cell embryo (23.5 ± 14.5%, n = 16 pairs), while the differences were pronounced among the developmental stages (Extended Data Fig. 2i). Two-cell nuclei also exhibited significant plasticity, in contrast to more elastic one-cell and four-cell nuclei (Fig. 1l) (shape recovery, 91.0 ± 4.3% for one-cell; 66.9 ± 8.0% for two-cell; and 87.9 ± 4.6% for four-cell). The deformable two-cell nuclear stiffness was consistent when embryos were examined using lower nanocapillary pressure that minimally perturbed development (1.2 × 101 kPa; Extended Data Fig. 2j,k). An alternative capillary-based approach, using an incremental suction pressure that aspirated nuclei, yielded consistent results (n ≥ 12 embryos per stage) (Extended Data Fig. 2l–n). These corroborate the distinct physical state of the two-cell nucleus.

Our nanocapillary-based assay at 1.2 × 101 kPa minimally perturbed development, enabling us to relate two-cell nuclear stiffness with embryos’ developmental competence (Fig. 2a). We found that all fertilized embryos that timely reached the blastocyst stage had soft nuclei (2.0 ± 0.6 nN µm−1; n = 66 of 66 embryos), while nearly half of the embryos that impaired development had stiff nuclei (n = 7 of 15 embryos; defined as more than twice the mean nuclear stiffness of the normal development) (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 3a). Detecting embryos with stiff nuclei was infrequent (8.6%; n = 7 out of 81 embryos). The outcome was regardless of the fluorescent tag type and marker expression levels (Extended Data Fig. 3b,c). A similar mechanical feature was observed for cloned embryos created through somatic cell nuclear transfer (Fig. 2a,b and Extended Data Fig. 3a,d,e) (n = 46 embryos). Thus, deformable two-cell nuclei are a signature of normal preimplantation development.

We next explored the molecular mechanism underlying the distinct physical state of the two-cell nucleus. Using immunofluorescence, we first examined four major lamins, A/C, B1 and B2, and found that lamin B1 transiently cleared from the two-cell nuclear envelope, nearly paralleling nuclear softening (Fig. 3a,b). The clearance was rapid and substantial, with an approximately 70% decrease in peripheral signal during the early-to-mid two-cell stage (21 to 24 h.p.i.; Fig. 3b). Lamin A/C and B2 levels also changed, but their trends were gradual and monotonic, with only a minor decline, if any, during the two-cell stage (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 4a). Another nuclear envelope protein, Lap2β, also changed little (Extended Data Fig. 4b). Live-embryo imaging confirmed the clearance of lamin B1 from the two-cell nuclear envelope with a decay time constant of 86 ± 44 min (n = 5 embryos; Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 4c). An immunoblotting assay revealed a reduction in lamin B1 during the two-cell stage (Fig. 3d), suggesting degradation.

Our pilot assay using wortmannin implicated that autophagy promotes lamin B1 clearance (Extended Data Fig. 4d). Autophagy is active in early embryos and is essential for preimplantation development21. In live-embryo imaging, the autophagy factor LC322 was diffusive at the one-cell stage and accumulated in the two-cell nucleus (Extended Data Fig. 4e). Our antibody microinjection targeting LC3 suppressed lamin B1 clearance (Extended Data Fig. 4f). Cell cycle progression as monitored by EdU was normal (Extended Data Fig. 4g). We therefore designed a construct that interferes with the endogenous interaction between lamin B1 and LC3, using a short fragment of lamin B1 carrying LC3-binding domains and an intrinsic nuclear localization signal23 (hereafter, sfLB1; Fig. 3e). When expressed in embryos, this fragment was predominantly nucleoplasmic (Extended Data Fig. 4h), while a major autophagy substrate p62 maintained stable protein levels (Extended Data Fig. 4i). Our analysis revealed that the clearance of lamin B1 was suppressed by sfLB1, whereas lamin A/C and B2 remained largely unperturbed (Fig. 3f,g). An accumulation of lamin B1 was detectable in western blot analysis of lysates (Extended Data Fig. 4j). A sfLB1 variant (sfLB1Δ), which was nucleoplasmic but with abrogated LC3 binding23 (Extended Data Fig. 4h,k), had little influence on lamin B1 clearance (Fig. 3f,g). The development of embryos was impaired by sfLB1, but not by sfLB1Δ (Fig. 3h).

To understand the mechanistic basis of lamin B1 clearance, we constructed fluorescent reporters based on full-length lamin B1 fused to mCherry. Consistent with the results shown in Fig. 3a–c, the wild-type (WT) construct (flLB1wt; Fig. 3e) localized to the nuclear envelope and exhibited rapid lamin B1 clearance (T1/2 = 83 ± 44 min, n = 18) (Fig. 3i and Extended Data Fig. 5a). The other three constructs carried mutations near or at an LC3-interacting region core motif (flLB1(5A) and flLB1(F454A/L457A)) or at the major N-terminal CDK1 phosphorylation site for nuclear-envelope breakdown24 (flLB1(S24A)) (Fig. 3e). These constructs also localized to the two-cell nuclear envelope (Extended Data Fig. 5a). Notably, the mutations that abrogated LC3 interactions (Extended Data Fig. 5b) suppressed the lamin B1 clearance, whereas the mutation at the CDK1 site did not prevent the clearance (Fig. 3i). The mutations in flLB1(5A) were at the site targeted for autophagic degradation of lamin B1 during oncogene-induced cell senescence23. The selectivity of lamin B1 over lamin A/C and B2 was also consistent with the phenomenon reported in senescent cells23.

Our assay further suggested that lamin B1 is degraded through an autophagy–lysosome pathway. This was confirmed by tracking the fate of flLB1wt and identifying its predominant lysosomal localization and formation of foci (n = 18 embryos; Fig. 3j,k). flLB1(5A) and flLB1(F454A/L457A) localized much less to lysosomes (n = 12 and 14 embryos; Fig. 3j,k), while the numbers of lysosomes remained comparable (Fig. 3l). We did not observe prominent lamin-B1-positive structures bulging from the outer nuclear membrane23 nor an accumulation of lysosomes on the nuclear surface25 within the timescale examined. Collectively, our mechanistic view of lamin B1 clearance is shown in Fig. 3m.

We next examined the effect of lamin B1 on two-cell nuclear stiffness. Nanocapillaries indicated that two-cell nuclei of which the envelope was enriched with endogenous lamin B1 were stiffer and less plastic than controls (4.2 ± 1.3 nN µm−1, n = 17 embryos) (sfLB1; Fig. 4a–d). These nuclei were as stiff as normal one-cell and four-cell nuclei (~5 nN µm−1, Fig. 1k). We next overexpressed lamin B1. As WT lamin B1 rapidly dissociated from the nuclear envelope, we used flLB1(5A) and flLB1(F454A/L457A) to compensate for the clearance. Whereas excess loading of these lamins resulted in collapsed nuclei, titrating expression levels preserved the overall nuclear shape, with lamin B1 loaded to the nuclear envelope at a level comparable to that using sfLB1 (Fig. 4a,b). We found that flLB1(F454A/L457A) profoundly increased nuclear elasticity and stiffness (4.0 ± 1.1 nN µm−1, n = 17 embryos) (Fig. 4c,d), while overall autophagic activity as assessed by LC3 signal26 was maintained (Extended Data Fig. 5c). The phenotype quantitatively mirrored the embryos perturbed through sfLB1. By contrast, flLB1(F454A/L457A)ΔC, which lacked the carboxy-terminal CaaX motif for farnesylation16 and remained nucleoplasmic, did not significantly alter nuclear shape and mechanics (Extended Data Fig. 5d). These data indicate that two-cell nuclear stiffness depends on the nuclear envelope localization of lamin B1.

Notably, embryos overexpressing flLB1(5A) showed nuclei of which the shape remained largely deformed as in normal two-cell embryos (Fig. 4a). These nuclei were soft and plastic (1.6 ± 0.4 nN µm−1, n = 10 embryos), despite their comparable level of nuclear envelope loading to sfLB1 and flLB1(F454A/L457A) (Fig. 4b–d). The minimal mechanical impact of flLB1(5A) was probably due to its poor assembly competency at the nuclear envelope for the following reasons: first, our nuclear isolation assay indicated that flLB1(5A) was more soluble than flLB1(F454A/L457A) (Fig. 4e). Second, sfLB1(5A) showed comparably slightly higher affinity for LC3 than sfLB1(F454A/L457A) (Extended Data Fig. 5b). Finally, our expansion microscopy (ExM) analysis revealed a less-uniform peripheral localization of flLB1(5A) in comparison to endogenous lamin B1 and flLB1(F454A/L457A)27 (Extended Data Fig. 6a–g). The difference in peripheral DNA signals appeared less pronounced between perturbed embryo groups (Extended Data Fig. 6h). Our nanocapillary-based assay further suggested an early reduction in nuclear stiffness before lamin B1 clearance (Figs. 1k and 3a,b). Collectively, our data suggest that lamin B1 controls two-cell nuclear stiffness depending on their assembly stability at the nuclear envelope and that the stability depends on the site to which LC3 binds.

We next examined whether the observed nuclear changes impact the two-cell transcriptional reprogramming. We analysed the above embryos using RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq; Extended Data Fig. 7a and Supplementary Table 1). The first cleavage and following cell cycle progression were normal in those embryos; the overexpressed protein levels were stable and comparable among conditions (Extended Data Fig. 5e,f). Our analysis indicated that the transcriptomes of flLB1(F454A/L457A) embryos were distinct from those of the flLB1(5A) and non-injected control embryos (Extended Data Fig. 7b–d). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified more than 3,000 downregulated genes in flLB1(F454A/L457A) embryos compared with the non-injected controls (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Table 2). The downregulation was evident even when compared with flLB1(5A) embryos (Fig. 4g), whereas very few DEGs were detected between flLB1(5A) embryos and the non-injected controls (Fig. 4h). Importantly, the genes downregulated through flLB1(F454A/L457A) overlapped more with major ZGA and two-cell-activated genes than with minor ZGA and one-cell-activated genes28 (972 out of 3,763 (25.8%) versus 251 out of 3,763 (6.7%); P < 0.01, Fisher’s exact test) (Fig. 4i). The overlap was even greater when compared with another major ZGA gene list29 (1,338 out of 2,508, 53.3%) (Fig. 4i,j). flLB1(F454A/L457A)ΔC embryos did not show an appreciably altered transcriptome at comparable expression levels (Extended Data Figs. 5g and 7e). Our analysis also indicated perturbed expression of key transcriptional regulators Obox15 in flLB1(F454A/L457A) embryos but it was not prominent in flLB1(5A) embryos (Fig. 4k). The profiles of maternal and zygotic Obox genes suggested a failure in minor-to-major ZGA transition15.

We next analysed how ZGA is altered when the nuclear envelope was enriched with endogenous lamin B1. Analysing embryos overexpressing sfLB1 or sfLB1Δ, along with the non-injected controls, we found that thousands of genes, including major ZGA genes, were downregulated through sfLB1 (Extended Data Fig. 7f–l and Supplementary Table 3). Many downregulated genes identified as DEGs were shared between sfLB1 and flLB1(F454A/L457A) embryos (71.1%; Fig. 4i (bottom right)). Developmentally essential genes were enriched among the downregulated genes (Extended Data Fig. 7m). Analysing published chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP–seq) datasets further indicated sharp H3K4me3 signals around the transcription start sites of the downregulated genes (Extended Data Fig. 7n). We therefore performed genome-wide profiling of H3K4me3 through an optimized cleavage under targets and tagmentation (Cut&Tag) analysis. By establishing a condition that maintained the nuclear structure, reproducible signals were obtained that agreed with previously published ChIP–seq data14,30 (Extended Data Fig. 8a–e). Our analysis indicated that H3K4me3 is more abundant at transcription start sites in sfLB1 embryos than in the non-injected control embryos (Extended Data Fig. 8f). It has been demonstrated that broadly spread noncanonical H3K4me3 is removed by histone demethylation for major ZGA14. Indeed, H3K4me3 remained accumulated in broad-domain H3K4me3 (bdH3K4me3) in sfLB1 embryos, whereas H3K4me3 enrichment in lamina-associated domains unchanged (Fig. 4l and Extended Data Fig. 8g). Analysing the expression levels of histone methylation-related enzymes, key lysine demethylase (KDM) genes, which are needed to eliminate bdH3K4me314, were downregulated through flLB1(F454A/L457A) and sfLB1, but the changes were not significant through flLB1(5A) and sfLB1Δ (Fig. 4m). These data suggest that the establishment of histone methylation status2 failed in embryos when lamin B1 stiffened the two-cell nuclear envelope.

There was a marked difference in nuclear stiffness between the embryo groups that failed in major ZGA and those that were transcriptionally less perturbed. To test whether nuclear stiffness per se was inhibitory to ZGA, we developed a molecular tether–anchor system that targets the nuclear envelope and stiffens nuclei (Fig. 4n and Extended Data Fig. 9a). The tether comprised an elastic I27 module of titin31 of which the ends were fused to a pair of GFP nanobodies. The anchor was made based on a minimal KASH domain fused to mEGFP for outer-nuclear-envelope localization32. When co-expressed, the tether was recruited to the anchor-coated nuclear envelope and increased nuclear stiffness by around twofold (4.3 ± 1.5 nN µm−1, n = 20 embryos; Fig. 4n,o and Extended Data Fig. 9b). Nuclear plasticity and deformation decreased accordingly (Extended Data Fig. 9c,d). Importantly, in the tether-stiffened nuclei, lamin B1 levels were as low as in normal mid two-cell embryos (Fig. 4p). Lamin A/C and B2 also remained largely unperturbed (Extended Data Fig. 9e). Our mRNA microinjection into late zygotes minimally affected pronuclear stiffness, first cleavage and cell cycle progression (Extended Data Fig. 9f–i). Moreover, our two mutant tether derivatives, one that weakened its affinity to the anchor (tether LaG16(5A)) and one that impaired its elastic force-generating property (tether titin(W34E)), showed no appreciable impact on nuclear shape and mechanics (Fig. 4o and Extended Data Fig. 9b–d,j). In contrast to the WT, neither of the two mutants significantly impaired development (Extended Data Fig. 9k). Thus, our tether–anchor system increases two-cell nuclear stiffness and perturbs embryonic development when it binds to the nuclear envelope and exerts force.

Our RNA-seq analysis revealed that embryos in which the nuclei were stiffened by the tether WT (tWT) exhibited distinct transcriptomes compared with those expressing the LaG16(5A) tether mutant and the non-injected controls (Extended Data Fig. 10a). A total of 634 genes, including major ZGA genes, were downregulated in the tWT embryos, while much fewer DEGs were identified in tether-mutant embryos (Fig. 4q and Extended Data Fig. 10b–e; versus the non-injected controls). Furthermore, the expression of the Obox cluster and other major ZGA genes was dampened (Extended Data Fig. 10f). Downregulation of key ZGA genes and transcriptional repression were detectable in quantitative PCR with reverse transcription (RT–qPCR) and 5-ethynyl-uridine (EU) incorporation analyses (Extended Data Fig. 10g,h). Whereas the impact on ZGA was broader and less variable for flLB1(F454A/L457A) than for tWT (972 versus 168 genes; Fig. 4i versus Extended Data Fig. 10e; Fig. 4j versus Fig. 4q), 82.2% of the downregulated genes in tWT embryos were shared with those perturbed through sfLB1 or flLB1(F454A/L457A) (Fig. 4r). Together, we conclude that deformable two-cell nuclei are integral to the execution of proper ZGA.

We next examined the effect of lamin B1 persistence on early development. As a result, approximately 50% of the embryos perturbed through flLB1(F454A/L457A) did not progress beyond the two-cell stage (Extended Data Fig. 5h), comparable to those perturbed through sfLB1 (Fig. 3h). flLB1(F454A/L457A)ΔC did not significantly affect development, indicating that the effective site of lamin B1 is at the nuclear envelope. Notably, embryos overexpressing flLB1(5A) also showed impaired development, despite their nearly normal transcriptome (Fig. 4h and Extended Data Fig. 5h). Our analysis indicated that those embryos accumulated DNA damage as detected through phosphorylated H2A.X (γH2A.X) foci (Extended Data Fig. 5i), a developmental impediment33. By contrast, DNA damage levels in embryos perturbed through flLB1(F454A/L457A) were as low as those of non-injection control. Our results do not exclude the possibility that the suppressed ZGA and basal DNA damage combinatorially impaired development. Nonetheless, our data indicate that embryos with stiff two-cell nuclei are unable to facilitate proper ZGA and early development. Embryos with tether-stiffened nuclei also exhibited a two-cell arrest (Extended Data Fig. 9k), supporting the necessity of nuclear softness for development.

Given the impact of nuclear stiffness on ZGA, we examined chromatin dynamics in deformable and non-deformable nuclei. Our ExM images at 24 h.p.i. showed peripheral layers of DNA and lamins, with the overlap comparable to those analysed using electron microscopy34 (~150 nm; Extended Data Fig. 6i). Our imaging further revealed a preferential localization of DNA near concave regions of the two-cell nuclear envelope (Fig. 5a–c and Extended Data Fig. 6j). Lamin B1 signals also exhibited a weak negative correlation with nuclear deformation35 (Fig. 5b,c). However, the biased DNA localization was shared among deformable nuclei regardless of lamin B1 enrichment (Extended Data Fig. 6k–m), suggesting that nuclear deformation is associated with peripheral DNA organization.

We next examined how deformable nuclei are linked to gene expression regulation during ZGA. In 3D images, large fluorescent foci positive for elongating RNA polymerase II (Pol II Ser2P) shifted their distribution towards the nuclear periphery, as nuclear deformation increased from 21 to 24 h.p.i. (Fig. 5d,e). Notably, the majority of the Pol II foci became more frequently associated with nuclear concavity (Fig. 5f). The focus sizes increased over time, and their growth was suppressed by tether-based nuclear stiffening (Fig. 5g). Concomitantly, DNA mass volume decreased by 14.7 ± 6.5% (Fig. 5h). We next performed sequential 3D fluorescence in situ hybridization (3D FISH) analysis36 to examine the link between peripheral transcriptional activity and ZGA (Fig. 5i–l). We analysed the genomic loci of Kdm5b and Obox6, along with Gabarapl1 as a non-ZGA control and found that their distributions were broad and changed from the early to the mid-to-late two-cell stage, but the average distance remained 2–3 µm from the nuclear periphery around the time of major ZGA (2.7 ± 2.0, 2.0 ± 1.6 and 2.1 ± 1.3 µm, respectively; 28 h.p.i.) (Fig. 5i–k). Pol II Ser2P intensities were greater when the Obox6 and Kdm5b loci were positioned >1 µm from the nuclear periphery, while DAPI signals remained unchanged, suggesting that the increased local transcriptional activity was not merely a consequence of higher chromatin density (Extended Data Fig. 10i,j). This trend was not observed in Gabarapl1 (Extended Data Fig. 10i). Our sequential FISH further enabled us to determine the locations of multiple gene loci within the same nucleus (Fig. 5l). Our data indicated a widespread positioning of Obox6 and Kdm5b loci at 21 h.p.i. and a change in the localization of gene loci at 28 h.p.i., with a frequent incidence of their concomitant presence near the nuclear periphery. This locus distribution was disrupted by tether-based nuclear stiffening (Fig. 5l). Notably, Pol II Ser2P intensities at Obox6 loci depended on the nuclear stiffening, while their changes at Kdm5b loci appeared less pronounced (Extended Data Fig. 10k). We therefore performed sequential 3D DNA–RNA FISH analysis to detect nascent Kdm5b transcripts and found a surge in the production around 2–4 µm from the nuclear envelope, and it depended on peripheral nuclear stiffness (Extended Data Fig. 10l–n). In summary, our data suggest that soft and plastic two-cell nuclei ensure the formation of active transcription sites a few μm beneath the nuclear envelope and enable the coordinated positioning of key ZGA gene loci around the sites for expression.

Our study reveals physical characteristics of early embryonic nuclei and their distinct changes that facilitate ZGA. Our data show that the autophagy factor LC3 and lamin B1 clearance confer nuclear plasticity and softness, and that deformable nuclei are key to robust zygotic transcription. Extensive studies have profiled epigenetic states of chromatin and provided a genome-centred view of this critical developmental transition. Our study highlights the importance of physical nuclear state for this transition and underscores the need for integration of diverse embryonic parameters, including their subcellular material properties.

Our work identifies a transient phase of nuclear deformability that manifests as cells transition through a totipotent state (Fig. 1). Notably, this nuclear change marks normal preimplantation development and contributes to transcription and gene locus movement (Figs. 2, 4 and 5). Notably, the sites of active DNA transactions appear a few μm beneath the nuclear envelope, often associated with local negative curvature, suggesting the generation of inward forces around these sites (Fig. 5d–h). During ZGA, chromatin undergoes dynamic remodelling and compaction37,38,39, while maintaining contact with the nuclear lamina40. A flexible nuclear envelope may accommodate these internal and peripheral chromatin activities, mediating a mechanical conflict for the consolidation of functional genome architectures. Our analysis also suggests that intense transcriptional activity near the deformable nuclear envelope enables the full activation of key ZGA genes that shape the two-cell epigenome14 (Fig. 4l,m). Such mechanical control may also operate in naive embryonic stem cells, in which soft nuclei are linked to pluripotency and priming for differentiation41.

We find that the embryonic mechanism governing lamin B1 clearance shares similarities with nuclear autophagy activated during oncogene-induced cell senescence23 (Fig. 3). As autophagy is accompanied by an increase in intracellular calcium levels and is independent of mTORC1 in early embryos21,42, the clearance is most likely developmentally programmed. Our data suggest a role of nuclear translocation of LC3 and its distinct effect on nuclear shape and mechanics. Further analysis of its regulated nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttling43 should reveal the mechanistic basis behind the physiological and pathological nuclear remodelling.

Our findings also raise several interesting questions for future studies. First, our imaging assay detected diluted lamin B1 at convex regions of the nuclear envelope35 (Fig. 5b,c). It remains to be explored whether the clearance of lamin B1 merely contributes to nuclear softening or controls DNA organization by sensing local nuclear curvature. Second, as lamins influence transcription44,45 and the chromatin–lamina contacts change during development40, understanding the interplay between mechanical cues and biochemical factors that ensures robust transcriptional control will be an essential next step. Third, soft and transcriptionally active nuclei, yet enriched with lamin B1 at the nuclear envelope, were prone to DNA damage (Extended Data Fig. 5i). As excess lamin B1 can cause persistent DNA damage46, autophagic clearance of lamin B1 may be necessary to protect against such damage. It would be interesting to dissect nuclear actin in two-cell nuclei47, as per its involvement in DNA damage repair48.

Physical nuclear stability is likely important for early embryos, given the potential mechanical challenges they face during passage through the uterine tube49. Yet this period coincides with a transient state in which nuclei become less mechanically resilient. Early mammalian embryos are also prone to DNA lesions and chromosome missegregation50,51,52. Understanding how embryos navigate the vulnerable state may reveal principles underlying developmental robustness.

The mechanical properties of nuclei at each cleavage stage in living embryos were measured using single glass nanocapillaries, which were held with a three-axis, low-drift hydraulic micromanipulator (MHW-3, Narishige) attached to the microscope’s body. The nanocapillaries were prepared by fabricating borosilicate glass tubes (B100-75-10, Sutter Instruments) using a capillary puller (PD-10, Narishige) such that the tip formed a nearly uniform taper with an inner diameter of around 1 μm (1.06 ± 0.02 µm, n = 5). To begin with, embryos injected with mRNAs (20 ng µl−1 of H2B-mCherry or 300 ng µl−1 of emerin-eGFP) were transferred to drops of M2 medium, which was placed in a glass-bottom, open-top culture chamber covered with paraffin oil (26137-85, Nacalai Tesque). The chamber was then placed onto the microscope’s sample stage and a single embryo was captured by using a large holding pipette (inner diameter, ~10 µm), which approached from one side of the embryo. A nanocapillary was then inserted from the opposite side of the embryo and its tip was contacted to the nuclear surface; the nucleus was then captured at its surface by applying a weak suction pressure to the nanocapillary. The level of the suction pressure was controlled using a syringe pump (PHD ULTRA, Harvard apparatus) and a digital manometer (PPA101-M, SMC), which were connected to the distal end of the nanocapillary through a vacuum tube. The pressure level (1.2–2.1 × 101 kPa) was such that the nuclear surface could sustain its attachment to the nanocapillary tip but could not be aspirated. Finally, the nucleus was stretched by moving the nanocapillary position away from the embryo’s centre at a velocity with which the viscous resistance from the cytoplasm could be low. At a typical movement speed of 0.25 µm s−1, the viscous resistance on the nucleus as estimated according to the Stokes–Einstein equation62 was 0.05–0.16 nN (see the ‘Image analyses’ section). This value was over 100 times smaller than the force generated at the nanocapillary tip (~17 nN), as estimated from the product of the applied pressure and the nanocapillary’s aperture size (0.8 ± 0.1 µm2; nanocapillary aperture size = π(d/2)2, where d is the nanocapillary’s inner diameter; n = 5). Internal nuclear pressure was disregarded as different nanocapillary pressure levels yielded similar results. Associated with the nanocapillary’s movement, the nucleus gradually deformed and then detached from the nanocapillary before reaching the embryo’s edge. The extent of the maximal extension that nuclei had exhibited was used to determine effective nuclear stiffness. The measurement was performed for nuclei at the one-cell, two-cell and four-cell stages using approximately the same micromanipulation parameters (that is, pressure level, nanocapillary size and nanocapillary movement speed). Embryos of which nuclei were properly captured, as assessed by (1) a sealed contact surface with no aspiration of nuclear material and (2) translocation of the nuclear surface in response to nanocapillary movement, were subjected to subsequent analyses. Individual nuclei were not subjected to repeated measurements. Where feasible, the order of the measurement for control and treatment groups was randomized. The measurements were highly consistent across experiments (mean two-cell nuclear stiffness of non-injected controls: 2.07 ± 0.20 nN µm−1, mean ± s.d., across four independent experiments). These control data were pooled across experiments and used as a reference, mitigating the limitation in the number of samples that could be analysed within the narrow time window per experiment (1 − 2 h for each developmental stage). The label-free measurement (Extended Data Fig. 2h) was performed using differential interference contrast (DIC) microscopy. For the analysis of nuclear stiffness and embryonic development (Fig. 2 and Extended Data Fig. 3), the above-described micromanipulation procedure was used but with a pressure level of 1.2 × 101 kPa.

The previously published Cut&Tag protocol76 was optimized for two-cell embryos. We especially searched for a condition in which the embryonic nuclear structure was maintained even after the initial permeabilization step in buffer containing Triton X-100 and found that the brief Triton X-100 treatment (30 s) enabled the retention of the two-cell nuclear structure as judged by DAPI staining while ensuring permeability for antibodies to gain access to nuclear antigens as summarized in Extended Data Fig. 8a. After three times washes in an extraction buffer without Triton X-100, the embryos were lightly fixed with 0.1% formaldehyde in PBS for 2 min. After the concentration check by BioAnalyzer (High sensitivity kit, Agilent Technologies), the library was subjected to sequencing on the DNBSEQ-G400 platform (paired end 150 bp, MGI).

Oligonucleotide pools were obtained from Twist Bioscience as oligo arrays and amplified essentially as described previously36. After purification (DNA Clean-and-Concentrator, Zymo Research), a second round of PCR was performed with seqDNA1F and seqDNA2R (5′-GAGCTAATACGACTCACTATAGCTCGACCAAGGCTGGCACAA-3′). For intron RNA sequential FISH (seqFISH), we selected Kdm5b and Gabarapl1 as target genes. Intronic target regions were defined by retrieving gene and exon annotations for each gene and computationally subtracting the merged exon intervals from the annotated gene body. Intronic segments shorter than 150 bp were excluded from further analysis. Candidate intronic probe sets were then designed using PaintSHOP. Each primary probe comprised a 30–37-nucleotide genomic targeting sequence and four identical 15-nucleotide secondary-probe docking sites. Two distinct 15-nucleotide barcodes were used, corresponding to the multi-cycle imaging scheme for Kdm5b and Gabarapl1. Twenty-four primary probes were designed per gene, and a list of all of the sequences is provided in Supplementary Table 5. Probe pools were synthesized as oPools by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT), dissolved in RNase-free water and stored as concentrated stocks until use. Immediately before use, the probe pool was diluted to 1 nM per probe in hybridization solution containing 50% (v/v) formamide, 10% (w/v) dextran sulfate, 2× SSC and RNase-free water.

The preparation of oligonucleotide DNA-conjugated primary antibodies was conducted as previously described36. In brief, a mouse monoclonal anti-Pol II Ser2ph antibody (Pc26; AB_2819246) (100 μg) underwent buffer exchange to PBS through 50-kDa Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filters (Millipore, UFC505096) and was treated with 10 equivalents of PEGylated SMCC cross-linker [SM(PEG)2] (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 22102) diluted in anhydrous N,N-dimethylformamide (Fijifilm, 043-32361). After incubation at 4 °C for 2 h, the solution was purified using 7 kDa molecular weight cut-off Zeba Spin desalting columns. Concurrently, 300 μM 5′-thiol-modified 18-nucleotide DNA oligonucleotides—composed of an AAA base sequence and a 15-base secondary probe binding sequence (Eurofins) ([ThiolC6]AAACGCTCTACGTTAACA)—was reduced with 50 mM dithiothreitol in PBS at room temperature for 2 h and purified using NAP5 columns (GE Healthcare, 17-0853-01). Maleimide-activated antibodies were then combined with 11 equivalents of the reduced form of the thiol-modified DNA oligonucleotides in PBS and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The resulting DNA–primary antibody conjugates were washed four times with PBS and concentrated using 50 kDa Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filters. The concentrations of the conjugated oligonucleotide DNA and antibodies were quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23225) and NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Functional validation of the conjugated antibodies was confirmed through SDS–PAGE and Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining, which demonstrated the expected reduction in electrophoretic mobility due to oligonucleotide conjugation. Additional verification was conducted through IF, using fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies that recognize the host species of each antibody; successful nuclear staining affirmed the functionality of the oligonucleotide-labelled antibodies.

Fixed embryos were rinsed in PBS and stored in PBS containing 1% polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, Mr ≈ 360,000) until use. Embryos were subsequently immobilized for the forthcoming hybridization and wash cycles by embedding them in a thin polyacrylamide hydrogel. For covalent anchoring of the gel, 24 mm × 50 mm no. 1 S glass coverslips (thickness, 0.16–0.19 mm; Matsunami) were first silanized with methacrylate groups: the coverslips were immersed for 5 min in a freshly prepared solution containing 0.1% (v/v) 3-(trimethoxysilyl)propyl methacrylate in 80% ethanol and 2% acetic acid, rinsed three times with absolute ethanol and air-dried in a low-humidity, dark environment. A 4% polyacrylamide solution (19:1 acrylamide:bis-acrylamide) was prepared in 1× PBS with 0.1% Tween-20; polymerization was initiated by adding 0.1% ammonium persulfate (APS) and 0.1% N,N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED). To embed the samples, individual fixed embryos were transferred to the methacrylate-coated slide in a small drop of PBS and allowed to settle onto the glass surface. Excess liquid was gently removed to avoid dilution of the monomer solution. Approximately 4 µl of the polyacrylamide solution was pipetted over the embryo, after which a non-stick Gel Slick-coated glass chip (5 mm × 7 mm, t = 1.0 mm; Matsunami Glass) was gently placed on top. Two stacks of Scotch Mending Tape (0.058 mm thick; 3 M) were affixed as spacers at opposite edges of the sample area, so that the chip rested on the tape risers and compressed the gel to a uniform thickness of around 100 µm across the embedded embryo. Gel polymerization was carried out in a humidified chamber at 37 °C for 2 h. To improve the polymerization efficiency, the chamber was purged with nitrogen gas for about 2 min to remove oxygen before sealing and incubation. After polymerization, the top coverslip was carefully removed, leaving the embryo embedded in a thin polyacrylamide pad affixed to the slide. A Fluorescent Friendly double-sided adhesive film (FL-S-2L; Grace Bio-Labs) was cut into a rectangular frame (24 mm × 50 mm) with a central elliptical aperture (major axis ≈ 22 mm, minor axis ≈ 19 mm; projected area ≈ 328 mm2; film thickness, 0.28 mm). One adhesive face was laminated onto the sample coverslip so that the hydrogel-embedded embryo lay precisely at the centre of the oval window. The opposite adhesive face was then sealed with a custom cover-glass (26 mm × 76 mm × 2 mm; Matsunami) bearing two circular access ports (diameter, 1.3 mm; centre-to-centre spacing, 18 mm; positioned at mid-height). This arrangement created a leak-tight chamber of around 0.28 mm internal height (approximately 92 µl volume), while the paired ports served as inlet and outlet for buffer exchange during sequential hybridization and imaging cycles.

The gel-embedded embryos were permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Blocking was performed by incubating the samples in PBS containing 10 mg ml−1 UltraPure BSA (Invitrogen, AM2616), 0.3% Triton X-100, 0.1% dextran sulfate and 0.5 mg ml−1 sheared salmon sperm DNA (Invitrogen, AM9680) for 15 min at room temperature. A DNA oligonucleotide–conjugated Pol II Ser2ph antibody (Pc26, AB_2819246) was diluted in blocking buffer supplemented with SUPERase·In RNase inhibitor (Invitrogen, AM2694; 1:100) and applied to the embryos at a final concentration of 0.2 µg per 100 µl. Embryos were incubated with the antibody at 4 °C for 48 h, washed three times with PBS and then post-fixed in freshly prepared 4% (w/v) formaldehyde in PBS for 5 min at room temperature to stabilize the antibody–antigen complexes. Cross-linking was further reinforced by treating the samples with 1.5 mM BS(PEG)5 in PBS for 20 min at room temperature. After cross-linking, any remaining reactive groups were quenched by incubating the samples in 100 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5) for 5 min. The embryos were then washed again in PBS.

The embedded embryos were incubated in a mixture of RNase A and RNase T1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, EN0551, 1:100 in PBS) at 37 °C for 1 h, then rinsed thoroughly in PBS. Genomic DNA was denatured in situ to allow probe binding: embryos were incubated in denaturation buffer (50% formamide and 2× SSC) at room temperature for 15 min. The chamber ports were sealed with Adhesive Seal Tabs (Grace Bio-Labs), after which the slide was rapidly heated on a 90 °C dry block for 4–5 min to denature the genomic DNA. Immediately after this heat-denaturation step, the samples were transferred into 4× SSC to avoid re-annealing of DNA and to wash away formamide. Primary probes were hybridized simultaneously to the denatured DNA. Hybridization was carried out in a humid chamber at 37 °C for 120 h. The hybridization buffer contained 35% formamide, 2× SSC and 10% dextran sulfate (w/v) to promote DNA annealing; probes were added at a final concentration of approximately 0.2 µM for each target. After the approximately 5 day incubation, the samples were washed to remove excess and non-specifically bound probes. After hybridization, the samples were rinsed for 15 min at room temperature in a 40% wash solution comprising 40% (v/v) formamide, 2× SSC and 0.1% Triton X-100, and then washed three additional times in 4× SSC. To covalently stabilize the bound probes, chemical amine-modification was performed. The samples were pre-equilibrated in 1× labelling buffer A (Mirus Bio) and then incubated for 45 min at room temperature in labelling buffer A containing a 1:10 dilution of Label IT amine modifying reagent (MIR 3900, Mirus Bio). Excess reagent was removed by three washes in 1× PBS. The embryos were post-fixed in 1.5 mM BS(PEG)5 and incubated for 30 min at room temperature, followed by quenching in 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) for 5 min. The samples were then washed for 5 min in 55% wash buffer (55% (v/v) formamide, 2× SSC, 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100) and rinsed three times in 4× SSC to remove residual formamide. For image processing and quantitative analysis, raw Nikon ND2 files from each hybridization round were opened in Fiji with the Bio-Formats importer and automatically concatenated into a single five-dimensional (x, y, z, channel, time) hyper-stack. Each fluorescence channel was denoised with a three-dimensional median filter (1-pixel kernel). The DAPI channel was smoothed with a 3D mean filter (two-pixel kernel) and corrected for photobleaching using the bleach correction–simple ratio method. Sample drift within each stack was removed with the Correct 3D Drift plugin, using the DAPI signal as reference; the sequential imaging rounds were then co-registered by the Descriptor-based series registration plugin with an affine 3D +t model. Out-of-focus slices were discarded. Nuclear regions were identified in the corrected DAPI volume by Gaussian blurring (σ = 30 pixels), global thresholding, morphological closing and connected-component labelling with the 3D Objects Counter, generating bounding boxes for every nucleus. These bounding boxes were converted to regions of interest (ROIs); all voxels within each ROI were duplicated into individual multipage TIFF files, and integrated as well as mean fluorescence intensities were measured within a fixed 118 × 118 pixel window centred on the ROI. Finally, maximum-intensity projections, annotated overlays and the registered 3D image stacks were exported.

seqFISH images of embryos were analysed using Imaris v.10.2 software (Oxford Instruments). For analysing distances from the nuclear periphery to gene loci, a nuclear surface was created based on the DAPI intensity of each nucleus. Spot objects were generated based on signal intensities of Obox6, Kdm5b and Gabarapl1 loci. Then, to measure the distance from each spot object to the surface, ‘Shortest Distance to Surfaces’ was selected as a parameter in the ‘Spot’ module. The mean intensity of DAPI and Pol II Ser2ph in each genomic locus was quantified using ‘Intensity mean’ in the ‘Spot’ module. The clustering analysis of distances from the nuclear periphery to gene loci was performed with a custom Python code (Python v.3.12.4) using the DBSCAN function (Minimum Points: 3, radius: 0.648). The radius was determined by the k-nearest neighbours algorithm using a custom Python code. To quantify RNA FISH signals, we first determined RNA FISH foci with intensities above the threshold (Min: 130, Max: 165) throughout all samples. The identified foci were automatically recognized as spots in Imaris, and the spot size was then adjusted to fit individual foci. RNA FISH foci were therefore labelled as individual spots, and the total signal intensities within each spot (Intensity sum) were measured.
