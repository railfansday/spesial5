FIRST ON FOX: Supreme Court justices confront what it will take to keep America's founding freedoms alive

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Justices Brett Kavanaugh, Neil Gorsuch and Amy Coney Barrett reflect on America's founding principles and urge Americans to embrace civic education and engagement to preserve the nation's freedoms for future generations. (Federalist Society)

FIRST ON FOX: As America celebrates 250 years of independence this year, three conservative Supreme Court justices are warning that preserving the nation's founding freedoms for future generations depends on Americans understanding their history, embracing civic education and actively participating in their system of government.

Justices Brett Kavanaugh, Neil Gorsuch and Amy Coney Barrett each implored Americans to familiarize themselves with their system of government and the history behind it to ensure that the nation’s legacy is preserved.

Though the three conservative justices were united in their call for civic education, they each approached the topic differently in a series of interviews filmed by the Federalist Society and obtained by Fox News Digital. The videos come ahead of the Federalist Society's 2026 National Lawyers Convention this week, which spotlights "250 Years of American Exceptionalism."

Gorsuch, for instance, struck a distinctly religious tone.

"I think it's very easy to take the rights granted in our Constitution and the ideals expressed in the Declaration for granted," he said. "They are, for us, a little bit like the air we breathe and the water a fish might swim in. They seem natural to us, but they weren't in 1776. The ideas in the Declaration of Independence that all of us are created equal, that every one of us has God-given rights — they don't come from government, they come from God."

Gorsuch, who was appointed to the court by President Donald Trump in 2017 following the death of Justice Antonin Scalia, went on to describe the importance of historical study to the preservation of the American way of life.

"Our country is a blessing from God, and that we owe great thanks for it, and we have great responsibility for it," he went on. "The reason why our Constitution and Declaration have survived is because people choose it anew in every generation. You need to know its history. You need to know what you're preserving. Daniel Webster said it took 6,000 years for us to generate a system of government like this. Don't expect that to happen easily or again."

Kavanaugh, similarly, emphasized the importance of education.

"I think our education system is absolutely fundamental to ensuring that in the next 250 years, we preserve this republic and improve upon this republic. So, teaching American history, teaching the values of this country, the history of this country, why this country exists, how this country has been preserved, how this country has been improved over time, for example, by our constitutional amendment process and otherwise," the justice, appointed to the bench following a tumultuous confirmation process in 2018, said.

"And I think all of us should be constantly striving to ensure civics education, not only for our youth, but for all of us, and that we constantly are learning and participating in our system of government," he continued.

The Federalist Society filmed its interviews ahead of its National Lawyers Convention, which will bring together minds from across the conservative legal movement to discuss matters deemed pressing.

While not interviewed by the Federalist Society, Justice Sonia Sotomayor shared her thoughts on America's 250th anniversary during an event at Mount Vernon's Washington Library, taking a slightly darker tone than her conservative colleagues.

During her talk, which centered primarily on civic engagement, Sotomayor argued that the United States is in a rough place at the moment and that it would take "some hard work" for it to get back on an "even keel."

"There are some norms we may have lost forever," she said. "But I don’t think that we will fail as a democracy. But I think we’re going to have a hard patch figuring out what that means."

Barrett, meanwhile, focused on the Declaration of Independence and how she believes it should inspire civic participation today.

"We're not passive observers in the American project. We're participants in it. It is our project," she said. "I might suggest that people look at the last line of the Declaration of Independence. It ends with a mutual pledge in which the signatories to the document mutually pledge their lives, their fortunes and their sacred honor to the success of the American project. And they made good on that promise. Some lost their fortune, lost their property to loyalists ... and they pledged with their honor not to deviate from the commitments that they had made in signing the Declaration of Independence."

"Surely that's the kind of commitment that it takes to have it keep going forward," said Barrett.
