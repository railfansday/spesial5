The most important idiom in C++

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

If you don't know the RAII idiom, you don't know modern C++

RAII (Resource Acquisition Is Initialization) is the idiom that defines modern C++. If you want to move from legacy code to modern C++, this is the place to start.

RAII is C++'s fundamental idiom for deterministic resource management. It ties the lifetime of a resource — memory, file handles, mutexes, sockets — directly to the lifetime of a stack-allocated object. When that object goes out of scope, its destructor automatically releases the resource, guaranteeing cleanup even when exceptions are thrown.

RAII is not a garbage collector. It relies on deterministic scope exit. It cannot manage resources that are not acquired at initialization or whose lifetime does not align with a lexical scope. Examples include CPU time, network bandwidth, or resources that must outlive the function's scope but have no clear owner.

RAII is not just "use a destructor to clean up." It is a contract: the object's lifetime is the resource's lifetime, and the object is valid from the moment it exists. The release half gets all the attention because it's the half that prevents leaks. But the acquisition half is what makes the contract airtight — it's what guarantees there is never an object that exists but doesn't work.

Legacy C++ is full of objects that need an init(), a start(), an open(), or a connect() before they're usable, and full of bugs where someone forgot to call it or forgot to check the result. Modern C++ says: acquire in the constructor, throw on failure, and let the destructor handle the rest. The compiler does the bookkeeping. You do the interesting part.

That's the idiom. That's why it defines modern C++. And that's why, if you don't know RAII — both halves of it — you don't know modern C++.
