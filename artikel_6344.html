Hot days with weak winds could become a major problem for the power grid

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Climate models project that extreme heat and weak winds will coincide more often, straining power grids as cooling demand rises and wind power drops.

A heat wave feels like a problem of too much: too much sun, too much demand for air conditioning.

For a grid that leans on wind turbines, the problem is also too little.

When extreme heat coincides with unusually weak winds, wind farms can generate less electricity just as demand for air conditioning rises.

A new analysis of 27 climate models projects that these spells of extreme heat and weak winds will become two to eight and a half times more frequent over the world’s land by 2100.

How much more depends on how much carbon dioxide is emitted.

The models also project that the longest such spell each year could last roughly two to four times as long.

Lu Shen and colleagues at Peking University in Beijing led the work, with Meng Qu as first author.

The results are model projections, not measurements, and the range reflects both the emissions path and real disagreement among the models.

The team defined a bad day by two thresholds. The wind at 33 feet (10 meters) had to fall into the slowest 5% of warm-season days while the temperature rose into the hottest 5%.

If heat and calm were unrelated, such days would be rare by chance alone. They aren’t unrelated.

Across the middle and high latitudes, hotter days tend to be calmer.

In parts of the northern high latitudes, Southeast Asia and northern Africa, the two coincided five to eight times more often than chance would predict in a NASA weather record.

From 1980 to 2014, the average patch of land saw about 0.6 such days a year. Russia, the southeastern coasts of the United States and China, and parts of the tropics saw 1.5 to 3 days.

Along the storm tracks between 35 and 65 degrees north, the longest spells ran more than five days.

A spell that long means most of a week of weak wind in the middle of a heat wave, in some of the most densely populated regions on Earth.

Europe’s summer of 2022 is the authors’ example. Persistent heat and weak winds coincided with surging electricity demand and rising prices.

The projections come from a NASA dataset that sharpens 27 global climate models to a grid of about 15 miles (25 kilometers).

Each model was scored against its own 1980 to 2014 climate, so the thresholds for “hot” and “calm” shift with the model rather than with the real world.

The models project more days of extreme heat and unusually weak winds by 2100, with the largest increase under the highest-emissions pathway.

Under the lowest pathway, the rise levels off after mid-century.

The tropics change fastest. Between 30 degrees south and 30 degrees north, the frequency rises by 188% under the lowest pathway and by 934% under the highest. The northern mid-latitudes rise by 106% and 689%.

The spells get longer as well as more common. By 2100, the models project that the longest such spell each year could last 84% to 315% longer worldwide, depending on the emissions pathway.

The team split the increase into four causes: hotter average temperatures, slower average winds, bigger swings in both, and a tighter link between heat and calm. Warming alone accounted for most of the rise in every latitude band.

Winds change differently by region. Between 30 and 60 degrees north, average winds slow by up to 0.07 miles per hour (0.03 meters per second) a decade.

The authors tie that stilling to the Arctic warming faster than the tropics, which flattens the temperature difference that drives wind.

In the tropics, winds pick up slightly, which offsets part of the warming effect.

The jet stream matters too. The models shift the northern jet about 1.6 degrees poleward by 2100. North of roughly 50 degrees, that shift strengthens the pairing of hot days with calm ones, and south of it the pairing weakens.

Twenty-seven models disagree, sometimes widely. To shrink the spread, the team checked which models best reproduced the observed trend in these compound events from 1980 to 2014.

Eight reanalysis datasets supplied the observed trend, and the projections were weighted accordingly.

The correction trimmed the projected global increase by 10% to 25% and cut the disagreement among models by 13% to 24%. The models that track history best now carry more weight, and the forecast tightens.

At the regional level it cut disagreement by 10% to 40%, raising projections for South Asia and Eastern Europe and lowering them for Southeast Asia and New Zealand.

The analysis uses daily averages, and the authors note that averaging smooths away the calmest hours, so the study likely undercounts the most extreme lulls.

The models also underestimate how tightly heat and calm are coupled in the northern high latitudes, which is where the events are most pronounced.

The stakes the authors describe are already visible. In the eastern United States, the PJM grid hit a peak load of 162.7 gigawatts during the July 2026 heat wave, close to its summer record.

India set a demand record of 270.73 gigawatts in May 2026, with outages in places. Europe’s June 2026 heat wave was linked to more than 10,000 excess deaths.

The authors flag the hours after sunset. Solar output disappears while demand stays high, and nights are expected to warm faster than days. European minimums already exceed 68°F (20°C) on so-called tropical nights.

The authors’ answer is long-duration storage to carry daytime solar power into those evenings, more mixing of renewable sources, and above all a lower emissions path.

In their projections, the odds of a spell of extreme heat and weak winds by 2100 are four to eight times higher under the higher-emissions pathways than under the lowest one.

What the models can’t yet say is how often the calmest hours, rather than the calmest days, will coincide with the hottest ones.

Answering that would take hourly projections the downscaled dataset doesn’t provide.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
