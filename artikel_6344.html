Physicists have found half the answer to why some materials carry electricity with no loss

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

In one copper oxide, flickering electron spins drive high-temperature superconductors, and coupling strength sets where resistance ends.

Every wire wastes a little of the electricity that runs through it, which is why a phone charger gets warm. Cool certain materials far enough and that waste disappears.

A family of copper-oxide ceramics become superconductors at temperatures well above the rest, and physicists have argued for decades about why.

For one branch of that family, a team has now matched the strength of one interaction to the temperature at which the loss vanishes.

Electrons in these materials scatter off rapid flickers in the magnetism of the surrounding atoms. The stronger that scattering, the higher the temperature at which the material stops wasting energy.

So, the authors concluded, those magnetic flickers are what pair the electrons up.

That covers only half of the superconductor family, and the half with the highest temperatures behaves differently. So the answer isn’t complete, and the authors said so.

Nigel Hussey, a professor of physics at the University of Bristol who also holds a post at Radboud University in the Netherlands, co-led the work. Caitlin Duffy, now a postdoctoral researcher at the French research agency CNRS, ran the experiments.

“Through measurements carried out at the European Magnetic Field Laboratory in the Netherlands and subsequent careful analysis, we have found the smoking gun which solves part of the equation and explains what causes this perplexing state,” Hussey said.

In a normal metal, electrons bump into vibrating atoms as they travel, and each collision wastes a little energy as heat. In the oldest known superconductors, those same vibrations do something else at very low temperatures.

They nudge electrons into pairs, and paired electrons flow without resistance.

That theory has a useful feature. The strength of the pull between vibrations and electrons shows up in a metal’s resistance above the transition, and the same strength sets the transition temperature. Measure one and you can predict the other.

The copper-oxide materials become superconductors at much higher temperatures, too high for vibrating atoms to explain. Physicists call that temperature Tc.

The leading candidate for a replacement glue has long been the electrons’ own spins, which act like tiny magnets and flicker in step with their neighbors.

What was missing was a measurement tying the strength of that flickering to Tc, the way resistance ties vibrations to it in ordinary metals.

Collaborators at the Chinese Academy of Sciences in Beijing grew a thin film of lanthanum cerium copper oxide, one atomic layer at a time.

Along the film, the cerium content changed smoothly from one end to the other. Cerium adds electrons to the material, and the amount sets Tc.

At one end, the film became superconducting at about 25 kelvin, or roughly -415°F (-248°C), which is close to the best this material can do.

At the other end it never did. Between those ends sat 19 distinct compositions, measured across two films grown the same way, so the amount of disorder stayed the same from end to end.

The team patterned electrical contacts along the film and measured its resistance at each position while varying the temperature and the magnetic field.

The field came from a 35-tesla magnet at the High Field Magnet Laboratory in Nijmegen. This is far stronger than the magnet inside a hospital MRI scanner.

A magnetic field bends the paths of electrons. How much the resistance rises in a field depends on how often the electrons scatter and from where.

In these films, the pattern pointed to scattering concentrated at particular spots, and those spots matched the ones where electrons meet the flickering spins of neighboring atoms.

The team then fit the resistance and the field response at all 13 measured positions on one film. Only one number was allowed to change from position to position – the strength of that scattering.

Across the film, as Tc fell from 25 kelvin to zero, that number fell tenfold. Duffy said the field measurements were what made the identification possible.

“Employing some analytical tricks on the data obtained in high magnetic fields, we were then able to identify the dominant source of resistance in this material as scattering off the fluctuating spins,” said Duffy.

“The correlation between the strength of this interaction and Tc then confirmed that the origin for superconductivity in this material is indeed the interaction between the conduction electrons and spin fluctuations,” she added.

One consequence cuts against an earlier idea. Some physicists had argued that the resistance in these superconductors sits at a universal quantum limit.

The scattering strength here varied by a factor of ten across the film, so in this branch of the family, at least, it does not.

Copper-oxide superconductors come in two kinds. Adding electrons, as cerium does here, makes one kind. Removing electrons, which physicists call adding holes, makes the other. The hole-doped materials reach the highest transition temperatures of all.

A tempting guess is that hole-doped materials simply couple more strongly to the same magnetic flickers. However, the team’s earlier high-field measurements on those materials say otherwise.

In the state physicists call a strange metal, their resistance responds to a magnetic field in a way the same model can’t reproduce. No standard picture of electrons as individual particles has yet accounted for it.

“Although electron- and hole-doped cuprates are made from the same cloth, they are stitched together in different patterns,” Hussey explained.

The result rests on one material, measured on two films, and on a model in which electrons still behave as particles with definite paths. The model’s success here is itself evidence, the authors wrote, because the same approach fails on the hole-doped side.

“While we have found the first key ingredient, the task now is to go and find the second,” Hussey said.

What the second ingredient is, nobody has measured. Finding it, the authors wrote, may take an approach that goes beyond the standard theory of superconductivity, or beyond treating electrons as individual particles at all.

The hole-doped materials are where that search has to happen.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Physics.
