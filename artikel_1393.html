Glioblastoma is incurable and resistant to therapy despite genomic stability under treatment1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8, suggesting that non-genetic cellular plasticity is a central driver of progression. Whether such plasticity reflects stochastic state switching or is governed by predictable gene regulation has remained unclear9,10,11,12,13. Here we introduce scDORI (single-cell deep multi-omic regulatory inference)—a deep-learning framework that infers enhancer-driven gene-regulatory networks at single-cell resolution—and apply it to single-nucleus multiome profiles of more than one million cells from primary glioblastomas. Integrating computational inference with systematic perturbation screens across patient-derived models, we show that a structured regulatory program with an asymmetric interplay of activators and repressors governs cellular plasticity in glioblastoma. The resulting transition landscape is hierarchical and predictable, but permits cross-lineage trajectories that are absent from normal neurodevelopment. Within this landscape, low-plasticity states such as the neuronal-like state are not stable differentiation end points but actively maintained configurations, stabilized by a network of safeguard repressors that silence alternative fates. We identify MYT1L as the dominant member of this network, and show that it binds to and represses master regulators of every alternative state to consolidate neuronal-like identity. MYT1L restricted plasticity even under standard-of-care therapy and suppressed tumour growth and invasion in vivo. Conversely, loss of MYT1L reactivated plasticity and accelerated malignant features, indicating that low-plasticity identity requires continuous repression. These findings reframe malignant cell state stability as a balance between activation and continuous repression, and nominate safeguard repressors as candidate therapeutic targets that are distinct from differentiation-inducing activators, with potential relevance across cancers in which phenotypic switching drives therapy resistance.

The capacity of cancer cells to switch phenotypic states, also called cellular plasticity, is a hallmark of cancer and is involved in tumour initiation and progression, including therapeutic resistance1. Glioblastoma (GB), the most common primary brain tumour in adults, is characterized by profound cellular heterogeneity and a dismal prognosis2,3. Although the genomic landscape of GB has been catalogued extensively, actionable genetic drivers remain scarce, and clinical responses to targeted therapies have been minimal4,5,6. Longitudinal studies have underscored the limited contribution of therapy-induced genetic evolution in GB and revealed remarkable clonal stability despite treatment7,8, suggesting that non-genetic mechanisms, such as epigenetic plasticity and transcriptional reprogramming, are central to adaptation and treatment resistance in GB. Seminal single-cell transcriptomic studies have shown that malignant GB cells occupy a continuum of cellular states resembling developmental progenitors (for example, oligodendrocyte precursor cell (OPC)-like and neural progenitor cell (NPC)-like), as well as astrocytic and mesenchymal-like phenotypes9,10. Patient-derived xenograft models have demonstrated that individual GB cells can give rise to multiple states, implying substantial plasticity10,11. However, whether these state transitions follow stereotyped routes or variable trajectories12,13, and how gene-regulatory mechanisms promote or constrain them, remain poorly understood.

Here, and in a companion study14, we address these questions by integrating single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) and chromatin accessibility (single-nucleus assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing; snATAC-seq) profiles from more than one million cells from isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)-wild-type primary GBs. Using a computational approach (single-cell deep multi-omic regulatory inference; scDORI) and systematic transcription factor (TF) perturbations, we decode enhancer-driven gene-regulatory networks (eGRNs) at single-cell resolution and map the regulatory landscape that underlies the identity and plasticity of GB cells. Specifically, we model the gene-regulatory processes that govern GB cell states, identify key transcriptional regulators for each state, elucidate their roles in driving cell-state conversions and reconstruct a hierarchical roadmap of GB transitions. Our analyses reveal a structured landscape of GB state transitions shaped by epigenetic ‘highways’ and ‘barriers’. Neuronal-like cells emerge as a constrained, low-plasticity state stabilized by a network of transcriptional repressors that actively silences alternative malignant fates. We identify MYT1L as the key repressor in this network: gain-of-function of MYT1L restricts plasticity and induces a neuronal-like identity in patient-derived GB cells, and reduces tumour growth after transplantation in mice; by contrast, removal of MYT1L enhances plasticity and malignant features. These findings suggest that enforcing repressive barriers could lock GB cells into less adaptive states, and identify plasticity restriction as a potential therapeutic strategy.

Together with our companion study mapping spatio-temporal trajectories in GB tumours14, our findings reveal a conserved, epigenetically controlled hierarchy of cell states and transition routes in GB. Our work establishes transcriptional repressors as key suppressors of plasticity and underscores the potential of targeting the epigenetic foundations of state switching in brain cancer.

To characterize the regulatory landscape of GB, we generated a multiome snRNA–snATAC-seq atlas (see the companion study14), spanning parallel gene-expression and chromatin-accessibility profiles of more than one million cells collected across multiple sites (4–15) from 12 IDH-wild-type primary GB tumours, including peritumoral regions. In line with previous studies10, malignant GB cells exhibited vastly heterogeneous transcriptional profiles, ranging from developmental-like (OPC-NPC-like, OPC-like, OPC-neuronal-like, NPC-neuronal-like and astrocyte (AC)-progenitor-like) to glial injury response and hypoxia-associated (AC-gliosis-like, gliosis-like and hypoxia) phenotypes, as well as cycling signatures (proliferative) (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 1a).

First, we investigated the epigenetic plasticity of GB cells, assuming that accessible gene regions without active transcription indicate epigenetic plasticity. Inspired by a previous study15, we quantified how well accessibility profiles of cell-state markers predict transcriptionally defined cell states (Fig. 1b, Extended Data Fig. 1b,c, Supplementary Table 1 and Methods). Cells whose chromatin profiles yield uncertain predictions are scored as more epigenetically plastic. Our analysis revealed that malignant cells exhibit greater epigenetic plasticity than non-malignant tumour microenvironment (TME) cells and analogous neurodevelopmental cell types (Fig. 1c and Extended Data Fig. 1d–f), with chromatin accessibility in malignant cells often being compatible with multiple cell states (Fig. 1d). However, the extent of this plasticity varied significantly across GB cell states (Fig. 1e), and NPC-neuronal-like and OPC-neuronal-like cells stood out as the least plastic states. The variations in epigenetic plasticity could not be explained by differences in global coupling between gene expression and accessibility, were robust to differences in cell numbers per state and were consistently observed across patients (Fig. 1f and Extended Data Fig. 1g–j). These data suggest that specific regulatory mechanisms underlie GB plasticity and govern cell-state heterogeneity.

To determine the specific gene-regulatory processes that underlie the observed differences in epigenetic plasticity, we aimed to identify relevant TFs, cis-regulatory elements (CREs) and their interplay across GB states using eGRNs. Established eGRN inference methods are not designed for the heterogeneity of a single-cell tumour atlas like ours16,17,18,19,20,21,22. Specifically, existing methods assume discrete, predefined cell-types and therefore cannot capture the continuous, cell-state-specific variation in eGRNs that defines malignant heterogeneity. Many also scale poorly to datasets of this size23. Moreover, previous methods focus mainly on signatures of transcriptional activation, despite the growing recognition of gene repression as a key regulator of cell identity and plasticity in cancer24,25.

To address these limitations, we developed scDORI, a computational approach for inferring continuous eGRNs and their relationships across cell populations using single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) and scATAC-seq multiome data (Fig. 2a and Methods). Building on autoencoders26, scDORI performs joint dimensionality reduction of gene expression and chromatin accessibility profiles, decomposing both into gene-regulatory modules16,27,28,29,30,31,32 that are hereafter referred to as ‘Topics’. Each scDORI Topic encodes an eGRN in which TFs bind to CREs to control target gene expression, so the factors carry regulatory meaning rather than describing co-variation alone (Fig. 2a). Coupled decoders enforce this structure through established eGRN signatures: peak co-accessibility, TF chromatin binding, CRE–gene linkages and TF–target expression correlations that distinguish activation from repression16,17,18,19,20,21,22 (Extended Data Fig. 2a). By modelling each cell as a mixture of Topics, scDORI can capture continuous variations in eGRNs across cell states, without relying on predefined annotations. Conversely, a single Topic can be active across multiple cell states, enabling the identification of shared aspects of gene regulation. scDORI scales to datasets comprising millions of cells, and performs favourably, compared with existing eGRN inference methods, on benchmarks such as the prediction of enhancer–gene links33 (Extended Data Fig. 2b,c).

Applied to our GB atlas, scDORI identified 31 active Topics with distinct patterns of activity across cells and patients (Fig. 2b,c, Extended Data Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 2), each capturing co-accessible chromatin regions that were cis-linked to co-expressed genes (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 4a). Notably, the mechanistic constraints came at no cost to descriptive power: scDORI Topics explained transcriptional variation in tumour cells as well as or better than conventional dimensionality reduction methods did18,29 (Extended Data Fig. 4b,c). Clustering transcriptional GB states according to Topic activity patterns revealed four major regulatory states alongside a distinct proliferative (cycling) population, including developmental states (neuronal-like and OPC-NPC-like) and AC–gliosis–hypoxia states (AC-like and gliosis–hypoxia) (Fig. 2c). Topics specific to individual states reflected gene programs that are characteristic of their developmental counterparts, such as neurotransmitter release in neuronal-like cells (Topic 37), PDGF signalling in OPC-NPC-like cells (Topic 34) and calcium ion homeostasis in AC-like cells (Topic 20)34,35,36 (Fig. 2e, Extended Data Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 2). These programs were also active in corresponding TME cell types, consistent with tumour cells co-opting developmental pathways.

As well as explaining differences between GB states, scDORI resolved functional heterogeneity within individual malignant states. For instance, in gliosis–hypoxia cells, distinct Topics captured hallmark features such as hypoxic stress (Topic 11), cytokine signalling (Topic 19) and vascular wound healing (Topic 12)37,38,39 (Fig. 2e). scDORI also identified ‘inter-state’ Topics (7, 9, 25, 29, 33, 36 and 49) that span several GB states. Most of these were enriched for programs linked to fate commitment and differentiation, indicating a potential role in mediating state transitions (Fig. 2c,e and Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). Finally, scDORI identified Topics that were broadly active across most tumour cells, reflecting tumour-wide programs, such as inflammatory response (Topic 31) and cell adhesion (Topic 18)40 (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). The inferred Topics were reproducible across model restarts, consistently represented across patients with GB and transferable to independent single-cell GB atlases10 (Extended Data Figs. 3c and 4d–f). Notably, scDORI Topic-specific eGRNs accurately captured differential expression between GB cell states of these independent atlases (Extended Data Fig. 4g,h).

The eGRN encoded by each Topic directly yielded Topic regulators (TRs), defined as the TFs with the strongest predicted activation of the Topic-specific gene program. On average, scDORI identified about nine TRs per Topic (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Table 2 and Methods). These included known GB cell-state markers, such as ASCL1 in OPC-NPC-like cells (Topic 36), SOX11 in neuronal-like cells (Topic 24) and FOSL1 in gliosis–hypoxia cells (Topic 19), as well as TFs associated with the respective Topic programs, such as hypoxia (NFKB1, Topic 19) and stress response (JUND, Topic 11)10,41,42,43,44. State-specific TRs included lineage-specific TFs, such as SOX6 (Topic 34, OPCs) and TRPS1 (Topic 9, ACs), further supporting the idea that GB cells reuse developmental TFs and gene programs45,46,47. Besides positive regulation, scDORI-inferred repressive links and identified Topic safeguards (TSs): TFs that regulate an identity by silencing gene programs of states distinct from their state of activity; that is, alternative states (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Table 2 and Methods). For instance, ST18 and SOX10 emerged as putative repressors of alternative state programs in neuronal-like and OPC-NPC-like programs, respectively. Several TSs also act as TRs of their own Topic, revealing factors that both activate their own identity program and repress those of alternative states. Collectively, scDORI redefines GB heterogeneity from a gene-regulation standpoint and reveals state-specific activators and repressors, many of which have not yet been described in GB.

The extent to which GB state transitions follow defined regulatory rules, and which TFs drive them, remain central questions in GB biology9,10. We reasoned that if scDORI faithfully captures these processes, then activating the predicted regulators should induce the associated cell states. To test this, we performed a pooled single-cell lentiviral overexpression (OE) screen of 55 TR and TS candidates across three patient-derived GB cell (PDGC) lines, co-cultured with primary cortical cells to provide a neural microenvironment (Fig. 3a, Extended Data Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 3 and Methods).

After quality control, we recovered 261,566 cells, with 54 of 55 candidate regulators showing robust and balanced induction (at least 100 cells captured with more than 1.5-fold change) across all three lines (Fig. 3b, Extended Data Fig. 5a–d and Supplementary Table 3). We used logistic regression models trained on the patient atlas to assign cell state and Topic identity to each screened cell, revealing perturbation-induced shifts (Supplementary Table 3 and Methods). Two-thirds of candidates induced state changes consistent with scDORI predictions, spanning both TRs (for example, SOX4, ASCL1, MEOX2 and EPAS1) and TSs (for example, ST18, RBPJ, ETV1 and FOSL1) (Fig. 3b). Perturbation effects were also mostly concordant across all three patient lines, despite their distinct baseline cell-state compositions (Fig. 3b and Extended Data Fig. 5e). Top-ranked regulators as predicted by scDORI induced their target states most strongly, particularly those of neuronal-like and AC-like states (Extended Data Fig. 5f). Performing the same analysis at the more granular Topic level, we confirmed that regulators from nearly all Topics induced their respective Topic programs, even when their gain of function was not sufficient to induce a full state conversion (for example, ESR2, BCL6 and NR3C2) (Extended Data Fig. 5g,h). A notable exception was regulators of hypoxia-response Topics 11 and 12, which are likely to require extrinsic hypoxic signals for complete activation. Overall, 76% of tested regulators showed identity shifts consistent with our predictions at either the Topic or the state level (Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 5h).

We defined high-confidence drivers for each of the four core GB states, combining three experimental lines of evidence: the strength of state change; the enrichment of the state gene signature after TF gain of function; and the dependence of state conversion on TF dose (Fig. 3d, Extended Data Fig. 5i,j and Supplementary Table 3). We then asked whether these regulators were also among the top-ranking scDORI predictions. We found that 81% (25 out of 31) ranked among the top predicted regulators of the corresponding state-associated Topics (Fig. 3d). Notably, scDORI also resolved distinct mechanisms of action among these drivers: some mainly activated state marker genes (MEOX2, PRRX1 and JUN), others repressed alternative-state marker genes (MYT1L, ST18 and SOX10), and a subset combined both modes (SOX4, FOXO1 and CEBPD) (Fig. 3d). Together, these results show that scDORI accurately nominates functional regulators of GB identity, and that their activation is sufficient to trigger corresponding state-specific transcriptional programs.

Having established that individual scDORI-predicted regulators can drive GB state transitions, we asked whether their endogenous expression and accessibility could explain how these transitions arise in patients. Examining each state’s TRs in cells of alternative states, we found that 39% (34 out of 87) were already expressed in at least one alternative state, whereas 68% (59 out of 87) were chromatin-accessible but transcriptionally silent, suggesting epigenetic priming for specific state transitions (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b).

To quantify primed transitions systematically, we computed a Topic activation potential (TAP) for each pair of Topics—an estimate of how readily cells in a source Topic can activate the TRs of a target Topic and mediate an identity shift. TAP scores integrate three components: the regulatory links from source-Topic TFs onto target-Topic TRs; the accessibility of those target TRs in source-Topic cells; and the importance of each TR in the target Topic (Fig. 4a, Extended Data Fig. 6c–f, Supplementary Table 4 and Methods). TAP scores revealed a structured and markedly asymmetrical landscape of transitions between GB states (Fig. 4b). Inter-state Topics (33, 36 and 49) acted as connectivity hubs linking multiple states, consistent with their enrichment for fate-change signatures (Fig. 2e). By contrast, state-specific Topics differed sharply: OPC-NPC-like (34) and AC-like (9, 20 and 44) Topics showed strong TAP connections to multiple alternative states, whereas connections from neuronal-like (24, 37 and 41) and gliosis–hypoxia (11, 12 and 19) Topics were mainly confined within their own state (Fig. 4b,e). Together, these results highlight how the regulatory architecture and epigenetic priming of each state jointly determine its permissiveness to transition (Extended Data Fig. 6d–f).

The overall low transition potential of neuronal-like and gliosis–hypoxia Topics raised the question of whether these states are deprived of activating cues or actively constrained by repression. We therefore defined an analogous Topic repression score (TRS), mirroring TAP but built from the inverse signals: the repressive links from source-Topic TFs onto target-Topic TRs and epigenetic inaccessibility of those TRs in source-Topic cells (Fig. 4c, Extended Data Fig. 6g, Supplementary Table 4 and Methods). Neuronal-like Topics (24 and 37) showed the most widespread repression of alternative state Topics and marker genes, attributable to the aggregated activity of several neuronal-like TFs (Fig. 4d,e, Extended Data Fig. 6h,i and Supplementary Table 4). By contrast, other Topics showed either state-restricted repression (2, 20 and 36) or minimal repression overall (9 and 44) (Fig. 4d,e and Supplementary Table 4). Total Topic-level repression negatively correlated with the epigenetic plasticity of associated cells (Figs. 1e and 4f), further supporting a role for repression as an active barrier to state transitions.

To assess how activation and repression jointly shape transitions, we defined a net transition potential as the difference between TAP and TRS (Extended Data Fig. 6j,k). Aggregation at the state level revealed a hierarchy of transition potentials and barriers, consistent with the epigenetic plasticity of the respective states (Fig. 4f–h and Supplementary Table 4). OPC-NPC-like and AC-like states showed predominantly activating interactions towards other states, reflecting broad permissiveness to transitions, whereas neuronal-like and gliosis–hypoxia cells showed limited activation and, in the case of neuronal-like cells, predominant repression towards alternative states (Fig. 4g,h and Extended Data Fig. 6j,k). These interactions were often asymmetrical, pointing to preferential, directional routes of state change.

We next asked how this landscape compares with normal development, applying the same analysis to the developing human dorsal telencephalon48. There, transitions remained confined within neurogenic and gliogenic lineages, confirming that the model recovers established lineage relationships49,50,51,52 (Extended Data Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 5). GB, by contrast, permits transitions across these lineage boundaries, identifying its expanded transition potential as an aberrant feature of malignancy rather than a rewiring of normal developmental hierarchies. Together, these results define a structured, asymmetrical transition landscape, jointly shaped by activation and repression, that qualitatively diverges from normal lineage programs.

Because we derived the transition landscape from profiles of dissociated cells, we wanted to find out to what extent this intrinsic logic could explain tumour organization in space and time. We therefore investigated the relationship between transition potentials and the spatial and temporal co-activity of Topic pairs in orthogonally derived data. In 97 matched spatial transcriptomics tumour sections (338,481 Visium spots)14, cross-state Topic pairs with a high transition potential (for example, Topics 9–25, 44–34 and 9–19) were more frequently co-active in the same regions than were pairs with weak or repressive links (for example, Topics 24–12, 24–2 and 37–44) (Pearson r = 0.488, P = 2 × 10−6, n = 84 pairs; Fig. 4i, Extended Data Fig. 8a–c and Supplementary Table 6). Across 57 pseudotemporally ordered clonal populations14, pairs with a high transition potential likewise showed stronger temporal co-activity (Pearson r = 0.466, P = 8 × 10−6, n = 84 pairs; Fig. 4j, Extended Data Fig. 8d–f and Supplementary Table 7). Transition potentials derived from Topic-specific scDORI eGRNs aligned more closely with both spatio-temporal patterns and epigenetic plasticity than did those from a single network averaged across Topics or eGRNs from alternative methods, showing the advantages of continuous, Topic-resolved modelling (Extended Data Fig. 8g–i). Although spatial and temporal co-activity alone cannot establish direct transitions, their convergence with the regulatory predictions supports the inferred GB transition roadmap. Together, these findings support a revised model of GB plasticity in which structured regulatory relationships both enable and restrict cell-state transitions, revealing potentially targetable effectors of malignant state change.

Increased cellular plasticity is likely to contribute to tumour progression and therapy resistance in GB53,54. Our transition analysis identified neuronal-like cells as the least plastic GB state, stabilized by transcriptional repression. We therefore asked whether neuronal-like regulators could be harnessed to restrict GB plasticity.

To this end, we profiled chromatin accessibility and transcriptional changes after gain of function of ten candidate TFs in PDGCs, comprising both predicted safeguard repressors (BCL11B, NR3C2, MYT1L, ST18, POU2F2 and BACH2) and activators of neuronal-like programs (SOX11, SOX4, ASCL1 and PBX1) (Fig. 5a and Extended Data Fig. 9a,b). All candidate TFs induced specific regulatory changes consistent with scDORI predictions (Fig. 5a–c), and the predictions of differentially regulated genes were more accurate than were those of other eGRN methods (Extended Data Fig. 9c,d). Notably, all repressors silenced non-neuronal-like state programs at both the chromatin and the transcriptional level, whereas activators predominantly induced neuronal-like gene expression (Fig. 5a–c and Extended Data Fig. 9e). Although activator-driven identity induction modestly suppressed alternative states, repressor-driven chromatin closure was substantially better at constraining other cell fates, indicating that repression restricts plasticity more effectively.

Among the regulators tested, MYT1L restricted plasticity most effectively, closing chromatin and silencing virtually all non-neuronal-like programs. MYT1L was also specifically and highly expressed in more than 90% of neuronal-like cells in our patient cohort (Extended Data Fig. 9f). To determine how MYT1L could suppress such a diverse set of alternative GB programs, we mapped its direct targets by CUT&RUN sequencing in PDGCs overexpressing MYT1L (Extended Data Fig. 9g and Supplementary Table 11). We found that 31% of differentially accessible genes (DAGs) were directly bound by MYT1L, almost exclusively at enhancer and promoter sites that closed after induction. By contrast, only 0.3% of bound genes coincided with chromatin opening, identifying MYT1L as a direct transcriptional repressor (Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 9h). MYT1L directly repressed multiple regulators from every alternative state, including TCF7L2 (Topic 2; OPC-NPC-like), MEOX2 (Topic 9; AC-like), ETV1 (Topics 9 and 34; AC-like and OPC-NPC-like) and JUN (Topic 19; gliosis–hypoxia) (Fig. 5e, Extended Data Fig. 9i and Supplementary Table 11). These binding data further validated the improved accuracy of scDORI, relative to alternative methods, in predicting direct targets of MYT1L (Extended Data Fig. 9j). Together, these results identify direct chromatin-level repression of alternative state regulators as the mechanism by which MYT1L restricts plasticity.

We next asked whether MYT1L retains this activity under conditions that are known to promote plasticity, such as a neural microenvironment or therapeutic pressure55,56. MYT1L suppressed the plasticity induced by primary cortical co-culture, reducing both the accessibility and expression of alternative state programs (Fig. 5f and Extended Data Figs. 9h and 10a,b). Similar but attenuated effects were observed when PDGCs were pre-exposed to radiation or temozolomide (Extended Data Fig. 10b–e), indicating that MYT1L mostly retains its capacity to restrict plasticity under therapeutic contexts. Under all of these conditions, MYT1L-overexpressing PDGCs also activated neuronal-like programs—an effect that was recapitulated after prolonged overexpression in neurospheroid monocultures of two independent PDGC lines and reversed by MYT1L loss of function (Fig. 5g and Extended Data Fig. 10f,g). This indicates that sustained repression of alternative state programs indirectly primes GB cells towards a neuronal-like identity, positioning MYT1L not as a classical differentiation factor but as a plasticity restrictor whose downstream consequences include identity consolidation.

In addition to restricting plasticity, sustained MYT1L activity drove the acquisition of functional neuronal-like features11,57. MYT1L-overexpressing PDGCs upregulated neuron-associated pathways, including synaptogenesis and neurite outgrowth (Extended Data Fig. 10h), and showed increased neurite-like complexity in both in vitro co-cultures and in vivo xenografts, reduced cell–cell interconnectivity via tumour microtubes and suppressed growth and proliferation (Extended Data Fig. 10i–k). Opposite effects on interconnectivity and proliferation were seen after MYT1L loss of function (Extended Data Fig. 10j,k). To test whether these effects depend on the repressive activity, we fused the MYT1L DNA-binding domain (DBD) to a transcriptional repressor (EnR) or activator (VP64). The DBD–EnR fusion recapitulated the growth and proliferation phenotypes, whereas the DBD–VP64 fusion reversed them (Fig. 5h and Extended Data Fig. 10l,m), verifying that repression, rather than transcriptional activation, drives these functional features.

If MYT1L acts as a plasticity restrictor rather than as a classical differentiation factor, the neuronal-like state it induces should be unstable in its absence. In line with this, withdrawal of MYT1L after successful reprogramming destabilized the restricted epigenetic configuration of PDGCs, reverting cells to a state indistinguishable from that of controls (Fig. 5i). By contrast, cells that were reprogrammed into a highly plastic AC-like state through MEOX2 induction retained their plastic configuration after driver removal (Extended Data Fig. 11a–c), revealing a mechanistic asymmetry in which low-plasticity states require the continuous enforcement of repressive barriers, whereas high-plasticity states become self-sustaining once established. Consistent with this, pseudotemporally ordered clonal trajectories in our patient atlas showed that neuronal-like-to-alternative state transitions were initiated by a broad shutdown of neuronal-like safeguard repressors, including MYT1L, followed by chromatin opening at regions associated with subsequent identities (Extended Data Fig. 11d,e). These safeguards are persistently silenced in all alternative states (Extended Data Fig. 11f), underscoring their central role in determining whether cells maintain or lose the neuronal-like identity.

Finally, to assess the effects of MYT1L-driven plasticity restriction on tumour progression in vivo, we orthotopically transplanted MYT1L-overexpressing PDGCs into the mouse cortex and followed tumour development by two-photon live imaging over 21 days (Fig. 5j). MYT1L-overexpressing tumours grew significantly more slowly and showed restricted brain invasion compared with controls (Fig. 5j,k), resulting in delayed tumour development and improved survival (Fig. 5l). Together, these results show that state-specific safeguard repressors such as MYT1L can be predicted and harnessed to constrain malignant plasticity, positioning plasticity restriction as a potential route to the development of therapies that target the epigenetic foundations of GB.

Whether the phenotypic plasticity of GB reflects stochastic state switching or follows a defined regulatory logic has remained unresolved10,58,59. Here, by combining large-scale multi-omic profiling of patient tumours, eGRN inference with scDORI, regulatory mapping of state transitions and gain-of-function perturbations across patient-derived lines, we show that GB plasticity is a structured regulatory program that is governed by an asymmetrical interplay of activators and repressors. The resulting transition landscape is hierarchical and predictable. However, it permits cross-lineage trajectories that are precluded in normal neurodevelopment, establishing GB plasticity as a cancer-specific phenomenon that is mechanistically distinct from developmental hierarchies. Within this landscape, low-plasticity states are not stable differentiation end points, but actively maintained configurations stabilized by safeguard repressors that silence alternative fates.

The regulatory architecture that underlies this landscape was mostly inaccessible to existing eGRN methods, which rely on discrete cell-type annotations and mainly capture activating regulation. scDORI models cell identity as a continuum of overlapping regulatory programs (Topics) and distinguishes activating from repressive interactions. Each Topic encodes an eGRN in which TFs bind to CREs to control target gene expression, and each cell is modelled as a mixture of Topics. Because Topics can be co-active, this representation captures the hybrid identities that naturally arise in highly plastic tumour cells10. The imposed mechanistic constraints transform a descriptive view of GB heterogeneity into a mechanistic model that links cell identity to underlying enhancer and TF activities.

Gain of function of 54 scDORI-predicted regulators across three patient-derived lines established that the activation of inferred regulators is broadly sufficient to drive cells towards specific malignant states, and uncovered unexplored GB-state-driving roles for factors such as HNF4G, NPAS2 and KLF12. Mapping the endogenous regulation of these regulators revealed a structured and asymmetrical transition landscape that aligns with the spatial and clonal organization of patient tumours described in our companion study14. Together, the two studies resolve complementary axes of GB plasticity: the regulatory architecture that defines which transitions are possible, and the spatio-temporal organization through which they unfold in patient tumours.

Our results show that low-plasticity and high-plasticity states differ qualitatively in how they are maintained. Whereas highly plastic chromatin configurations, such as in the MEOX2-driven AC-like state, seem to be self-sustaining once established, low-plasticity configurations in neuronal-like cells persist only under the continuous activity of safeguard repressors. Removal of the safeguard repressor MYT1L destabilizes the restricted chromatin configuration and reverts cells to a state indistinguishable from controls. Pseudotemporally ordered clonal trajectories in patient tumours mirror this mechanism, with exit from the neuronal-like state being initiated by a broad shutdown of safeguard repressors followed by chromatin opening at alternative state regulators. MYT1L emerged from our analysis as the dominant safeguard of the neuronal-like state, within a repressor network that also includes BCL11B, ST18, POU2F2, NR3C2 and BACH2. Although MYT1L safeguards neuronal identity during development, and previous work has linked it to tumour-suppressive effects and a favourable prognosis, its role in controlling cancer cell identity remains undefined60,61,62,63,64. We find that GB cells co-opt MYT1L to restrict malignant plasticity broadly. MYT1L binds to and represses regulators of every alternative malignant state, including TCF7L2 (OPC-NPC-like), MEOX2 (AC-like) and JUN (gliosis–hypoxia), constraining the full repertoire of accessible fates rather than enforcing a single lineage. Its gain of function suppresses tumour growth, restricts invasion and prolongs survival in orthotopic xenografts. These effects depend specifically on repression, as DBD–EnR fusion recapitulates the phenotypes and DBD–VP64 fusion reverses them. This shows that active repression is the mechanism that underlies the tumour-suppressive function of MYT1L. Classic lineage-inducing factors that establish a cell state by activating a specific transcriptional program have been proposed as potential therapies, such as ASCL1-driven differentiation of glioma stem cells65. Plasticity restriction works in the opposite direction, holding alternative programs off and removing the regulatory degrees of freedom that allow cells to escape an existing state1,66. The existence of similar safeguard repressors in liver and colon cancer suggests that the principle extends beyond GB24,25.

Our findings establish a revised model of GB plasticity in which tumour cells occupy a structured landscape of epigenetic potential shaped by the asymmetrical interplay of activating and repressive forces, and in which constrained identities are actively maintained, rather than stably differentiated. Several questions remain: how this regulatory architecture is reshaped during recurrence; whether sustained therapeutic pressure selects for escape from constrained states; and whether the neuronal-like state itself promotes tumour growth through non-cell-autonomous neuron–cancer interactions. Beyond GB, we present a generalizable strategy for uncovering the regulatory principles that underlie cellular plasticity, which can be applied to other cancers in which phenotypic switching is decoupled from genetic evolution and drives therapy resistance, including SCLC, melanoma and pancreatic cancer15,67,68.

Accessible chromatin at a gene locus that is not yet transcribed indicates a primed configuration poised for potential activation. We used the degree of decoupling between chromatin accessibility and transcription at marker gene level to quantify epigenetic plasticity—the capacity of a cell’s chromatin landscape to support transcriptional identities other than the one currently expressed. Following a previous study15, we trained a classifier to predict cell states from gene-expression profiles and applied it to gene accessibility profiles of the same cells. Cells whose accessibility is compatible with several transcriptional states are classified with low confidence and scored as more plastic.

Epigenetic plasticity scores were computed on metacell groupings of the patient atlas, constructed by Leiden clustering (Scanpy74, tl.leiden(); resolution=1) of the scVI RNA embedding latent variable within each cell state, donor and batch stratum, resulting in a total of 16,423 metacells. Cell-state–donor–batch groups with fewer than 40 cells were directly considered a metacell. For each metacell, we computed mean RNA counts and mean ArchR gene scores, the latter of which aggregate ATAC signal over promoters, gene bodies and distance-weighted distal elements. We normalized both matrices to library size, and log-transformed the RNA count matrix. A multinomial logistic regression model was trained on the expression of 3,160 highly variable genes to predict the 17 cell states shown in Fig. 1a (80% of metacells as training fraction; 20% held-out fraction; Extended Data Fig. 1b). The model was then applied to scaled ATAC gene scores, and epigenetic plasticity scores were estimated as the Shannon entropy of the resulting class probabilities. Metacell scores were projected back to constituent single cells for visualization (Fig. 1c). Variance components (Fig. 1f) were estimated by ordinary least squares, with cell state and donor as fixed effects, followed by ANOVA. Supplementary Methods section 2 provides further information on factors contributing to epigenetic plasticity variance and on global RNA-to-ATAC coupling.

scDORI infers eGRNs from paired single-cell or single-nuclei RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data. Dimensionality reduction and eGRN network inference are commonly considered as separate steps, and networks are inferred within discrete, predefined cell type annotations. scDORI unifies the two, so that latent factors themselves carry regulatory meaning. An encoder maps each cell to a low-dimensional representation, and mechanistically constrained decoders reconstruct the observed accessibility and expression from it. The constraints are what separate scDORI from conventional dimensionality reduction: a factor reconstructs the data well only if it corresponds to a coherent program in which TFs bind to accessible elements to control nearby genes. We call these factors Topics. Each Topic is a signed, enhancer-mediated network linking co-accessible peaks, co-expressed genes and their predicted activating and repressing regulators, so a factor’s targets and mode of action can differ between Topics depending on which peaks are accessible. Each cell is modelled as a mixture of Topics rather than assigned to a cluster, so networks vary continuously across cells and a single Topic can be active across several states. Training runs in mini-batches, enabling us to scale to millions of cells without aggregation. Detailed explanations of scDORI components and their motivations, as well as the training schedule and empirical null to filter eGRN links, are provided in Supplementary Methods 1.1 to 1.9, with the rationale relative to previous methods in Supplementary Methods 1.10 and 1.11. A schematic representation of scDORI architecture is shown in Extended Data Fig. 2a.

scDORI was trained on the single-nuclei ATAC-seq and RNA-seq profiles of 95% of the patient atlas cells and evaluated on the held-out 5%, using 182,677 highly variable peaks, 3,192 highly variable genes and 195 TFs to learn 50 latent factors (Topics). During phase 2 training for GRN inference (module 4), we froze the encoder and modules 1–3 to preserve the Topic representations learnt in phase 1 (see Extended Data Fig. 2a and Supplementary Methods 1.6). In addition, we used empirical Topic-level TF expression for GRN pruning, computed by averaging TF expression across cells in each Topic as input to module 4, rather than using the denoised TF estimates from module 3. Full hyperparameters are provided in Supplementary Methods Table 3 and guidance on their selection can be found in Supplementary Methods 1.8. We verified that scDORI-inferred Topic programs were consistent across model restarts (three distinct random seeds) by computing the Jaccard similarity between the top 500 genes of the most active Topic in each GB state and that of the reference run 0 (Extended Data Fig. 4d). Run 0 was used for all analyses hereafter. All Topics with a maximum activity greater than 0.1 across cells were considered for downstream analyses (31 out of 50). Topic activity across states and cell types, as well as TF activity across Topics, can be found in Supplementary Table 2. Dominant gene programs per Topic were identified by gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA, decoupler v1.6.0)75 comparing the top 250 genes of the Topic–gene matrix (Supplementary Methods 1.9.3) against all gene sets (15–500 genes in size) in MSigDB (v.2023.1.Hs) subcategories H, C2, C5 and C8. Enriched pathways across Topics are summarized in Supplementary Table 2 and Extended Data Fig. 3a.

We assessed the performance of scDORI across three complementary benchmarks: peak–gene link predictions, the learnt latent space and the ability of learnt eGRNs to explain differential gene expression. Peak–gene links were benchmarked against SCENIC+ on the ENCODE76,77 cell lines IMR90, K562 and GM12878, using simulated single-cell multi-omic profiles derived from bulk RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data for these lines, as described previously18. Predictions were evaluated against the benchmark of candidate enhancer–gene interactions (BENGI)33, a curated set of peak–gene interactions built from CRISPR perturbations, chromatin interaction assays and expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) data for these lines, with performance quantified by the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC). The learned latent space was benchmarked against MOFA29 and SCENIC+18, the latter represented by AUCell78 scores over the same set of TFs as scDORI, both run on metacell profiles owing to memory demands. scDORI Topic activities were aggregated at the metacell level for fair comparison. The biological relevance of each latent space was scored by its ability to predict cell-state labels through logistic regression under fivefold cross-validation. Finally, inferred eGRNs were evaluated by how well they explain differential expression in an independent single-cell GB atlas10, following the GRaNPA framework21. For each pair of states or Leiden clusters, we formed a network from the union of TF–gene links in Topics with a mean activation higher than 0.2 in either population, then used logistic regression on signed TF–gene links to predict whether a gene was upregulated or downregulated. Model performance was scored by AUPRC and compared against SCENIC+ eGRNs and randomized eGRNs. Full details on the execution of each benchmark are provided in Supplementary Methods section 4.

Topic-level TF activity scores were defined as the aggregate regulatory strength of each TF across its target genes within a Topic, separately for activating and repressive links. Activity scores are normalized across both TFs and Topics so that they reflect the intensity of a TF’s activity in a Topic and its specificity to that Topic. TRs were defined as TFs with the highest downstream activation of genes within each Topic, ranked by activity score. We quantified repression toward alternative Topics as the number of binarized repressive links from each TF to the top 250 genes of every alternative Topic, z-scored across TFs. TFs with the strongest repression of genes from Topics of at least two alternative states were defined as TSs. Because a TF can be both a TR and a TS, we also defined a Topic regulation score as the sum of the activating and repressive scores, and a state regulation score as its activity-weighted aggregate across the Topics active in a state. Full details on the computation of each score are provided in Supplementary Methods 1.9.7.

To assess the epigenetic priming of TRs, we examined discrepancies between their expression and chromatin accessibility across the patient atlas. Given the high heterogeneity among GB cells, we used Topic activity patterns to define finer-grained cell subsets sharing similar regulatory dynamics: for each Topic, we selected the top 5,000 cells by Topic activity score and computed average TR expression and accessibility (ArchR gene score) within this subset. We min–max scaled expression and accessibility values across GB Topics to enable comparison. A TR was defined as epigenetically primed in a given Topic-defined substate if its scaled expression was lower than 0.3 and its scaled accessibility exceeded 0.5 (Extended Data Fig. 6a,e).

We define the TAP as a measure of how readily cells in a source Topic A can activate the program of a target Topic B. It rests on the premise, supported by our gain-of-function screen and by classical reprogramming work79, that activating the TRs of B is sufficient to drive its program. The TAP between a given Topic pair is computed by analysing all activating links between TFs expressed in the source Topic A (min–max scaled average expression > 0.5; TFi) towards all TRs in the target Topic B (activity score > 0.05; TRj). Specifically, the product of three terms is computed for every TFi–TRj pair: (i) presence of an activating link from TFi to TRj (binary variable); (ii) scaled average accessibility of TRj in cells of Topic A; and (iii) total activation strength of TRj in Topic B (activity score). The sum of all TFi–TRj values reflects the potential of Topic A to activate Topic B. We use all regulatory links from ATAC-derived Topic eGRNs rather than the expression-pruned eGRNs, as they include all regulatory links towards regulators that are accessible but not necessarily expressed. See Supplementary Methods 1.4.6 for details on the ATAC-derived and expression-pruned eGRN versions. The TRS mirrors the TAP but focuses on repressive interactions between Topics. To that end, it considers repressive links between TFi–TRj pairs, in this case from expression-pruned eGRNs to corroborate repressive interactions, and the degree of chromatin closure of TRs (1 − accessibility) in Topic A for computation. Empirical significance levels for both were estimated by computing TAP and TRS values from randomized eGRNs (1,000 TF–gene permutations), and considering links above the 95th percentile of the null as significant. Both metrics are normalized by the number of TFs expressed in the source Topic and the self-activation of the target Topic to make scores comparable across sources. Total repression exerted by each Topic is computed as the aggregated TRS values from a source Topic towards all alternative Topics. Pan-state Topics 18, 31 and 39 were not included in TAP and TRS computations, because their non-specific activity across cells limits interpretation of the resulting links. Full details on TAP and TRS computation and implemented variations on the components of these metrics are described in Supplementary Methods sections 5 and 6. TAP and TRS scores across Topics, as well as individual contributions of each TF to the final scores are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

We define the net transition potential between two Topics as the difference between respective TAP and TRS values. The total plasticity enabled by each Topic (Topic-driven plasticity) is directly derived from this value as the aggregated net transition potential from a source Topic towards all Topics active in alternative states. We assessed the concordance between Topic-driven plasticity and the average epigenetic plasticity scores of Topic-associated cells (top 5,000 cells) by computing the Pearson correlation between values across Topics. State-level regulatory interactions were derived by aggregating TAP and TRS values from all Topics active in state A towards all Topics specific to state B, weighted by the proportional activity of each Topic in its respective state. To filter out noise from Topics with low levels of activation, proportional activities are computed only across Topics whose average activity score exceeds two-thirds of the most active Topic in each state. The state transition potential is finally defined as the net effect between transition-promoting (aggregated TAP) and transition-restricting (aggregated TRS) interactions. State transition potentials reflect the permissiveness of the regulatory landscape in state A towards transitioning to state B; thus, positive or negative values should not be interpreted as fixed or impossible transitions. Further details on net transition potential and its comparison to related methods are provided in Supplementary Methods section 7.

We used cell2location80 to map Topics onto spatial locations of 97 Visium spatial transcriptomics sections (338,481 spot transcriptomes) from the same 12 tumours that were profiled in our patient multiome atlas (see our companion manuscript for details on spatial section pre-processing14). The Topic–gene matrix (Supplementary Methods 1.9.3) was provided as the cell2location reference, thereby treating each scDORI Topic as a distinct transcriptional program for spatial deconvolution. For each Topic, spot assignment was determined by two criteria: (1) the inferred abundance of the Topic exceeded the median abundance for that Topic across all spots; and (2) the abundance exceeded a baseline threshold of 5, applied to filter low-signal assignments and retain only biologically meaningful spot–Topic associations. For each Topic pair in each tissue section, we computed the Euclidean distance between all spot pairs and identified the bottom 10th percentile of these distances, which represents the most proximal locations between these Topics in the section. We determined the degree of spatial co-activity between Topic pairs as the percentage of sections in which the mean pairwise Topic distance in this percentile is zero, corresponding to direct co-activity at the spot level. We only considered sections in which both Topics are active for this analysis. Each Topic pair was observed in at least 17 sections (60 on average) from at least 7 patients (10 on average) (Extended Data Fig. 8b). Pairwise Topic distances at the section level, number of sections and patients considered per Topic pair and Topic spatial co-activity across sections are provided in Supplementary Table 6.

We determined temporal patterns of Topic activity along pseudotemporally ordered clonal populations in our patient atlas. Clonal populations were inferred jointly from snRNA-seq and snATAC-seq by detecting large-scale copy-number variations (CNVs) using inferCNV (RNA)71 and epiAneufinder (ATAC)81, matching modalities by shared cells and CNV profile similarity and assigning high-confidence clone identities through label spreading (full details in ref. 14). Pseudotemporal ordering with Cell2fate82 yielded 60 clonal trajectories with sufficient cell counts and pseudotime convergence. For each cell, scDORI Topic activity was smoothed using a 100-cell moving average over pseudotime-ordered cells, balancing resolution and noise reduction, and a net pseudotime score per Topic was defined as its activation-weighted average pseudotime within each clone. Directionally co-active Topic pairs were identified by first selecting clonal trajectories with significant activation of a reference Topic A (95th percentile activation > 0.3). Within these clones, a candidate target Topic B was considered if it met either of two criteria: (1) its 95th percentile activation was below 0.3 (no activity in the trajectory); or (2) its net pseudotime score exceeded that of Topic A (later activation in time). Co-activity between Topics A and B was computed for clonal trajectories in each directional set as the cosine similarity between their activation vectors across cells in the clone. A similarity of 0 was assigned when Topic B’s 95th percentile activation fell below 0.1 to minimize spurious associations. For each Topic pair, values were averaged across the top 10% of clones ranked by similarity to derive a temporal co-activity score, ensuring low scores genuinely reflect low transition likelihood rather than dilution from clones in which Topic B was simply inactive. A total of 57 out of 60 clones ranked among the top 10% for at least one Topic pair. Clones considered per Topic pair and temporal co-activity values are shown in Supplementary Table 7.

To assess whether the regulatory logic captured by our net transition potential metric explains the spatio-temporal organization of tumours, we tested its consistency with spatial and temporal co-activity patterns, assuming that Topics connected by high transition potential should be spatially proximal and show coordinated activation over pseudotime. For each reference Topic A, we computed the Pearson correlation between (i) the vector of net transition potential values from Topic A to all other Topics and (ii) the corresponding vector of spatial or temporal co-activity scores. For spatial co-activity, we averaged net transition potential values from A→B and B→A to match the symmetrical nature of spatial distance. For temporal co-activity, we computed correlations separately for outgoing (A→B) and incoming (B→A) directions. Correlations with P < 0.05 were considered significant. Finally, to test whether transition-prone pairs preferentially co-occur, spatial and temporal co-activity of cross-state Topic pairs were stratified by net transition potential (Fig. 4i–j). We computed Pearson correlations between these values across n = 84 cross-state Topic pairs, considering P < 0.05 as significant.

To assess the importance of eGRN predictions in estimates of net transition potential, we inferred eGRNs with SCENIC+, trained on metacells from our patient atlas as described above, and scored TF activity with AUCell. To accommodate the Topic-based TAP framework, the mean AUCell activity across the top 5,000 cells with the highest activation of each Topic was used to determine TF regulators per Topic. Accordingly, TAP and TRS values were computed between scDORI Topic-defined cell populations, using (i) activating and repressive links from SCENIC+ GRNs and (ii) AUCell activity scores to rank the importance of each targeted TF in cells of the target Topic. We derived net transition potentials, Topic-driven plasticity and total repression per Topic, as described in the respective sections. Although the resulting SCENIC+-derived values still depend on scDORI Topic-derived populations, this approach isolates the contribution of eGRN inference to the final transition potential estimates. To further assess the benefit of Topic-specific eGRNs, we computed net transition potential values using a union scDORI GRN, comprising all TF–gene links captured across GB Topics. Method performance was determined by comparing Pearson correlations between (1) Topic-driven plasticity or total repression per Topic and average epigenetic plasticity of Topic-associated cells; (2) net transition potential and spatial co-activity; and (3) net transition potential and temporal co-activity. For the latter, correlations from outgoing and incoming links were averaged to yield a single value per Topic. Correlations with P < 0.05 were considered significant, and two-sided paired t-tests were also performed for (2) and (3) to determine significant differences between methods (P < 0.05 considered significant). Method comparisons are shown in Extended Data Fig. 8g,h.

For two pseudotime-ordered clonal trajectories starting from neuronal-like cells, we reconstructed the regulatory interactions between TFs over time to explain exit from the neuronal-like state. We split each trajectory into four stages reflecting distinct states or transitional periods, as determined by the temporal activity of Topics, and defined a set of active TFs for each stage on the basis of temporal expression patterns. The stage 1 set was restricted to safeguard repressors so that their regulation on state exit could be followed. Mean accessibility and expression of each stage TF group were computed across cells of each trajectory and plotted over pseudotime, with single-cell values binned in groups of 200 adjacent cells to denoise (moving average window, centred on each cell). Regulatory interactions at each stage were read from the merged eGRNs of all Topics co-active in that stage (Extended Data Fig. 11d,e).

To contextualize patterns of plasticity regulation inferred from our patient atlas, we performed analogous analyses on a published multiome atlas of the human cortex48. This atlas comprises 45,549 nuclei from 12 donor samples, collected across six time points spanning mid-gestation to adulthood. Analysis of this dataset served two purposes. First, as a reference for the epigenetic plasticity score, it contextualized the chromatin permissiveness of GB cells against the normal developing brain. Second, as a ground-truth benchmark for scDORI and the net transition potential framework, its well-characterized lineage hierarchy allowed evaluation of both the recovery of canonical regulators by scDORI module 4 and the inferred transition relationships. To score epigenetic plasticity, cells were grouped into six types of neuroectodermal cell (radial glia cells, NPCs, neurons, ACs, OPCs and oligodendrocytes), with microglia retained as a negative control. We trained a linear regression model to predict cell types on the basis of the gene expression of metacells (80/20% train/test split of 2,559 metacells), built as for the patient atlas but using cell type × sample stratum, and computed epigenetic plasticity scores on the basis of predictions using gene accessibility (see ‘Epigenetic plasticity score’ above and Extended Data Fig. 1d,e). scDORI was trained at the single-cell level using 30 instead of 50 Topics to reflect the smaller cohort and more compact lineage hierarchy. Modified hyperparameters for model training are shown in Supplementary Methods Table 4, and inferred Topic activity and TF regulation across cell types (author-defined label groupings) are in Extended Data Fig. 7b,c. Net transition potentials were computed for all Topics active in cells of dorsal telencephalic origin (13 of 30), defining TF expression and accessibility per Topic as the average across the top 500 cells of each Topic and considering TFs with an activity score greater than 0.05 as TRs. The resulting TAP, TRS and net transition potential values are shown in Extended Data Fig. 7d. Computed epigenetic plasticity and transition potential patterns between neurodevelopmental cell types were compared against lineage relationships previously confirmed by lineage-tracing experiments49,50,51,52. Cells from the TF gain-of-function screen that passed all quality-control filters were projected onto the patient atlas by pooling them with 96,295 atlas cells (union of the top 5,000 cells per GB Topic), computing 200 principal components on 4,315 features and batch-correcting with Harmony88 using sample as a covariate. TME states (OPCs) and Topics (3: ACs and 40: OPCs) were also included during training as negative controls, and proliferative cells were excluded from the core-state classifier owing to their unstable state identity. The balanced accuracy of state and Topic assignment on held-out cells was 0.89 and 0.87, respectively. Full details on regression classifier training are provided in Supplementary Methods 9.3. We quantified overexpression efficiency per line as the log2-transformed fold change relative to control cells. Because both perturbed and control populations showed bimodal TF expression (Extended Data Fig. 5c; bimodality coefficient (BC) > 0.555, n = 55/55 TF OE, n = 54/55 control), analyses were restricted to perturbed cells with TF expression at least 1.5-fold above the control mean, and for every TF OE versus control comparison, control cells with endogenous TF expression (>10th percentile) were also excluded. Effects were reported as the mean difference in state or Topic probability scores alongside Cohen’s d. Significance was tested by label permutation with matched subsampling over 1,000 permutations, Benjamini–Hochberg (BH)89 corrected across all TF-by-line-by-state comparisons, and called significant at P < 0.05 with absolute d > 0.2. High-confidence drivers of each state were determined by assessing concordance with two additional lines of evidence: (i) dose dependence on observed state shifts, assessed by correlating TF expression with state probability across 2,702 metacells; and (ii) state signature enrichment among differentially expressed genes (DEGs; per line Wilcoxon rank-sum test, FDR < 0.05) per TF, determined by GSEA between the top 250 state markers and DEGs ranked by signed FDR score, averaged across lines. a, Two-phase training scheme. Phase 1: Topic construction by joint training of the core autoencoder modules. scDORI embeds paired scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq profiles in a shared latent Topic space, from which three modules reconstruct the data. Module 1 maps Topic proportions to peak accessibility through a linear decoder with batch-specific adjustments. Module 2 predicts gene expression from the denoised Topic-specific accessibility of Module 1, using a learnable gene–peak linkage matrix restricted to a fixed genomic window around each gene. Module 3 reconstructs TF expression through Topic-TF weights. The three modules are trained jointly, minimizing the sum of their reconstruction losses together with regularization terms. Phase 2: Fine-tuning for eGRN inference. A fourth module infers Topic-specific TF–gene-regulatory links. In silico TF–peak binding scores, signed for activation or repression, are weighted by Topic-specific peak accessibility (Module 1) and propagated to target genes through the gene–peak links (Module 2). Learnable TF–gene-Topic weights adjust these links according to TF–target expression co-variation, and activating and repressive contributions are combined into a net regulatory score that predicts gene expression from Topic-level TF expression (Module 3). The encoder, gene–peak links, and Topic–peak decoder are optionally frozen during fine-tuning, with sparsity constraints on the learned TF–gene weights. b, Training runtime of scDORI as a function of dataset size, shown for Phase 1, Phase 2, and both phases combined, measured on subsampled multi-ome data from the GB atlas. Runtime scales to datasets of millions of cells. Empirical runtimes on a single NVIDIA V100 GPU (32 GB). c, Assessment of peak–gene link predictions in three cell lines (IMR90, K562, GM12878) of the published ENCODE dataset76,77, converted to semi-synthetic single-cell multi-ome data as in18 and scored against curated experimentally supported enhancer–gene links from BENGI33 as ground truth. Benchmarks span nine evidence x cell-line combinations available in BENGI, drawn from seven evidence sources derived from chromatin configuration, perturbation, and genomic interaction assays.
