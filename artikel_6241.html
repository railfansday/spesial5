American Airlines Dropped One Bogus Award Fee. Alaska Airlines And Air Canada Keep Collecting Theirs.

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

American Airlines once charged members $75 to book an award ticket close to departure. The airline eventually eliminated that fee, but a new account from its former loyalty chief offers a revealing reminder: the work used to justify a charge can disappear long before the charge does.

On the latest Airlines Confidential podcast, former AAdvantage chief and American Airlines Chief Information Officer Maya Leibman discussed an airline fee she had trouble defending, as flagged by View From The Wing.

American continued collecting its close-in award booking fee after the ticketing process became automated. Members without an applicable elite waiver paid $75 when booking within 21 days of departure. Leibman recalled that “we were still charging an expedite fee, like, within 21 days,” even though the transaction was “all done automatically.”

American eliminated the fee in January 2020. Like Gary, I appreciate hearing a former executive acknowledge the tension with collecting money for an expedited process that no longer required anyone to expedite it. I would rather hear that candid admission than a strained argument trying to justify it.

I can also understand why manually processing an award ticket once required time and resources and understand why an airline might have charged more to complete that work shortly before departure. But when a computer issues the ticket, what exactly is being expedited?

The airline still has expenses, of course. Maintaining a reservation system costs money. So does operating a loyalty program. But those expenses also exist when someone books a ticket three months before departure and do not explain why an otherwise identical automated transaction should suddenly cost another $75 inside a 21-day window.

The more straightforward explanation is that American could collect the money, so it did.

Airlines do not price everything according to the cost of providing it, and I do not expect them to. A last-minute seat may command a premium because demand supports it. But if that is the rationale, call it a premium for booking at the last minute. Calling it a processing expense just makes frequent flyers jaded. And whatever you call it, customers are understandably going to hate it because it does not correlate to any legitimate expense unless you count the sunk cost of the investment in the automation system.

American dropped its fee, but I see the same problem today with partner award booking fees at Alaska Airlines Atmos Rewards and Air Canada Aeroplan.

I covered the Atmos fee increase earlier this year. Aeroplan also spells out its charge in its flight reward policy and makes it clear when booking online or on the Air Canada app.

These are airline-imposed charges, of course, not government taxes, though they are often lumped into the same section to suggest that you’re paying taxes, not fees. Here, you can do the searching, select the flights, enter your information, and complete an ordinary booking online, yet still pay extra for the privilege of redeeming your points on a partner.

I consider these bogus fees. They are revenue-generating measures, and I will not defend them as some necessary surcharge for the work involved in issuing my ticket. But by bogus that doesn’t mean that I fail to understand why they are levied.

Yes, programs pay partner airlines for award travel, bu that is part of offering partner awards in the first place. It should already inform the economics of selling points and setting redemption prices. Collecting another cash charge from the member is a commercial choice.

For my family of four, the Atmos fee alone adds $160 to a round-trip partner award unless a waiver applies (and I don’t have the credit card right now). That is real money, especially when we are already redeeming a substantial number of points.

There is one concession I will make: these fees are published and displayed during booking. We can account for them when deciding what the points and the program are worth.

If an award costs 50,000 points plus taxes and a partner booking fee, that cash payment belongs in my valuation. I should not calculate the value of the redemption as though the fee does not exist.

A program with an annoying booking fee can still offer a better overall deal than a program without one. I would happily pay $20 to save thousands of points on an otherwise comparable itinerary. But that makes the redemption worthwhile despite the fee. It does not make the fee itself worthwhile.

The same applies when earning or buying points. If I expect to redeem primarily on partners, those charges reduce what I am willing to pay for the currency. Transparency lets us make that calculation, so at least there is that.

American Airlines deserves credit for eventually eliminating its $75 close-in award booking fee. Leibman’s recollection helps explain why members were right to question it: the process became automated, but the charge remained.

Partner award booking fees at Atmos Rewards and Aeroplan strike me as similarly unappealing revenue measures. I still use both programs, and a good redemption can easily outweigh the extra cash required. But I bake those charges into my valuation rather than pretend they are justified by some special service I am receiving.

Sometimes a fee survives because collecting it is profitable, which his hardly breaking news, but something I appreciate Leibman confirming explicitly.
