The 27 Best Places to Go in 2027

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

As travel-magazine editors, we are forever asking—and answering—the same question: Where to next? The choices are, fortunately, endless. So once a year we call upon our savviest writers, travel specialists, and global editors to help us land on a definitive guide to the Best Places to Go in the year ahead. For months we research, whittle down, and poke and prod everything that crosses our desks in the hunt for the latest and greatest experiences. Now we lift the veil.

Our resulting list of The 27 Best Places to Go in 2027 is more than a roll call of fantastic destinations. It is a reflection of how and why we are traveling now. There is the timeless Luxor, Egypt, the gateway to several new Nile cruises, which in August 2027 will fall beneath the “eclipse of the century,” an epic sequel to this past year’s fanfare-inspiring celestial event. The evolution of Shanghai, meanwhile, proves that Chinese cities are prioritizing both business and pleasure in 2027: In November the Shanghai Biennale will honor a wave of new gallery openings in West Bund, just across the water from the new, Snøhetta-designed Shanghai Grand Opera House. And in Jamaica, which just passed the one-year mark since Hurricane Melissa, the strongest to ever hit the country, hotels are once again welcoming travelers and inviting them to be part of the island’s recovery—a reminder of how powerful a mere vacation can be.

And then there are the places that urge us to slow down and allow the connection between host and visitor to deepen—whether that be on a new hiking route in Armenia or while sailing among the postcard-worthy Cook Islands. We know we’ll continue to chase great food and wine experiences, but this year we’re ready to buck the drops-at-midnight restaurant reservations in favor of meals with a straight-from-the-earth mindset and long-held culinary traditions. Finally, a desire to really, truly escape those Euro summer crowds means more of us will see the appeal of cooler climes, whether that’s a new riviera farther north in Denmark or in the Southern Hemisphere for off-season ice trekking in Argentine Patagonia.

With the whole world at our fingertips, these are the 27 places topping our lists for the year to come. Read on for our complete guide to the Best Places to Go in 2027. —Arati Menon & Megan Spurrell

Go for: untamed wilderness and peaceful pockets

A boat sails on Komani Lake in Northern Albania, where turquoise rivers, inn-to-inn hiking, and snow-dipped peaks are increasingly calling to travelers.

As Albania’s allure continues to grow, travelers flock to the Adriatic beaches of the south. But in the northern alpine region, the scenery offers a different type of drama—jagged snow-dipped peaks, turquoise rivers, inn-to-inn hiking—and in place of shiny seaside hotels, Albanian-owned wooden B&Bs welcome alpinists for home-cooked meals after long, satisfying days on the many surrounding trails. This area was largely isolated during the communist era, given its strategic location along the borders with Montenegro and Kosovo, which means that it has been slower to open its arms to the world (that’s also why you’ll stumble into countless abandoned bunkers from this period on your hike). The feeling here is that you can still show up to a guesthouse without a reservation, you can still camp wherever you find a spot, and, yet, there is also the sense that if you aren’t seeing this place now, or within the next couple years, you’ll miss the precious moment when it still feels like a secret to be let in on.

Start your trip in the charming city of Shköder, known as the gateway to the Alps, where bikepackers cycle through the city, and restaurant patios spill over with guests enjoying glasses of Albanian wine. From there, many travelers plan stops in the villages of Theth and Valbona (a stunningly scenic one-day through-hike links the two of them), or embark on ambitious multi-day journeys on routes like the Peak of the Balkans, which traces a two-week-long loop crossing into Kosovo and Montenegro. Infrastructure improvements have made roads and trails to access this region better in recent years, with much more where that came from. What was once only known by residents and those who purchased their paper guides now exists online, meaning it is also possible to base yourself in, say, the Eden-like Valbona Valley, and embark on a new route each day. Increasingly, there are elevated places to stay that preserve the soul of this place, opening in a slow trickle like Valbona Resort and Spa (2024) and SeSam Villas in Theth (2025). There may be folklore calling these the Accursed Mountains, but only because they are so vast and dramatic that their geography has historically kept intruders out. For hikers, that means a literal playground, with Albanian hosts leading the way. —Megan Spurrell

Go for: nature, history, and slow travel on foot

The Historic Tatev Monastery, perched on a cliff edge above the Vorotan Gorge, is the type of sight promised along Armenia's long-distance trails—which will be easier than ever to navigate in 2027, thanks to new maps, signage, and trekking services.

Built among the peaks and valleys of the Caucasus, Armenia’s ancient roots and mountainous landscapes reward slow travel. Along the country’s two long-distance trails, the Armenian National Trail (ANT) and the Transcaucasian Trail (TCT), medieval monasteries rise above dramatic gorges, Silk Road-era caravansaries dot historic mountain routes, and ancient caves recall thousands of years of winemaking history. In Yerevan, one of the world's oldest continuously inhabited cities, historic fortresses and pale pink stone buildings sit side by side with a colorful café and restaurant scene.

In 2027, Armenia's growing outdoor scene makes it especially exciting for adventurous travelers. The TCT, which will eventually span nearly 1,900 miles across Armenia, Georgia, and Azerbaijan, is becoming easier to tackle with new downloadable maps, updated trail sections and signage, and trekking services offering itinerary planning and luggage transfers. The Armenian section of the TCT extends from Arevik National Park in the south, past Syunik’s cave dwellings and the Tatev Monastery rising against deep river gorges, through the volcanic massifs of the Gegham Mountains, the forests of Dilijan National Park, and ending at Lake Arpi National Park near Georgia.

Meanwhile, the ANT, a 620-mile network that will eventually connect more than 100 villages, opened its first sections in June 2026 with more to come next year. Trails currently extend through Tavush and Lori, and soon Vayots Dzor, where visitors can birdwatch in alpine steppes, stay with families, and cook herb-filled meals with local hosts, while sampling lavash (Armenia's UNESCO-listed flatbread) baked in a deep, clay oven, and regional bounties like forest berries, mushrooms, cornelian cherry, and rosehip soup. Collectively, the trails offer a new way to experience Armenia's landscapes and communities on foot. —Alicia Erickson

Go for: a museum boom and a vibrant cultural milieu

History and culture run deep in Benin, and a host of new museum openings in 2027 will put both its past and present on dazzling display.

Benin is a place where history runs deep: It’s home to the ancient Kingdom of Dahomey, the spiritual traditions of Vodun, and Ouidah—one of West Africa’s significant ports in the transatlantic slave trade. Now the country is turning that history into a new era of cultural tourism, while creating ways for travelers, particularly people of African descent, to reconnect with their heritage.

Ouidah, an especially important destination for those tracing their ancestry, is central to that effort. In 2025 the government announced plans to invest $1.4 billion in tourism and cultural development, including for infrastructure, heritage restitution, and indigenous traditions. In 2027 that transformation is expected to become more visible with a host of new museum openings, like the Museum of Modern Art in Cotonou; the International Museum of Memory and Slavery in Ouidah; and the Museum of the Epic of the Amazons and Kings of Dahomey in Abomey. The latter of which is expected to house 26 royal artifacts repatriated by France in 2021.

Vodun Days, an eight-day celebration of Benin’s indigenous spiritual traditions taking place January 2–9, 2027, will showcase ceremonies, music, and dance. Meanwhile, Villa Karo in Grand-Popo—a bridge between artists and researchers from Finland, Sweden, and West Africa—will continue its residency program, which brings artists to its seaside research center to connect with the local community through workshops, concerts, and exhibitions. The famous stilt village of Ganvié, meanwhile, remains open to visitors as its Reinventing the Lakeside City of Ganvié project upgrades tourism infrastructure. Benin is also beginning to make appearances on cruise itineraries. Two new Swan Hellenic sailings in 2027 sailings feature the country, including an April voyage from Luanda to Accra and an October sailing from Ghana to Angola. —Harriet Akinyi

Go for: chef-led experiences, new Cachapoal Valley bungalows, and up-and-coming wine scenes

In 2027, Chile's wine valleys will be home to new chef-led experiences, bottles worth seeking out, and, at the country's most extravagant wine hotel, VIK, an art-filled wellness center.

Chile’s central wine valleys have long been defined by their vines—emerald rows sprawling along riverbanks, blanketing a terrain reminiscent of the Mediterranean, yet hemmed between towering Andes and a thrashing Pacific. Recently, dynamic chefs from Santiago have begun adding their touch to the region, crafting new culinary tours and elevating the work of emerging bodegas and wineries. Kurt Schmidt is among them. He first rose to fame crafting nine-course tasting menus at Santiago’s 99 Restaurante, built around the traditional foodways of Chilean viticultural regions. In 2027 he’ll be teaming up with the rural tourism cooperative Ruta de los Abastos (“The Supply Route”) on menu-to-territory day tours that will begin at 99 and expand out to wine valleys like Huasco, Maule, and Aconcagua, where Schmidt has spent years building relationships with vintners, cheesemakers, foragers, and fisherfolk (later multiday itineraries are in the works). Conveniently, Santiago lies just south of the Maipo Valley, which is famed for bold Cabernet Sauvignons and also home to the striking new Centro del Vino from Concha y Toro, Latin America’s largest wine producer. The 129,000-square-foot facility features contemporary art installations, barrel-side tasting rooms, and an interactive wine museum, plus access to manicured gardens and (for an additional fee) the 19th-century mansion of founder Don Melchor.

If you have days to spare, consider heading three hours south to the Cachapoal Valley. Here, Chile’s most extravagant wine hotel, VIK, expanded in 2026 with eight glass-mirrored bungalows and an art-filled wellness center. Five cabins are next, set to open in an oak forest on the property, where winemaker Cristián Vallejo ages natural wine in clay amphorae. Continuing south, you can explore the next generation of experimental and low-intervention vino emerging from the neighboring Maule and Itata valleys, where Millennial and Gen Z winemakers are injecting fresh energy into one of South America's oldest regions at bodegas like González Bastías and Carter Mollenhauer. Add it all up and it makes for an epic wine country road trip.

As if that weren’t enough to keep you busy, know that excellent vino isn’t the only reason to dedicate your big vacation to Chile this year. Once you wrap up in Santiago, consider hopping over to Rapa Nui (Easter Island) for some extreme set-jetting. This remote Chilean territory in the Southeastern Pacific is the backdrop of Academy Award winner Martin McDonagh’s new film, Wild Horse Nine, and it’s sure to become a rising star on more travelers’ itineraries this year. —Mark Johanson

Go for: a coveted summer destination at its sweet spot

Luxury openings, like Oku, have made Turkey's scenic Bodrum peninsula a buzzing euro-summer hotspot—with even more on the horizon.

For the past few summers, the glittering Bodrum peninsula has welcomed a wave of luxury openings, transforming this once-sleepy harbor town into one of the Aegean's most coveted corners. Aman, Mandarin Oriental, and homegrown Maçakizi were early champions of the scene and remain firmly at the heart of its appeal, but a new generation of hotels, restaurants, and beach clubs has since made Bodrum one of Europe’s most sought-after summer escapes.

Mayfair and Knightsbridge restaurant favorites Zuma, Bagatelle, and Novikov have all made the journey east in recent years, accompanied in 2026 by oh-so-chic arrivals from Dubai, Paris, and Marbella (Gaia, Gigi Rigolatto, and Maison Revka). Together they have helped recast Bodrum as a playground for the international jet set, bringing with them a distinctly cosmopolitan, see-and-be-seen energy.

This summer also brought the long-awaited arrival of the adults-only hideaway Oku, the latest in the brand's collection of design-led retreats in Ibiza, Kos, and Andalusia. Throw in annual beach-club takeovers from the likes of Jacquemus and Alo, as well as boutique openings by brands like Cartier, and it seems there’s barely a name in luxury that isn’t lining up to plant its flag here.

Enjoy it while it lasts. In 2028, all eyes will return to the peninsula as Bvlgari opens its sprawling hillside resort above yacht-speckled Paradise Bay, giving Europe's luxury travel crowd fresh reason to return. —Sophie Prideaux

Go for: wild coastal landscapes and a new Indigenous-owned luxury lodge

Adventurers have long known about Vancouver Island's wild western edge. But now, the beloved Clayoquot Wilderness Lodge (above) will be joined by the Ahousaht First Nation-owned Wahous.

Clayoquot Sound has long drawn adventurous travelers to Vancouver Island’s wild western edge—a maze of forested islands, secluded inlets, and old-growth rainforests. But now, Indigenous-owned hospitality is giving this remote luxury escape a deeper sense of place, shaped by the First Nations that have stewarded its lands and waters for generations.

A UNESCO-designated biosphere region spanning nearly 864,868 acres of land and sea, Clayoquot Sound is already home to the much-lauded Clayoquot Wilderness Lodge. But the July 2026 opening of Wahous Wilderness Lodge in secluded Quait Bay introduces a different kind of high-end stay. Owned and operated by the Ahousaht First Nation, the 16-room floating lodge and four forest cabins were reacquired in 2022 by the Nation’s hereditary chiefs through the Maaqutusiis Hahoulthee Stewardship Society (MHSS). Guests are welcomed into Ahousaht territory with tide-to-table dining, a shoreline spa, and a ceremonial welcome in the cedar longhouse. A stay begins and ends in the longhouse, concluding with a traditional klup-chus-tapčas, or salmon barbecue, in an evening that brings together food, storytelling, and cultural exchange in a final gathering rooted in Ahousaht tradition.

The three-Michelin-key Clayoquot Wilderness Lodge is evolving too. Set on 600 acres at the mouth of the Bedwell River, the off-grid retreat is known for its 25 safari-style canvas tents, cedar-plank boardwalks, and wilderness adventures. Newer experiences in 2026 include after-dark bioluminescence kayaking, a floating sauna, and a guided walk across Flores Island through Ahousaht territory.

New experiences and places to stay aside, Clayoquot Sound remains remote enough to preserve its deep sense of seclusion. This vast coastal labyrinth is best explored by water and on foot: Paddle between forested islands, walk beneath ancient cedars on Meares Island, hike Flores Island’s remote Wildside Trail, or soak in the geothermal pools at Hot Springs Cove. From March to October, join a whale-watching trip to spot orcas, humpbacks, or grey whales. Elsewhere, wildlife may simply find you: Black bears forage along the shoreline at low tide, and sea otters paddle through the inlets. Some excursions can be arranged directly through the lodges, while Tofino-based operators, such as Ahousaht-owned Ahous Adventures or Tofino Water Taxi, make the wider sound accessible. —Ashleigh Stewart

Go for: paradise islands, ancient history, and a new five-star stay

Crystal-clear turquoise waters and white sandbars of the Aitutaki Lagoon are just part of what makes the Cook Islands the type of hideaway we're seeking in 2027.

The Cook Islands’ 15 isles stipple roughly 849,000 square miles of the South Pacific, but total less than 100 square miles of land. Most first trips center on a southern pair of islands: Rarotonga and Aitutaki. On the one-hour propeller flight between two, the entire country arrives in flashes: a volcanic ridge inside a white reef, miles of open Pacific, then a blue lagoon with motu gathered along its rim. Rarotonga’s 20-mile coast road threads between reef and pleated peaks, while Aitutaki is defined by its broad lagoon.

On the west side of the island Rarotonga, a narrow road climbs from the lagoon into Maungaroa Valley, where a canopy of multi-trunked mato trees covers the remains of a centuries-old settlement. Taken together, the surviving sites form a cultural landscape that will appear before UNESCO’s World Heritage Committee in 2027 as the Cook Islands’ first nomination. At Highland Paradise, a cultural site within the old settlement and managed by descendants of Chief Tinomana, the Tapu’ae Day Tour follows those paths past chiefly house sites and sacred ground as guides explain how the stonework organized life here.

On Aitutaki, customary stewardship extends well below the waterline. In 2025 three lagoon zones came under ra’ui, a traditional temporary closure that allows coral and fish populations to recover, with protections scheduled to rotate in 2027. Aitutaki Coral Restoration takes small groups beyond the usual motu-and-sandbar circuit to its underwater nursery and a planting site, where guests clean a dead coral base and secure a naturally broken fragment. Part of each tour fee supports the restoration work.

Tourism is growing alongside the conservation push. In March 2027 a new, locally owned hotel, Grand Resort, billing itself as the island’s first five-star hotel, is scheduled to open with 61 adults-only suites on Rarotonga’s west coast. The Destination Stewardship Plan 2026–2030, meanwhile, shifts the focus from mainly growing tourism to managing its impact with protections for culture and the environment with a greater share flowing to island communities. —Paul Jebara

Go for: rugged landscapes and cinematic coastlines

Hotel Basgi Basgi in the north of Corsica is drawing an increasingly design-conscious crowd.

In 2027, the Orient Express Corinthian yacht will call on Corsica's spectacular coastline.

When Simon Jacquemus chose Phare de la Pietra, a 19th-century lighthouse on a rocky Corsican peninsula, as the backdrop for his Spring 2027 fashion show this summer, it was easy to see why. The designer, long inspired by the interplay of place, light, and raw emotion, described his show Le Bonheur (meaning “happiness” or “bliss”) as “a cliff that I add colors on top. An impressionist painting.”

It's true. Corsica is a place where cinematic elegance meets rugged natural beauty. It's why the island was once named Kallisté, meaning “most beautiful,” by the ancient Greeks. Here, dramatic mountains meet wild hinterlands and fiery red rockfaces jut against turquoise waters, making for breathtaking hiking through UNESCO-listed landscapes such as the Scandola Nature Reserve.

The island long slipped under the radar compared with neighboring Sardinia and the French Riviera. But Jacquemus’ arrival, coupled with a flurry of recent smart hotel openings, suggests that may not be the case for much longer. Leading the charge is A Mandria di Murtoli, a 10-room retreat spread across five restored stone buildings in the Ortolo Valley, which is a little sister to epicurean Domaine de Murtoli, where farm-to-table living is the order of the day. Elsewhere, Hotel Mylos on the west coast and Basgi Basgi in the north are drawing an increasingly design-conscious crowd.

Adding to the island’s momentum is the maiden voyage of the Orient Express Corinthian, a sailing yacht with 2027 itineraries calling at Bonifacio, Calvi, and Saint-Florent, opening up Corsica's fishing ports, medieval citadels, and spectacular coastline to a new wave of travelers. —Ashleigh Spiliopoulou

Go for: a coolcation destination with Michelin status

The Danish Riviera is a 60-mile stretch of fishing villages and sandy beaches that still remains under the international radar.

Many of the region's charming seaside hotels date back to the late 19th century—but in 2027, there will be plenty of fresh energy, too.

With rising summer temperatures across Europe, the Danish Riviera has emerged as the continent's favorite coastal coolcation destination. This 60-mile coastal stretch in Northern Zealand brings travelers the aesthetic appeal and culinary credibility of Copenhagen in a relaxed setting of fishing villages, sandy beaches, forests, and historical seaside resorts that remain largely undiscovered by international visitors.

The Louisiana Museum of Modern Art in Humlebæk marks the southern end of the riviera, but many visitors staying in Copenhagen don't venture any further north to seaside towns. Many of the region's charming seaside hotels date back to the late 19th century. But a new generation of stays is rejuvenating the landscape with contemporary design. Cori in Hornbæk brings barefoot energy to a beautifully repurposed former hospital, and Hôtel de Ville in Gilleleje transforms an old town hall into an eclectic boutique spa hotel. MasterChef Denmark judge Thomas Castberg recently opened Hotel Villa Lejet in a restored 19th-century building with modern summerhouse vibes and sharing-style Nordic dining.

Exceptional gastronomy abounds, with Michelin-starred standouts such as Jordnær, The Samuel, and Parsley Salon representing New Nordic Cuisine at its finest. For something more casual, recently opened Betty Beach combines beachfront dining, street food, and a brewery for sun-kissed imbibing. Denmark's first official wine route launched last year in North Zealand and Odsherred, with nine wineries offering tours and tastings of cold-climate grape varietals such as Solaris and Rondo. —Amber Gibson

Go for: wild Austen-esque adventures and spa stays galore

The stately Boringdon Hall Hotel and Spa captures the Austen-esque allure of Dartmoor.

Countryside dining is hardly simple at Àclèaf, the Michelin-starred restaurant in Boringdon Hall Hotel and Spa.

Dartmoor has always lent itself to drama. Wild ponies pick their way through heather, granite tors loom out of the mist, and the ever-changing weather plays a starring role. Now it’s getting its close-up as the backdrop of Georgia Oakley’s new Sense and Sensibility adaptation in theaters in September 2026. The film was shot around Princetown, Tavistock, and Gutter Tor, and its release coincides with Dartmoor National Park’s 75th anniversary. As the Austen connection pulls focus to Dartmoor, 2027 is the year to stay a little longer and explore a little further.

It’s unusually special in the depths of winter, when the weather gets wild. Misty mornings walking the ancient tracks and climbing tors, afternoons yomping across moorland in conditions that demand retreating somewhere cozy for a hot bath and a roaring fire. For a hands-on experience, book one of the free conservation days running on selected Sundays in 2027 to learn more about Dartmoor’s prehistoric archaeology, wildlife, and rare species, while helping to preserve its future. Don’t miss Tavistock, the old market town on Dartmoor’s western edge. Its handsome Pannier Market is bursting with antiques and curiosities, ideal for a spot of treasure hunting. For a change of pace, visit 11th-century Buckfast Abbey, where the Benedictine monks still make their famous tonic wine to a secret recipe.

To feel like a main character in your own period drama, book a room at Boringdon Hall. This show-stopping historical hotel on the edge of the moor combines roaring fires and old-world grandeur with one of Devon’s best spas. Its Michelin-starred Àclèaf restaurant retained its star in 2026, a quietly heroic achievement in a world obsessed with shiny new openings. There is nothing like spending the wildest part of the year getting wind-beaten on Dartmoor, then retreating for a spa treatment, proper grub, and a fire. —Zoe Cook

Go for: new conservation-minded cruises, stargazing experiences, and an endless parade of wildlife

It's been a busy couple years for National Geographic–Lindblad Expeditions, who celebrate their 60th anniversary of taking travelers to the Galápagos Islands in 2026, and will launch a new Ultimate Galápagos 13-day itinerary in January 2027.

The Galápagos Islands, an archipelago of 13 major isles and over 100 rocks and inlets off Ecuador’s coast, have long attracted travelers seeking solitude and wildlife: endemic giant tortoises and marine iguanas, native scalloped hammerhead sharks, and blue-footed boobies, for starters. Nearly 97% of the Galápagos’ land area is a single national park. Visitor access remains tightly managed, making the destination one of the rare places where travelers can experience wildlife on nature’s terms (and, as scientists are learning, occasionally encounter new species, like the deep-sea Microeledone galapagensis, or blue octopus). This year National Geographic–Lindblad Expeditions celebrated 60 years since Lars-Eric Lindblad introduced travelers to the Galápagos on the first non-scientific civilian expedition voyage, establishing the islands as a premier destination for conservation-minded exploration. The brand doubled its Galápagos fleet in 2025 by adding the 16-guest catamaran Delfina and the 48-guest Gemini. In January 2027 the brand will launch Ultimate Galápagos, a 13-day itinerary aboard the 96-passenger Endeavour II that visits 11 islands including remote Genovesa, known for its red-footed booby colonies. Elsewhere, Natural Habitat Adventures’ Galápagos Tortoise Camp just transitioned to 100% solar power and introduced the Galápagos’ first fully electric panga (a small skiff for excursions)—part of trips booked aboard the brand’s Petrel luxury catamaran—reducing emissions for wildlife excursions and setting a new benchmark for sustainable travel in the destination. —Hannah Selinger

Go for: volcano trekking, peak birdwatching, price points that range from shoestring to big spender

Guatemala, with its dramatic volcanos (like the above, Volcán de Fuego), rich heritage, and artful craft traditions, will be trending in 2027 for good reason.

Long a backpacking destination on the Central America 101 route known as the Gringo Trail, Guatemala is an increasingly popular destination for a generation of young travelers seeking slower, more enriching tourism that won’t break the bank. INGUAT, the Guatemala Institute of Tourism, says that arrival numbers support the TikTok thesis—and are only projected to increase. Some of the appeal is timeless: Guatemalan day-to-day life is deeply steeped in ancient heritage and culture. Sprawling artisan markets like Nim Po’t in Antigua offer easy access to hand-carved and painted wood animal masks, for example, but deeper dives are offered by Antigua-based textile studio Luna Zorro, which organizes craft retreats throughout the country. The brand’s recently opened La Valiente (February 2026) is a two-bedroom guesthouse in the heart of the city, and almost everything at the property—from the bedding to ceramics—is artisan-made by local creators. The country’s natural beauty is also an evergreen draw, but as younger generations embrace the mores—and aesthetics—of hiking (and even birdwatching), trails like the rigorous two-day Acatenango trek are list-toppers in the central highland region that is ripe with some of the 780 bird species recorded in Guatemala. Many choose to continue to the Tikal National Park in the northeast, where 3,000-year-old ruins of the Maya city sit amid a lush green jungle setting. At the park’s recently reopened Conservation and Research Center, visitors can observe the behind-the-scenes preservation process of ruins and artifacts, only now visible to the public. It all makes for welcome news that Guatemala is getting even easier to access: Frontier is offering daily, seasonal, nonstop service from Los Angeles to Guatemala City from December 17 until January 4, if you need a last-minute holiday getaway, and United Airlines has a permanent direct route from Houston. —Anna Zou

Go for: the Pink City’s 300th anniversary

In 2027, Jaipur will turn 300 years old, and a slate of events will celebrate the historic milestone.

In 1727, Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II established Jaipur as the capital of his kingdom, envisioning it as a financial and artistic hub. As the city marks its 300th year in 2027, the City Palace—the current residence of the erstwhile royals—readies for the occasion with new exhibits across its galleries, as well as its private spaces not usually accessible to the public. Traces of Us, an exhibit by the Jaipur Centre for Art, marks the anniversary with contemporary works by artists like M.F. Husain and Bharti Kher displayed across the palace. A terrace pavilion once used for an audience with the maharaja is now the stylish, open-air restaurant the Sarvato, where Sawai Padmanabh Singh partners with hotelier Abhishek Honawar to showcase a modern, six-course menu championing both the everyday and the celebratory food of Rajasthan, served with a spectacular view of Jaipur.

Honawar, best known for his boutique Michelin Key property the Johri, a restored 19th-century haveli in the Old City, added three more suites this year to the hotel’s original five keys. Set to open later in 2026 is Hawa Haveli, an eight-key stay with a restaurant and cocktail bar, housed in a 19th-century merchant mansion—another intimate boutique property from the same team.

For three days in December 2026, the Jaigarh Heritage Festival brings folk artists and craftspeople to the 11th-century Jaigarh Fort to celebrate the city’s 300th anniversary, while the Jaipur Literature Festival in January brings the stalwarts of the literary world to the Pink City.

A themed walk, Jaipur 300: A City of Craft and Courtyards, led by Neeraj Chauhan of Jaipurnama, traces three centuries of living heritage. And to carry a piece of Jaipur back with you, the Jaipur Watch Company has released two special timepieces inspired by the City Palace’s beautiful Leheriya Gate and Peacock Gate. —Shalbha Sarda

Go for: an off-the-beaten-path, community-focused escape—and a chance to support tourism’s recovery after Hurricane Melissa.

One year after Hurricane Melissa hit Jamaica, the island is ready to welcome visitors back.

The beloved family-run Jakes Hotel in Treasure Beach reopened in 2026, along with many of its neighbors.

This October marks the one-year anniversary of Hurricane Melissa, the strongest and costliest hurricane to ever hit Jamaica. With most of the damage centered in the west and southwest of the island, Jamaica’s South Coast has been at the forefront of recovery efforts—and is now welcoming travelers back with open arms.

Jamaica is so much more than reggae and resorts, and there’s never been a better time for more meaningful travel to one of its most under-the-radar coastal regions. The best-known South Coast area, Treasure Beach, is a mellow seaside town known for its black sand beaches and slow-paced bohemian lifestyle. Every other year, it’s also host to the Calabash International Literary Festival, the largest in the Caribbean (in 2027 it will be held in May). The beaches here are lined with colorful guesthouses and design-forward boutique stays like Jakes Hotel, a beloved family-run establishment founded by local artist Sally Henzell. Further up the coast, Bluefields Bay Villas & Suites is another family-run gem that reopened in February 2026. For a resort stay, Sandals South Coast will reopen in November 2026 following a multimillion-dollar renovation.

It’s also become easier for travelers to pair the beachy South Coast with the lush hills of St. Elizabeth Parish—Jamaica’s breadbasket. The island’s agriculture industry took the biggest hit from the hurricane, prompting Jakes Hotel to double down on its agro-tourism offerings. In March, the hotel launched farm tours and monthly full-moon farm-to-table dinners for guests; its nonprofit arm also provides direct cash grants and equipment vouchers to farmers in the region.

For travelers planning a longer trip, the drive between Negril and Treasure Beach is one of Jamaica’s most scenic. Along the way, stop at the natural swimming pools at YS Falls, sample Jamaican street food beneath the shaded archway of Bamboo Avenue (don’t skip Middle Quarters’ famous peppered shrimp), and take a dip in the turquoise waters of Bluefields Bay. If you have a few days to spare, continue driving east to Kingston, the urban epicenter of Jamaican nightlife and culture. —Hannah Towey

Go for: a ground-breaking cross-border safari project and birding adventure

Anantara Tented Camp, along the Kafue River, is one of several swanky stays in the new cross-border KAZA conservation area.

In a world where ecological degradation and global discord are reasons to despair, Kavango-Zambezi Transfrontier Conservation Area, or KAZA TFCA, is more rising sun than ray of hope.
