How the UEFA Nations League works: promotion, relegation and Euro routes

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Free daily briefing on global business news.

UEFA Nations League promotion and relegation decide where Europe's national teams play, and the results also open routes to the Euros and World Cup

The UEFA Nations League gives Europe's national teams a tiered competition in which results carry consequences. A strong campaign can lift a team into a higher league, and a weak one can drop it down. Results can also open a route to major tournaments.

UEFA's Executive Committee approved the competition in 2014, and the first edition was played in 2018-19. Teams are sorted into four tiers, League A through League D, according to UEFA rankings. League A holds the strongest national teams and League D the weakest. In the 2024-25 edition, Leagues A, B and C each had 16 teams, split into groups of four.

UEFA Nations League promotion and relegation follow from that tiered structure. A team that wins its group in League B or League C moves up a tier for the next edition. A team that finishes fourth in a League A or League B group drops down one tier. Third-placed teams in those two leagues face two-legged play-offs against runners-up from the tier below. A place in the higher league is at stake.

League A carries its own prize. Since the 2024-25 edition, the top two teams in each League A group have advanced to two-legged quarter-finals. The four winners then meet at a finals tournament in a single host country. Portugal, France and Spain have all lifted the trophy, and Portugal has won it twice.

The results also reach beyond the Nations League itself. For Euro 2024, play-offs gave Nations League group winners that missed out in the main qualifying groups a second route to the European Championship. Georgia, Ukraine and Poland all reached Euro 2024 that way. For the 2026 World Cup, four Nations League group winners that finished outside the top two of their qualifying groups received places in UEFA's play-offs.

They then explain how teams move between tiers and how the competition links to Euro and World Cup qualifying. The final slide lists every winner from 2019 to 2025.

UEFA created the Nations League to replace many low-stakes international friendlies with competitive matches between national teams of similar strength. UEFA's Executive Committee approved the competition in 2014, and the first edition was played in the 2018-19 season.

Before the competition began, friendlies filled many of the dates reserved for national-team football outside tournament qualifying. A friendly result earns no points, no trophy and no qualification place. Nothing carries over beyond the match itself.

That absence of consequence shaped how the games were used. Coaches could treat them as a chance to try fringe players or new tactics. Opponents could be picked for convenience rather than competitive balance. That sometimes left a strong side facing a much weaker one.

The Nations League takes over many of those calendar slots and attaches stakes to them. Each match feeds into a group table. Final positions carry consequences for the next edition of the competition. In some cases they also affect qualification for major tournaments.

Matching teams of similar strength is the other half of the design. UEFA does not throw every European national team into a single pool. It sorts them into tiers using its own rankings. A team therefore plays its group matches against opponents near its own level.

That structure changes what an international break offers at both ends of the scale. A smaller football nation gets competitive games it has a realistic chance of winning. A leading side gets regular meetings with other leading sides, with something riding on the result.

The Nations League did not end friendlies altogether. National teams still arrange them, particularly as warm-up matches before major tournaments.

It also did not replace the qualifying campaigns for the European Championship and the World Cup. Those campaigns continue, and the Nations League runs alongside them as a separate competition.

UEFA places each national team in the Nations League into one of four tiers — League A, League B, League C or League D — based on its UEFA ranking. League A contains the strongest sides, and League D contains the weakest.

The letters run from the top down. A team's letter reflects its standing among Europe's national sides at the start of an edition.

Ranking-based tiers put teams of similar strength in the same league. That follows UEFA's aim of matching national sides against opponents close to their own level.

The top three tiers were the same size in the 2024-25 edition. Leagues A, B and C each had 16 teams that season. That put 48 national sides in those three tiers combined.

League placement tells fans what level of opposition a side will meet. A team in League A plays other top-ranked nations. A team in League C or League D plays nations closer to its own standing, not the strongest sides on the continent.

The assignment is not permanent. Results in one edition decide which league a team enters for the next. The rankings set the starting order, and the matches reshape it over time.

Every team in Leagues A, B and C begins the Nations League in a group of four. Each side plays its three group opponents twice, once at home and once away, for six group matches in all.

The double round means no pairing is settled by one game at one team's ground. Each team hosts every opponent once and visits every opponent once. That gives every side an even split of three home matches and three away matches. Home advantage is therefore shared equally across the group.

A group of four contains six distinct pairings. With each pairing played twice, every group stages 12 matches.

Those 12 matches fit into six matchdays. On each matchday, all four teams in a group are in action across two games. No team sits out a round while its rivals play.

League A shows how those numbers scale. In the 2024-25 edition, League A was divided into four groups. Each League A team met three other League A sides home and away. Together, the four League A groups staged 48 matches.

Leagues B and C used the same layout in 2024-25, with four groups each. That made 12 groups across the top three leagues. Those 12 groups accounted for 144 group matches between them.

Because each league is a single tier, all three of a team's group opponents come from the same level of the competition. A League A side does not meet a League C side in the group stage.

Every team in a group finishes with the same number of games played. No side ends the group stage with a game in hand. The final standings compare teams on equal terms.

The final group table decides what happens next. A team's finishing position can send it forward, up or down, and the consequences differ by league.

League A teams compete for the UEFA Nations League title through a knockout bracket. Since the 2024-25 edition, the top two finishers in each League A group advance to two-legged quarter-finals. The four winners move on to a finals tournament hosted by one country.

League A is the top tier of the competition, and only its teams can win the title. Its four groups each send their top two forward, producing eight quarter-finalists. Those eight teams meet in four ties.

Each quarter-final is played over two legs. Every team plays once at home and once away. The aggregate score across both matches decides who advances. That gives each side a home fixture before the competition moves to one host nation.

The four quarter-final winners then travel to the Nations League Finals. This stage is a short tournament held in one country. It includes two semi-finals and a final, and the winner of the final takes the trophy.

The current bracket replaced a simpler route. Before 2024-25, only the four League A group winners qualified for the finals. There were no quarter-finals. Finishing second in a League A group therefore ended a team's chance of winning the title.

The change doubled the number of teams with a path to the trophy, from four to eight. A runner-up can now reach the finals without winning its group. A group winner no longer goes straight through. It has to win a two-legged tie first.

The format also lengthens the campaign for the strongest sides. On top of its group fixtures, a side that reaches the final plays four more matches: two quarter-final legs, a semi-final and the final itself.

Group position in League A therefore carries two kinds of stakes. The top two in each group chase the trophy through the knockout rounds. The bottom two play for their place in the top tier, through direct relegation or a play-off.

Group winners in League B and League C are promoted. Each one moves up a single tier for the next edition of the UEFA Nations League.

The step is always one league. A League B group winner joins League A, the top tier. A League C group winner joins League B.

No team can skip a tier. A side starting in League C needs to win its group in two consecutive editions to reach League A. The first group win lifts it to League B. The second lifts it to the top tier.

Only first place brings automatic promotion. A runner-up in either league does not go up directly, and neither does a third-placed team.

League B and League C each contained four groups in the 2024-25 edition. That meant four teams earned direct promotion from League B and four from League C, eight in all.

The reward is a higher level of opposition. A side promoted from League B spends its next campaign in League A, against the highest-ranked national teams in the competition. A side promoted from League C moves into the tier directly below League A.

Promotion to League A also changes what a team can win. Only League A teams can reach the quarter-finals and the finals tournament. A side that climbs out of League B becomes eligible for the trophy in its first season in the top tier.

The system means a team's league is not set by its ranking alone. Group results in one edition decide where it plays in the next.

The team that finishes fourth in each League A group drops straight to League B for the next edition. The same rule applies one level down: fourth place in a League B group means automatic relegation to League C.

Fourth place is the bottom of the group. In the 2024-25 edition, every group in Leagues A and B had four teams. Each side played the other three home and away, for six matches. A team that finishes last after those matches does not enter the promotion and relegation play-offs. Its move to the lower tier is settled once the group stage ends.

League A had four groups in 2024-25. That meant four League A teams went down directly. League B also had four groups, which sent four more teams from League B to League C. Across the top two tiers, direct relegation covered eight teams in that edition.

Relegation from League A carries a specific cost. League A holds the strongest national teams under UEFA's ranking-based placement. It is also the only tier that plays for the Nations League title. A team that drops to League B has no route to the quarter-finals or the finals tournament in the following edition. It must first win promotion back before it can contest the trophy again.

Relegation from League B to League C is a drop to the third of four tiers. The relegated team then plays opponents from a lower tier in its next campaign.

Relegation does not have to last long. A relegated team that wins its group in the lower league is promoted for the next edition. That means a side can return to its old tier after a single campaign.

Direct relegation applies only to fourth place. Third-placed teams in Leagues A and B are not relegated automatically. They play two-legged play-offs against runners-up from the tier below. The winner of each tie takes the place in the higher league.

The Nations League settles some movement between its top three tiers through play-offs. Third-placed teams in League A face League B runners-up, and third-placed teams in League B face League C runners-up. Each tie runs over two legs, with a place in the higher league at stake.

These ties sit between the automatic outcomes. Group winners in League B and League C move up without extra matches. Fourth-placed teams in League A and League B go straight down. The teams in between get either a last chance to stay up or an extra route to climb.

For a League A side, finishing third means it has to defend its place. If it wins the tie, it stays in League A for the next edition. If it loses, the League B runner-up takes its spot, and the League A team drops a level.

The same structure applies one tier lower. A League B team that finishes third must beat a League C runner-up to remain in League B. If the League C side wins instead, it is promoted, and the League B team moves down.

League B is the only tier involved in both sets of ties. Its runners-up play upward against League A opposition. Its third-placed teams play downward against League C opposition. As a result, no finishing position in a League B group keeps a team in League B automatically. The winner moves up, the fourth-placed side drops, and the two teams in between go into play-offs.

League A is arranged differently at the top. In the current format, its first- and second-placed teams advance to the quarter-finals. Their place in the top tier is not in question. Only the bottom two in each League A group face any risk of dropping.

A League C runner-up has two ways to reach League B. It can win its group outright, or it can finish second and then beat a League B side over two legs.

The two-legged format gives each team one home match and one away match. The outcome depends on the combined result across both games, not on a single match.

Nations League results give national teams a second path to the European Championship. A team that misses out through the main qualifying groups can still reach the tournament through play-offs filled on the basis of Nations League performance.

Group winners get first claim on those play-off places. When a group winner has already qualified through its group, its place passes to another team based on Nations League results.

The main qualifying groups remain the primary way into the European Championship. The play-offs come into play only for teams that miss out there. A team's Nations League campaign decides whether it gets that second chance.

For Euro 2024, which Germany hosted, the 2022-23 Nations League set the play-off field. Twelve teams entered three separate paths. Each path had one-off semi-finals and a final, and each path winner took one of the tournament's 24 places.

Georgia beat Greece on penalties in its path final in Tbilisi after a 0-0 draw. That result sent Georgia to its first major tournament. Both teams had reached the play-offs as League C group winners.

Ukraine won another path final, beating Iceland 2-1 in Wroclaw, Poland. Ukraine played that home tie in Poland because of Russia's invasion.

Poland beat Wales on penalties in Cardiff after a 0-0 draw to take the third place. Each of the three winners reached Euro 2024 after falling short in the main qualifying competition.

Two of the three deciding matches — Georgia against Greece and Poland against Wales — went to penalty shootouts. In both, a full European Championship place rested on a single shootout.

The route also changes what a poor qualifying campaign means for a team. Finishing outside the automatic places in a qualifying group does not end a team's Euro bid if its Nations League results earned a play-off spot. The two competitions run as separate tracks. A strong result in one can make up for a weak result in the other.

Nations League results carried over into qualifying for the 2026 World Cup. Four Nations League group winners that did not finish in the top two of their World Cup qualifying groups received places in UEFA's play-offs.

The rule gave those teams a fallback after their main qualifying campaigns fell short. A finish below second place in a qualifying group would otherwise have ended their chances. A Nations League group title kept them in contention instead.

The route depended on how a team's qualifying group ended. Teams that finished in the top two of their qualifying groups did not use it. A Nations League group title mattered most to teams whose qualifying campaigns went poorly.

The 2026 World Cup is co-hosted by the U.S., Canada and Mexico. It is the first edition with 48 teams, up from 32 at the 2022 tournament in Qatar. UEFA's share of that field is 16 places.

The link gave Nations League matches weight beyond the competition's own standings. A single group game could shape a team's chances of reaching a World Cup. That stake sat alongside the promotion and relegation consequences of the same result.

A play-off place does not guarantee a spot at the finals. Teams that enter through the Nations League must still come through the play-offs to qualify.

The World Cup rule mirrors the route UEFA already offered for European Championship qualifying. In both cases, Nations League form serves as a safety net when main qualifying falls short.

Portugal has won the UEFA Nations League twice, in 2019 and 2025. France and Spain have each won it once, and Spain has played in more finals than any other country.

Portugal took the first title in 2019 with a 1-0 win over the Netherlands in Porto. The first champion won the trophy in one of its own cities.

France claimed the 2021 title. It beat Spain 2-1 in a final played in the Italian city of Milan.

Spain won in 2023 after its final against Croatia in Rotterdam went to penalties. That title came two years after Spain lost the 2021 final to France.

Portugal added its second title in 2025. The final against Spain in Munich finished 2-2, and Portugal won the shootout. No other country has won the Nations League more than once.

Spain has played in three of the four finals, in 2021, 2023 and 2025. It won one and lost two, to France and then Portugal. That makes Spain the only country to lose more than one Nations League final.

The Netherlands and Croatia have each reached one final and lost it. The Netherlands fell to Portugal in 2019. Croatia lost to Spain in 2023.

Penalties settled two of the four finals: Spain against Croatia in 2023 and Portugal against Spain in 2025. The other two were decided by a single goal. Portugal won 1-0 in 2019, and France won 2-1 in 2021.

Each final so far has been played in a different country. The hosts run from Portugal in 2019 to Italy in 2021, the Netherlands in 2023 and Germany in 2025. Portugal is the only host nation to win the final on home soil.

Every champion has come from League A. It is the only tier whose teams can reach the finals tournament.
