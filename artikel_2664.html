Detroit Lions grades: Offense, defense, coaching all take blame in loss to Bears

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Let's hand out some grades for the Detroit Lions' 39-35 loss to the Chicago Bears on Thanksgiving.

Jared Goff got off to a slow start against the Chicago Bears and missed on a couple of early throws. But, my goodness, he was absolutely on fire from the end of the second quarter on. In the second half alone, he was 12-of-14 to 120 yards and an interception that wasn't really his fault. He was hitting on just about every kind of throw: deep posts, comebacks, crossers, seam shots. Goff was going toe-to-toe with Caleb Williams and, man, it was fun to watch until it wasn't.

Jahmyr Gibbs may have wrapped up Offensive Player of the Year honors with his 252 scrimmage yard performance that also included three trips to the end zone.

Gibbs finished with 114 rushing yards over expected in this game—a single-game high for any running back this year—averaging +5.4 RYOE per rush (second-best among all RB games this year). I've run out of things to say about Gibbs. He's electric, and we're all lucky to be watching him on a weekly basis.

Sam LaPorta's touchdown grab at the end of the first half was damn impressive and long overdue. He finished the game with four catches for 44 yards and that score. It was a quieter day from the other tight ends, but Detroit found some success in the run game with 12 personnel, so they deserve credit for doing some of the grunt work.

Amon-Ra St. Brown was torching the Bears' secondary for most of the game, while also making some ridiculous contested catches. He posted a +22.7% catch rate over expected, his best output of the year. His seven catches for 146 yards were second-most he's had this year and seventh most in his career.

The rest of the crew was relatively quiet, and Jameson Williams deserves at least part of the blame for the interception, even if it was tipped before it got to him. He did have a couple of deep routes that he beat the defender, including one Goff hit him for a 39-yard gain, but the interception proved too costly to ignore.

If this were based just on play on the field, the offensive line may have earned an A+. The Bears registered just 13 pressures per PFF (for comparison's sake, the Lions had 27), and the Lions were creating gaping holes in the run game. Jeffery Simmons was shockingly not much of a factor in this game, outside of some games he was playing.

Unfortunately, I have to drop this to an A-, because of the penalties. Forced or unforced, four false start penalties are unacceptable in a home game, and Christian Mahogany's petty taunting penalty cost the Lions a valuable point.

Derrick Moore and Alim McNeill were absolute game wreckers in the first half. The two combined for three sacks, and Moore's two forced fumbles were the biggest reason Detroit had a two-score lead at halftime.

Unfortunately, the magic disappeared in the second half. Caleb Williams had plenty of time to go through his progressions in the second half, leading to 218 passing yards and two touchdowns in the second half alone.

Additionally, the Lions run defense took a massive step back. The Bears finished with 146 yards, three touchdowns, and a ridiculous 61% success rate running the football.

It was a brutal day from the linebackers, who consistently lost track of running backs and tight ends on play-action rollouts, or route combinations designed to create conflict. Here's how PFF credited each linebacker in coverage:

Malcolm Rodriguez: 6 catches, 8 targets, 103 yards allowed (116.7 passer rating)Trevor Nowaske: 1 catch, 1 target, 12 yards (116.7)Derrick Barnes: 3 catches, 4 targets, 18 yards, 1 PBU (83.3)K.J. Britt: 3 catches, 3 targets, 33 yards (112.5)

They, too, shared their blame in the run game woes.

I thought it was another strong game from Roger McCreary, who tallied three passes defended and wasn't credited with a single allowed catch. Brian Branch and Kerby Joseph made some strong plays in the run game, as well.

Unfortunately, the other two starters in the secondary—Rock Ya-Sin and Andre Cisco—were huge liabilities out there. Ya-Sin, who took full blame for the performance after the game, was credited with five catches allowed for 65 yards and both touchdowns. Cisco was more of an issue in the run game, getting caught out of position several times.

Jake Bates wasn't at his best, missing a long extra point and putting a few kickoffs into the end zone. Jack Fox was decent, but one of his punts landed several yards deep into the end zone. There was nothing noteworthy from the return or coverage teams, so just an average overall day.

There is so much going on with coaching that I'm going to break things down into sections:

Forgoing the late field goal to go for it — It seems counterintuitive, but in a lot of ways, being up three is better than six late in the game. Being up six means the offense will do everything they can to score a touchdown and win the game. Being up three means they will settle for a field goal, if necessary. On top of that, all analytical models were decisively in favor of the decision. And with the defense struggling and the offense rolling, I have zero problem with the decision. The play call was pretty good, too. You just have to credit T.J. Edwards for making a great open-field tackle on Gibbs.

Taking the safety — At the time, I wasn't sure what I'd do. It's a tough, unique decision to either take the ball at Chicago's 10-yard line or take the safety and the ball. Ultimately, I think it was the right decision because if Detroit had taken the ball, they'd likely score 3 or 7. But there was plenty of time left for Chicago to eat into that lead, because they had all three timeouts. Taking the safety and the ball pretty much ensured you were going to be, at worst +2 at the end of the half, +9 at the best. It worked out optimally in Detroit's favor.

Throwing on second down — Many believed facing a second-and-8 late in the game, Detroit should have continued to run the ball, force the Bears to use their timeouts, and grind out a late win. I don't mind prioritizing getting the first down over running clock, and the pass game was working pretty well. That said, the Lions were also moving the ball pretty damn well on the ground, and it's hard to stomach not giving Gibbs an opportunity to put it away (although he got his opportunity on fourth down).

Timeout usage on defense — Many believe Campbell was being reckless with his timeout usage, giving the Bears enough time to make the game-winning score. Campbell justified his decision by saying he was hoping to have enough time to get the ball back. Ultimately, I think Campbell was damned if he did, damned if he didn't. The fact of the matter is the Bears got the ball with 2:17 left and a timeout. They had plenty of time to score, and they would've adjusted their tempo no matter how Detroit used their timeouts.

Passing on third-and-1 — At the Bears' 36-yard line up three points with 6:13 left, the Lions opted to run a play-action pass on third-and-1. It was intercepted. Detroit had gotten so good recently with these short-yardage runs, whether it was a Goff sneak or Sione Vaki run, but they got too cute here, and it was a huge mistake.

I still think there was more good than bad here, but there's no doubt that coaching had an impact on this one.
