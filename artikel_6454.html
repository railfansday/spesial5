Crocodiles outlived the dinosaurs, and a 250-million-year-old habit may explain why

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Warm regions near the equator may have helped crocodile relatives survive the asteroid impact that wiped out the non-avian dinosaurs.

When an asteroid struck Earth 66 million years ago, the non-avian dinosaurs died out. Some crocodile relatives survived.

A new study suggests their long association with warm climates may have helped them weather the aftermath.

Alfio Alessandro Chiarenza and Philip Mannion of UCL Earth Sciences led the work.

The evidence is computer models of past climate matched against fossils, so the maps show where the weather suited the animals, not how they lived.

“The asteroid did not make the whole planet equally hostile to every group,” Chiarenza said.

Crocodiles come from a line older and stranger than the animals on riverbanks today.

Its extinct relatives included armored plant eaters, land-dwelling predators that could move on two legs, and giants weighing more than 2,200 pounds (1,000 kilograms). The living ones look alike by comparison.

Chiarenza’s team gathered more than 7,000 fossil records covering over 400 of those species, living and extinct. They added about 130,000 records of where the 26 living species occur today.

The team compared all of it with 52 reconstructions of Earth’s climate and geography reaching back 245 million years.

Through every warm spell and cold snap, the group’s preferred climate rarely changed. The animals went where that climate was.

About 50 million years ago, the planet was roughly 18°F (10°C) warmer than today and the poles had no ice. Crocodile relatives lived far to the north in North America and Europe, and in Antarctica.

As the world cooled again, their range shrank back toward the tropics.

Rainfall turned out to be an even tighter rule than temperature.

In the models, a lineage that drifted from its usual rainfall returned to it more than four times faster than one that drifted from its usual warmth.

To test what the asteroid did to that habit, the researchers simulated the decades and centuries after the impact 66 million years ago.

They dimmed the sun by 5%, 10% and 15% to mimic dust blocking sunlight. They added layers of ash, raised carbon dioxide to mimic the volcanic eruptions underway in India at the time, and combined several of those at once.

Then they asked where a crocodile could still have lived. In every scenario but one, warm regions near the equator and in the subtropics stayed suitable.

Only a 15% drop in sunlight eliminated suitable climate everywhere, and the authors wrote that a cut that deep is beyond most estimates of what the impact actually did. Extra carbon dioxide, if anything, widened the suitable area.

The fossils fit that picture. Crocodile relatives from the first few million years after the impact turn up around the Gulf of Mexico, in northwestern Africa and in northern South America.

All of those places are inside the warm belts. From there, the group spread out again as the planet warmed.

The 2020 simulations found that the impact sharply reduced suitable climate for non-avian dinosaurs.

“Crocodiles’ long-standing preference for warm environments may have placed survivors exactly where suitable climates held up best, like around the equator. These refuges could have sheltered populations that later recovered and radiated out,” Chiarenza said.

The authors were careful not to make climate the whole story. A dinosaur and a crocodile living in the same place presumably tolerated similar air temperatures, they wrote.

Smaller bodies, a life split between water and land, a flexible diet and the low energy needs of a cold-blooded animal probably all helped. Other survivors of the impact fit the same pattern.

What the climate maps add is a geography: the places crocodiles already favored were the places that stayed livable.

The habit was not perfectly fixed. Twice in all that time, the models found a lineage whose preferred climate itself shifted.

The first shift came about 94 million years ago, during one of the hottest stretches of the age of dinosaurs. Global temperatures then ran up to roughly 25°F (14°C) above today’s. Most of the family kept its old preference even then.

One branch near the base of the modern crocodile family did not. It moved its preferred temperature from around 73°F (23°C) to around 95°F (35°C), hotter than the line tolerated at any other time.

The second came about 34 million years ago, as the planet cooled and Australia dried out. An extinct group of Australian crocodiles adapted to desert-like conditions.

Their preferred rainfall was about 9 inches (225 millimeters) a year, against a starting point for the whole group of about 76 inches (1,929 millimeters).

“Crocodiles are often called living fossils, but their ancestors and relatives changed enormously throughout their history, ranging from giants the size of a bus that could have devoured a T. rex, to small, cute animals with the face of a pug,” Chiarenza said.

“Our study shows that, despite all this diversity, what stayed surprisingly consistent was their relationship with climate.”

The climate reconstructions come in steps of roughly 5 million years, so the models miss short events such as ice ages. The oldest maps also describe a world unlike any the living species have been measured in.

Gaps in the fossil record add more uncertainty. South America and Antarctica have stretches, around 30 million years ago, with suitable climate on the maps and few or no fossils.

The authors pointed to Jurassic-age rocks in India and Antarctica as places to search.

And a suitable climate doesn’t prove an animal lived there. The authors wrote that their maps are a necessary part of the survival story, not a sufficient one.

Mannion said the pattern matters now. “If crocodiles mostly coped with past climate change by moving towards suitable habitats, today’s species will need room to move as the planet warms.”

The loss of suitable areas to roads, farms and cities is making that harder, Mannion added.

Eleven of the 26 living species are already listed as threatened, and butterflies are shifting their ranges as the climate warms.

Whether a crocodile surrounded by farms and cities can do the same is a question these maps can’t answer. The models can show where suitable climate goes next. Whether the animals can follow it is up to them.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
