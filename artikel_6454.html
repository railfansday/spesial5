A new island formed off the coast of Iceland in 1963, but very few people have ever been allowed to visit

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Born from a volcanic eruption in 1963, Iceland's protected Surtsey island offers scientists a rare chance to study how life colonizes new land.

On November 14, 1963, fishermen off the south coast of Iceland noticed signs of an eruption at sea. Over the next three and a half years, the eruptions built Surtsey, a volcanic island about 20 miles (32 kilometers) from the mainland.

Iceland protected the island while it was still forming. Since then, scientists have used it as a natural laboratory to watch life arrive on brand-new land.

Going ashore without a permit is prohibited, and tourists may not land at all.

The Surtsey eruption continued until June 5, 1967, the longest continuous eruption in Iceland since the country was settled, according to the Environment Agency of Iceland.

When it ended, the island covered about 1 square mile (2.6 square kilometers) and rose 558 feet (170 meters) above sea level.

By 2006, waves, wind and rain had cut the island to about 0.54 square miles (1.4 square kilometers), and its highest point had dropped to 509 feet (155 meters).

Drill cores taken in 1979 and 2017 show pores in the island’s volcanic rock filling with new mineral cements, including a mineral called aluminous tobermorite. Marie Jackson, a research professor of geology and geophysics at the University of Utah, led that work.

“It’s not often that we have geologic processes that we can watch change at the human scale,” Jackson said in a Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory feature.

In Jackson’s account, the only other places with comparable amounts of that mineral are ancient Roman harbor structures, where it formed in concrete. “Surtsey basalt is providing a geologic analogue for Roman seawater concretes,” she noted.

Surtsey became a nature reserve in 1965, while the eruption was still underway. The reserve was enlarged in 2006 and now covers about 25 square miles (65 square kilometers) of sea and island.

Visits have been restricted since 1965, mainly to prevent biological contamination. Landing on the island requires permission from the Environment Agency of Iceland.

Visitors may not disturb the island’s features, bring in organisms or soil, or leave waste. Diving near the island is not allowed.

Only one to three visits take place each year, with no more than ten to 12 visitors per trip, according to the IUCN World Heritage Outlook.

The Surtsey Research Society coordinates the research, usually with one group of about ten scientists per year. Boat trips within the reserve and viewing from the air are allowed.

Scientists have studied the island since 1964. Seeds arrived on ocean currents, and molds, bacteria and fungi appeared.

The first vascular plant appeared in 1965. It was a sea rocket, a coastal plant, found near the northern shore.

Ten vascular plant species had arrived by the end of the island’s first decade. By 2004, the count had reached 60, along with 75 bryophytes, 71 lichens and 24 fungi.

In study plots surveyed over the years, the average number of plant species rose from 4.8 in 1996 and 1997 to 10.4 in 2014 and 2015.

Scientists have also recorded 89 bird species on Surtsey, 57 of which breed elsewhere in Iceland, and 335 species of invertebrates.

A 2022 study of plant colonization on Surtsey by Sigurður H. Magnússon and colleagues at the Icelandic Institute of Natural History dates the start of a dense gull colony on the southern lava fields to 1986.

Plant colonization had been considerable from 1965 to 1979, then stalled for six years. After the gulls settled, it rose sharply, peaking in 1992 to 1995 and again in 2006 and 2007.

The authors attribute the jump to the birds bringing in more seeds and improving conditions for seedlings. Within the colony, vegetation became denser and richer in nutrients.

From 1965 to 2015, the botanists counted 74 vascular plant species on the island, 64 of them still living in 2015. The total of 74 equals about 15 percent of Iceland’s native vascular plant flora.

Even so, “most of the island still has sparse vegetation cover,” the authors write. They expect nutrients from seabirds to keep adding plant cover.

UNESCO added Surtsey to its World Heritage List in 2008. Coastal erosion is expected to remove another two-thirds of the island, leaving only its most resistant core.

The island’s measured area fell from 366 acres (148.1 hectares) in 1998 to 317 acres (128.3 hectares) in 2016.

No invasive alien species have been recorded on Surtsey so far. Invasive plants do grow on Heimaey, about 11 miles (18 kilometers) away.

The Icelandic Institute of Natural History monitors the island with the Marine Research Institute and the Agricultural University of Iceland, in cooperation with the Surtsey Research Society.

IUCN’s latest assessment of the site, finalized in October 2025, rated its conservation outlook as “Good.”
