How To Communicate The Value Of Complex Tech To Buyers

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

The more sophisticated a technology product is, the harder it can be to explain to buyers who aren’t familiar with the technical language behind it. Lean too heavily on technical details and you risk losing their interest, but oversimplify and you may undersell what makes the product valuable.

Getting the balance right matters: Nontechnical buyers need to understand what a product can do and why it’s worth considering, even if they don’t need to know exactly how it works. Below, Forbes Technology Council members share ways tech companies can make complex products understandable without losing the nuance that sets them apart.

We put value in the buyer’s terms, anchoring every presentation in their data or like-for-like use cases they know well, and then we spell out the time or money saved, growth impact, or projected NPS gains. Compliance with SOC 2, ISO 27001/42001, and GDPR reassures without overwhelming. We avoid AI model internals and technical deep dives. It’s tempting to put an “AI expert” before customers, but buyers are evaluating how the product helps them; AI depth can confuse, not reassure. - Keith McFarlane, Globality, Inc.

Tell the story before sharing the specs. We never teach developers “Hello World.” We explain why a technology exists, then have them build something they instantly get, like their own AI assistant. Buyers are no different. Show them the problem the product was born to solve and let them build one real thing with it, and the technical depth will finally make sense. - Phil Kwok, EasyA

Forbes Technology Council is an invitation-only community for world-class CIOs, CTOs and technology executives. Do I qualify?

Most users and customers care more about outcomes than the underlying technology. That’s why we lead with tangible customer benefits, which naturally spark curiosity among both technical and nontechnical audiences. This creates an opportunity to engage more deeply and explain the technology in a way that is accessible without oversimplifying its value. - Sachin Khanna, IQSIGHT

Focus on relatable, industry-specific storytelling that centers around product outcomes rather than how the product works to achieve those outcomes. Most products are built around solving a specific pain point. Speak to that pain point and purpose first, and position the product as the clear solution using case studies and ROI stories. Technical education can come later in the buying journey once a customer has bought into the value. - Mark van Berkel, Schema App

Show them the moment they missed. We don’t open by explaining how our models read video. We show a buyer the TikTok that was driving the conversation about their brand while their dashboard showed nothing. Lead with an outcome they’d recognize from their own week, and let the technical depth answer the “how” when they ask. - Ofer Familier, dig

Hide the complexity, not the value. Buyers don’t need an architecture lesson. They need to see how the product changes the way they work. Show them a real workflow, what becomes easier and what they can now accomplish that wasn’t practical before. Complexity should be absorbed by the technology, not passed on to the customer. - Hugh Cumming, Vena Solutions

Describe the outcome you own, not the task or technology. A nontechnical buyer does not need to understand harness architecture. They need to know which outcome or task it will handle and how they will verify it. Then show it by running a demo on the buyer’s real workflow. - Aidan Guo, Attention Engineering

In my experience as a user-centric product leader, the best tool companies can use to help make complex products understandable is to provide real-life use cases that make sense to nontechnical buyers. Demonstrating the possibilities that can be unlocked by your product through these examples really helps clients imagine the future with your product integrated in their flow. To level up, uncover a novel use case that the client never even thought of. - Mugdha Gogte

Write for the AI that will explain your product first. Many nontechnical buyers now ask ChatGPT or Gemini what a product does before they ever talk to sales. If your product content is vague, the answer flattens you into a generic category. Publish specific, plain-language pages on who the product is for, what it replaces, its limits and the proof behind it. AI will simplify you both ways; make sure it keeps your product value. - Mohammed Razem, Vardot

Visual metaphors help companies translate their value for nontechnical buyers. It turns “look at this cool tech” into smaller moments that invite further exploration. The tip of the iceberg will be the leading value, but it won’t be all of the value. It’s hard to make peace with that and let it be as a technologist. We tend to want to show all of the value, like an infomercial. - Diane Weaver, Baryons Inc.

One method I use to explain the technical to the nontechnical is analogies. It was very helpful when I was a CIO during my previous agency’s transition to zero-trust architecture. I explained to them how traditional cyber network solutions were designed as a castle and a moat. Implementing zero trust makes it so that even if you get across the moat, you still need the keys to get in and around the castle. - Jim Tunnessen, GOVERNBOX.ai

Anchor the product in a single customer outcome, then layer the technical depth behind it. Lead with the problem the buyer already feels and show the result in their terms, like hours saved or risk reduced. Let a concrete example carry the explanation, and keep the details available for anyone who wants to go deeper. - Monisha Somji

Design your message for the second conversation, not the first. A nontechnical buyer rarely decides alone. They have to repeat your pitch to a CFO or board who never sees the demo. Test every message by asking the buyer to explain it back. Where they stumble is where your story breaks. Give them language they can own, and the full value travels intact. - Ivan Kan, Hackindia

One way is to describe the change, not the product. Nontechnical buyers don’t need to know how it works to know what it’s worth. Show them the day their life gets easier, the worry that goes away, and the thing they can finally do. They’ll remember the moment they realized they could stop trying to hold everything together. The depth should remain available for anyone who wants it; it just isn’t the lead-in. - Anila Siraj, OpsBridge Advisory

The best way to explain a complex product is to get outside of the product team. Brainstorm with marketing and test the language with people across the company who aren’t in technical roles to see where they get stuck. If they can’t explain the value back to you, it’s a sign the language needs to change, not that the customer needs to learn more jargon. - Todd Fisher, CTM (formerly CallTrackingMetrics)

Show, don’t tell. Instead of expecting nontechnical buyers to care about your technology choices, show them the product in action. Then, show them the specific outcomes they’ll achieve if they use the product. Ideally, these outcomes should be well-documented and involve real testimonials. Boring nontechnical buyers with technical and process content is an anti-pattern, and we should avoid it. - Michael Quoc, Product.ai

Showcasing how tech solutions fit into daily life can be an effective approach; consumers have little time and short attention spans. Using clear, direct storytelling to demonstrate how a product can improve their lives—without oversimplifying its use, but keeping the message honest and clear—can resonate directly with their emotional side. - Nino Letteriello, FIT Group

Nontechnical buyers quickly tune out when presented with abstract feature lists or complex architecture diagrams. Instead, focus on showing the tangible difference in an employee’s daily routine by comparing their current manual process with the new streamlined workflow. Demonstrating that clear “before and after” makes the business value obvious without needing to explain every technical detail under the hood. - Neil Lampton, TIAG
