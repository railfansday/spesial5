A Former College Athlete’s Battle With Depression

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

He spent years trying to power through his depression, cycling through several traditional oral antidepressants, before finding a medication that worked for him.

JEREMIAH FELT GLUED to his couch, too sad and unmotivated to move. He was supposed to be getting ready for his wedding—a day when he expected to feel more confident than ever. Instead, his groomsmen had to come to his apartment and help him get there. A former college athlete, Jeremiah was still a big guy, and it took all of them to lift him from the couch and carry him out the door.

It’s not what you think. Jeremiah had absolutely no doubts about marrying the love of his life. He was in the throes of a depressive episode, one of many he had experienced dating back several years. For Jeremiah, becoming a husband meant becoming a provider and protector. But on the day he was supposed to step into that role, he was the one who needed help—and he felt ashamed.

The wedding-day episode was just one part of a much longer struggle. Jeremiah’s symptoms first appeared in college and worsened after graduation. When he entered the workforce, his symptoms worsened and he became exhausted and increasingly apathetic—to the point of neglecting his hygiene. Eventually, he became too depressed to get out of bed in the morning and lost his first job.

In 2012, Jeremiah was officially diagnosed with major depressive disorder (MDD), a mental health condition that can have a significant impact on daily life and make it difficult to find motivation or energy for self-care. Over the next nine years, persistent symptoms made it difficult for him to maintain employment, and he eventually went on disability.

DEPRESSION IN MEN often hides in plain sight. In 2021, 6.2 percent of U.S. adult men and 10.3 percent of U.S. adult women experienced a major depressive episode, according to the National Institute of Mental Health.

Those numbers tell us how common depression is, but not what it looks like. Men and women can experience many of the same symptoms, including sadness, hopelessness, fatigue, changes in sleep, and loss of interest in favorite activities. However, depression often shows up differently in men. Feeling sad or emotional isn’t always the main symptom. For many men, symptoms can show up as headaches, digestive problems, tiredness, irritability, or long-term pain. Feeling isolated or seeking distractions to avoid dealing with feelings or relationships can also be signs.

According to Craig Chepke, MD, DFAPA, a psychiatrist and medical director based in Huntersville, North Carolina, men may have a hard time talking about their feelings and asking for help, making diagnosis and treatment difficult. “Depression in men is often missed or misread, including by men and others close to them, because it doesn’t always look like what people expect depression to look like,” he explains. “Instead of sadness, it may show up as irritability, anger, withdrawal, or self-medication. Sometimes it takes an ultimatum from someone who cares about them just to get them into the office. Depression is a medical condition, not a character flaw, but I hear men with depression say variations of the same thing over and over: ‘If I was just stronger, I’d be able to handle this on my own.’”

Jeremiah agrees. “You feel like less of a man, like you should be able to just power through somehow, or snap out of it,” he says. “But you can’t. You don’t. Depression affects your confidence in every aspect of your life. It becomes a dehumanizing experience.”

BEYOND HIS OWN self-doubt, Jeremiah had a hard time finding healthcare providers who would take his symptoms seriously. He dealt with therapists who minimized his struggles. One provider even attributed his symptoms to restless legs syndrome.

At times, Jeremiah felt that some doctors viewed depression as a “woman’s disease,” subtly calling his masculinity into question. Being a Black man added another layer as he navigated a healthcare system that didn’t always understand his experience.

He experienced more difficulty when he opened up to loved ones. “Being a man is lonely in general. There are very few men you can talk to beneath the surface level about things even though you call each other friends. I shared my struggle with MDD with my two closest friends, and it kind of freaked them out,” he says. “We didn’t talk much after that for about a year.” He received a similar reaction from cousins and extended family members, who weren’t sure what to say or whether to treat him differently. Things improved once they saw that his personality hadn’t fundamentally changed.

Jeremiah’s marriage, however, became a turning point. His wife, a clinical therapist, was uniquely positioned to recognize his condition. Starting around 2016, she began noticing subtle changes, how he would pick up and lose interest in activities and hobbies, and how Jeremiah would withdraw into himself around the house. It was then that she encouraged him to seek professional help. But asking for help was only the beginning. Finding a treatment that adequately addressed his symptoms would take years.

IN 2021, JEREMIAH was diagnosed with treatment-resistant depression (TRD), which affects about 30 percent of people with MDD. TRD is generally defined as MDD that has not resolved after trying at least two traditional oral antidepressants. “By the time someone checks off medication after medication on an intake form, they’ve usually lost something more valuable than time,” Dr. Chepke says of people living with TRD. “They’ve lost hope that anything can get better.”

Jeremiah knew his feelings were real. “TRD is a bully and it will hit you in the face from the moment you wake up,” he says. “Your best days are not behind you. You’re just going through something that a lot of men go through and just aren't able to admit it. You’re working to hold onto the life you want to live, not just your health, but your lifestyle, who you want to be, and the type of man you want to become or already are.”

For a while, Jeremiah kept trying new medications and started losing hope when they didn’t work. But with help from his wife and doctor, he found a different path.

LOOKING BACK, WHAT mattered most to Jeremiah was his wife’s consistent support through the process. In the early years of treatment, she helped him remember appointments and his medication schedules that kept him moving forward when motivation was scarce. Jeremiah’s doctor recommended he try SPRAVATO® (esketamine), an FDA-approved nasal spray for adults with TRD, used alone or with an oral antidepressant.

It is not known if SPRAVATO® is safe and effective for use in preventing suicide or in reducing suicidal thoughts or actions. SPRAVATO® is not for use in place of hospitalization if your healthcare provider determines that hospitalization is needed, even if improvement is experienced after the first dose of SPRAVATO®. SPRAVATO® is not for use as a medicine to prevent or relieve pain (anesthetic). It is not known if SPRAVATO® is safe or effective as an anesthetic medicine. It is not known if SPRAVATO® is safe and effective in children. (Please see additional Important Safety Information including Boxed WARNINGS, limitations of use, and a list of side effects below. Click here to learn more.)

SPRAVATO is only available through a restricted distribution program called the SPRAVATO Risk Evaluation & Mitigation Strategy (REMS). SPRAVATO can only be administered at a certified healthcare setting, and patients self-administer the pre-filled nasal device at a REMS-certified treatment center under direct clinical supervision and are monitored for at least two hours after taking SPRAVATO. SPRAVATO may have serious side effects such as sedation, dissociation, and respiratory depression, and the risk of abuse and misuse.

SPRAVATO represents a different treatment approach for TRD. Unlike traditional antidepressants that work on serotonin, norepinephrine and dopamine, SPRAVATO acts on glutamate, a chemical messenger in the brain that is thought to regulate depression. The role of glutamate in depression and the exact way SPRAVATO works are unknown.

As his depressive symptoms improved, Jeremiah began to engage more with others. He also joined a support group for Black men navigating these challenges. Hearing other people’s stories reminds him that he’s not alone. Today, he works as an IT and cybersecurity instructor in Chicago, speaks at industry conferences, and builds robots in his spare time.

Jeremiah’s experience is his own, and treatment decisions are personal. “There’s no one treatment that’s right for everybody,” Dr. Chepke says. In his practice, Dr. Chepke has witnessed reductions in certain patients’ depressive symptoms when incorporating SPRAVATO into their treatment. While not all of his patients may benefit, seeing some move from repeatedly cycling through different treatments to an option studied specifically for TRD has helped shape how he approaches care for this population.

If you haven’t found relief from your depressive symptoms, talk with your doctor about the options that may be appropriate for you—and most importantly, know that there is hope.
