Hotel revenue is having a good year. The share that reaches the people who own the building hasn't. And that dynamic may not change anytime soon.

Stronger room revenue in the third quarter may not mean a better bottom line for hotel owners. Fresh data shows many hotels are keeping a smaller share of each guest’s spending year after year, while rising costs make new hotels harder to justify building.

Kalibri CEO and co-founder Cindy Estis Green and MCR Chairman and CEO Tyler Morse described that squeeze from different vantage points at the Skift Commerce Summit.

Labor, insurance, taxes, debt service, renovation costs, utilities, and customer acquisition costs have together been rising at a faster clip than average room rates in recent years, Estis Green said.

This isn't a distress story. "Business is great right now," Morse said. U.S. revenue per available room, or RevPAR, is forecast to rise 4.4% this year, according to CoStar and Tourism Economics after a 0.3% decline last year.

Higher RevPAR isn't translating into better owner economics, according to executives speaking at the Skift Commerce Summit. Kalibri CEO Cindy Estis Green said labor, insurance, taxes, debt service, renovation costs, utilities, and customer acquisition costs have been rising faster than room rates, and Kalibri expects expenses to grow 5% to 10% a year over the next few years against RevPAR growth of flat to 3%. Her firm's measure of "revenue capture" — the share of guest spending a hotel keeps after acquisition costs — fell 1.11 percentage points last year; holding it flat would have left U.S. hotels with $2.3 billion more and asset values almost $30 billion higher. MCR CEO Tyler Morse said business is still good — U.S. RevPAR is forecast to rise 4.4% this year per CoStar and Tourism Economics — but construction costs have risen about 50% in five years, putting a Home2 Suites at $250,000 a key, math that doesn't work without a $200 ADR. Conversions made up more than 40% of Marriott openings in the first nine months of the year, while U.S. and Canada new-build starts stayed below pre-pandemic levels. Luxury is the exception, with luxury chains raising rates 9% in the third quarter versus 1% for budget. Morse predicted AI search would develop paid-placement economics like Google's, with one dominant player; Estis Green said it's always the owner who ends up paying.
