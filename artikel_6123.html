At the Races: The new Trump Democrats

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Editor’s note: At the Races will not publish next week on Thanksgiving Day. We’ll be back the following week.

By Niels Lesniewski, Mary Ellen McIntire and Daniela Altimari

Senate Democrats powered their way to a majority by flipping nine seats – their biggest gain in nearly 70 years.

Eight of those wins were in states that Donald Trump has carried every time he’s run so it’s no surprise that some of the incoming senators are talking up the prospect of working with him over the next two years.

“We don't have time to wait until we have a different administration in the White House,” South Carolina Sen.-elect Annie Andrews told reporters this week. “We have two years ahead of us. We have very real problems that we need to address.”

Andrews is the first Democrat elected to the Senate from South Carolina since 1998. A pediatrician, she cited reducing prescription drug costs as a potential area of common ground with Trump.

“I support the president's attempts to stop taxes on tips and taxes on Social Security,” Andrews said, citing two selling points of last year’s GOP reconciliation law. During the campaign, she issued a policy paper expressing support for making those provisions of the tax code permanent.

James Talarico, the incoming senator from Texas, told fellow Democrats gathered in Denver last week that if Trump wanted “the same plaques and portraits and busts as LBJ, we can help make that a reality.”

“We should make the final two years of his presidency the most productive of any presidency in American history,” said Talarico, the first Democrat in nearly 40 years to win a Senate race in the Lone Star State.

The idea of Trump and congressional Democrats working together might seem far-fetched, but with the incoming Democrats hailing from places such as Alaska, the Carolinas, Mississippi, Kansas and Texas, there’s a political incentive to appeal to Republican-leaning voters back home.

But intentionally or not, Talarico and other newly elected Democrats might be borrowing from the playbook of New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani, who has welcomed Trump when goals align, including on an effort to redevelop the Sunnyside rail yard in the president’s native Queens.

Trump has stressed that he will veto legislation he opposes from the incoming Democratic Congress, but, at least for the moment, he seems open to deals.

“I have a feeling that this is going to be an unbelievably productive two years,” Trump told Fox News early Thursday morning in Shenzhen, China, adding that  he would invite Democrats to the White House.

“I respect them,” he said. “Politics is a nasty game, and they won.”

Peltola prevails: Former Rep. Mary Peltola’s victory over Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan in Alaska brings the Senate Democratic Caucus to 56 seats in the 120th Congress. Peltola finished with just over 50 percent of the vote, negating the need to use the state’s ranked choice voting system. The race for the state’s at-large House district was decided by a ranked choice tally, with Republican Rep. Nick Begich defeating independent Bill Hill 51 percent to 49 percent in the final round. Alaska voters also rejected an attempt to eliminate ranked choice voting in the state by a vote of 54 percent to 46 percent.

Leadership elections: House Democrats nominated Hakeem Jeffries to serve as the next speaker in a 251-11 closed-door Wednesday vote, Roll Call’s Lauren Green reports. That puts Jeffries in prime position to become the first Black speaker when the full chamber votes in January. Meanwhile, Senate Democrats are scheduled to pick the next majority leader on Dec. 1, as our colleague Savannah Behrmann reports. Charles E. Schumer, who’s being challenged by Maryland’s Chris Van Hollen, maintains he has the votes to win.

A victory lap and a warning: Even as the Congressional Hispanic Caucus celebrates its largest roster ever, the group’s leaders had a warning for their fellow Democrats: “Latino voters gave Democrats a two-year lease, but it expires in 2028 unless the party changes its playbook for good,” said California Rep. Linda T. Sánchez, who leads the group’s campaign arm.

#KYGOV: Republican Rep. James R. Comer, who was just elected to a sixth full term, launched his much-anticipated run for Kentucky governor this week. The Bluegrass State is one of three states holding statewide executive  elections in 2027, along with Louisiana and Mississippi.

Rocky Mountain roundup: Rep. Lauren Boebert, an outspoken Republican with a national reputation as a Second Amendment provocateur, lost her bid for reelection in Colorado’s 4th District to Democrat Eileen Laubacher, a retired Navy rear admiral. Meanwhile, Republican Gabe Evans, who fell short in his bid for a second term in the neighboring 8th District, announced he plans to challenge Laubacher in two years, Roll Call’s Samantha Freeman reports.

They’re back: Our colleague Nina Heller tracked down former congressional staffers who are back on the Hill as newly elected members. “I never thought this would be the path, but I’m honored to do it,” said Rep.-elect Sydney Gruters, who worked for fellow Florida Republicans Greg Steube and Vern Buchanan, her soon-to-be House predecessor.

RIP: Former Rep. Jim McDermott, a longtime advocate for universal health care, died last week at the age of 89. The Washington Democrat, who retired to a small town in southwestern France after 28 years in the House, was hailed by his home state’s senior senator, Patty Murray, “as one of the first and most powerful voices fighting to fix our broken health care system in Congress.”

Where the House stands: As of 3 p.m. Eastern time, House Democrats have flipped 46 seats to three for the Republicans and are on track to hold at least 257 seats in the next Congress. Six races remain uncalled by The Associated Press, including two in Louisiana that will be decided by December runoffs. The AP is also yet to call Tennessee’s 4th District, where Republican Rep. Scott DesJarlais has conceded to Democrat Victoria Broderick, who leads by 272 votes out of nearly 200,000 cast.

Thompson loses: California Rep. Mike Thompson lost his bid for a 15th term to former venture capitalist Eric Jones, a political newcomer who poured millions into his campaign. Thompson is the second California member to lose to a fellow Democrat this year, after Rep. Jimmy Gomez. But Rep. Doris Matsui held off an intraparty challenge from democratic socialist Mai Vang.

2028 draft: Supporters of former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg launched Team Pete PAC on Thursday, an effort to urge him to launch a second presidential bid.

Battleground rep: Michigan Rep. Kristen McDonald Rivet will be House Democrats' battleground leadership representative in the next Congress, acting as a bridge between members in competitive districts and party leadership. McDonald Rivet, who ran unopposed for the position, will succeed Nevada Rep. Susie Lee.

#INGOV: Indiana Gov. Mike Braun launched his 2028 reelection campaign as speculation swirls that state Attorney General Todd Rokita could challenge him for the Republican nomination. Rokita, a former congressman, has criticized Braun for not disciplining state educators who posted controversial comments after Charlie Kirk’s assassination but said last week that he wanted Braun to “run for governor” and “be successful.” The two previously faced off in a 2018 Republican Senate primary, with Braun going on to serve one term in the Senate.

New leader for New Dems: Illinois Rep. Nikki Budzinski will chair the New Democrat Coalition next year, taking over from fellow Illinois Rep. Brad Schneider. New Mexico’s Gabe Vasquez, Ohio’s Greg Landsman and Washington’s Kim Schrier and Marilyn Strickland will serve as co-chairs of the center-left group, the largest ideological entity in the Democratic Caucus.

Remembering Holden: Former Pennsylvania Rep. Tim Holden, who served 10 terms in the House before losing a Democratic primary to Matt Cartwright in 2012, died Sunday at the age of 69. House members held a moment of silence for Holden on the floor Thursday. Republican Rep. Dan Meuser, whose district includes territory Holden once represented, described him as “well-liked and widely respected, including by those on both sides of the political aisle.”

Investigated: Ohio Rep.-elect Brian Poindexter is under investigation for alleged sexual harassment after the Democrat’s campaign hired a human resources firm to investigate the complaint. Both the Blue Dog PAC and the Congressional Progressive Caucus, which had endorsed Poindexter’s candidacy, dropped their support in the closing days of the campaign after learning of the allegation.

Tennessee two-step: Tennessee Democrat Jeri Green, who narrowly lost the open governor’s race to Republican Sen. Marsha Blackburn, announced a bid for the state Senate seat GOP Rep.-elect Brent Taylor is expected to vacate. Taylor narrowly defeated Democratic state Rep. Justin Pearson in the redrawn 9th District, though both men were simultaneously reelected to their legislative seats.

The  historic “blue tsunami” that swept Democrats into commanding Senate and House majorities has some pundits questioning whether Republicans will ever be in charge again, observes Roll Call elections analyst Nathan L. Gonzales of Inside Elections.

But you just have to look at the election results from two years ago to realize that when it comes to winning party coalitions, nothing is permanent anymore, Nathan writes.

Only the young: New York magazine catches up with young Republicans in South Carolina and finds that immigration policy and the war in Iran have turned many of the MAGA faithful against the GOP.

From satirist to senator: The Washington Post traces South Carolina Democrat Annie Andrews’ journey from an online agitator who made relentless fun of Republicans to a newly elected senator who says she’s eager to work with her GOP colleagues.

Bad blood: The Boston Globe explores the long-standing rivalry between Maine’s two newest members of Congress: Democratic Sen.-elect Troy Jackson and Republican Rep.-elect Paul LePage.

Schumer’s choice: Uncertainty around whether Schumer will seek another term in 2028 has risen as the New York Democrat seeks to become Senate majority leader next year, Politico reports.

That's how long it's been since the Senate Democratic Caucus last saw a contested race for its top leadership position, with Maryland’s Van Hollen now challenging Schumer for the job.

After Democrats lost eight Senate seats (and their majority) in the 1994 Republican Revolution, 42-year old South Dakota Sen. Tom Daschle edged out Connecticut's Christopher J. Dodd by a single vote, 24-23, to succeed outgoing leader George Mitchell of Maine.

The last time a sitting Democratic Senate leader faced a formal challenge was a decade earlier, in 1984.

West Virginia's Robert C. Byrd, who’d been leading his party in the chamber since 1977, easily fended off a bid by Florida’s Lawton Chiles.

The last time either party experienced a contested race for the position was four years ago, when Florida Sen. Rick Scott challenged Republican leader Mitch McConnell of Kentucky.

As Byrd did before him, McConnell handily defeated his challenger in the secret-ballot vote.

Machine recounts in a pair of House races in North Carolina are expected to be completed Monday. Republican Rep. Virginia Foxx trails Democrat Chuck Hubbard by 331 votes, while GOP Rep. Pat Harrigan leads Democratic challenger Ashley Bell by just over 2,500 votes. The trailing candidate could then request a partial hand recount.

Monday is also the deadline for Republican Rep. Brad Finstad to formally request a recount in his razor-thin race against Democrat Jake Johnson in Minnesota’s 1st District. Finstad, who reportedly said he’ll seek one, trails Johnson by 321 votes.
