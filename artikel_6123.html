The Best Places to Go in Australia, New Zealand, and the South Pacific in 2027

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

When it comes to the best places to go in Australia, New Zealand, and the South Pacific for 2027, there is a prevailing theme: Familiar destinations are finding new ways to surprise us. In Queensland, old pubs have become home to some of the hottest tables. Just take a look at Noosa’s restored 100-year-old Kin Kin, which has a new lease on life thanks to the arrival of Michelin-trained chef Oscar Holgado. And there’s much more where that came from. Across the country in the East Kimberley, this is the year to dine on native bush foods in the shadow of ancient boabs and red cliffs, and rest your head at the area’s stalwart bush lodges, two of which have just reopened after major renovations. Then there’s Tasmania’s east coast, which is always deserving of a long road trip—and now Tasmanian malt whisky is being made in an oyster hatchery, not far from where little penguins (the world’s smallest penguin species) return to shore after feeding at sea. Across the Tasman, Wellington’s new Michelin guide reminds us that even those stars can’t capture the effortless excellence of this area’s food and wine culture. Plan your visit around a culinary festival, several of which come to life in winter, and you’ll see what we mean. Meanwhile, in the Cook Islands at Highland Paradise on Rarotonga, descendants of the original Tinomana community guide visitors through a 600-year-old ancestral mountain settlement, sharing its history and traditions as they optimistically await UNESCO’s answer on recognition. On our below list of the best places to travel in Oceania for 2027, there is a trip for every type. —Chloe Sachdev

Go for: ancient cultural sites, eco-tourism, dazzling island landscapes

On the South Pacific, the 15 isles of Cook Islands are scattered across nearly 900,000 square miles.

The Cook Islands’ 15 isles stipple roughly 849,000 square miles of the South Pacific, but total less than 100 square miles of land. Most first trips center on a southern pair of islands: Rarotonga and Aitutaki. On the one-hour propeller flight between two, the entire country arrives in flashes: a volcanic ridge inside a white reef, miles of open Pacific, then a blue lagoon with motu gathered along its rim. Rarotonga’s 20-mile coast road threads between reef and pleated peaks, while Aitutaki is defined by its broad lagoon.

On the west side of the island Rarotonga, a narrow road climbs from the lagoon into Maungaroa Valley, where a canopy of multi-trunked mato trees covers the remains of a centuries-old settlement. Taken together, the surviving sites form a cultural landscape that will appear before UNESCO’s World Heritage Committee in 2027 as the Cook Islands’ first nomination. At Highland Paradise, a cultural site within the old settlement and managed by descendants of Chief Tinomana, the Tapu’ae Day Tour follows those paths past chiefly house sites and sacred ground as guides explain how the stonework organized life here.

On Aitutaki, customary stewardship extends well below the waterline. In 2025 three lagoon zones came under ra’ui, a traditional temporary closure that allows coral and fish populations to recover, with protections scheduled to rotate in 2027. Aitutaki Coral Restoration takes small groups beyond the usual motu-and-sandbar circuit to its underwater nursery and a planting site, where guests clean a dead coral base and secure a naturally broken fragment. Part of each tour fee supports the restoration work.

Tourism is growing alongside the conservation push. In March 2027 a new, locally owned hotel, Grand Resort, billing itself as the island’s first five-star hotel, is scheduled to open with 61 adults-only suites on Rarotonga’s west coast. The Destination Stewardship Plan 2026–2030, meanwhile, shifts the focus from mainly growing tourism to managing its impact with protections for culture and the environment with a greater share flowing to island communities. —Paul Jebara

Go for: red dirt, remote lodges, and wilderness on an epic scale

After a multimillion-dollar transformation, Bullo River Station will reopen in May 2027.

Bullo River Station guests will have the opportunity to watch the seasonal mustering of cattle.

It’s not just helicopter blades creating a hum in Australia’s East Kimberley. A wave of luxury investment is putting this remote frontier on the global-travel radar—without sanding down the wilderness that makes it so worth the journey.

After devastating cyclone damage, Berkeley River Lodge welcomes guests back to a revived 15-villa hideaway, accessible only by private air charter from Darwin. Individual decks overlook a croc-filled coastline, while open-air en suites allow galaxy gazing from the bathtub. Just across the border in the Northern Territory, Bullo River Station sits on the eastern fringe of the East Kimberley. Come May 2027, it will reopen after a multimillion-dollar transformation, pairing a new level of comfort with conservation across its 400,000 acres. The good news is that it remains a dusty working cattle station: Guests can watch seasonal musters, fish for barramundi in the river, or helicopter to watering holes for swims beneath waterfalls or to Aboriginal rock-art sites.

There is even more to come. Abercrombie & Kent, in conjunction with the Billingjul Aboriginal Corporation, is developing its first Australian property: an off-grid eco-retreat offering access to the UNESCO World Heritage–listed Purnululu National Park, which contains the Bungle Bungle Range, where countless tiger-striped domes rise from the earth like sculpted beehives. (Originally slated for 2027, the lodge has been delayed, with a new opening date yet to be announced.)

Connecting much of this wilderness is Kununurra, a proper Outback town on the banks of the Ord River and the main jumping-off point for the Bungle Bungles, Lake Argyle, and the wider East Kimberley. If your itinerary calls for a night in town before heading deeper into the region, don’t waste it: The surrounding Ord River irrigation area is a surprisingly fertile food bowl. The Gourmet Camp Oven Experience puts that produce to work over four courses, cooked by fire in a secret off-grid location outside town, with dinner served beneath the stars and swinging chandeliers illuminating ancient boabs. —Katrina Lodge

Go for: heritage buildings, creative energy, and a new wave of boutique stays

In Western Australia, Fremantle's distinct creative scene centers on new boutiques, galleries, and stays.

Poppy Lissiman’s High Street flagship shines among a cluster of indie boutiques and maker pop-ups.

In 1987 the America’s Cup turned an unvarnished Western Australia port town into a global stage overnight. Forty years on, Fremantle has forged an identity of its own. Just 12 miles southwest of central Perth, Freo, as it’s known to locals—and Walyalup, as it’s known to the Whadjuk Noongar people—is the big city’s grittier cousin and the epicenter of one of Australia’s most eclectic creative scenes. This is, after all, fertile ground for Aussie homegrown musicians from Bon Scott to Tame Impala and John Butler. Now, right here at the mouth of the Swan River, a new generation of tastemakers are transforming warehouse skeletons and limestone heritage façades into galleries, natural wine bars, and boutique stays.

The energy will hit fever pitch in November 2027 with the return of the Fremantle Biennale, which will bring immersive art into the city’s forgotten spaces (dates are yet to be announced, but the 2025 event stretched over two full weeks). It crowns a year-round festival calendar of street performances, exhibitions, and community celebrations steeped in Fremantle’s multicultural roots. On the fashion front, a revitalization of the central piazza—just down the road from Aussie cool girl Poppy Lissiman’s High Street flagship—is helping spotlight indie makers and pop-up retailers. Fremantle’s historic West End has welcomed several hotly anticipated bars from beloved names in Western Australia hospitality, beginning with much-loved merchant Wise Child Wine Store, which expanded its bottle shop in September 2026 to include a 50-seat wine bar. Around the corner, Cafe Music, from the team behind Northbridge institution Wines of While, earned cult status with teaser weekends of mini martinis and vinyl DJs ahead of its permanent 2027 opening. At the heritage-listed P&O Hotel, listening bar and restaurant Ode to Sirens occupies the ground floor, while bartenders at Reuben’s Supper Club have been shaking cocktails since October 2026.

The West End’s newest stay follows the same past-meets-present script. The 11-room Owston Hotel (March 2026), inside the former Roma Restaurant, is a modern locanda honoring the Italian families who settled here in the 1950s. Breakfast comes from Common Bakery downstairs; later, slip next door for a pour at Vin Populi. —Jessie Stoelwinder

Go for: new sailings, revived resorts, and a quadrennial sporting showcase

Traveling by ship to French Polynesia has never been more accessible, thanks to new yachts and cruises.

With 118 palm-studded islands and atolls scattered across retina-popping blue waters, French Polynesia hardly needs help making a case for itself. But 2027 brings new ways to explore this far-flung corner of the South Pacific—especially by ship.

The Ritz-Carlton Yacht Collection’s French Polynesia sailings debut in December 2026, with 149-suite Evrima cruising the mountainous Society Islands, where volcanic peaks meet reef-ringed lagoons. In February, Windstar adds a second ship to sail these waters year-round, with the newly refurbished Wind Star joining Star Breeze throughout the Society Islands and the low-lying Tuamotus, a scattering of coral atolls and luminous lagoons. In March 2027, Aranui Cruises will launch Aranoa, a 91-cabin hybrid freighter-passenger ship bound for the remote Austral Islands, the Tuamotus, and Pitcairn. Wild, mountainous, and little visited, the Australs offer a different face of French Polynesia from the one seen in postcards showing overwater bungalows.

Though there are plenty of those accommodations on stilts, if that’s what you’re after, a wave of arrivals on land and lagoon add to the momentum. On mountainous Moorea, Sofitel Kia Ora Moorea Beach Resort has emerged from a major renovation in 2026, with revamped public spaces and refreshed overwater bungalows. In October, Bora Bora’s legendary Bloody Mary’s returned with a new 53-key hotel on Povai Bay and its sandy-floored restaurant restored alongside it. In early 2027, ELYT Charter Tahiti sets sail as a four-bedroom floating villa for private adventures around Bora Bora.

But 2027’s biggest arrival isn’t a hotel. In July the XVIII Pacific Games—the largest sporting event ever held in French Polynesia—will bring more than 4,500 athletes to Tahiti, Moorea, and Raiatea for 24 sports and more than 350 events. Among the sports is va’a, traditional outrigger canoe racing that is deeply embedded in Polynesian culture, alongside surfing, sailing, and open-water swimming. —Sarah Reid

Go for: Southland’s new cultural landmark, wild kiwi, and dark skies

Known in Māori as Rakiura, or “glowing skies,” Stewart Island is an International Dark Sky Sanctuary.

New Zealand doesn’t end at South Island. Farther down the map is Stewart Island, where the roads run out and the forest takes over. Rakiura, its Māori name, means “glowing skies”—fitting for an island that affords fiery sunsets, a visible Milky Way, and appearances of the aurora australis (southern lights) and is an International Dark Sky Sanctuary.

For many travelers heading to New Zealand’s deep south, the journey begins in Invercargill, where the Te Unua Museum of Southland will open in 2027, returning a major regional museum to the city for the first time since the Southland Museum and Art Gallery closed in 2018. Telling the stories of Murihiku Southland and its people, its collection includes rich holdings of Māori taoka, archaeology, photography, social history, and artifacts from the Subantarctic Islands.

Across Foveaux Strait, Stewart Island (Rakiura) comes into view. Oban, its only settlement, curls around horseshoe-shaped Halfmoon Bay. Wildlife vastly outnumbers humans here, where fewer than 500 people live. As many as 15,000 southern brown kiwi, or tokoeka, are thought to live on Stewart Island and, unusual for kiwi, can sometimes be spotted foraging in daylight. Just offshore, pest-free Ulva Island (Te Wharawhara) is a sanctuary for native birdlife like native kākā parrots and flightless weka birds.

Around 85% of the island is protected within Rakiura National Park, a wilderness of native forest, wetlands, dunes, and granite ranges. For a deeper look at its cultural history, take a boat from Oban to remote Oneke, or The Neck, an early Māori settlement and the tūrangawaewae—place of belonging—of Rakiura Māori. The three-hour guided experience begins with a whakatau, a traditional Māori welcome, and a visit to the Oneke whare (Māori meeting house) before guests follow coastal and native-bush trails as guides unpack the area’s Māori and European history, traditional plant use, and conservation. —Louise Goldsbury

Go for: lo-fi beach towns, long sweeps of sand, and a fast-evolving food scene

Elysium Noosa Resort joins Noosa's ever-improving food scene, serving an Italian-meets-Australian menu of local ingredients.

Elysium Noosa Resort is one of several new stays on Queensland’s low-rise, low-key Sunshine Coast.

An hour north of Brisbane, stretching along 62 miles of coastline and backed by rainforest-covered hills and volcanic peaks, Queensland’s Sunshine Coast is the antidote to both Brisbane’s big-city buzz and the Gold Coast’s five-star flex: lower-rise, lower-key, but home to an increasingly sophisticated run of ambitious openings that is raising its profile at home and abroad.

In Noosa the newly opened Elysium Noosa Resort has kicked lively Hastings Street up another notch. Cibaria Noosa trattoria, helmed by acclaimed Sydney chef Alessandro Pavoni, raids the Sunshine Coast food bowl for an Italian-meets-Australian menu of Queensland spanner crab risotto, Mooloolaba king prawns sharpened with finger lime, and locally sourced bistecca. Fifteen minutes south in Peregian Beach, two-hatted (”hats” are Australia’s equivalent of Michelin stars) restaurant Bask similarly puts local seafood, meat, and peak-season vegetables front and center.

Farther south, Avani Mooloolaba Beach Hotel, which opened in May 2026, has brought the lo-fi coastal town Mooloolaba its first internationally branded resort, with dazzling views and passionfruit-and-melon vodka cocktails at Sully’s Rooftop. On the waterfront at the Mooloolaba Spit, Fish House Steak & Grill jazzes up lobster rolls with a sunshiny yuzu emulsion. The region is serious about beer too: More than 20 independent brewhouses make the Sunshine Coast Australia’s craft-beer capital.

Inland, Casa Renoir, one of Australia’s chicest stays, will be completed in summer 2027 with six solar-powered villas. Limestone, olive trees, faded market finds, and private pools give the place the air of a Puglian masseria. That same connection to the land is shaping the hinterland’s food scene. Ēthos at Alsahwa Estate serves farm-to-table meals inside a barn on a 30-acre regenerative farm, while, in a plot twist for the 112-year-old Kin Kin Hotel, Michelin-trained chef Oscar Holgado, who has worked in the kitchens of Angela Hartnett’s Murano and Marcus Wareing’s The Berkeley, whips up a daily tasting menu—driven by whatever he’s plucked from the garden—in a converted barn called The Woodshed.

For all these developments, the Sunshine Coast hasn’t lost its low-key appeal; it has simply acquired better places to sleep, eat, and drink. Luckily, it is becoming considerably easier to reach with growing international connections and a $170 million expansion of Sunshine Coast Airport’s domestic terminal underway and due for completion in 2027. —Ute Junker

Go for: Rugby World Cup fever, landmark hotels, and a reinvigorated country escape

In 2027, the Men's Rugby World Cup returns to Australia for the first time since 2003, with the final Cup decider taking place in Sydney.

UNESCO-listed Greater Blue Mountains occupy more than a million acres of eucalyptus forest, sandstone escarpments, and deep-cut canyons.

Sydney has never struggled for attention, but 2027 is shaping up as a blockbuster year. The momentum began in November 2026, when Powerhouse Parramatta, the largest museum ever built in New South Wales, opened, bringing cultural heft to the city’s west. Also in November, Chanel staged its cruise 2026–2027 collection in Sydney. First shown in Biarritz, the fashion show found a natural second home in a city where salty hair, ocean swims, and year-round resort dressing are not just an aspiration but a way of life.

In 2027 sport takes over. The Men’s Rugby World Cup, expanded from 20 to 24 teams, returns to Australia for the first time since 2003, and Sydney lands the sharp end of the tournament. Over six weeks the city will host 13 matches, including major head-to-heads like Australia versus New Zealand, England versus Wales, two quarterfinals, both semifinals, and the bronze final, before 82,000 fans pack Stadium Australia for the final on November 13—the first rugby World Cup decider in Sydney in 24 years.

Australia’s first Waldorf Astoria opens in early 2027 on Circular Quay, with 220 rooms and serious architectural pedigree along with floor-to-ceiling views of the Opera House and Harbour Bridge. Kengo Kuma & Associates—behind Scotland’s V&A Dundee and Tokyo’s Olympic stadium—has designed a striking glass-fronted tower on the harbor. Inside, Melbourne-born Curtis Stone will lead the hotel’s culinary efforts, opening a bar and a restaurant—his first in Australia, in fact, after graduating from television chef to serious Los Angeles restaurateur with Maude and Gwen.

Two hours beyond Sydney, the UNESCO-listed Greater Blue Mountains stretch across over a million acres of eucalyptus forest, sandstone escarpments, and deep-cut canyons, promising the perfect side trip. Near Katoomba, the main hub for exploring the Blue Mountains, the historic Lilianfels hotel emerged from a multimillion-dollar overhaul in 2026 as Ardour Lilianfels Blue Mountains, its 89 renovated rooms and suites set on an 1889 country estate that is a well placed base for days on the trails. The bigger arrival comes in 2027, when Emirates’ long-shuttered Wolgan Valley returns from a $35 million-plus overhaul as the world’s first Ritz-Carlton Lodge, with 40 lodges scattered throughout a 7,000-acre conservation reserve roamed by kangaroos and wombats. —Chloe Sachdev

Long-known for its quiet beaches, Bicheno welcomes new names to the food and drink scene.

Dolphin Sands is a rural locality in the local government area of Glamorgan–Spring Bay in Tasmania

This area has long been home to one of Australia’s great road trips, a coastline drive linking Hobart with Launceston via Freycinet and the Bay of Fires. It’s a route of contrasts: the Tasman Sea on one side, forested hills on the other, past beaches and granite headlands, and a succession of fishing towns where boats still pull up with the day’s catch. Lately, though, there are more reasons to pull over.

Bicheno, two to three hours north of Hobart, is a case in point. The former fishing town turned low-key surf haunt remains better known for little penguins and deserted beaches than sceney restaurants, but a clutch of openings is evening the equation. At homegrown Waubs Harbour Distillery, award-winning whisky is made mere feet from the ocean in a converted oyster hatchery, with fireside tastings overlooking the Tasman Sea. Nearby, Le Gulch pours Tasmanian wines against pastel sunsets, while Parisian expat Tristan Punelle’s Le Coq, serving produce-led dishes by candlelight to a mix of both salty-haired locals and vacationers, is a humble ode to French cuisine. And after days spent surfing with dolphins and walking serene shorelines, Selkie Wellness restores balance at its beach-shack-housed massage studio and contrast therapy garden.

Farther south, beneath the pink-granite peaks of The Hazards, Saffire Freycinet enters a new chapter. In December 2026 the landmark wilderness lodge is unveiling The Saffire Jewel, an 8,600-square-foot, three-bedroom villa with a 59-foot-long infinity pool, a Jacuzzi, a private chef, and an excursion guide. Then, over Easter 2027, the Australian Three Peaks Race returns after a 14-year absence. The competition involves teams heading down Tasmania’s east coast in a nonstop combination of offshore sailing and mountain running. Spectators can watch the fleet depart from Beauty Point, catch the runners changing over at Coles Bay before tackling Mount Freycinet, or see the race finish in Hobart. —Alex Mitcheson

Go for: independent restaurants, wineries, and creative energy

In walkable harbor city Wellington, dining is defined by spots like Graze, Margot, The Ram, and Hillside.

For the best expression of New Zealand (Aotearoa) food, head to Wellington. Seriously. Michelin might have shone an international light on the country’s culinary scene in June 2026 but Wellington has been diligently perfecting its food culture long before the inspectors came to town.

This compact, walkable harbor city with a strong sense of community has been a go-to weekend escape for eating and drinking at some of the country’s best restaurants, bars, cafés, bakeries, and food festivals. A cohort of passionate chefs and owner-operators embody this sense of independent hospitality, taking advantage of local produce and the area’s abounding cultural diversity. In Wellington, you’ll find the kind of informal dining that the Michelin system often overlooks.

So how to tap in? At Graze in Kelburn, chef Max Gordy and sommelier Stina Persen serve a daily-changing menu built around New Zealand–grown produce and sustainably caught seafood. Over in Newtown chef Tom Adam and Juno Miers’s intimate Margot pairs seasonal cooking with an excellent drinks list. Elsewhere, The Ram recasts the Cuba Street pub as a serious food destination, while tiny vegetarian restaurant Hillside follows a low-waste philosophy grounded in local produce. Another to watch is award-winning Rita, which, in early 2027, will relaunch inside its century-old workers’ cottage as Super Rita, a deli and urban pantry showcasing local growers. The waterfront at Oriental Bay gets a new draw in early 2027, when The Pavilion opens its doors as an all-in-one food and wellness destination in a former 1930s bathing pavilion and band rotunda, with an all-day restaurant, a rooftop bar, and a bathhouse with hot pools, a cold plunge, a sauna, and treatments.

Bringing more of the creative energy Wellington is known for back to its Civic Square, City Gallery Wellington, a significant hub for contemporary art, reopened in October 2026 with a major exhibition by artist Cornelia Parker. Wellington’s city center continues to emerge butterfly-like from a long period of construction, and 2027 is a compelling time to visit, as several major civic buildings reopen to the public. Hot on the heels of the revitalized Te Matapihi ki te Ao Nui Central Library, the 120-year-old, heritage-listed Town Hall opens in February 2027 after more than a decade behind construction fencing, housing a new, world-class National Music Centre. From April 9–11, the restored venue will stage a new production of Andrew Lloyd Webber’s Sunset Boulevard, starring Tony-nominated performer Keala Settle. —Johanna Thornton

Go for: major resort openings, reimagined experiences, and fresh ways to explore beyond the beach

The Whitsundays' reborn Le Méridien Lindeman Island Resort & Spa and Hook Island eco-lodge are much anticipated for 2027.

The Great Barrier Reef may be the Whitsundays’ headliner, with Whitehaven Beach its opening act. Now, though, there are fresh perspectives on the beloved archipelago. Explore Whitsunday Island beyond its coral reef by hiking the 20-mile, three-day Ngaro Track, which threads through subtropical forests and coastline. Along the way, you'll learn its stories through Traditional Ngaro Custodians. There’s also a new way to experience the Great Barrier Reef itself. Some 40 nautical miles offshore, at Hardy Reef on the outer Great Barrier Reef, Cruise Whitsundays has reimagined its floating Reefworld pontoon as an overnight-only experience: Sleep beneath the stars in one of 12 new glamping-style Reefbeds, or below the waterline in one of just two Reefsuites, Australia’s first underwater accommodations, offering floor-to-ceiling windows onto the reef and, if you’re lucky, a passing turtle. Next door the newly upgraded Heart Pontoon, a second floating platform, caters to day-trippers.

But the biggest news for the Whitsundays is the 2027 rebirth of two long-dormant island resorts. The 209-key Le Méridien Lindeman Island Resort & Spa—the brand’s first Australian resort—will bring an Explore Spa and a raft of water sports to Lindeman Island. More remarkable is the revival of Hook Island, which has been without a resort for more than a decade. The Whitsundays’ rugged second-largest island will get a low-impact eco-lodge with 39 cabins, forest pools, and wellness spaces, making the largely undeveloped landscape of lush rainforest, secluded bays, and color-popping coral reefs available to overnight guests once again.

On the mainland, don’t miss Sollos Estate, Australia’s first agave distillery, which has just opened north of Airlie Beach. The Whitsunday Skyway sightseeing cable car and a new five-star resort in development for Airlie add more reasons to visit this Sunshine State paradise. —Sarah Reid
