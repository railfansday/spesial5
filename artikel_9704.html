Recovering LLM Costs in SaaS Pricing

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Posted on Nov 12 • Originally published at calidadinfotech.com

Yes, you can recover LLM token costs from SaaS customers. First you need to measure those costs for each tenant and each feature. Most SaaS teams do best with a hybrid model: a subscription that includes an AI allowance sized for typical use, plus metered overage or higher tiers for heavy users. Caps on top of that protect your margin.

Every AI request costs you money each time it runs, while a flat price stays the same however much a customer uses.

In classic SaaS, serving one more user costs almost nothing. With LLM features, every prompt, retrieved document and generated answer is billed by the token. The bill also varies a lot by model. CloudZero's breakdown of OpenAI's rate card lists the current flagship at $10 input and $50 output per million tokens, while the cheapest model in the same table costs $0.20 for input. Output tokens cost several times more than input, so long answers and agent loops add up quickly.

A user who runs it 50 times a working day makes about 1,050 calls a month and costs you about $73. If the seat sells for $40, every heavy user loses you money, and light users can't make up the gap if heavy users are common.

Many vendors have moved away from flat pricing for this reason. Metronome's field report found the move to usage-based or hybrid pricing was often a defensive move to deal with real, variable costs rather than a strategy.

Send every model call through one gateway. The gateway tags each call with the tenant, the feature and the model, and records the token counts the provider returns.

Collect at least one full billing cycle of this data before you change any prices. The spread decides your pricing model more than the average does. We cover the setup in more detail in our guide to cutting cloud and LLM costs at a SaaS company.

All three can recover costs. Which one fits depends on how uneven usage is across tenants and how much your buyers need predictable bills.

Hybrid models are now the most common choice. Metronome reports that most enterprise AI deals in 2025 still rely on usage-based or hybrid pricing. Orb, citing Stripe, says that among AI company leaders, 56% use hybrid pricing and 38% use pure usage-based pricing.

Several large vendors already use these models. GitHub Copilot business plans include a set number of premium requests, and an admin policy decides whether to allow charges beyond that or block usage. Intercom sells annual credit buckets with optional pricing per resolution.

Charge business buyers in a unit they recognize, such as a request, a document, a report or a resolution. Use tokens only to set that price internally. That way, when you switch to a cheaper model, your margin improves and the customer's price list doesn't change.

Absorb LLM costs when the cost per tenant is small and stable compared with the price. Pass them on when usage is uneven, heavy or controlled by the customer.

Deciding whether to absorb LLM costs or pass them on

Price LLM costs only after you can see them. Meter every call by tenant and feature, then look at how unevenly usage is spread. Most SaaS companies end up with a hybrid: an included allowance, then overage or tiers, budgets enforced in the gateway, and usage that customers can see before they're billed.

Usually not for business buyers. Tokens are hard to predict and depend on which model you use. Developer-facing API products are the exception, because their buyers often expect to see tokens.

No single markup is right. Start from the gross margin you want on each plan, and allow for retries, embeddings, infrastructure and support as well as tokens. Check what the price of your chosen unit looks like to the buyer against the value they get from it, not only against your cost.

If you charge in your own units rather than tokens, a cheaper model raises your margin without any change to the price list. Decide whether to keep the savings, add more included usage, or lower prices for competitive reasons. Recheck how many units each budget covers whenever a rate card changes.

Doing it mid-term risks breaking trust and the contract itself. A safer approach is to keep current terms until renewal, give clear notice and show customers their actual usage beforehand. That way the new pricing reflects usage they've already seen.

Collect at least one full billing cycle of data tagged by tenant and feature, and longer if usage changes with the season. You need enough data to see the 95th-percentile tenant, not only the average. Pricing on a short sample tends to set quotas too generously or too tightly.
