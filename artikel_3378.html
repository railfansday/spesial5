53 Years Later, Peanuts’ Greatest Thanksgiving Moment Hits Even Harder Today

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Thanksgiving and Peanuts go together so well that it’s hard to imagine one without the other. Snoopy’s been a fixture of the Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade since 1968, and he’s back for the 100th parade this year. Add Snoopy and Woodstock popping up in Google’s 2026 Thanksgiving Doodle, and it’s safe to say the gang has late November covered.

Apple TV’s free streaming window for the 1973 classic A Charlie Brown Thanksgiving already passed on November 14 and 15, but that hasn’t stopped viewers from firing up subscriptions or free trials to relive Charlie Brown’s chaotic backyard feast of buttered toast, popcorn, and jelly beans. The special gets most of the attention every year, and for good reason. But two newspaper strips published 50 years ago deserve a little more of the spotlight.

For all its kid-friendly charm, Peanuts was never just about the punchlines. Charles M. Schulz could capture Charlie Brown’s very real holiday blues or have Linus van Pelt quietly remind everyone what Thanksgiving is about, all without losing that familiar humor. Back in November 1976, he was midway through a five-strip storyline in which a terrified Woodstock disguised himself to avoid becoming turkey-day dinner. Then Schulz delivered two unforgettable strips that put Snoopy’s quiet, fiercely loyal love for his tiny best friend front and center.

Running from November 22 to 26, 1976, right across Thanksgiving Day on November 25, Charles M. Schulz’s five-strip storyline turned one of Peanuts’ sweetest friendships into holiday gold. In the 1973 TV special A Charlie Brown Thanksgiving, Woodstock happily digs into a turkey dinner alongside Snoopy. Three years later, Schulz gave the newspaper version a much sharper twist.

Woodstock is terrified that being a bird in late November makes him a walking Thanksgiving dinner. Snoopy, the only character in the neighborhood who actually understands his chirping, doesn’t laugh off his tiny friend’s panic. He goes straight into protector mode, helping Woodstock put together a disguise with a hat and a fake mustache.

The payoff comes in the very next strip. Woodstock proudly struts past the doghouse in his hat and borrowed mustache, safe from anyone who might mistake him for the main course. Snoopy watches, pleased that his feathered sidekick can walk around in plain sight without anyone realizing he’s a bird. Then comes the line that makes the whole setup:

Thanksgiving is a great day if you don’t get eaten.

Fifty years later, those two strips still land because Schulz turns the holiday upside down with such a simple joke. A cozy Thanksgiving feast becomes a survival mission from a bird’s point of view. Schulz once explained that Woodstock was his lighthearted way of showing how small and vulnerable everyone can feel in an overwhelming world. Snoopy’s response says everything about their friendship: he’s willing to hand over his own disguise props just to help his smallest friend feel safe. That mix of dark humor and genuine warmth is exactly what makes Peanuts feel timeless.

Looking back at those November 1976 strips feels especially fitting now, with Peanuts having another huge year. Fresh off its 75th anniversary, the gang has been everywhere in 2026. Apple TV brought out the new holiday special Snoopy Presents: 12 Days of Snoopy, while the brand kept popping up in major collaborations.

Starbucks kicked off fall in September with a global, Great Pumpkin-inspired merch drop featuring a barista Snoopy plush and secret-menu Frappuccinos. Timex followed in early November with four winter watches featuring a bundled-up Snoopy and Woodstock across its T80 and Weekender lines. And on December 8, Crocs is set to release a red puffer-inspired Classic Lined Clog starring Joe Cool, Snoopy’s sunglasses-wearing alter ego.

In a story of perseverance very much in keeping with Peanuts itself, Charles Schulz stood his ground once he included this new character in 1968.

All that modern hype keeps Charlie Brown’s world in front of a new crowd, but Peanuts’ staying power was built on the newspaper page. Fifty years later, Schulz’s Thanksgiving punchline still lands exactly as it did the first time. In just a few panels, he could bring together dark comedy, quiet loyalty, and genuine holiday warmth. Nobody played with words quite like Schulz, and these strips are a lovely reminder of why his work still hits home.
