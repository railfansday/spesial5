Turning Audience Attention Into Better Marketing Decisions

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

This post was created in partnership with Viant

Two ads can cost the same and reach the same number of viewers yet receive dramatically different levels of attention. Traditional advertising metrics can tell marketers how many ads were served, but not how many people paid attention.

At ADWEEK Exchange in Minneapolis, the final stop on a national roadshow from Viant and ADWEEK, marketing leaders explored how a better understanding of viewer attention can help advertisers make smarter media investments, particularly as brands compete for audiences during high-value live sports programming.

Hassan Babajane, chief revenue officer at TVision, opened the event by explaining how traditional TV metrics, such as reach, viewability, and completion rates, reveal little about whether viewers paid attention to an ad.

TVision addresses this problem by measuring attention at the individual level in consenting households. The company tracks whether viewers are in the room and looking at the screen. “We don’t model attention; we actually measure it, and we measure it from real-life human experiences at the person level,” Babajane explained.

This more precise measurement shows advertisers how attention varies across streaming apps, content, and even individual moments within a program. TVision’s “State of Streaming” data shows that viewers aren’t paying attention to the television approximately 60% of the time, while no one is in the room for another 30% of viewing time. This highlights a fundamental limitation of impression-based measurement: An ad can be served without being seen.

Live sports is one of television’s most valuable attention environments and offers a particularly valuable illustration of how attention changes in real time.

Babajane demonstrated this with data from a recent Minnesota Vikings football game against the Detroit Lions. Attention increased from 53.8% in the first quarter to 54.3% in the second, 58.3% in the third, and 58.6% in the fourth. Minute-by-minute analysis also revealed fluctuations tied to specific moments in the game, including a 10.7-point increase after a Lions field goal and Vikings touchdown.

The data shows that even within a live sporting event, attention can vary considerably, despite advertisers potentially paying similar rates for spots throughout the game. As brands invest heavily in live sports, TVision can help marketers look beyond the size of an audience to identify the moments when viewers are most engaged and get more value from their existing media budgets.

The brand panel that followed explored how marketers can use attention data to make better advertising and marketing decisions. Moderated by Will Lee, CEO of ADWEEK, the discussion featured Lana Slygh, SVP, head of brand and integrated marketing at Thrivent; Nick Lopezzo, VP of brand marketing and fan engagement at the Minnesota Vikings; Kristin Atherton, senior director of marketing planning and activation, global media and incentives, at General Mills; and Chris Vanderhook, COO and co-founder of Viant.

The panelists started the session by discussing gaps in CTV measurement. Atherton pointed to the difficulty of connecting media attention with creative performance, emphasizing that reaching an attentive audience means little if the creative fails to communicate the intended message.

For the Minnesota Vikings, capturing attention means engaging a broad and evolving fan base. Lopezzo described the challenge of creating content for longtime season ticket holders, who may care about the game’s technical details, as well as younger audiences drawn to players’ fashion, personalities, and culture.

“Ultimately, we are in the attention business,” Lopezzo shared. “As we think about the segmentation of consumers or fans that we serve, it’s probably a lot broader than a lot of more traditional businesses.”

The team must balance content that supports current commercial opportunities with programming that builds relationships with the next generation of fans.

Vanderhook highlighted another limitation of CTV buying, explaining: “You can buy Paramount Plus, you can buy Hulu, but you can’t buy the show.” That lack of control makes it harder for advertisers to match their ads with relevant content.

Slygh encountered a similar issue at Thrivent, where her TVision report identified underperforming Freeform inventory bundled with Disney+. She sees attention data as a way to negotiate with networks. She sees attention data as a way to negotiate with networks, asking for more of the inventory earning attention and less of what isn’t.

She also emphasized the importance of acting on those insights while campaigns are still running. “I’m not waiting for a post-campaign report. This is not a set it and forget it strategy anymore,” Slygh said.

The panel also discussed how brands account for viewer attention when developing creative and deciding where to spend. Atherton emphasized the relationship between media and creative: “Media can give you the stage, but creative puts on the show,” she said.

General Mills adjusts its strategy and media spending accordingly, using recognizable brand cues and simple messaging in lower-attention environments and more complex storytelling when viewers are watching closely.

Tim Vanderhook, CEO and co-founder of Viant, closed the event by explaining how marketers can incorporate attention into their daily decisions. “Replace the word ‘attention’ with the word ‘actual.’ It’s not attentive reach; it’s your actual reach. That’s what you got,” he said.

He outlined how advertisers can use TVision reports to evaluate attention across media placements and creative, then apply those findings through Viant’s DSP to adjust campaigns while they’re running.

Vanderhook illustrated how even a modest increase in attention can produce more attentive impressions from the same media budget. For example, a campaign delivering 100 million impressions at 40% attention generates 40 million attentive impressions. Increasing attention to 52% would bring that total to 52 million, a 30% increase at the same spend. He recommended starting with one campaign and an existing business KPI, making adjustments based on attention data, and measuring the results within a quarter.
