Most Venture Capital Is Going To Companies That Are Founded Or Led By Billionaires

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

The majority of U.S. venture capital nowadays goes to companies founded or led by men who are already billionaires.

This is not a difficult stat to unearth. So far this year, just seven companies raised more than half of all seed- through growth-stage investment. They’re mostly familiar names: OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI (now part of SpaceX), Anduril Industries, Databricks, Prometheus and The Boring Co.

These companies raised more than $260 billion in equity funding in the first 10 months of the year. That’s just over 50% of the U.S. seed and venture investment over the same period, per Crunchbase data.

Founders and CEOs of these seven companies are also well-known — and exceptionally  well-off to boot.

Two founders are particularly famous. They are the world’s wealthiest men: Elon Musk and Jeff Bezos.

Musk founded xAI and The Boring Co., and also co-founded OpenAI, (though he has no known equity stake in the latter). And while outside investors funded these companies, Musk could have also afforded to do so himself.

Ditto for Bezos, who in 2025 launched the physical AI startup Prometheus, which raised $12 billion earlier this year. Bezos’ own net worth, meanwhile, is calculated at more than $350 billion.

The two highest valuation startups — OpenAI and the soon-to-be-public Anthropic — also have prominent billionaire founders and CEOs. However, they have mixed backstories to their wealth.

OpenAI co-founder and CEO Sam Altman primarily made his vast wealth through investments in Y Combinator startups, including Stripe and Reddit. Altman has said he does not have an equity stake in OpenAI beyond a sliver from an old YC round. Co-founder Greg Brockman, by contrast, has a larger stake in OpenAI that was reportedly valued around $30 billion earlier this year.

At Anthropic, meanwhile, all seven company co-founders have similar-sized equity stakes, and each is worth billions. This includes the AI giant’s most famous co-founder, CEO Dario Amodei.

Databricks and Anduril, meanwhile, both have founders serving as CEOs who have amassed billion-dollar-plus net worths during their tenures.

Databricks co-founder and CEO Ali Ghodsi has a reported $7.6 billion net worth, with the company he leads valued at around $190 billion following an August financing.

Anduril co-founder and CEO Brian Schimpf, meanwhile, has an estimated $1.5 billion net worth as a result of his stake in the $61 billion valuation defense industry disruptor. Co-founder Palmer Luckey, who sold his virtual reality startup Oculus to Meta for $2 billion in 2014 before starting Anduril, has an estimated $5.9 billion net worth.

Several other founders and CEOs of startups that raised some of this year’s largest rounds are billionaires too. This includes Wonder founder Marc Lore, Figure founder Brett Adcock, and Surge AI founder and CEO Edwin Chen, among others.

We can split the billionaires who founded this year’s most heavily funded startups into two broad subcategories. There are those who became billionaires through the startup that just raised capital. And there are those who were already billionaires through prior companies or investments.

Ghodsi, Amodei and Brockman fit in the first group. In the second group, of course, we can put Musk and Bezos, as well as Altman and Luckey, who became a 21-year-old billionaire following the Oculus sale.

Historically, neither billionaire type was a particularly common recipient of venture funding rounds. The term “unicorn” as a moniker for private companies valued at $1 billion or more only entered the lexicon in 2013, when this asset class became noticeably large. In prior decades, founders and CEOs mostly only hit billionaire status after their startups went public or sold in high-priced acquisitions.

But times change. And in tandem, our image of what a startup funding recipient looks like should evolve as well.

True, quite a bit of capital still flows to the stereotypical venture recipients: first-time founders or serial entrepreneurs whose prior companies didn’t make them fabulously wealthy. But a majority of venture funding now does not go to these classic startup types.

Instead, it goes to companies with valuations already high enough to warrant billionaire status for their stakeholders as well as ventures founded by men who are already billionaires.
