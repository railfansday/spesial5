A ‘mismatch in timing’: Data center power demand is growing 19% a year. The grid is growing 2%

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

The tech industry is famous for moving fast. The power grid is famous for not. That collision now sits at the center of the AI buildout, a project estimated to cost trillions: Citigroup projects Big Tech will spend more than $2.8 trillion on AI infrastructure through 2029, and a Brookings study puts U.S. investment at $10.3 trillion between 2025 and 2032.

The grid isn’t built for that pace. Since 2000, power to data centers has risen an average of 19% a year while U.S. grid capacity has expanded just 2% annually, according to an analysis by insurance broker Howden. Data center power use is growing 10 times faster than the system meant to supply it.

That gap has become one of the biggest bottlenecks facing the AI industry. And after this month’s midterms, in which voters rejected data center projects almost everywhere they appeared on the ballot, power is now a political problem, too.

Data centers took about 5% of U.S. grid capacity in 2025. Howden estimates that to hold that share through 2030 at current growth rates, they would have to source up to 58% of their power off the grid, roughly 110 gigawatts, a level the firm itself considers unlikely.

Consumption tells the same story. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory projects data centers could use as much as 12% of U.S. electricity by 2028, about six times their 1.9% share in 2018, before the AI boom.

Rob Gramlich, president of consulting firm Grid Strategies, told Fortune in September that there’s a real “mismatch in timing” between how quickly data centers want power and how quickly the grid can expand. Grid capacity is slow to build because thousands of interconnected parts must work together, he said, which requires “deliberate study and planning” that can take years.

The industry is also rusty. After roughly 25 years of little growth in power demand, Gramlich said, utilities “got out of practice on building new infrastructure.”

Kathryn Burke, who leads U.S. specialty energy and power growth at insurance firm Marsh, told Fortune she expects around 50% to 60% of data center projects to be delayed beyond the one-to-two-year timelines developers are counting on.

Public backlash over grid strain and rising residential electricity prices has added a second hurdle. On Nov. 3, voters rejected data center development in 40 of 41 local ballot measures, the same night Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress.

Opposition runs deepest among the young. A University of Pennsylvania Annenberg Public Policy Center survey conducted this summer found that 70% of adults under 30 oppose new data centers in their area, compared with 61% of all adults.

Voters’ fears aren’t unfounded. Northern Virginia hosts the world’s largest concentration of data centers, and Virginia sells 59% of its electricity to commercial customers, including data center clients. As of 2025, commercial energy demand exceeds residential demand in 23 states and Washington, D.C.

A study in Nature Energy by Cornell University researchers Cheng Feng and Fengqi You found that data centers raised wholesale power prices unevenly. In California’s southern Bay Area, data centers were estimated to lift wholesale prices by nearly 20% in 2025; around Dallas–Fort Worth, the increase approached 17%.

Across the four grids studied, the researchers found that data centers paid only 38% of the infrastructure costs tied to their growth in 2025: $3.12 billion of an $8.25 billion bill.

Wholesale prices make up only about 30% to 50% of a household bill, but experts say residential customers will end up covering part of the AI buildout. The Cornell researchers found the costs land hardest on lower-income and rural households, a pattern they call a “paradoxical mismatch.”

“The idea of now having to grow up and compete in terms of water prices and energy prices with a tech company is not something I can say I’m excited about,” Elijah Lee, an 18-year-old organizer with the Gen Z advocacy group Voters of Tomorrow, told Fortune before the midterms.

Tech companies are adapting by moving into energy themselves, under a model the industry calls “bring your own power.”

Some states now require it. In August, Delaware became the first state to legislatively enact a “bring your own generation” policy, requiring large energy users such as data centers to produce or secure 100% of the power they use, including a share of clean energy, within 10 years of starting operations.

President Donald Trump’s Ratepayer Protection Pledge asks signers to “build, bring, or buy” the new-generation resources and electricity they need. Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Oracle and xAI signed in March, and more than 200 utilities, cooperatives, developers and Republican governors have since joined. The pledge is not binding.

On-site generation is still small but growing fast. BloombergNEF counted just 2 gigawatts of on-site gas generation operating at data centers worldwide as of March. It has since tracked 181 gigawatts of announced gas-fired generation proposed alongside data centers, more than 90% of it in the U.S.

SpaceX, for one, has proposed a roughly $24 billion supercomputing facility and gas-fired power plant at Starbase, Texas. The shift carries a climate cost: planned gas plants for data centers could raise U.S. power-sector emissions by 20%, according to a Bloomberg News analysis published by Fortune.

It isn’t a cure-all. Data center development experts say the model isn’t a long-term solution because major swings in power demand at AI data centers can cause outages and damage on-site generation equipment.

The next tests come quickly. Democrats take control of congressional committees in January. Texas Gov.-elect Gina Hinojosa has proposed a moratorium on new data center development, and Ohio, which already has 125 local data center moratoriums, is set to add more.

While those buildouts are planned, none are promised.
