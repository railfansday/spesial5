5 Interesting Startup Deals You May Have Missed: Reflecting Sunlight From Space, Better Breathing And Smarter Garbage Trucks

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

This is a monthly column that runs down five interesting startup funding deals that may have flown under the radar. Check out our previous entry here.

What if solar farms could keep generating electricity after sunset with a little help from mirrors orbiting Earth? Or researchers could send an experiment into space for less than the price of a car?

Those are two of the more unusual ideas that attracted startup investment in the past month. Our latest roundup also includes a company bringing AI to the old-school business of garbage hauling, another rethinking how factories are programmed, and a consumer brand betting that better breathing could become a big business. Let’s take a closer look.

Solar power has one rather obvious limitation: The sun goes down.

To that end, Reflect Orbital is working on a solution that sounds straight out of science fiction. The Hawthorne, California-based startup wants to put giant mirrors in orbit to redirect sunlight toward specific locations on Earth, potentially allowing solar farms to keep generating electricity longer after sunset.

The company recently announced $73.5 million in new funding, bringing its total funding to more than $102 million, per Crunchbase. Existing investors Sequoia Capital, Lux Capital and Starship Ventures participated, alongside new backers including Buckley Ventures, Convective Capital, The Uncommon Group and Salesforce Ventures 1.

Reflect Orbital is developing lightweight, foldable reflectors that it says can be launched aboard satellites and deployed in low Earth orbit. The company envisions using them not only to extend solar power generation, but also to illuminate disaster zones for nighttime search-and-rescue operations or construction sites after dark.

The idea remains unproven in orbit, however. Reflect Orbital is targeting the second quarter of 2027 for its first demonstration mission, which has received FCC approval. The funding will help support those initial launches, further development of its reflector technology, and commercial partnerships.

The raise comes during a record year for space technology funding. By late August, investors had poured $20.3 billion into space- and satellite-related startups globally, per Crunchbase data, already comfortably exceeding previous annual records.

Nasal strips have been around for decades, but Intake Breathing Technology thinks there’s room for a serious upgrade.

The Santa Barbara, California-based consumer wellness company recently announced a $62 million investment from Camino Partners, the investment platform founded by KIND Snacks entrepreneur Daniel Lubetzky.

Intake makes a patented magnetic nasal dilator designed to open nasal passages and improve airflow. Rather than relying on a conventional adhesive strip stretched across the nose, its system uses adhesive tabs and a reusable magnetic band to pull the sides of the nose outward.

The company markets its product to people looking to breathe more comfortably while sleeping, exercising or going about their daily routines. It says it has sold more than 100 million pairs of breathing tabs since 2023, with triple-digit revenue growth over the past three years.

Intake plans to use the new capital to expand beyond its direct-to-consumer business and into more mainstream retail channels. That’s where Lubetzky’s experience building KIND into a household name could prove useful.

Its funding comes as venture investors show renewed enthusiasm for fitness and wellness. Startups in those categories raised more than $3.6 billion globally in the first half of 2026, putting the sector on pace for its strongest funding year since 2022, Crunchbase data shows.

Interestingly, much of this year’s investment into the sector has flowed to wearable devices and health platforms built around AI and data. Intake is a somewhat different bet: a relatively simple physical product that has apparently found a sizable audience without promising to reinvent healthcare through software.

Taking out the trash isn’t exactly rocket science. But managing the businesses that collect it turns out to be surprisingly complicated.

San Francisco-based TrashLab Software recently raised $12 million in a growth funding round led by Ventana, with participation from existing investors Bienville Capital, Harlem Capital and CreativeCo.

The startup builds an AI-powered operating platform for waste-hauling businesses, an industry where many smaller operators still rely on spreadsheets, paper routes and clunky software to manage their fleets.

TrashLab says its platform combines dispatching, route planning, customer management, billing and payments. Its AI agent, called Haul-E, can answer customer calls and take orders, while cameras in garbage trucks can help document problems such as overflowing dumpsters, blocked containers or bins that weren’t put out for collection.

The company says hundreds of waste haulers across the U.S. and Canada now use its software, and that more than 5 million tons of material have been managed through its platform.

It’s not the only startup finding an AI opportunity in the waste industry. We recently covered Greyparrot, a London-based company using computer vision to identify and analyze waste moving through recycling facilities. While Greyparrot focuses on what happens after trash reaches a processing plant, TrashLab is tackling the messy logistics of getting it there.

Factory automation has come a long way, but programming the machines that run industrial production lines can still require specialized expertise and a lot of time.

Gardena, California-based Neuron Industries wants to make the process a lot simpler.

The startup recently announced a $10 million seed round co-led by FCVC and Obvious Ventures, with participation from Y Combinator, Thin Line Capital, Zee Prime Capital and Founder Factor.

Neuron is developing a new kind of industrial controller — the hardware that coordinates sensors, motors and other equipment in factories — alongside AI-powered software that lets engineers describe what they want a machine to do in ordinary language.

Its system generates control code that engineers can inspect, modify and test in simulation before deploying it to real equipment. The goal is to reduce the time and specialized programming knowledge needed to get new production lines up and running.

The startup says it has already demonstrated its technology on existing factory equipment, including replacing an aging controller on a wood-processing line with roughly three hours of engineering work and eight hours of downtime. It plans to release its Cortex AI Controller commercially in early 2027.

The investment fits into a broader surge in funding for physical AI — technology that applies artificial intelligence to robots, autonomous vehicles, industrial equipment and other machines in the real world. Startups in those categories raised $47.4 billion globally across 521 deals in the first half of 2026, nearly 4x the amount invested in the second half of 2025, according to Crunchbase data.

Sending an experiment into space has traditionally required years of preparation, specialized engineering expertise and a very substantial budget.

Austria-based Tumbleweed wants to bring the cost of entry down to as little as 15,000 euros.

The Vienna startup recently raised 6.9 million euros (roughly $8 million) in seed funding led by PactVC, with participation from Vanagon Ventures, Prototype Capital and EWOR, among other investors. The company describes it as the largest space-tech seed round raised in Austria to date.

Tumbleweed develops standardized containers, called Pods, that allow researchers and companies to conduct experiments in microgravity without building their own satellites or navigating the complex certification process required for space missions.

Customers can place their experiments inside the pre-certified containers, which provide power and communications capabilities while in orbit. The startup handles the logistics of launching the payloads and transmitting experimental data back to Earth.

Unlike many space startups still working toward their first launch, Tumbleweed has already put hardware in orbit. Its inaugural Oasis Alpha satellite launched in July carrying experiments for four paying customers, including research into biological processes and space-resource technologies. The mission was fully booked a year before launch.

Tumbleweed says its new funding will help it carry out three more missions in 2027 and develop a next-generation spacecraft that would allow customers to bring physical samples back to Earth beginning in 2028.

Tumbleweed is part of a growing European space startup ecosystem, although funding remains concentrated elsewhere. As of late August, European companies had attracted about 10% of global space-tech venture investment in 2026, compared with the more than 60% going to U.S. startups, Crunchbase data shows.
