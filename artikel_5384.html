5 recalls and a federal rule that show how ingredient problems spread

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

An ingredient recall can spread across many brands when manufacturers share one supplier, as four past recalls and an FDA tracing rule help explain

A single batch of an ingredient can leave one supplier's plant and end up inside products sold under many different brand names. Each of those products carries its own label and its own lot code. When that batch turns out to be contaminated, the recall cannot stop at the supplier's door. It has to follow the ingredient to every company that bought it.

That tracing problem explains why one ingredient recall can reach so far. Manufacturers often buy shared inputs such as flavorings, proteins, pastes and active drug ingredients from a small number of suppliers. A processor may mix one supplier's batch into many finished foods or medicines. Each buyer then has to work out which of its own products used the affected material. It also has to find which batches of those products reached customers.

The basic chain is short. A supplier sells to manufacturers. Manufacturers sell under brand names, and retailers stock those brands side by side. A shopper sees separate products. The supply chain behind them may trace back to one shared source.

Lot codes are the tool meant to make that work possible. A lot code lets a company say exactly which production batches are affected. The company can then pull those batches and leave the rest on sale. The system only holds together if every buyer in the chain keeps records linking incoming ingredient lots to the finished products that used them. A missing record at any step makes it harder to say which finished products are affected.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration sorts recalls into classes based on health risk. Class I is the most serious category. It applies where there is a reasonable probability that using the product will cause serious adverse health consequences or death. Class II and Class III cover lesser risks.

The five examples below show the pattern in different settings. They include a pet food recall, two food ingredient recalls tied to Salmonella, a generic blood pressure drug recall and a federal rule written to speed up tracing. They are a selection, not a complete or ranked list. Each one shows a different part of the tracing problem.

The 2007 Menu Foods recall showed how one tainted input can surface under several pet food brands at once. Wheat gluten contaminated with melamine came from a supplier in China. It went into food that Menu Foods produced for a range of labels.

Menu Foods, based in Ontario, Canada, announced its recall in March 2007. The company worked largely as a contract manufacturer. It made wet foods, such as chunks in gravy, that other companies sold under their own names. Those customers included national brands and store brands.

That business model explains why the problem crossed brand lines. A pet owner reading a can saw a familiar label, not the name of the plant that made it. Nothing on the packaging linked one brand to another. A single contaminated ingredient at one manufacturer still reached shelves under more than one brand name.

Wheat gluten is a concentrated protein extracted from wheat. In wet pet food it helps bind ingredients and form chunks with a meat-like texture. It also contributes to the protein content printed on the label.

Melamine is an industrial chemical used to make plastics and resins. It is rich in nitrogen. Common protein tests estimate protein by measuring nitrogen. Melamine can therefore make a diluted ingredient look more protein-rich than it is.

Investigators also found cyanuric acid, a related compound, in the contaminated material. Melamine and cyanuric acid can combine to form crystals in the kidneys. Affected cats and dogs suffered kidney failure.

The gluten passed through more than one company before it reached pet food. ChemNutra, a Las Vegas importer, brought it into the U.S. from Xuzhou Anying Biologic Technology Development in China. In 2008, a federal grand jury indicted both companies and ChemNutra's owners.

Each handoff added distance between the source of the contamination and the pet owner. Investigators had to work backward from sick animals to finished food, then to the plant and the gluten shipments. They then had to work forward again to every product made with those shipments.

The recall did not stop at Menu Foods. Some other pet food makers that had used wheat gluten from the same importer also recalled their own products. The trail from one importer's shipments led into several separate companies' production lines.

Peanut Corporation of America was not a household brand. It worked as a processor, turning raw peanuts into products that other companies bought in bulk. Investigators focused on its plant in Blakely, Georgia. A second company plant, in Plainview, Texas, also came under scrutiny and was shut down in 2009. The outbreak strain was Salmonella Typhimurium.

That structure shaped the recall. Once investigators identified the Georgia plant as the source, the recall moved outward through the processor's list of buyers. A peanut butter sandwich cracker, a peanut ice cream and a candy bar could all trace back to that one plant.

The case also carried legal consequences. Peanut Corporation of America filed for bankruptcy in February 2009. Its former chief executive, Stewart Parnell, was later convicted on federal charges connected to the outbreak, including conspiracy and fraud. In 2015 he was sentenced to 28 years in prison.

In 2010, a Salmonella problem was tied to hydrolyzed vegetable protein made by Basic Food Flavors of Las Vegas. Food companies that used the ingredient recalled products containing it. The case showed that a minor flavoring, present in small amounts, can still set off many recalls.

Hydrolyzed vegetable protein, often shortened to HVP, is a flavor enhancer. It is made by breaking down protein from plant sources such as soy, corn or wheat into its amino acids. The result has a savory, brothlike taste that deepens the flavor of processed foods.

Manufacturers add it to soups, sauces, dips, snack seasonings and similar products. They use it in small quantities. U.S. ingredient lists run in descending order by weight. HVP therefore tends to sit near the end of a label.

That small dose helps explain how one supplier's output can reach so many products. A modest batch of a flavor enhancer can season a large volume of finished food. The same ingredient can also travel inside a seasoning blend or flavor base that another company mixes before selling it on.

Each of those steps adds a layer to the trace. A food maker that bought a seasoning blend may need records from its blender to learn whether HVP from the affected supplier was inside. Only then can it check its own production records for affected products.

Dry ingredients pose a particular challenge with Salmonella. The bacteria cannot multiply without moisture. They can, however, survive for long periods in low-moisture foods such as powders and seasonings. A dry ingredient does not become safe simply by sitting on a shelf.

How a product is eaten also matters. Thorough cooking can kill Salmonella in a soup simmered at home. Seasoned chips and dips served straight from the container are often eaten with no further cooking.

U.S. rules require a protein hydrolysate's label name to identify its food source, as in "hydrolyzed soy protein." That naming tells consumers what plant the protein came from. It does not tell them which company made it. That is the detail a recall depends on.

The 2018 valsartan recall showed that medicines can follow the same supply pattern as food. Several generic versions of the blood pressure drug were recalled because they contained an impurity called N-nitrosodimethylamine, or NDMA. Their active ingredient came from Zhejiang Huahai Pharmaceutical in China.

Valsartan belongs to a drug class called angiotensin II receptor blockers, often shortened to ARBs. Doctors prescribe it for high blood pressure and heart failure. Many patients take it daily for years. A recall therefore created practical work for pharmacies and prescribers, on top of the safety question.

NDMA is a nitrosamine, a type of chemical that can form during some industrial and food processes. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies NDMA as probably carcinogenic to humans. Its presence in valsartan tablets led to regulatory action in the U.S. and Europe.

The route from one supplier to many labels runs through the active pharmaceutical ingredient, or API. That is the compound that produces a drug's effect. Generic drug companies often buy their API from specialist chemical manufacturers instead of making it themselves.

Those companies then turn the API into finished tablets. They add inactive ingredients, press and package the product, and sell it under their own names. The chemistry happens at one company, while the label belongs to another.

That division of labor meant one API maker could sit behind several finished drugs. Once the impurity was traced to valsartan from Zhejiang Huahai Pharmaceutical, each buyer of that ingredient had to check its own batches. Bottles with different company names on them shared the same upstream source.

The check also had to cover combination pills. Valsartan is sold alone and in single tablets paired with other blood pressure drugs. Any of those products made with the affected ingredient fell into the same review.

Generic substitution made the problem harder for patients to see. A pharmacy can fill a valsartan prescription with any approved generic version. The maker can change from one refill to the next. A patient may not know which company made the tablets in a given bottle.

Pharmacists had to match the manufacturer name and lot number on each container against recall notices. The FDA advised patients on recalled valsartan to keep taking it until a doctor or pharmacist provided a replacement or another treatment. Stopping a blood pressure drug abruptly carries its own risks.

The concern later widened beyond valsartan. Nitrosamine impurities were found in some versions of other ARBs, including losartan and irbesartan, and those products were recalled too. The FDA later issued guidance asking drug makers to assess and control nitrosamine risks in their medicines.

The FDA food traceability rule requires companies that handle foods on the agency's Food Traceability List to keep extra records. Those records are meant to help investigators follow a food and its ingredients through the supply chain faster during an outbreak or recall.

The rule comes from Section 204 of the FDA Food Safety Modernization Act, the food safety law signed in January 2011. The FDA finalized the traceability requirements in November 2022. Compliance was originally due on January 20, 2026. The agency has since extended that date by 30 months, to July 20, 2028.

The rule is built around specific points in a food's journey. The FDA calls these points critical tracking events. They include harvesting, cooling, initial packing, shipping, receiving and transformation. At each event, a company must record key data elements, such as quantities, locations, dates and a traceability lot code.

The traceability lot code ties those records together. A code is assigned when a food is first packed, first received on land after harvest at sea, or transformed into a new product. Transformation covers steps such as cutting, mixing or cooking a food into something else.

When a firm transforms a listed food, it assigns a new lot code to the result. It also records which incoming lots went into it. That link lets an investigator move from a finished product back to a specific ingredient batch.

The rule also sets a deadline for producing records. When the FDA asks, a covered company must hand over the information within 24 hours or within a reasonable time the agency agrees to. In some situations, such as an outbreak investigation, the firm must provide it as an electronic sortable spreadsheet.

The list itself is limited. It names foods including leafy greens, fresh herbs, tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, melons and sprouts. It also covers shell eggs, nut butters, cheeses other than hard cheeses, finfish, crustaceans, molluscan shellfish and ready-to-eat deli salads. Foods that contain a listed item as an ingredient can also be covered. That generally applies only while the listed ingredient stays in the same form, such as fresh.

This scope means the rule does not reach every ingredient. Dry flavorings, flours and many other shared inputs fall outside it. For those, tracing still rests on the one-step-forward, one-step-back records required under the Bioterrorism Act of 2002. Those records show where a company got an ingredient and where it sent its products. They do not carry the Section 204 rule's critical tracking events, key data elements or 24-hour spreadsheet requirement.
