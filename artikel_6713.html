Restaurant Booths Are Back—and Better Than Ever

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

“When I walk into a restaurant and see a worn-in bright red-and-white striped booth, I already know it’s going to be good,” says Anna Albury, designer and writer behind Cool Stuff NYC, the cult-followed newsletter she curates with her husband, Garrett. She, like many booth loyalists, can wax poetic about her favorite seats in the city, citing the coat-rack-clad booths of Cobble Hill’s Long Island Bar, Le Veau d’Or’s cherry-red banquettes on the Upper East Side, and Carroll Gardens newcomer Trudie’s, which features charming two-top booths.

While booths have long been considered the “best seat in the house,” there was a time in the not-so-distant past when business diners preferred private dining rooms and social diners sought out communal tables. But there’s good news for booth lovers like Albury: this past year has brought on a booth boom of never-before-seen proportions. Restaurants, hotels, and members’ clubs of all distinctions are installing booths and banquettes by the mile. From design-forward diners like London-based Supernova to fine dining institutions like the Roman and Williams–designed Marcel and swanky cocktail lounges like Andres Diaz’s newly opened Bar Leone, without a doubt, booths are back.

Mohair-upholstered booths are a key feature of the newly opened Marcel restaurant in New York, designed by Roman and Williams.

Interior designers who cater to hospitality venues are seeing the shift in demand firsthand. “Across daytime and evening-only restaurant projects, clients are coming in with booths already on the brief, more as a starting point than an afterthought,” says Sarita Posada, whose eponymous New York City-based design firm is behind some of the hottest tables in Paris and London, including the aforementioned Supernova, as well as Cherry and 74 Duke. “I think people are a little fatigued overall on overexposure, whether that be digitally or when they go out,” she adds, citing booths as a way to carve out your own piece of real estate in a room.

It wasn’t all that long ago that communal tables reigned supreme as the seating setup du jour. David Prior, founder of the editorial-led travel company Prior, cites the farm-to-table movement and food leaders like Alice Waters as popularizing long communal tables roughly from 2009 to 2016. He also points to Jim Denevan’s beloved roving dinner series, Outstanding in the Field, which became famous for its extremely long dinner tables.

But this all changed in 2020, when the pandemic transformed how people ate and gathered. During that period—and the 18 months that followed—private dining became the default. “People wanted to be hermetically sealed,” says Prior with a laugh.

Sleek leather booths at Le Cafe Nac in London, designed by Sarita Posada Interiors.

Now, Prior says that he’s seeing what he calls an “alone-together moment,” and booth seating sits perfectly within that larger movement. “We want to have our intimate moments, but we also want to feel that we’re part of a similar thing,” he says, citing his recent launch party for his podcast, Traveling Through, where groups of people clustered in booths like small islands within a greater archipelago. “It’s all about connection right now, because people are so disconnected.”

Red leather booths at Bar Primi Penn District, designed by Meyer Davis.

Cafe Zaffri, the stylish Mediterranean restaurant within The Twenty Two members’ club in Union Square, is one such setting known to welcome the power lunch crowd in its sumptuous horseshoe booths. “People still want to experience the energy of a lively dining room, but they also want space for real conversation and connection,” says Leigh Salem, a partner at Post Company, whose firm designed the space.

Velvet booths at Cafe Zaffri, inside the New York outpost of The Twenty Two.

From a design perspective, booths also add a sense of storiedness to a space. “There’s something about the memory and history they carry in great, old rooms like Bemelmans Bar or Minetta Tavern,” says Posada, who installed booths in two of her recent restaurant projects to infuse the rooms with intimacy and nostalgia.

But today’s booths are adding a modern twist. At Supernova, Posada drew inspiration from 1970s American diners but made the space cinematic with careful restraint on color—keeping to chrome, black, and butter yellow. In Auric Room 1915, the members-only supper club and speakeasy at Lone Mountain Ranch in Big Sky, cowhide booths bring levity to the space while staying entirely on brand. And how could one miss the chocolate-brown banquettes at Marcel in the iconic Breuer building that made the rounds on Instagram with its buzzy opening?

A ’70s twist on the booth at Supernova in London, designed by Sarita Posada Interiors.

More than just contributing to a feeling, booths add functionality. At the beloved West Village institution Demo, booths were “one of the best ways to utilize a narrow pathway,” says co-owner Jacob Nass. In Bufon, the team’s second restaurant, the perimeter of the dining room is lined with blue banquettes, creating an almost conversation-pit feel.

While booths might look simple, designing one is more technical than one might think. Davis notes it’s important to consider how seating supports different dining durations, service styles, and group sizes and says that the firm carefully evaluates material durability, placement, and seat height when installing a booth. “We use a range of booth typologies which include perimeter banquettes that define a room’s edge, curved or semi-enclosed booths, and integrated seating that becomes part of the architectural millwork.” All of this is in service of supporting the overall design concept.

Outdoor booths are a key feature of the Tatjana von Stein–designed Hotel Corazon in Mallorca.

Booths work in the most serious and stately of spaces, as well as the most fun. Hotel Corazon, the beloved Mallorca hotel run by fashion photographer Kate Bellm and her husband, artist Edgar Lopez, installed bespoke round booths last spring in its revamped restaurant. “The intention was to create a space that feels intimate, warm, and relaxed, while retaining a sense of playfulness,” says designer Tatjana von Stein, who brought the space to life with circular booths crafted from powder-coated metal and made in Portugal with the hotel’s sun motif. “We wanted guests to feel comfortable lingering, connecting, and enjoying the convivial spirit that sits at the heart of Hotel Corazon.”

At One4One, the chic sports bar opened by Dylan Hales and Ronnie Flynn, booths are part of the owners’ broader mission to “create a true local hang.” At The Flower Shop, their first foray into hospitality, there’s a brass plaque above the corner booth that reads “Captain’s Quarters,” reserving it for friends and family.

The expansive, warmly lit booths at One4One.

As you might imagine, securing one of the highly sought-after seats at One4One or The Flower Shop isn’t something the owners take lightly. “A booth creates comfort and, in a way, commitment. You get a booth when you mean it. When you’re settling in for the night, waiting for more people to join, and really planting your flag in a place,” says Flynn. “That is, if you’re lucky enough to snag one.”
