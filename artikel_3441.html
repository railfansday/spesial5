Why Sunrise On The Reaping's Movie Cuts The Most Brutal Death In Hunger Games Book History & It Was Wrong

Warning: Spoilers for The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping

The Hunger Games series has never been one to shy away from putting its characters through the most unimaginable horrors. But the latest movie in the franchise, The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, just might take the cake. Author Suzanne Collins took the brutality in this one to a whole new (and devastating) level, putting readers through some of the darkest moments in the franchise's history. From the Capitol's calculated cruelty to the horrifying propaganda they promote, this story is a reminder of just how little control the districts held before Katniss entered the Games.

Set during the Second Quarter Quell, the latest from the Hunger Games franchise centers around one of the most brutal Games yet, with 48 tributes fighting to the death. With twice as many children thrown into the arena, the stakes are that much higher and the losses are seemingly endless. The book itself doesn't hold back from these heart wrenching losses either, going into the nitty-gritty gory details of what really went down in the arena.

While it's totally understandable that the filmmakers need to make adjustments to the story to maintain its PG-13 rating, several of the novel's more disturbing moments unfortunately didn't make the cut. One particularly disturbing moment was missing from Sunrise on the Reaping's ending, which was the grisly death of Wellie.

One of the most horrifying moments in the Sunrise on the Reaping book is when Wellie meets her demise. While the movie doesn't nearly showcase how gruesome her death is, Suzanne Collins did not hold back. During the Games, Silka, District 1's tribute, kills Wellie by cutting off her head with an axe, making it one of the most memorable and brutal deaths the series has seen so far.

The violence isn't the only part of Wellie's death that makes this moment so painful. It also reveals how ruthless Silka has become. At this point in the Games, whatever humanity Silka had left has ultimately disappeared. And all that's left is her animalistic instinct to survive, no matter what it takes. She's no longer just a tribute determined to win, she is a savage, willing to commit unimaginable acts of violence in order to win. Her transformation is so important to the story, especially in contrast to Haymitch's much more merciful attitude.

And of course, the broadcast doesn't necessarily see this death play out the way Haymitch does in the book. Wellie's death all points back to Sunrise on the Reaping's theme of how the Capitol uses propaganda to maintain their power. This death, in particular, shows the devastating consequences on the kids' psyche while they're in the Games, and the movie's choice to leave it out is all the more disappointing.

It's not particularly surprising that Sunrise on the Reaping changed the way Wellie's death is depicted in the film. Despite the already dark subject matter of the series, the films have kept a PG-13 rating, meaning that filmmakers have had to be strategic about how much graphic violence they can actually show.

These movies certainly aren't lacking in death or violence, but they often rely on quick cuts and character reactions instead of showing every brutal detail. In an interview with Entertainment Weekly, SOTR director Francis Lawrence explained:

“What’s great about Suzanne’s writing is that she pulls no punches. The only thing we have to do in the movies is make them PG-13. So there's certain things she describes in the book that I could never put in the movies. There’s a character holding the head of a child at one point in the book, and that’s just not something you can do in a PG-13 movie.”

Wellie's death presents a very difficult challenge because her decapitation is much more graphic than a PG-13 audience would expect to see. Showing that death the way it's written in the book would have pushed the movie into a more restrictive place, with the risk of losing a huge part of its audience. As a result, Silka still murdered Wellie, but it is significantly less graphic.

While it's understandable that Sunrise on the Reaping couldn't recreate every gruesome detail of Wellie's death without potentially jeopardizing its PG-13 rating, that doesn't mean the filmmakers made the right decision by toning down the moment. In fact, leaving out the nature of her death takes away from one of the most disturbing turning points in the story.

Wellie's decapitation isn't just part of the story for shock value, but it's an important indicator of how high the Second Quarter Quell's stakes have become. It shows how far tributes are willing to go to survive.

More importantly, the moment says a lot about Silka and the role she has in the Sunrise on the Reaping cast. Wellie was one of the younger tributes and not particularly dangerous, so Silka didn't necessarily need to decapitate her. Wellie was never a true threat to Silka, or anybody, for that matter. But in losing this moment in the film, her transition into true savagery isn't shown.

Now, the filmmakers didn't necessarily need to show the death in every graphic detail to preserve the meaning of the scene. A longer sequence, stronger reactions from Haymitch and Silka, or more attention to the aftermath could have communicated the same horror without pushing the movie past its PG-13 rating.

Beyond changing the circumstances of Wellie's death, Sunrise on the Reaping missed the opportunity to do more with her as a character. With 48 tributes, it naturally has a lot of characters to introduce with stories to balance, but that shouldn't mean the minor characters have to fade into the background.

In the Sunrise on the Reaping novel, Wellie is a District 6 tribute and a member of Haymitch and Ampert's alliance, The Newcomers. She is also one of the kids in the arena who quickly realizes everything is poisonous. On the last day of the Games, Haymitch sticks with Wellie and hides her under a tree while he goes to locate dry kindling. In his mind, Wellie was to be the victor, and he was going to try his hardest to make that happen.

This is what makes her death even more heartbreaking. Haymitch saw Wellie as worth protecting because he thought she'd be a much better victor than himself. His choice to stay with her also shows that he genuinely believed she'd have a chance of making it out alive.

In the movie, Wellie is only seen for a couple of seconds when Haymitch offers her his lunch. Had that connection been established earlier, viewers would have had more of a reason to care about her before she died. Her relationship with Haymitch could've reinforced his arc in The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping even more.
