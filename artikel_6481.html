NASA Smashed Into an Asteroid. Now ESA’s Hera Spacecraft Is About to Reveal the Damage

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

The European Space Agency’s (ESA) Hera spacecraft has reached the two asteroids it was sent to investigate, marking a major milestone in a mission designed to strengthen Earth’s defenses against dangerous space rocks. Roughly the size of a small car, Hera is now traveling just 25 to 30 kilometers from the Didymos asteroid system, which consists of a larger asteroid and its smaller companion, Dimorphos.

The spacecraft has entered its initial observation period, known as the ‘early characterization phase’. During this stage, researchers are collecting essential information about both asteroids, with particular attention to Dimorphos, which NASA deliberately struck with a spacecraft in 2022 to test whether an asteroid’s path could be changed.

“Our aim right now is to gather the big-picture findings of relevance to supporting the mission’s planetary defense goals,” explains mission scientist Michael Kueppers.

“During this phase, our images will reveal for the first time what a small asteroid looks like globally, having experienced a hypervelocity impact in the context of the first asteroid deflection test, when NASA’s DART spacecraft impacted it to change its orbit in 2022.

“Previous predictions by our impact models suggested many different ways the asteroid body might react, but Hera will reveal which forecast was the correct one – if any!”

Navigating around two small asteroids presents challenges unlike those encountered by spacecraft exploring planets. The biggest difference is gravity, which is extraordinarily weak around these small bodies.

Didymos, the larger asteroid, measures approximately 780 meters across and has gravity roughly 40,000 times weaker than Earth’s. Its smaller moonlet, Dimorphos, is about 151 meters wide, with gravity approximately 200,000 times weaker than Earth’s.

These conditions make a conventional circular orbit impractical. Instead, Hera moves around the shared center of gravity of the two asteroids at a remarkably slow speed of approximately 12 centimeters per second, comparable to a fast crawling insect.

Rather than continuously curving around its targets, Hera follows a series of nearly straight paths. During a repeating sequence of three days followed by four days, the spacecraft fires its thrusters while moving away from the asteroids, changing direction by 90 degrees. These carefully timed maneuvers produce a series of rectangular flight patterns that keep Hera approximately 25 to 30 kilometers from the asteroid pair.

The technique builds on lessons from ESA’s Rosetta comet mission. Its biggest advantage is safety. If a planned thruster firing fails, Hera will naturally drift farther into space instead of heading toward a potentially damaging collision. The slow flight speed also means that each course correction requires very little fuel.

Hera’s current observations are only the beginning. Next month, the mission is expected to enter its ‘detailed characterization phase’, bringing the spacecraft closer to the asteroids for more thorough measurements.

During this stage, Hera will begin following more complicated trajectories shaped like bowties. These paths, made up of hyperbolic arcs, will initially take the spacecraft to distances of approximately 20 kilometers from its targets.

The mission is directed from ESA’s ESOC mission control center in Darmstadt, Germany. Controllers communicate with Hera for approximately eight hours each day, despite the spacecraft being about 196.5 million kilometers away.

Those communications travel through four massive Deep Space Antennas, each measuring 35 meters in diameter, at facilities in Argentina, Spain, and Australia.

Supporting the controllers is ESA’s Flight Dynamics team. Specialists analyze information transmitted by Hera to pinpoint its location relative to the asteroids. They then calculate the commands needed to guide the spacecraft safely to its next position.

Hera’s primary scientific target is Dimorphos, which became the focus of a groundbreaking planetary defense experiment on September 26, 2022.

That day, NASA’s Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) spacecraft collided with Dimorphos. The 580-kilogram spacecraft struck the asteroid at 6.1 kilometers per second, sending a vast cloud of debris spreading across approximately 10,000 kilometers of space.

The experiment achieved its main objective. The collision shortened Dimorphos’ orbital period around Didymos by approximately 33 minutes, demonstrating that a spacecraft could alter an asteroid’s motion through a direct impact.

However, the results also raised important questions. The change in Dimorphos’ orbit was significantly greater than scientists had predicted, suggesting that the asteroid responded to the collision in ways that existing models did not fully capture.

Researchers still do not know exactly how much physical damage the collision caused. Did DART create a large crater, or did the force of the impact substantially reshape Dimorphos? Scientists also need more precise measurements of the asteroid’s mass, composition, and internal structure to understand why the deflection was so effective.

Answering these questions is essential before the technique, known as ‘kinetic impact’, can become a dependable option for protecting Earth from a potentially hazardous asteroid.

Over the coming months, Hera will conduct a detailed ‘crash site investigation’ of Dimorphos, using its scientific instruments to examine the asteroid and the aftermath of the DART collision.

The spacecraft will also release two shoebox-sized ‘CubeSats’. These miniature spacecraft will approach the asteroids even more closely, gathering additional observations before the mission eventually progresses to landing operations.

Together, the measurements could reveal how Dimorphos absorbed and responded to the impact, helping scientists determine which predictions were accurate and why the asteroid’s orbit changed more than expected.

By turning NASA’s successful experiment into a better-understood planetary defense method, Hera could play an important role in preparing humanity to deflect an asteroid that might otherwise threaten Earth.
