What Perpetuates the Family Cycle of Violence?

Posted November 12, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

This post is the second in an occasional series on the family cycle of violence.

In May 2026, we posted "Do Abused Parents Become Child Abusers?" which we subtitled: "Multiple interacting factors shape the intergenerational cycle of maltreatment." That post is intended to be the foundation for an undetermined number of future posts examining the intergenerational transmission of increased risk for maltreatment to the offspring of parents who were maltreated themselves as children. The larger focus of this series is the two-part question: “Can we break the family cycle of violence?” and “If so, how?”

The key points of the earlier post are that the single most powerful risk factor for a child being maltreated is having a parent who was maltreated. Most (~70 percent) parents with child maltreatment histories do not become abusive parents. However, the risk of becoming an abusive parent is 6 to 12 times higher than for parents without a childhood maltreatment history. The mechanisms underlying this increased risk may be indirect (e.g., decreased parental vigilance) or direct (perpetration). This increased risk is a result of multiple, interacting levels of factors (e.g., depression, dissociation, substance abuse, domestic violence, unsafe households, poverty, neighborhood factors, public policies). To be cost-effective, prevention programs must seek to address the most critical shared underlying factors synergistically.

The post also notes that individuals can promote positive social change by openly acknowledging their own and others’ childhood maltreatment, thereby validating survivors, reducing stigma, and keeping public attention focused on this enormous public health problem.

How do we tease out the most critical factors on which to focus the always limited prevention resources? This conundrum is further complicated by the need to follow these multiple interacting factors in large general population samples for decades. Not to mention that even the most dedicated family violence researchers are lifetime-limited to studying only two or three generations during their careers.

There is another way, however, to get at least a ballpark understanding of the interacting factors that promote the family cycle of violence across generations. This involves the use of Agent-Based Models. ABMs are computer simulations in which individual “people” are represented by computational entities (agents) that can behave and interact with each other according to predefined rules (Smaldino, 2023). Think SimCity or other computer games that involve characters with past histories, have current and future agendas, and develop complicated interrelationships that can evolve apart and together “over time” to produce a range of outcomes.

Computer simulations, both equation-based and agent-based, are widely used to investigate systems and processes that are too large, too complex, and too costly to study on real-world scales. Examples include the weather, epidemics, and the Big Bang. One advantage is that computer simulations can be run thousands of times as needed to sweep through the numerous combinations of potential variables, searching for fundamental trajectories, collapses, singularities, best fits with real-world data, and critical tipping points that may serve as prevention targets.

Perhaps the best way to understand an ABM is to briefly describe a model simulating the intergenerational transmission of risk for maltreatment of offspring. The model begins with a number of unmarried adult males and females (typically 50 each to limit CPU time, as this breeds hundreds of new agents over three to five generations). These male and female adults are randomly scattered on a multicolored checkerboard “city” map of neighborhoods that hold different levels of risk for children born in them.

In generation 0, each agent (males are blue, females green) is randomly assigned a lifespan and a level of positive and negative “childhood” baggage, some of which may be passed to offspring. These adults proceed to seek out an opposite-gender partner with which to have “children.” Each adult can “see” the childhood baggage of the adults immediately around them and chooses a partner based on a variable called “match” (more on “match” below).

If there is not a suitable “match” in sight, agents take off randomly seeking one. Some agents never meet their “match” and never have children. In addition, each agent is randomly assigned a lifespan at birth. When their lifespan is up, they die and are removed from the board.

When a pair of agents do match—that is, their combined childhood baggage is within the bounds set by the “match” variable—they can “marry,” and every so often they breed a child. They also move around as a family, landing in positive or negative neighborhoods, which affect their growing children. The children pass through three developmental stages (infancy, childhood, adolescence), eventually becoming adults who carry forward into the next generation the risk accumulated over their childhoods. Childhood risk may be concentrated at certain developmental stages or distributed randomly across development, with the weight that earlier acquired risk has a greater lifetime impact.

When the adolescents become young adults, they leave their families to seek a partner with which to have their own children. A new “generation” occurs when a specified percentage (typically 70 percent) of children born to the preceding generation have reached adulthood. Models are typically run for three to five generations, and a combination of inputs is examined for their effects on outputs such as percent of population abused, average risk scores, female-to-male victim ratios, or geographic density of abuse/non-abuse clusters.

A typical experiment involves sweeping through a pre-set combination of variables (e.g., threshold for domestic violence, gender sensitivity coefficients, degree of match) to see how various combinations affect selected outputs. Typically, each combination of inputs is run at least 100 times, and the results are saved to a file for detailed statistical analyses.

The “match” variable was included among the input parameters because of research on the nonrandom or “assortative” mating of individuals with mental health diagnoses. Epidemiologists have found that individuals with mental health diagnoses are significantly more likely to partner with mates who have the same or related diagnoses than are comparison subjects with medical diagnoses having similar degrees of lifestyle impact, such as diabetes or epilepsy. Large-scale Swedish studies have found the frequency of assortative mating for 11 major mental health conditions ranges between two and six times that of equivalent medical conditions (Nordsletten et al., 2016).

“All computer models are wrong, but some are useful” —Old programmer saying

Somewhat to my surprise, “match” has turned out to be a critical variable driving rates of intergenerational transmission of risk for maltreatment of offspring. The ABM finds that the impact of two parents with childhood maltreatment histories significantly increases the risk that their offspring will also suffer maltreatment. Much of this effect is driven by the increased rate of domestic violence found when both parents have a childhood maltreatment history.

Thus, among the potential insights gained from the ABM model is that a childhood history of maltreatment in both parents is potentially a major risk factor for continuation of the family cycle of violence. How often this double parental maltreatment history occurs in real life is difficult to establish at present. However, if we accept that the assortative mating research on psychiatric diagnoses also applies to some degree to individuals with trauma-associated psychiatric disorders, it could happen among a significant number of family violence cases.

Computer models are “thinking machines.” They require their authors to think through dynamic processes and to formulate and parameterize the resulting algorithms. An ABM model is not real life, but sometimes it can yield insights that would be impossible to glean from real life without investing many years and millions of dollars.

Smaldino, P.E. (2023). Modeling Social Behavior: Mathematical and Agent-Based Models of Social Dynamics and Cultural Evolution. Princeton University Press.

Nordsletten, A.E. et al. (2016) Patterns of Nonrandom Mating Within and Across 11 Major Psychiatric Disorders. JAMA Psychiatry. 2016;73(4):354–361.
