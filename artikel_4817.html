Pleasure powerfully contributes to overall well-being. If your sex life feels good, it's likely that you feel good, too—emotionally, mentally, physically, and relationally. That's why, especially for women and people with vulvas, having multiple orgasms can be an awesome way to amplify your life in—and out—of the bedroom.

Simply, “multiple orgasms refer to a person having more than one orgasm in a single session, with short or no recovery time in between,” says Shamyra Howard, PhD, CST, LCSW, a sex therapist and author of Use Your Mouth. Just to be clear: “There’s no set or universal number of orgasms a person can achieve in one setting,” she adds. “Some people may experience two or three; others five (or more) in a single session.”

For those with vulvas, multiple orgasms might begin with one climax that immediately blends into the next with little or no notable pause. Sometimes called rolling orgasms, these “can feel like riding wave after wave of feel-good sensation,” says sex and relationship expert Tara Suwinyattichaiporn, PhD, a professor of relational and sexual communication at California State University and author of How Do You Like It?.

Other folks may have a brief comedown period followed by a slower climb back up to arousal before the next orgasm happens. Regardless of the exact rhythm of the orgasms, and whether a person is having two orgasms or ten, the defining feature is continuity. “Multiple orgasms are all about sustaining arousal after the first orgasm’s peak and continuing the pleasure response, instead of shutting it down,” says Howard.

If you think that sounds enjoyable, you’d be correct. But beyond being just plain fun, orgasming multiple times can actually improve your health, says Howard. Each and every orgasm releases feel-good hormones, like dopamine and oxytocin, while simultaneously decreasing levels of cortisol, the stress hormone. Getting multiple doses of those neurochemicals can feel connective, deeply satisfying, and relaxing—even if each climax varies in intensity.

Plus, orgasms release prolactin, a peptide hormone strongly correlated with sleep duration. That post-climax chemical cocktail “really helps you relax and get a much better quality of sleep,” Miriam Sivkin, MD, an ob-gyn in New York, previously told Women’s Health.

But you don’t just benefit from reaching O-town, the journey to get there may also be informative and connective. “[Exploring multiple orgasms] can help people learn their body’s rhythms and increase body awareness,” says Howard. In a partnered setting, the same lessons apply to each other's bodies, which can build more trust and pleasure over time.

In fact, this sexual exploration can even encourage you to redefine pleasure, Howard says. Many people were socialized to treat achieving orgasm like racing toward a finish line. Meaning, you stop as soon as you cross it. But trying to experience multiple climaxes requires you to approach orgasming—and with that, sex more broadly—as something other than an end goal, says Howard. With practice, this can help you and your partner(s) engage with pleasure as something that can unfold slowly and even ebb and flow.

“If you have a body, you’re capable of sexual arousal and can experience multiple orgasms,” says Howard. However, coming again and again usually comes (heh) easier to vulva-owners because they don’t have the same refractory period—essentially a recovery window—after orgasms as most people with penises. The vulvar and vaginal tissues often remain receptive to stimulation after climax, so most women can stay aroused, though sensitivity might spike for a short moment, says Howard. This makes it easier to have another orgasm soon after the first one.

The majority of penis-having people, however, usually need time and distance after an orgasm before pleasure becomes accessible again, she says. Of course, there are no absolutes in sex and pleasure. A penis-owner can train their body to have non-ejaculatory or full-body orgasms—often with the help of breathwork, pelvic floor control, and stimulation—that make multiple orgasms more accessible, says Howard. Along your sexual exploration, you may have heard of simultaneous orgasms and blended orgasms. These terms might sound similar, but they describe separate sexperiences.

Multiple Orgasms vs. Simultaneous Orgasms

“Simultaneous orgasms are when two people climax at the same time,” says Howard. With that, two (or more) people are a prerequisite for simultaneous orgasms, whereas multiple orgasms can absolutely occur during a solo session.

Blended orgasms, on the other hand, refer to an orgasm that stems from two or more pleasure centers being stimulated at once—for example, combining clitoral stimulation and vaginal penetration, or clitoral stimulation and anal play.

Sure, both blended and multiple orgasms might be considered amplified versions of the standard O. Still, while blended orgasms are about layering multiple sensations, multiple orgasms involve many separate climaxes, which can all stem from the same type of stimulation repeated.

It all depends on a variety of factors, such as your unique physiology, arousal levels at the time of play, comfort with your own body and your partner, the techniques used, where you are in your menstrual cycle, hydration and stress levels, and more, says Howard. Expectations, as the saying goes, are the thief of joy. And sexually, pressure to perform is the antithesis of arousal. When you focus intensely on achieving orgasm(s), your system shifts into a goal-oriented mode that can make it harder to enjoy the experience—no matter what happens, says Howard.

Research on multiple orgasms is limited, but less than one-fifth of women report having had multiple orgasms in a single session, according to the International Society for Sexual Medicine. So, it might not happen for you, and that’s okay!

Even if you do wind up joining the swath of pleasure-seekers who have had multiple orgasms, there’s no promising it will rock your world. “Each orgasm doesn’t always get more intense; sometimes, the second or third are softer,” says Howard, adding that the point of exploring multiple orgasms isn’t to stack intensity, but to stay tuned into your body.

It is common for people on their multiple orgasm journey to try to get the first orgasm “out of the way,” as if rushing through the first will facilitate the next ones.

Unfortunately, this usually has the opposite effect, says Holly Richmond, PhD, a sex therapist, licensed marriage and family therapist, and author of Reclaiming Pleasure. “Our bodies and nervous systems need to be open and relaxed to achieve multi orgasms.” So, rushing is entirely counterproductive.

Rather than treating orgasm like a yellow light that you need to speed through before it turns red, take your sweet, sweet time building arousal, says Richmond. Let yourself revel in the entire experience, including all the parts and pleasures that preface a (potential) orgasm, she adds.

“Experiencing multi-orgasms is a process; almost no one achieves this on the first try,” says Richmond.

There will likely be a learning curve where you have some one-and-done, mediocre, and no-orgasm sessions. But every experience teaches your body something about what it likes, what it needs, what it responds to, and what it doesn’t, says Richmond.

Stay curious to avoid getting frustrated (sexual or otherwise!). “The more you can relax, open up to all pleasure possibilities, and enjoy the moment for what it is, whether it’s a single or multiple orgasmic experience, you set yourself up for more pleasure going forward,” she says. “Not every sexual experience is going to be a ten out of ten, but every sexual experience can be a good sexual experience if we allow it to be.”

There are a million different reasons multiple O’s may not be in the cards the first time you try. “Sex and orgasms are full-body experiences; the state of your mind, body, and hormones all play a role,” says Howard.

Case in point: “Dehydration can lower blood flow and make tissues less responsive,” she says. Even being a little parched can interfere with Mission Multiple Orgasms.

“Stress raises cortisol, which can block arousal altogether,” she adds, so a high-stress week may mean your body simply doesn’t have the capacity to go beyond one orgasm that day. Further, “where you are in your menstrual cycle often matters, too, since many people notice higher libido and easier orgasms around ovulation when estrogen peaks,” says Howard.

In other words, if multiple O’s don’t happen on the first, second, or tenth try, it doesn’t mean you did anything wrong, or that you’ll never climb two peaks in one sesh. It just means your body has been communicating where it was at, and what it needed, the times you’ve tried.
