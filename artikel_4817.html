Ultra Muscular = Fake? Addressing Male Body Image Concerns

A common stereotype is that appearance concerns are a "woman’s thing," and men simply don’t worry as much about their looks. The taboo around men speaking about their body concerns certainly doesn’t help: Without speaking about them, men are less likely to receive support, and we continue to assume that men just aren’t as worried about their bodies.

Yet, research shows that many men do experience body concerns, and the percentage of men who worry about their bodies has been increasing in recent years, particularly among young people. This is a serious problem because, like body concerns among women, body concerns among men may lead to mental health risks, such as disordered eating, excessive exercise, anxiety, and low self-esteem. Motivated by the need to address body concerns among men, my colleagues and I at Maastricht University conducted an experiment to test a novel, computer-based intervention technique.

In our experiment, young men (18-29 years old) were tested individually at our lab. First, they completed questionnaires to measure how they were thinking and feeling about their bodies. Then, they were randomly assigned to complete either the intervention technique or a "placebo" technique, both completed via the computer.

Participants who received the intervention technique where they were repeatedly shown images of men who represented today’s "ideal male body"—namely, young men with lean, ultra-muscular bodies. They were also shown images of sports items (e.g., tennis racket) or neutral items (e.g., stapler). After each image appeared on the screen, they were instructed to click on the image as quickly as possible, and then a word would appear. The images of the ultra-muscular men were always followed by negative words like fake, unnatural, and artificial. In contrast, the sports items were always followed by sports-related words (e.g., net), and the neutral items were always followed by color-related words (e.g., green). Participants completed this sequence—viewing an image, clicking on it, seeing a word—more than 300 times in rapid succession.

The idea behind this seemingly simple computer task is based on fundamental principles in psychology. In brief, repeatedly pairing idealized bodies with negative words should create a more negative association with the male body ideal. As a result, participants should be less likely to view these idealized bodies as appropriate standards of comparison, and should feel less negatively about their own bodies. Further, we focused the intervention technique on images of the male body ideal based on the wealth of research showing that they are a key factor contributing to body concerns among men.

Participants who received the "placebo" technique completed a computer task where they viewed the same images and words; however, the words following each image were random, so there was no systematic association between any image or word category.

After the computer task, all participants again completed questionnaires about how they were thinking and feeling about their bodies. Importantly, participants had not been informed that the experiment was about an intervention technique to reduce body concerns. In this way, we could minimize the risk that participants might answer the questionnaires based on how they expected we would want them to answer the questionnaires.

After collecting all of our data, we used men’s questionnaire scores to analyze whether there was a change in men’s thoughts and feelings about their bodies from before to after the computer task—and, importantly, whether this change was significantly different between the intervention and placebo group.

Our main finding is that men who completed the intervention technique, compared to men who completed the placebo technique, reported an increase in muscle appearance satisfaction from before to after the computer task. We did not find any significant changes in one of our other key outcomes, self-objectification (i.e., the tendency to view one’s body from an appearance-based perspective).

Our experiment provides first evidence that repeatedly pairing images of the male body ideal with negative words (fake, unnatural, artificial) can lead to reductions in body concerns among young men. Specifically, the intervention technique led to an immediate increase in muscle appearance satisfaction. Potentially, creating a more negative association with the male body ideal may have compromised these images as appropriate sources of body comparisons, leading men to feel more satisfied with their own bodies.

Importantly, this experiment was a first, "proof of concept" study into whether this intervention technique holds promise. Testing this intervention technique again in other samples of men—ideally with longer follow-up measurements—will be important. If the technique continues to prove effective, it may be a useful complement to other larger-scale interventions for men’s body concerns. It will also be important to investigate how this technique can be integrated into men’s daily lives, where they are continuously confronted with images of the male body ideal, for example on social media. We look forward to conducting other research into men’s body image and future avenues for treatment.
