The referee and his role in what’s wrong with football.

One of the many problems with football in England is the refereeing.

Not necessarily the refs themselves, more the ever-changing guidance and policies they are expected to obey when it comes to their refereeing.

The Premier League is the world’s most popular, watched all around the globe on television in far higher numbers than any other rival.

It is a money-spinning product that cannot afford to lose its pre-eminent position. Those in charge, such as Richard Masters, the Premier League’s chief executive, have millions of reasons to protect the brand.

In his particular case, 2.6 million for the financial year ending on July 31, 2025. That included a £1.1m bonus awarded in part for his help in negotiating record-breaking broadcasting deals with Sky, TNT and Amazon.

That £2.6m remuneration package (as they like to say in the City of London) was £700,000 up on the previous year. Don’t expect any reduction when the 2025-26 figures are lodged at Companies House . . .

The Premier League’s popularity relies on more than having some of the world’s best players. It also depends on the characteristic style of play being distinctly different from La Liga or the Bundesliga or Serie A. Which is where the refereeing comes in.

You might believe a referee’s primary role is to ensure, to the best of his ability, the game is played fairly. You might be right, though I suspect the job of protecting the brand has become the driving force for the man in the middle and his colleagues.

The more free kicks, the less attractive the spectacle. Those in charge of the Premier League cannot afford a stop-start product, because viewer figures are paramount.

Pro Ref knows this, the players know this, the managers know this. That’s why so many fouls go unpunished. Not because the referees should go to Specsavers. They see them and choose to ignore them.

It’s the coward’s way. Or should that be Howard’s Way? Mr Webb, the head of Pro Ref, was pilloried when VAR kept halting the action. He responded by telling the boys and girls at Stockley Park to intervene in only the most extreme cases.

Outcome: a non-decision by the ref is not corrected by the VAR.

The most blatant mistakes are later scrutinised by the Premier League’s key match incidents panel. Its verdicts read like an Orwellian nightmare.

In a typical case, a penalty is not awarded for foul play. The VAR, obeying Howard’s Way, looks and agrees. Days later, the key match incidents panel says: “A penalty was probably the right call but VAR was correct in doing nothing. If the ref had blown for the foul, the VAR would have agreed.”

So there you have it. Whichever call the referee makes is correct. It must be, because the VAR backed him up. Reminds me of the olden days when the referee’s decision was final.

Before each season starts, changes are announced to the way the laws of the game will be enforced. Among recent initiatives was the five-second countdown on throws and goal kicks if the referee detected time wasting. They are rarely enforced because the threat of being penalised has encouraged quicker play, as intended. There’s also a 10-second limit for a substituted player to leave the field.

Nobody would argue against those changes, though they smack of window dressing when much more important problems are being ignored. Here are just a few suggestions to make the game fairer.

1. Warn any player who refuses to retreat 10 yards promptly from an opponent’s free kick. The second time he or a teammate does it, book the offender. Third time, red card.

2. Issue a yellow card for pushing whenever no attempt is made to play the ball.

3. Award a throw-in to the other team if the ball is not released from behind the head. Or if both feet are not grounded behind the touchline. Or if the throw is taken more than two yards forward of the right point. Players know.

4) Book any player who pulls an opponent’s shirt, shorts or socks.

5) Stop players taking free kicks from the wrong spot. If they do it again, book them. Keepers in particular move the ball up the field or towards their goal with impunity, knowing the referee will do nothing.

6) A yellow card for all offences of dissent, either physical or verbal, directed at the referee or his assistants. Including the captain.

7) An automatic card to any player who dives. In the opponents’ penalty area, make it a red.

8) Add on at least one minute for every goal celebration.

9) Play an advantage if there is the chance of one but go back and book any player who has fouled off the ball.

You might think there would be a blizzard of yellow and red cards. I believe the far more likely consequence would be a massive reduction in gamesmanship, cheating, time wasting and other examples of the dark arts that are blighting the beautiful game.

Once players realised they couldn’t foul with impunity, they would quickly learn to play the game as a sport, rather than as a cynical win-at-all-costs exercise.
