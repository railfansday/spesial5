Counting filler words when speech-to-text deletes them

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

We wanted a one-minute speaking test that gives a learner three numbers: words a minute, filler words and long pauses. The browser's speech recognizer would do the hard part. We'd count.

It turns out the transcript fights you on all three.

Speech recognizers are trained to produce readable text, so "um" and "uh" mostly vanish. Count fillers from the transcript and a nervous speaker looks fluent.

There's no fix for that from the page. What you can do is count the fillers that survive transcription because they're real words:

"Matlab" is the Hindi "I mean", and it turns up in Indian English speech often enough to earn a slot.

"I like Python" isn't a filler. "It was, like, a huge refactor" is. We check the word before it:

It's crude and it works well enough for a self-check. "Looks like", "feel like" and "I like" pass. A bare "like" in the middle of a clause gets counted.

A transcript has no timestamps per word in this API, so you can't see a pause directly. But you can see when results arrive. If two onresult events are more than two seconds apart, the speaker stopped:

It's a proxy, and a noisy one on slow networks, since Chrome's recognizer runs on Google's servers. For a learner who stalls for four seconds looking for a word, it's accurate enough.

With continuous = true, desktop Chrome sends each final result once. Android Chrome sends each new final result with the earlier text still inside it. Join them naively and every sentence appears two or three times, and your words-a-minute doubles.

Two smaller ones while we're here. The browser ends a "continuous" session early when it feels like it, so onend restarts it if the timer is still running. And after stop(), the last final result can take over a second to arrive, so we wait up to 1,800 ms before scoring.
