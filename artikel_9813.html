ACHNET's Talent Personality Questionnaire (TPQ) Starts With the Job Description, Then Asks About the Candidate

The Talent Personality Questionnaire reads a candidate's working preferences against competencies chosen for each role, and tells the hiring manager what to ask in the interview.

Two candidates can describe their working style in exactly the same way and still be different hires. A preference for adapting as situations change may carry particular importance in one role, while planning, consistency and execution may receive greater weight in another. The appropriate balance depends on the responsibilities of the position. A personality profile read on its own cannot tell a hiring manager which of those situations applies.

That gap is what ACHNET set out to close with its Talent Personality Questionnaire, or TPQ, which is available as part of its Unified Talent Selection Platform. TPQ begins with the vacancy. The hiring team supplies a job description or the role's key responsibility areas, and the questionnaire and its report are configured around what that position demands before a candidate answers a single question.

Configuration runs through iJupiter™, the AI Super Agent at the center of ACHNET's platform. From the job description or responsibility areas, iJupiter™ recommends which competencies matter for the role, how they should be prioritized, what level each should reach, and how much weight each carries in the final profile. The hiring team reviews those recommendations and adjusts them before the questionnaire goes out.

Most of TPQ's design follows from that sequence. In ACHNET's view, a general personality profile leaves the hiring manager to work out what it means for the vacancy in front of them.

"A general personality description may provide context, but hiring teams still need to understand what that information means for the position they are filling," said Manouj Gupta, CEO of ACHNET.

The questionnaire uses a forced-choice format. Candidates see groups of three statements and pick the one most like them and the one least like them. Each statement in a set can sound reasonable, so a candidate cannot rate all three highly and has to show which preferences come first.

Forced-choice formats are used in personality assessment partly to address applicants' tendency to present themselves favorably. A 2019 meta-analysis in the Journal of Applied Psychology by Mengyang Cao and Fritz Drasgow reported an overall standardized score-inflation effect size of 0.06 across the forced-choice measures studied. In selection settings, inflation was lower than comparison estimates for single-statement measures across most personality facets, although findings varied with questionnaire design and scoring.

The same study carries a caution that applies directly to a role-configured tool. Cao and Drasgow found that the facets most relevant to the target job showed more inflation than facets with little bearing on it. The inflation is largest, in other words, on the traits an employer is most likely to be looking for. A questionnaire built around one role's competencies asks about exactly those traits, so a format that limits inflation matters more for TPQ than it would for a general personality inventory.

TPQ measures 35 competencies grouped into six domains. They are Cognitive and Strategic Capabilities, Interpersonal Effectiveness, Self-Management, Execution, Integrity, and Adaptability. Between them, they cover how a person approaches thinking, collaboration, managing their own work, delivery, responsible behavior, and change.

Different roles can assign different priorities, target levels, and weights to the competencies being evaluated. TPQ uses an adaptive approach in which questions can adjust based on previous responses to explore relevant behavioral areas further. The resulting profile is interpreted against the configured requirements of the role.

The output is a role-configured competency profile. It sets out where a candidate's stated preferences match what the role needs, where there may be gaps, and which areas a hiring manager should explore further in conversation with the candidate.

The report can help hiring managers identify areas for follow-up. In a 2022 re-analysis, Paul Sackett, Charlene Zhang, Christopher Berry and Filip Lievens revised earlier estimates of how well selection methods predict job performance, with structured interviews emerging as the highest-ranked procedure examined. That finding does not validate TPQ's interview prompts. Structured interviews require standardized questions and consistent scoring criteria; TPQ's prompts provide additional areas to explore.

ACHNET is explicit about how far the questionnaire's findings should be taken.

"TPQ supports human judgment. It should not be presented as a pass/fail test, a fixed personality label, or a definitive predictor of job performance," Gupta said.

The company presents the report as material for a conversation. The same findings can carry past the hiring decision, into onboarding priorities and development discussions once the candidate has joined.

TPQ is one stage in ACHNET's platform, alongside applicant ranking, technical assessments, AI video interviews conducted by iJupiter™ and fraud detection. Each produces a different kind of evidence. A resume records experience and qualifications. A technical assessment provides evidence of a candidate's knowledge or skills in the areas assessed. TPQ adds a structured account of how the candidate prefers to work, interpreted against the demands of the specific role.

Holding those inputs in one place lets a hiring team weigh them together when it reaches a decision. Reviewing these inputs together gives hiring teams a broader view of the candidate's experience, demonstrated skills, and workplace behavioral preferences. TPQ contributes one source of evidence alongside assessments, interviews, and other role-relevant information.

TPQ asks about the competencies a specific job needs, makes candidates choose between their own preferences, and passes what it finds to the person running the interview. How much it improves a hire will depend on what that person does with the report, and ACHNET has left that decision with the hiring team.
