Kalshi's $40 Billion Valuation Signals Growing Investor Confidence in Prediction Markets

Kalshi raised another $1 billion this week at a $40 billion valuation, up from $22 billion in May.

The company’s latest Series G funding round, co-led by Sequoia Capital and Wellington Management, brings its valuation to eight times the $5 billion reported in October 2025.

Kalshi also disclosed that its non-sports businesses now generate over $1 billion in annualised revenue. Its institutional business is also growing, with approximately 500 entities trading on the exchange in September.

The valuation comes amid ongoing regulatory disputes, suggesting investors are increasingly confident in Kalshi's long-term prospects. Whether that confidence reflects expectations of favourable regulatory outcomes or a broader conviction that prediction markets are here to stay remains an open question.

Trading activity provides some context for the latest valuations.

Prediction Frontier’s Markets dashboard, using Dune data through October 25, shows $115.8 billion in notional volume, up 61.5% against the preceding 30-day period. Transactions reached 610.8 million, an increase of 37%.

The figures suggest trading activity is growing faster than participation. Existing traders may be generating more volume, although the aggregate data alone cannot establish how much of the increase comes from more frequent trading.

It is a distinction worth tracking as the industry continues setting volume records.

Another interesting development this week is the NBA naming Kalshi and Polymarket its preferred prediction-market exchanges this week, granting both platforms access to official league data, intellectual property and marketing rights.

The agreements also introduce restrictions on permissible markets, prohibit league and team personnel from trading NBA event contracts, and require information sharing around suspicious activity.

Separately, the NBA signed a memorandum of understanding with the CFTC on integrity oversight.

Beyond the commercial agreements, the interesting development is the league's role in determining which markets can be offered. The NBA retains approval rights over permissible contracts, giving it direct influence over market design.

This raises a question about how responsibility should be divided between sports leagues, exchanges and regulators.

Whether this arrangement becomes a wider model for sports prediction markets will depend partly on how effectively the restrictions and monitoring obligations work in practice.

Regulatory approaches remain fragmented outside the US. Gibraltar has established a dedicated framework for prediction markets, while Britain, Germany and the wider EU apply different legal classifications and restrictions.

Prediction Frontier’s latest analysis, Prediction Market Regulation in Europe: Can Gibraltar Offer a Model?, examines whether Gibraltar's approach can offer a workable framework for operator authorisation, contract approval and participant protection.

The discussion comes ahead of Prediction Markets Summit Europe, taking place on 9–10 November at Sunborn Gibraltar, organised by Eventus International.
