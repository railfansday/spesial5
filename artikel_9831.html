Blockchain Was Raised in the 2008 Financial Crisis. Now It’s Respawning It

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

President & CTO of the Casper Association, the Swiss nonprofit organization that oversees protocol development and growth.

Remember the message immortalized by Satoshi Nakamoto in Bitcoin’s Genesis block? The one that’s since become the most famous Times headline of all time? “Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks.”

Of course you do. With Bitcoin’s whitepaper appearing just 46 days after Lehman Brothers’ collapse, you can’t consider the latter's fate without pondering the former's creation. Bitcoin didn’t cause the collapse of Lehman any more than Lehman caused the creation of Bitcoin. But it’s hard not to view the implosion of one without the inception of the other.

Almost 20 years on, even those of us who weren’t around at that pivotal point in modern history know the cliff notes. Lehman Brothers collapsed due to reckless lending and extreme leverage that left the financial system vulnerable.

And Bitcoin, together with the blockchain ecosystem it spawned, began as the antidote to all that. Because when everything’s onchain and independently inspectable in real-time, subprime lending is impossible.

At least that’s what we were told. But in the years since Bitcoin was built, the onchain landscape has become more sophisticated and more complicated to boot. Financial chicanery that was once the preserve of early aughts Wall Street is now equally possible on crypto rails if you know how.

It’s commonplace to hear pitches today about how tokenization is going to fix real estate, finance, and every other industry you care to think of with the promise of much Efficiency. 24/7 asset trading, baby. No borders because it’s all on blockchain.

Cool pitch, but if that’s your USP, you ain’t got no USP.

Take a mortgage, for example, a financial instrument that starts as a reasonably simple arrangement between a borrower and a lender. Pool it with thousands of others, divide the payments into securities, then use some of those securities to construct another product, and the picture becomes considerably harder to follow.

Similarly, if a Real World Asset (RWA) changes hands but the underlying data determining whether that guy in Nevada actually paid his mortgage this month remains locked in some private database, what have we really achieved?

Sure, we’ve made the asset easier to trade, but we've still left the buyer with the same information problem. All we’ve done is place the shortcomings of this transaction onchain for all the world to see.

If RWAs are to correlate with the real-world assets they purportedly represent, there needs to be a dynamic connection to the information behind the token. In the case of the mortgage, that starts with knowing whether payments are arriving.

If this occurs through an external banking system, the blockchain needs an authorized source to report it. And, should the expected update never arrive, the system should be able to flag the record as overdue and initiate a review under rules agreed in advance.

That is the kind of tokenization we should be building: assets whose behavior correlates with the condition of the information behind it.

Placing Lehman Brothers onchain simply recreates the same mistakes of nearly two decades past. Blockchain is capable of better. Programmable, responsive, and fully accountable, it can provide real-time insights into what’s happening where and why. The onus is on us to start using it to its full capacity.

If we can settle a trade in seconds but still need weeks to establish what the buyer has inherited, all we’ve done is reinvent an old problem.

Lehman Brothers was double-tapped for a reason. Let’s not resurrect it.
