Amid Legal Controversy, Christie’s Withdraws Frida Kahlo Painting Hours Before Auction

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Christie’s pulled a Frida Kahlo painting from an evening sale hours before it was to be auctioned amid a debate over whether the work was legally permitted to leave Mexico.

“In collaboration with the seller, we decided to withdraw the lot at this time for further research. We look forward to selling this work at a later date,” a spokesperson for Christie’s told ARTnews. The work, which carried a pre-sale estimate of $2 million to $3.5 million, was to be included in the 20th Century Evening Sale in New York, scheduled to begin at 6:30 p.m. on November 17. In the days leading up to the sale, Mexico’s cultural authorities had faced accusations of failing to protect the painting, which is considered part of the nation’s heritage and legally protected. An online campaign argued that the 1952 work, titled Congreso de los pueblos por la paz (Congress of the Peoples for Peace), was part of the trust that Kahlo’s husband, the artist Diego Rivera, created for the Casa Azul after her death in 1954 and that it cannot legally leave the country. It is unclear where the painting was held prior to it coming to New York for this week’s auction.

The Frida Kahlo Museum told ARTnews that the painting is part of an ongoing investigation by Mexican authorities. At the time of publication, the National Institute of Fine Arts and Literature (INBAL), which is responsible for the country’s patrimony, maintained that Kahlo donated it during her lifetime and that it left Mexico before her work was declared an artistic monument in 1984.

In a statement Monday, the collective behind the campaign, Defendamos la Colección Gelman (Let’s Defend the Gelman Collection), called on INBAL to stop the sale of Congreso de los pueblos por la paz. The collective asserts that the oil-on-masonite work was among the hundreds of artworks and objects at Casa Azul that Rivera inventoried after Kahlo’s death, when he established the trust. The inventory, the collective says, carried explicit instructions from Rivera that the items remain on the premises both during his lifetime and after his death.

Queried on the collective’s claims, a representative for the Frida Kahlo Museum told ARTnews that, “In accordance with established protocols, the auction house Christie’s has been informed that the work is part of an ongoing investigation being conducted by the competent Mexican authorities.” It added, “An investigation file exists that gathers information on several works, including Congreso de los pueblos por la paz.”

According to the museum, INBAL “reported that, through its legal department, it has requested from Christie’s documentation proving the provenance and ownership of the painting.” Christie’s did not address whether it provided INBAL with the documentation in its statement to ARTnews.

A provenance published on Christie’s website, that has since been removed, listed the work’s earliest owner as Galería Ramis Barquet, a the Monterrey-based operation with a New York branch, run by Ramis Barquet, though it did not provide a date when Barquet came into possession of the work. An unnamed collector bought it from the gallery and put it up at auction at Sotheby’s in 2020. The current seller bought it at that sale for $2.6 million and is now consigning it to Christie’s.

INBAL did not respond to ARTnews’s request for comment by publication time. In a statement dated November 16 to the Mexican newspaper Excélsior, however, the institute said that Congress of the Peoples for Peace, “a painting created by Frida Kahlo in October 1952, was donated by the artist during her lifetime and left Mexico before the declaration of her work as an artistic monument.” It is unclear to whom Kahlo would have donated the work.

Kahlo painted Congreso de los pueblos por la paz two months before the Congress of the Peoples for Peace, which met in Vienna in December 1952. INBAL’s statement cited research from art historian Luis-Martín Lozano, who collaborated in cataloguing the painting for Christie’s. According to Lozano, Rivera took the painting to Vienna in late 1953, as recorded in the memoirs of Emma Hurtado, Rivera’s wife from 1955 until his death in 1957. The statement also pointed to Raquel Tibol’s 1977 book Frida Kahlo: Crónica, testimonios y aproximaciones, which reproduces the painting on plate 17.

In its statement, also from November 16, Defendamos la Colección Gelman disputed Lozano’s timeline of events. “The account—attributed to historian Luis-Martín Lozano—that the work was taken to the Congress in 1953 is chronologically impossible, as the Congress had already concluded months earlier. Furthermore, there is no evidence that Rivera returned to Europe that year; his travels were extensively documented—notably in the files that the FBI kept on him,” they wrote.

In its request for comment, ARTnews also asked INBAL about the discrepancy in Lozano’s research.

Furthermore, INBAL said that the export would not have covered Kahlo’s Congreso de los pueblos por la paz, claiming that it had left Mexico prior to July 25, 1984, when a decree declared her art an artistic monument and subject to Mexico’s strict cultural patrimony laws. Once designated “artistic monuments” of the nation, protected works may be bought and sold within Mexico but can leave the country only temporarily, unless INBAL grants an exceptional authorization.

“In previous cases, the INBAL has specified that the definitive export prohibition applies to works that were in national territory when the declaration of July 25, 1984 was published,” INBAL said.

After Kahlo’s death, Rivera notarized an inventory of the couple’s possessions as the basis for a trust administered by the Banco de México. The trust holds the properties and collections and oversees the reproduction rights to both artists’ work. Its proceeds were intended to support the upkeep of two museums: the Frida Kahlo Museum (Casa Azul) and the Museo Anahuacalli. It remains active today.

The inventory became the subject of public controversy in April 2025, when Hilda Trujillo, director of the Museo Anahuacalli and the Frida Kahlo Museum in Mexico City from 2002 to 2020, published a lengthy online post alleging that several works listed in Rivera’s inventory were currently unaccounted for. Among them, she wrote, was Congreso de los pueblos por la paz, which she said appears on Rivera’s list under the number A.F. 18. Defendamos la Colección Gelman provided ARTnews with a reproduction of the inventory including a zoomed-in, but blurry, image that reads, “18. – Congreso de los pueblos por la paz. – Oleo sobre masonite. – 1952. – 0.19 x 0.25.” (Christie’s lot entry gave the dimensions of the work as 18.7 x 24.8 centimeters.)

In her post, Trujillo traced the painting’s later whereabouts through a 2011 list that notes it as belonging to the collection of gallerist Mary-Anne Martin, and through the art historian Helga Prignitz-Poda’s 1988 catalogue raisonné, which attributed ownership to Barquet.

“It is unclear how or why it left the Frida Kahlo Museum,” she wrote. Trujillo accused the trust that manages both museums of mismanagement and negligence, claiming that in two episodes, in 2009 and 2013, its administrators dismissed evidence that works belonging to the Casa Azul may have been stolen and sold to private collectors in the United States. The trust responded to Trujillo’s allegations in a public statement that April that read in part: “The person making these accusations today never filed a formal complaint during their professional association with the trust.”

Defendamos la Colección Gelman described the case of Congreso de los pueblos por la paz as confirming “a troubling trend” that began with the Mexican government’s alleged lack of transparency surrounding the loan of the Gelman Collection to the new Faro Santander museum in Spain. “[W]hat once appeared to be an exception, the indefinite removal from the country of works designated as ‘Artistic Monuments’ with the consent of INBAL, is beginning to become the rule.”

The Gelman collection comprises more than 300 artworks, including a significant number of paintings by Kahlo and Rivera, as well as other important Mexican artists such as José Clemente Orozco and David Alfaro Siqueiros.
