The 6 Best Flashlights for Everyday Use

Yes, they’re better than the one on your phone.

Smartphones may give us an easy access to a light source, but for seeing objects at a distance or brightening a workspace in places like the attic or under the sink, you need the power and range of a LED (light-emitting diode) flashlight. Flashlights are vital for navigating the outdoors at night and absolutely essential during a blackout or emergency, when you don’t want to drain your phone’s battery.

We’ve tested a variety of popular models to find the best flashlights, whether you’re looking for one to use in the midst of a blackout or simply want to put a decent one in the glove box of your car, just in case.

Perhaps the most important element of testing flashlights is to ensure they’re as bright as manufacturers claim. Brands typically list a lumen count, though they’re often inflated. Flashlights tend to dim slightly after you turn them on and your eyes adjust, and how quickly they do so is key. It’s not just about the peak brightness, but how long that brightness actually lasts and how bright the light is after it dims.

To measure lumen, you need an integrating sphere, which is an expensive piece of equipment. So for testing flashlights, we built our own. We consulted with product engineers at two different companies to go over our plans. We also used their commercial spheres to verify lumens on calibration lights to make sure that our own sphere sensors would be accurate.

After construction and calibration, we measured each flashlight individually at specific time intervals. We recorded our measurements immediately after turning on the light, every 30 seconds for the first 3 minutes, at 5 minutes, and then every 5 minutes for a full half an hour. Our official figure is based on our measurement at 30 seconds because most flashlights drop brightness quickly after that before eventually stabilizing at a dimmer level.

After completing the quantitative portion of our testing process, we used each flashlight in a variety of real-world applications, noting aspects including quality of build, ergonomics, weight, water-resistance, and other key specs.

Our best overall is an evolution on the classic hefty Maglite you remember in your parents junk drawer that could blind you if you looked directly at it. This model trades in the D batteries for a rechargeable and replaceable battery. Furthermore, the flashlight has an LED bulb that shines just as brightly as the old-school models you remember.

While Maglite lists that the flashlight shines with a brightness of 1,082 lumens, our own testing measured an even higher figure, not dropping below 1,082 lumens for more than three minutes.

Beyond this, I really love how it feels in my hand. It isn’t quite as clunky as the giant models of the past, but it still boasts a similar heft that feels solid and durable. The aluminum frame and IPX4 water resistance rating backs that feeling up.

The starting point of 554 lumens is already solid, but in testing, the flashlight settles between 334 and 324 lumens, which is a far higher than nearly all other options we tested.

The relatively compact design doesn’t detract from the overall performance, as the light beam extends for close to 300 feet. That said, the reflector is not adjustable, so you’ll have to look elsewhere for a floodlight.

Beyond this, we liked the nylon polymer body which was grippy and a lot more comfortable to hold in comparison to a fully metal exterior, which can be icy to the touch in cold temperatures.

There are three preprogrammed function sets we were able to access using Streamlight’s Ten-Tap selector switch. The selection process is a little awkward, but most people will likely only use it sparingly to set the preferred programs, which include low/medium/high, high/strobe/low, and high only. We recommend reading the instructions thoroughly to get this right.

From a fully charged state, the Polytac X ran for almost 5.5 hours during our runtime test before going dark.

While Jeep’s involvement isn’t more than some design elements and a logo, this particular option is part of a larger lighting collection the brand did in collaboration with Energizer, featuring headlamps and other flashlights.

Traditional light bulbs, while nostalgic, are inefficient and waste a lot of energy. According to the U.S. Department of Energy, as much as 90 percent of the energy required to illuminate an incandescent light is given off—essentially lost—as heat. Halogen bulbs are an improvement on classic incandescent lights, but still generate a ton of heat. Beyond this, the bulbs themselves are also quite delicate. If dropped, they can easily shatter.

In contrast, LEDs are 70 to 75 percent more efficient than incandescent lights. That efficiency doesn’t lead to lower brightness either, with LEDs actually shining far stronger than most classic bulbs. The LED itself is a semiconductor that emits light when current passes through it. Furthermore, without a delicate filament inside, LEDs are far more durable.

While LEDs in flashlights are more efficient than traditional bulbs, they still generate a fair amount of heat. Especially as brands continue to push to create brighter and brighter flashlights, the heat generated grows too. That said, most LED flashlights have circuitry designed to manage power consumption and prevent your light from burning up.

For the most part, as long as you use common sense and don’t max out your light for hours on end, your LED flashlight should be fine. Take care of it responsibly and you’ll never deal with heat issues.

The actual brightness of your flashlight is measured in lumens. Lumens are essentially measuring the total light created by flashlight, with the higher the figure, the brighter the flashlight should shine.

Even accurate figures reported by a company can often refer to the absolute highest possible lumen output, which is not realistically what you end up using most of the time. Being able to measure them ourselves provides a lot of much needed reassurance.

That said, lumens aren’t the end all, be all. A lot of the perceived brightness and clarity of the beam is due to the reflector shape and lens. So, the width and length of the light beam from your flashlight will likely be a better determination of whether it works for you or not.

IP is short for ingress protection, meaning protection from stuff getting inside the flashlight. Those letters will be followed by two numbers: The first digit indicates protection from solids like dust or grit. The second digit indicates protection from moisture, in this case the concern is usually water. Sometimes there will be an “X” in place of one of those numbers, which means the flashlight hasn’t been tested for protection from solids (if the X is where the first number should be) or liquids (if it’s where the second should be).

For the first number, protection from solids, the highest rating, 6, indicates the light should be impenetrable by dust and sand. The highest value for the second number, representing protection from liquid, is 8. The flashlight can be submerged in water deeper than 1 meter for longer than 30 minutes, with no harmful effects.

A flashlight’s IP rating gives a partial picture of its durability. You’ll also want to take into account things like the materials used—ranging from lightweight but less-than-durable plastic to rubberized enclosures to heavy-duty anodized aluminum—and whether the flashlight floats in water in addition to being waterproof, not to mention the warranty.

As our first-hand testing showed, manufacturer estimates for flashlight specs are relatively accurate, especially regarding the peak brightness. It’s important to keep in mind that there are a variety of factors that can influence a flashlight’s moment-to-moment performance, such as its battery life and how long it has run in the past few hours.

As we mentioned, it is normal for flashlights to only shine at peak brightness for a few minutes before sliding back to the middle of their range. The good news is your visibility won’t suffer for that.

That depends on how you’re using your flashlight. Manufacturer Nitecore suggests a range of 1 to 300 lumens should be enough to light up a room or an intermittently lit outdoor area. Look for 300 to 980 lumens for walking in the dark without any additional light sources. Mechanics and other workers who need to see precise details in complete darkness might go a little higher, say 1,000 to 1,300 lumens.

Hunters or law enforcement might use brighter flashlights than that, but it really depends on the task. Furthermore, it’s important to remember that powerful flashlights can be disruptive to other people and wildlife, so choose your light carefully.

Flashlight enthusiasts often recommend making a flash part of your “everyday carry,” keeping it on you at all times as you would with your keys or smartphone. Personally, we recommend being proactive and keeping it around whenever or wherever you think there’s a chance you may need some extra light. For example, if you’re going on a hiking trip or evening walk, it’s a no-brainer.

It’s never a bad idea to stash a flashlight on each floor of your home in case of a power outage or other emergency. That way, you can easily grab it without going up or down stairs in the dark. It’s best to keep them in a place that you know you’ll remember and is easy to find in the dark, such as a small drawer.

We also stronglyrecommend keeping a flashlight in your car, as you never know when you might have a mechanical issue at night, and you’ll want to save your phone battery.

Separately, FEMA and The Department of Homeland Security recommend that you keep an emergency kit with a flashlight and other essentials in a cool, dry place somewhere at home, as well as in your car. In order to keep the kit intact and ready for an emergency, the flashlight in your emergency kit should be a backup, not your primary flashlight.
