How Certain Foods in Adolescence Are Linked to IBD Risk

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

— Study finds associations with fruit, fish, and salty snacks, but not always in the same direction

Eating certain foods during childhood and adolescence was associated with higher or lower risks of later inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), prospective data suggested.

In pooled analyses of three cohorts in Norway, Denmark, and Sweden, high versus low intake of fruit in childhood was associated with a reduced risk of Crohn's disease (CD; adjusted HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.47-0.94), as was high versus low intake of fish (aHR 0.65, 95% CI 0.44-0.97), reported Annie Guo, PhD, of the University of Gothenburg Institute of Medicine in Sweden, and colleagues in Gut.

On the other hand, high versus low fruit intake in childhood was associated with an increased risk of ulcerative colitis (UC; aHR 1.54, 95% CI 1.07-2.20).

During adolescence, high versus low fruit intake was associated with a reduced risk of CD (aHR 0.57, 95% CI 0.36-0.88), but no significant associations were observed between fruit consumption and UC.

Meanwhile, high versus low intake of salty snacks during adolescence was initially associated with an increased risk of IBD (HR 1.43, 95% CI 1.11-1.86), but after multivariable adjustment, the risk was attenuated (aHR 1.32, 95% CI 0.99-1.77). The adjusted HR for UC with a high versus low intake of salty snacks in adolescence was 1.51 (95% CI 0.97-2.37).

"To our knowledge, this is the first prospective study to examine associations between food-group intake during both childhood and adolescence and the subsequent risk of IBD, CD, and UC," Guo and team wrote. "If our findings are confirmed in other cohorts and by other study designs, promoting healthy dietary habits could be a potential strategy for IBD prevention."

As to what explains the associations between fruit intake and reduced CD risk, the authors noted that the "dietary fiber content of fruit may partly contribute to the observed inverse association between fruit intake and CD risk by promoting a favorable gut microbial environment."

Conversely, they suggested that the association with an increased risk of UC "could potentially be explained by differences in the etiology between the subtypes, since CD has been suggested to be more related to dysbiosis and UC to mucosal immune dysregulation."

As for the link between higher intake of salty snacks and an increased risk of IBD, they pointed out that the salty snack food group across the three cohorts was mostly composed of items high in saturated fats, and saturated fatty acids "have been shown to promote proinflammatory immune responses."

For this study, Guo and colleagues pooled data from three prospective population-based birth cohorts in Norway, Denmark, and Sweden encompassing 111,999 participants born in 1996 through 2009, contributing a total of 1,541,247 person-years of follow-up.

Information on dietary intake in childhood (7-8 years) and adolescence (11-15 years) was obtained from food frequency questionnaires, and included intake of fruit, vegetables, dairy products, meat, fish, sugar-sweetened drinks, and sweet snacks. Questions on dietary intake of salty snacks, such as potato chips and popcorn, were only asked during the teen years.

Diagnoses of any IBD were captured in national patient registries by 2024.

During the observation period, 653 incident cases of IBD were identified, with country-specific incidence rates of 45, 39, and 62 per 100,000 person-years in Norway, Denmark, and Sweden, respectively.

Following dietary assessment in childhood, the median ages at diagnosis were 15.6 years, 18.3 years, and 25.7 years in Norway, Denmark, and Sweden, respectively.

While the authors observed associations between fruit, fish, and salty snack intake and IBD risk, there were no associations with other food groups such as vegetables and meats.

Guo and colleagues acknowledged that their study had limitations, particularly since it was observational, and thus unmeasured and residual confounding could not be excluded.
