“It was the best of times, it was the worst of times.”

That line was written to describe the sprawling complexity of 18th century Europe. But it could just as easily apply to the Department of Health and Human Services. Sure, Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. is facing an oppositional Democratic Congress, a growing measles epidemic (with two more deaths recorded, bringing the total to 10), and a weakened MAHA movement.

And yet, sources told STAT’s Chelsea Cirruzzo that Kennedy has new pep in his step, thanks to recently-appointed White House Chief of Staff Stephen Miller. Miller is purportedly a MAHA superfan, with a particular interest in vaccine schedule shakeups and redefining ultra-processed foods. And one week into the job, he’s already handed message control back to cabinet secretaries.

Kennedy and his team are preparing to charge hard at major MAHA targets. One close aid is even telling staff that it’s go time for MAHA. Read more here.

Meanwhile, Democrats have been preparing plans on their own …

It doesn’t have quite the same ring to it as “Medicare for All.” But coming out of a retreat, House Dems have coalesced around a top health policy priority — bringing government-run health care to more Americans by allowing people to access Medicare earlier in their lives. A related proposal, ‘Medikids,’ would provide coverage from birth to 26 years old.

“I think we’ve got to lower the age of eligibility as it relates to Medicare,” House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries (D-N.Y.) told reporters after the Denver gathering. “I think we gotta make sure we cover hearing, vision, and dental. And we’ve got to make sure that every child in America can be able to see a doctor with high-quality, affordable health care.”

STAT’s John Wilkerson writes that this unified message is new territory for a party once split between “those who support Medicare for All and those who prefer incremental changes.” But the comity could obscure deeper differences down the road. Find out more here, and read to the end for a crystallizing boat metaphor from New York Times columnist Ezra Klein.

China’s ascendant drug industry is no monolith, despite the way it’s often described by Western competitors, pundits and policymakers. The country is home to scores of companies with diverging ambitions, founded last year or last century, each vying for attention and capital amid a domestic scientific renaissance and a geopolitical standoff.

It’s complicated. To help make sense of it, STAT and the risk intelligence platform WireScreen on Monday published China’s Biotech Powerhouse 25, a ranking of the country’s most valuable publicly traded firms that tracks the varying strategies — and fortunes — of the world’s rapidly growing new medicine chest. — Damian Garde

As a physician on the edge of burnout, Frances Mei Hardin heard all the tips. Light some candles. Pop over to the doctor appreciation pizza party. Complete these mindfulness worksheets.

“The advice to take a warm bath for respite particularly appealed to me, although before long one bath per day became two,” she writes. “That yielded a yeast infection, which does not exactly help one unwind.”

The problem with all of these suggestions, Hardin writes, is that they put the onus on the physician rather than on the increasingly broken system many doctors operate in: “We already know what works for physicians, and it isn’t any of that level one relaxation stuff. A meta-analysis from 2017 found a 2.5 times greater effect from measures such as workload reduction and schedule changes than stress management and mindfulness education for physicians on an individual basis.” Read her column here.

Increasingly, the tranquilizer medetomidine is making its way into the U.S. drug supply, almost always laced into opioids.

Medetomidine is a powerful sedative, and standard overdose treatments like naloxone won’t always reverse all of its effects. Now, the American Society for Addiction Medicine has released new guidance on how to treat patients who’ve taken the drug. A key piece of advice — providers need to know whether medetomidine is in their local drug supply, since its symptoms can mimic other conditions, including sepsis. Read the full writeup here.

Nearly as soon as doctors-to-be reach medical school, they begin to prepare for the U.S. Medical Licensing Exam Step 1. Step 1 is a bear of a test, and the first two years of medical school are theoretically meant to prepare students to pass.

But a lot of students have concluded their courses just aren’t that useful for this purpose. Many (50 percent, by some estimates!) skip lectures, instead spending thousands of dollars on top of tuition for commercial prep material.

This has created an untenable situation, two medical ethics professors write. Their solution: Break medical school apart. Let students prepare for Step 1 by any approach that makes sense for them — on their own, through an online course or one-year program, as part of pre-med. Once they pass, they can apply for a shortened medical school focused on clinical experience, professional identity formation, and deeper dives into ethics and population health.

This could help lower the cost of medical school attendance, the authors write, while also diversifying the pool of candidates who apply to school. It would also let medical schools refocus their resources on the training that matters. “We strongly believe that medical school should be about learning how to be a doctor, not how to pass a test,” they write.
