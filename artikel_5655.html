How Impactful Is Stock Epinephrine for Anaphylaxis at Schools?

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

— Survey of school nurses suggests failure with relying on students to carry autoinjectors

Most schools that keep a stock epinephrine autoinjector available to treat allergic reactions also require children with known severe allergies to bring their own, a survey of school nurses found.

While 87% of their schools carried stock epinephrine, 71.3% of these still required students to bring a personal supply, reported Mahi K. Singh, MD, of University of Chicago Medicine, at the American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (ACAAI) annual meeting.

With 10% of nurses reporting an emergency when epinephrine was not available for a student who needed it, schools without stock were 4.42 times more likely to report epinephrine unavailability when needed compared with schools that did have it (P<0.001).

All U.S. states now have laws that authorize students with severe allergies to carry and self-administer epinephrine. By 2023, all but four had laws to allow or require schools to stock emergency supplies of epinephrine autoinjectors.

"In anaphylactic emergency, having stock is extremely helpful, even if the school is also requiring personal supply," Singh told MedPage Today. However, "there's a lot of overlap suggesting potentially a lot of excess supply," she said, pointing to a high rate of unused, expired autoinjectors.

"That's also underscored by the fact that epinephrine use in schools is infrequent," Singh added. Almost three-quarters of schools reported no epinephrine administration in the past year, from either personal supply or stock; 97.0% never used more than two epinephrine devices in a single day.

Despite this excess supply, most nurses surveyed (73.6%) said they still favor a dual-coverage model over stock-only supply.

"Our thought was that potentially we could switch or propose switching to a stock-only model to prevent some of this excess or unused supply," Singh said. "It would be more cost effective, and also it would reduce administrative burden on students and families and school nurses. But there remains this discomfort with moving to a stock-only model ... So that definitely needs some more exploration, because nurses know what's happening in schools more than any of us do."

Stock epinephrine doesn't help if there are no school nurses or they don't feel comfortable administering it, commented Jay Lieberman, MD, of the University of Tennessee Health Science Center in Memphis.

Newer forms of epinephrine might help both with hesitancy and other obstacles, he said. "A teacher may be much more willing to do a nose spray on a kid or drop a little film under the kid's tongue rather than jab him with an autoinjector."

The approved nasal epinephrine spray (neffy), a sublingual film he presented data on at the conference, and a nasal dry powder all show better stability data than the autoinjectors, both withstanding freezing and then thawing as well as higher temperatures before degrading, Lieberman said.

And whereas an autoinjector might have an expiration date a year from purchase, the nasal spray lasts typically 2 years, he said. "That helps out a school ... Less changing out of the device if they ever were to stock it. And obviously our patients, because if they don't use it lasts them longer."

The cross-sectional survey presented by Singh was distributed to a national database of 1,847 U.S. school nurses from August through December 2025. The 349 nurses who responded (18.9% response rate) represented 31 states, although almost 40% came from Illinois, where the University of Chicago holds an annual conference for school nurses. Nearly all of the nurses came from public schools (90.8%), with an average of 762 students per school.

Limitations of the study included the low response rate, the outsized influence on the results of Illinois policies, and unknown generalizability to schools that don't have a dedicated school nurse.
