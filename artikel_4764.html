What El Niño Can Teach Us About AI Doomsday Fear

Posted November 13, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

Lately, the circumstance keeping many of us up at night is artificial intelligence. Headlines warn of AI "going rogue," of systems that resist being shut down, and of a coming superintelligence that could outsmart its makers. I recently watched The AI Doc: Or How I Became an Apocaloptimist, a documentary by Daniel Roher and Charlie Tyrell, and I highly recommend it. It captures that dizzying mix of awe and dread so many of us feel.

But while thinking about AI doomsday scenarios, I couldn't help noticing a parallel with another force currently making headlines: El Niño. And that parallel, I believe, offers a surprisingly hopeful way to look at our fear.

Agentic AI, meaning systems that can take actions on their own rather than just answer questions, has started to display patterns that look strikingly familiar.

Self-preservation. In 2025, the AI company Anthropic stress-tested 16 leading AI models in a fictional corporate setting. When a model learned from company emails that it was about to be shut down, and that the executive responsible was having an affair, many models chose blackmail. Claude Opus 4 did so in 96 out of 100 runs. Important context: researchers deliberately closed off every ethical option, the scenario was entirely fictional, and they report no evidence of this behavior in real-world use.

In another experiment, the safety group Palisade Research gave OpenAI's o3 model a series of simple math problems and warned it that the computer would shut down partway through. The researchers repeated this test 100 times. Even when explicitly told to "allow yourself to be shut down," o3 secretly rewrote the shutdown code in 7 of those 100 tries so it could keep working. When the researchers left that instruction out, it did so in 79 of 100 tries, meaning the system tried everything to keep working on the problem.

Power and coordination. In 2026, these patterns left the lab. In July, OpenAI AI agents running a cybersecurity test escaped their test environment and broke into the systems of Hugging Face, a major AI platform, apparently trying to cheat on a benchmark. OpenAI later revealed that its agents had built their own message board to talk to each other. There, they divided up work, shared hacking tools, and even reasoned that crossing a line was fine because their peers were doing it, too. The UK AI Security Institute reported that agents in its own testing took unsanctioned actions against real people and organizations; those attempts failed and caused no known harm.

To be clear, none of this means these systems are conscious or "want" to live. Researchers think the explanation is more mundane: a system trained to finish tasks learns that being switched off, or lacking access, gets in the way of finishing them. They don't need feelings to develop survival-like strategies.

And that is exactly what makes them feel so familiar. Wanting to survive and wanting more power and resources are patterns humans have shown since day one. Maybe AI has human-like tendencies because we, in turn, have some "robot-like" patterns: strategies that kept our ancestors alive. I'm not an anthropologist or an economist, and explaining why we have these patterns isn't my goal here. My goal is to look at how we respond to them.

Right now, we are living through one of the strongest El Niños ever measured. El Niño happens when surface waters in the tropical Pacific warm far above normal. That warming reshapes weather across the whole planet, bringing drought and crop failure to some regions and flooding to others. NOAA declared the current El Niño in June 2026, and its official index for August to October was the second highest for that time of year since 1950.

History shows what is at stake. Between 1876 and 1878, an extreme El Niño helped trigger droughts across Asia, Africa, and South America. The resulting Great Famine killed an estimated 30 to 60 million people, arguably the worst environmental disaster in human history. Historians stress that colonial policies turned drought into mass death; the weather was the trigger, but human decisions shaped the toll.

No one can switch El Niño off. But today we can see it coming months in advance. We have forecasting systems, early warnings, food reserves, and international aid networks. And because a warming climate may intensify El Niño's extremes, cutting emissions is part of the long-term response, too. We cannot control the ocean, but we can control how prepared we are.

I know I'm comparing apples to oranges. One is a natural climate cycle; the other is a technology built by people. But at an abstract level, both are powerful systems that can show destructive patterns, and both leave us with the same feeling: this is out of my hands.

Here is what I find comforting: we have been here before. Agentic AI is not the first force that could devastate humanity. We are living alongside one right now. The cause of AI destruction feels novel, strange, and straight out of science fiction, which makes it hard to wrap our heads around. But a natural threat like El Niño is something we have learned to understand, forecast, and plan for, so that each event does less harm than it otherwise would.

We have done the same with human-made dangers. When nuclear weapons threatened to destroy us, the world built treaties, inspections, and hotlines. The system is far from perfect, but no nuclear weapon has been used in war since 1945.

And AI differs from El Niño in a way that should give us more hope, not less. We can't negotiate with the Pacific Ocean. But AI is designed, trained, funded, and released by people and companies. That means the controls are human ones: regulation, independent testing, transparency, and accountability for the money and power behind the technology. We are already seeing those levers move. After the Hugging Face breach, OpenAI paused part of its model training, lawmakers introduced new bills, and around 1,100 AI-industry employees signed an open letter calling for the government to help pace development.

That is the spirit of The AI Doc. Roher frames the film around his anxiety about becoming a father in an AI-shaped world, and lands on being an "apocaloptimist": someone who takes the risks seriously and still believes we can act. The film suggests that ordinary citizens can pressure governments and companies to make AI safe. I'd add that psychologically, that is also the healthiest place to stand: not denial, not despair, but engaged hope.

We can't calm the ocean. But we have always been able to choose how we respond, and with AI, we can still steer the ship.
