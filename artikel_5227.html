Meta’s Internal Prediction App Uses Points, Not Cash — and That Distinction Matters

Meta is reportedly experimenting with a prediction game for employees in which participants forecast outcomes using points rather than real money. That may sound like a small internal product test, but it offers an interesting window into how large technology companies are thinking about forecasting, social engagement and the mechanics behind prediction markets.

The essential distinction is that a points-based game is not automatically a cash-settled financial market. No public launch or real-money service has been confirmed in the reporting on the experiment, so it would be misleading to describe Meta as having entered the regulated betting or event-contract business.

The Block, citing The Wall Street Journal, reported that Meta was testing an employee application known as Takes, previously called Arena. Participants can make forecasts and earn or lose an internal points balance referred to as cred. The structure resembles a prediction market in some respects, but without the same money flows or legal arrangements as a traded contract.

An internal trial can help answer useful product questions. Do people enjoy making forecasts? How should results be scored? Does a ranking system reward genuine insight or simply encourage attention-grabbing guesses? Those questions matter whether the eventual outcome is a staff game, a public social feature or nothing at all. The existence of a test does not establish which direction Meta will choose.

Real-money prediction markets can involve financial risk, market manipulation concerns, customer eligibility, settlement disputes and questions about gambling or derivatives law. A points-based contest may avoid some of those features, depending on its design. But a game can still have integrity problems if employees feel encouraged to trade on private information or if forecasts become workplace performance signals.

There is also a difference between a prediction with a numerical score and a security or derivative. Those categories are shaped by contract terms and applicable law, not simply by whether the interface looks like a market. Readers should be cautious when comparing an internal staff experiment directly with companies offering event contracts to paying customers.

Regulated prediction markets are attracting scrutiny from federal and state authorities, particularly around sports-related contracts. NewsBTC previously examined a CFTC-related lawsuit involving Coinbase and Kalshi, an example of how disputes over jurisdiction can arise as firms offer products that do not fit neatly into traditional categories.

That uncertainty makes Meta's use of non-cash points noteworthy. A platform can learn about user participation and forecasting behaviour without immediately building the custody, settlement and compliance systems required for real-money products. Whether that is the strategic reason for the design has not been established; it is simply a material distinction between the two models.

Big companies routinely trial features that never reach the public. Employee feedback can reveal problems early, and an internal rename does not by itself mean a product is ready for commercial deployment. Similarly, parallels with AI-agent payment experiments covered by NewsBTC show how companies use small tests to explore unfamiliar transaction designs before the market is settled.

The next meaningful development would be an official description of the experiment's objectives, product scope or possible external release. Until then, Takes is best understood as a reported internal forecasting test — an interesting sign of curiosity, but not evidence that Meta has launched a new exchange.
