Big Walk Set to Miss Out on Indie Awards Over AI Code Controversy

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Daniel is a lifelong gamer with a love for various genres and gameplay styles who enjoys exploring games and playing from various angles.

In March 2027, we’ll be seeing the next iteration of the Independent Games Festival, a celebration and awards show highlighting some of the biggest achievements in the indie gaming sphere. As far as indie games this past year go, an obvious contender is House House’s Big Walk, a cozy exploration game that encourages groups of users to traverse a large island and solve various puzzles using only basic coordination and communication skills. Big Walk did very well for itself, and was part of the discussion for the IGF awards, but a recent disclosure has put a rather sizable damper on those hopes.

Yesterday, a coalition of judges and jurists for the IGF, represented by game journalist and developer Austin Walker, publicly announced their stance on games made with AI: specifically, after lengthy discussion, the judges decided to refrain from voting for any game that utilized AI in any capacity whatsoever. Walker and the judges’ statement, which was posted to his Bluesky account, didn’t specifically mention Big Walk or House House, but it seems it didn’t take long for onlookers to put the pieces together.

Following intense speculation, one of House House’s developers, Bruno Dias, confirmed on his own Bluesky account that, yes, Big Walk does have a small portion of AI-written code within it that was not originally disclosed to players, though it was disclosed to the IGF judges. As a result of this, there is a distinct chance that any awards Big Walk was previously under consideration for are now forfeit.

Around the same time as the judges’ announcement and Dias’s Bluesky post, House House updated the official FAQ on its website with information about the usage of AI in Big Walk.

“In the final stretch of Big Walk’s development, LLMs were used sparingly by House House to help work through difficult technical challenges and bugs,” the FAQ reads, estimating that “well under 0.1% of Big Walk’s code had anything to do with this usage, and generative AI was not used in any other context when making the game.”

The FAQ also adds that this statement is not “an endorsement of, or argument for, the use of AI tools, however minimal.”

“We support the work of those who are trying to limit the harm generative AI is doing to videogames and our world, and apologise that Big Walk’s LLM usage may not meet their standards.”

In his Bluesky post, Dias offered clarification on several points in an effort to “combat misinformation.”

“While some have characterized the LLM usage as just fixing one individual blocking bug, this is a bit inaccurate; the description given by House House mentioned there being several instances, though they did also say it was mostly "consultative" use and not code gen or agents.”

In the final stretch of Big Walk’s development, LLMs were used sparingly by House House to help work through difficult technical challenges and bugs.

He added that this disclosure prompted a lot of discussion within IGF’s internal forums, some of which Dias found disingenuous or dumb, but he has no intention of outing anyone for their opinions or discussing the matter further. He also noted that, at least as far as his understanding of it goes, Big Walk’s particular usage of AI is not in violation of Steam’s AI disclosure policies, which are slightly different from those used by IGF.

As has frequently been the case in the last couple of years, this particular late-stage disclosure hasn’t gone over great with players. While Big Walk’s overall rating on Steam still remains at Overwhelmingly Positive, at the time of writing, an outpouring of negative reviews have begun to crop up, each expressing disappointment that House House waited as long as it did after the game’s very successful release to disclose its AI usage.

This entire event is rather similar to what transpired with the Indie Game Awards last year, when Clair Obscur: Expedition 33 had its title of Game of the Year stripped after the developers admitted to a similarly small usage of AI in the game’s development.

The specter of generative AI usage isn’t just limited to the deluge of overtly vibe-coded slop that’s been flooding Steam and itch.io recently; even the smallest usage of AI, especially when it’s not disclosed from the jump, can feel extremely disingenuous to players, doubly so in the indie scene, which prides itself on from-scratch, creativity-first game development.
