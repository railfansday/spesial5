Germany news: Deportations on the decline

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Despite a decrease in overall deportations, more people were sent back to Turkey. Volkswagen is facing a fresh 'Dieselgate' trial. And Germany's leading DAX stock index hit a new high. DW has more.

Here are the latest headlines from across Germany on Thursday, November 5:

Despite increasing signs that Europe's biggest economy is recovering after several tough years, a new study shows that one in three companies in Germany still plans to slash investments in 2027.

"Only one in five manufacturing businesses plans to expand capacity - only half as many as in 2018," said the German Chamber of Commerce and Industry (DIHK), which surveyed companies across the country.

Additionally, the DIHK found that for most firms planning to spend in the next fiscal year their money will largely be spent on replacing machinery and equipment rather than innovation or development.

"As long as private companies do not invest more again, neither more growth nor new jobs will be created," said DIHK chief Helena Melnikov.

Recently, data showed that after two years of recession, the German economy is expected to grow by 1.3% by the end of 2026 and that trend should continue into next year.

Melnikov cautioned, however, that "this growth has been bought with taxpayers' money and new public debt. State aid must not obscure the deep structural problems of Germany as a business location."

"Anyone who wants sustainable growth again must release the brakes on investment and innovation," she added.

Business daily Handelsblatt has reported that the federal government has reached a deal with the  KNDS defense firm for a takeover of the German part of the business by the state.

Although headquartered in the Netherlands, KNDS is a holding company for French weapons maker Nexter and the German defense company Krauss-Maffei Wegmann (KMW).

Up until now, KMW, a major tank manufacturer, has been family-owned and controlled.

Handelsblatt said it had received a copy of an agreement, labeled "confidential," which states that the German government will now have a 50% stake in the business.

The deal has long been in the works, but this was the first confirmation that negotiations were finished.

Authorities in Berlin have ordered a full autopsy for Hubert Aiwanger, the economy minister and deputy premier of Bavaria, who was found dead in his hotel room in the German capital late on Wednesday.

Officials told German news agency DPA that they hope this would determine the 55-year-old's cause of death, which is still unclear.

He had been in Berlin to open a new office for his conservative populist "Free Voters" party, which rules in a coalition with the Christian Social Union (CSU) in Bavaria. The CSU is the sister party of Chancellor Friedrich Merz's Christian Democrats (CDU).

Known for his antagonistic political style, Aiwanger has made a name for himself despite his party's small standing on the federal level — including accusations of being far-right and antisemitic.

Those who had seen him prior to his death said he had been complaining of not feeling well and had canceled an appearance due to health concerns.

Germany's leading DAX stock index hit a record high on Thursday morning, reaching 26,644.33 points. The previous record, set in August, was about 30 points lower.

Most European stock markets recorded an increase on Thursday due to optimism following the results of the US midterm elections. This is partly due to President Donald Trump's signaled willingness to work with a Democrat-controlled Congress and the perception that regulatory and trade policy will now be less erratic.

This was also coupled with a slight ease in oil prices, after the Trump-led war with Iran sent fuel costs skyrocketing in recent months.

The federal government is set to give the German Swimming Association some €20 million ($22.5 million) over a four-year period in order to get more young children into swimming lessons.

A recent survey found that more than half of elementary school children cannot swim properly, prompting concerns about their safety around water.

In recent decades, lack of funding in infrastructure has seen public swimming pool closures around the country, with people in rural areas especially having to travel further to reach increasingly crowded pools. This was compounded by the COVID-19 pandemic, which saw a number of programs close and never reopen.

In some areas, parents put their children on waiting lists for years for subsidized public swimming lessons or else have to pay exorbitant amounts for private ones.

The Bundestag is also currently debating driving school reforms, which are largely private and usually charge thousands of euros for instruction — increasingly becoming unaffordable for large sections of the German population, yet still required to apply for a driver's license.

Lawmakers hope to finalize the reform soon, which includes more transparent pricing, online theory lessons, and the ability to have some of the required practice hours on the road led by a relative rather than a driving instructor.

Ballon d'Or winner and England captain Harry Kane has extended his club contract with Bayern Munich, the footballer's team announced Thursday.

"I feel at home at Bayern Munich. I love the club, the fans, the city of Munich. We have a fantastic team and Vincent Kompany is a fantastic coach. It am extremely proud to a part of this club," Kane said of signing on for two more years.

Kane scored an incredible 26 goals in the Bundesliga last year, more than any other player in the league.

Police in the central German city of Kassel said on Thursday that they had cleared a student protest overnight, removing some 47 people from a lecture hall.

Three of the "occupiers," were charged over the incident, police said, and the other 44 were released after officers documented their identities.

However, "all 47 are being investigated for disturbing the peace," the authorities added.

The students were protesting changes the University of Kassel has made to what is called the "civil clause" employed by most public German universities. It is part of the university charter that ensures all research at the institution is conducted solely for civil purposes and never for military or corporate interests.

University officials have said that the change to their civil clause is part of their new "peace initiative," and simply seeks to clarify "the difficulty of distinguishing between civilian and military purposes within this research institution."

More than 10 years and many lawsuits and investigations later, the Volkswagen 'Dieselgate' scandal has once again landed in front of a judge.

Also referred to as the Volkswagen emissions scandal, the news broke in September 2015 that the German auto giant had been using a "defeat device" to cheat United States emissions tests.

Thursday's proceedings in Braunschweig are the latest in a series of legal battles against several former VW executives who have appealed their sentences.

There is also the case of then-CEO Martin Winterkorn. He resigned in the wake of the scandal but has not faced any legal consequences. After years of investigations, his trial was delayed multiple times by the COVID-19 pandemic, and then by health issues. It remains unclear when his trial will restart.

The number of people deported from Germany decreased by nearly 19% from January to September 2026 compared to the previous year, the Interior Ministry announced following a request from the Left Party.

Some 14,324 people were deported in the first nine months of this year, down from 17,651 in 2025. Turkey was the most common destination, followed by Georgia and Algeria.

The numbers reveal that deportations to Syria remain difficult due to the political situation there, even for convicted criminals. Six people have been returned there since the beginning of 2026.

Deportations to Turkey, however, appeared to buck the trend, as 2,025 people have been sent there.

According to the Interior Ministry, the number of foreign nationals leaving the country voluntarily has also slightly increased. Some 14,140 people have received assistance to return to their countries of origin, compared to 12,263 the year before.
