The 2-Hour Rest Rule Ultra-Long-Haul Airbus A350 Pilots Have Before Landing

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

He became involved in the flight simulator and VATSIM communities, which sparked his interest in aviation and eventually led him to earn his private pilot license. Ultra-long-haul flights are built around a rule written backward from the landing. Federal regulations governing augmented operations require that the pilot who will fly the aircraft during landing must have two consecutive hours of in-flight rest available during the second half of the flight duty period. The requirement does not simply demand that pilots rest. It specifies which pilot, and when, based on what they will be responsible for at the end of the flight.

Singapore Airlines operates Singapore to New York with four pilots across a duty period of roughly 20 hours, double the crew the Airbus A350 requires. The Points Guy describes two ways the flight is divided: an even 8/8 split and a 6/8/2 rotation that trims relief-crew sleep so the operating crew is back on the flight deck around three hours before landing. Separately, a different rule governs the shorter naps pilots take in the seat rather than the bunk, capping them at 40 minutes and requiring a 20-minute recovery period before the pilot resumes any duty. Both rules exist for the same reason.

Federal regulations governing augmented flight operations contain a provision written specifically around the landing. During the flight duty period, the pilot who will fly the aircraft during landing must have two consecutive hours of in-flight rest available during the second half of the duty period. The rule does not simply require that pilots rest. It requires that a particular pilot rests at a particular point in the flight, determined by which phase of the operation they will be responsible for.

The provision sits within a broader framework for augmented operations. An augmented flight crew includes more pilots than the aircraft's minimum certification requirement, which allows one pilot to be replaced by another for inflight rest. US regulations define an augmented flight crew in those terms, and Part 117 establishes separate duty limits for augmented operations than for standard ones. Throughout the flight, at least one qualified flight crew member must remain at the controls.

Rest requirements extend beyond the flight itself. Before a duty period begins, Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) rules require at least ten consecutive hours of rest, including a minimum of eight uninterrupted hours of sleep opportunity. The two-hour inflight provision therefore applies to a crew that has already met a pre-flight rest standard, and it governs how that crew distributes the additional sleep available to them once the aircraft is airborne.

Singapore Airlines operates Singapore to New York with four pilots, comprising two captains and two first officers, across a duty period of roughly 20 hours. The pilots split into two pairs, each taking turns at the controls while the other rests.

How the split is structured is a planning decision, not a fixed formula. The Points Guy described two approaches used on a flight of around 17.5 hours. The first is an 8/8 split, which gives each crew pair roughly eight hours of rest and divides the flight evenly between them. The second is a 6/8/2 rotation, which breaks the flight into three segments and distributes the rest unevenly.

The second option deliberately trims the relief crew's sleep. Under the 6/8/2 structure, the operating crew returns to the flight deck roughly three hours before landing rather than shortly before the approach. That extra time lets them assess weather changes and operational updates before descending. The relief pilots give up some rest to make it possible. Whatever structure is chosen, the handover itself is formal. Swapping crews involves a briefing covering fuel, weather, and traffic, which means a pilot returning from rest is brought up to date on the flight's current state rather than simply resuming where they left off.

The A350's reputation as a 'passenger favorite' masks a more complex truth for flight crews, whose rest experiences can vary widely.

The A350's flight crew rest facility sits above the forward galley, accessed through a hatch in the ceiling that most passengers walk beneath without noticing. It contains two bunks and, depending on configuration, a reclining seat and an entertainment monitor, with independent temperature controls. Simple Flying previously explored how the facility is used only when three or more pilots are on duty, making it specific to augmented operations.

The classification matters more than the furnishings. The FAA defines three classes of rest facilities under 14 CFR Part 117, and the ranking runs opposite to what the numbering suggests. A Class 1 facility is a bunk or other surface that allows a flat sleeping position, located separately from both the flight deck and the passenger cabin, temperature-controlled, with crew control over lighting, and isolated from noise and disturbance. Class 2 is a cabin seat allowing a flat or near-flat position, separated from passengers by at least a curtain. Class 3 is a seat in the cabin or on the flight deck that reclines at least 40 degrees and provides leg and foot support.

That classification determines how long the aircraft can legally fly. FAA guidance states that each rest facility carries a classification defining the maximum flight duty period limits for augmented operations, with a Class 1 facility providing for the longest duty period and a Class 3 the shortest. The FAA also specifies physical requirements: Class 1 sleeping surfaces measure 78 by 30 inches, surrounded by 35 cubic feet of space, plus a communal area of 65 cubic feet for entering, exiting, and changing. The A350's two-bunk overhead compartment meets the Class 1 description.

Bunk rest is not the only way a pilot sleeps in flight. Controlled rest is a separate procedure in which a pilot naps in their seat on the flight deck while the other pilot remains at the controls, and it operates under entirely different rules. Aviatize drew a clear distinction, describing controlled rest as different from uncontrolled microsleeps, which are dangerous, and from bunk or class-rest breaks taken away from the controls by augmented crews on long-haul flights.

The procedure focuses on avoiding deep sleep rather than achieving it. Flight Safety Foundation guidance specifies that a controlled rest period should be no longer than 40 minutes to minimize the risk of sleep inertia, keeping the nap short enough that the pilot does not slip into slow-wave sleep. Afterward, a recovery period of at least 20 minutes is required, during which the resting pilot takes no part in flight duties or briefings while sleep inertia dissipates. A crew briefing follows the recovery period, and the guidance states that both the recovery and the briefing should be completed at least 30 minutes before the top of descent.

The rule does not apply uniformly across the industry. Aviatize notes that the FAA does not authorize in-seat cockpit naps for Part 121 operations, having considered and deliberately excluded controlled rest when the modern flight and duty rules were written. The FAA does permit in-flight rest for augmented crews who leave the controls, which is the bunk arrangement rather than the seat one. Under EASA and many other authorities, controlled rest is permitted under defined conditions. A pilot flying for a European carrier may therefore take a 40-minute nap in the seat with a mandatory 20-minute recovery afterward, while a pilot flying the same aircraft for a US operator may not.

Sleep inertia is the transitory period of impaired performance and alertness that occurs immediately after waking. It is not drowsiness in the ordinary sense but a measurable degradation in the ability to perform tasks, and it can significantly affect a pilot's capacity to carry out normal duties after waking. That is why a period free of both duties and briefings must follow any period of actual sleep in the flight deck.

The severity scales with sleep depth. Short naps of around 10 minutes produce relatively mild inertia, while longer periods of sleep generally produce more severe inertia and add to the recovery time before a pilot is fully alert. That trade-off is the reason controlled rest is capped at 40 minutes. A longer nap delivers greater restorative benefit but leaves the pilot less usable immediately afterward, and in a seat on the flight deck with one pilot already resting, immediate usability matters.

Bunk rest sits at the other end of that curve. A pilot sleeping for six or eight hours in a Class 1 facility reaches deep sleep by design, which is the entire point of carrying additional crew and building a compartment above the forward galley. The inertia on waking is correspondingly greater. That is the logic underneath the two-hour provision and underneath Singapore Airlines' 6/8/2 rotation alike. Neither positions the rest immediately before the approach. The regulation requires two consecutive hours in the second half of the duty period, and the airline's own planning returns the operating crew to the flight deck roughly three hours before landing. Both leave a gap between waking and the most demanding phase of the flight, which is the same principle that the 20-minute controlled rest recovery applies at a much smaller scale.
