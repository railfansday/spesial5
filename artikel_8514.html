Start a Food Truck in 10 Steps – Price Permits & Parking Before You Buy

Call your local health department and city licensing office before you shop for a food truck, and price what they require: the permits, a licensed commissary kitchen for restocking and cleaning, and a legal place to park overnight. Add insurance and cash to carry a slow month, then buy the truck only if the plan still works.

A truck your health department won’t permit, or one with no legal place to park, earns nothing however good the food is.

Two offices set those rules and most of the costs that come with them. The health department decides how the truck is built and where it’s serviced. Los Angeles County won’t issue a permit until it has approved the truck’s commissary, a licensed kitchen where trucks restock, dump wastewater, and clean up. In New York City, every permitted truck has to use a facility the health department approves for cleaning, servicing, and storage. The city sets where you can sell and what it charges, and that varies widely. In its 2018 Food Truck Nation study of 20 cities, the U.S. Chamber of Commerce Foundation found that owners paid about $17,066 to Boston’s city government, nearly 29 times what they paid in Indianapolis.

Some of those costs come due every month. The commissary’s rent and the insurance premium don’t shrink when a rainy week keeps customers home, so the plan has to cover them before you buy the truck.

Choose food you can cook well in a small kitchen, and keep the menu short. The menu decides the equipment the truck needs, and often the health permit too. New York City charges $200 for a two-year permit on a truck that prepares food on board and $75 for one whose food is prepackaged or prepared elsewhere. Testing recipes costs little more than ingredients. Ask the health department which permit your menu calls for before you settle on it.

List every startup cost and every monthly bill, then work out how many meals a day cover them. For a food truck, that means the truck, its equipment, permits, commissary rent, insurance, fuel, ingredients, and cash for a few slow months. The plan costs nothing but time. Take its numbers from the fee schedules your health department and city licensing office publish and from the rate sheets of two or three commissaries.

The plan’s total tells you how much you need. Decide how much comes from savings and how much you’ll borrow, and put any loan payment in the plan’s monthly bills, since it’s due in a slow month too.

Register the business as your state requires, and get an employer identification number (EIN), the business’s federal tax ID, which the IRS issues free. Choosing between a sole proprietorship and an LLC is its own decision, and so is opening a bank account for the business.

Then price the permits that follow: a city business license, a state sales tax permit, a food safety certificate, the health permit, the city’s vending license, and a fire safety permit if you cook with propane or run a generator. Chicago charges $1,000 for a two-year license to prepare food on board and $100 for that fire safety permit. New York City’s permit fee is small, but you can apply for a year-round street permit only when its health department contacts you from a waiting list. The health permit comes last, once the truck passes inspection, so price it now and apply later.

Find out where your city lets trucks sell before you count on a corner. Chicago bars trucks from parking within 200 feet of a street-level restaurant’s main customer entrance, except from midnight to 2 a.m., and keeps each stop to four hours or less on a block. Breweries, office parks, and festivals each set their own terms for hosting a truck, so ask before you plan around one. Your city’s licensing office has the street rules.

Where your health department requires a commissary, you’ll go there to fill the water tank, empty the wastewater tank, store food, and clean the truck. Some health departments also decide where the truck is parked overnight. Los Angeles County doesn’t allow a truck to be stored at home, so a legal overnight spot belongs in the plan too.

Rent varies widely. Esser’s Commissary in Durham, North Carolina, charges food trucks $350 a month, and Fast Dash Commissary in Anaheim, California, charges $1,300 a month for a food truck spot. Confirm that your health department approves a commissary before you sign with it.

Get the health department’s rules for a truck’s layout and equipment before you shop, since some counties won’t let a truck operate until they’ve approved its plans. Sonoma County, California, asks buyers to call before purchasing to make sure a truck meets current food code.

WebstaurantStore, a restaurant supply retailer, puts new food trucks at $100,000 to $150,000, used ones at $50,000 to $100,000, and leases at $2,000 to $3,000 a month. A trailer needs a vehicle to tow it, and a cart limits what you can cook, but either costs less than a truck. Have a mechanic check a used truck’s engine before you pay.

Insure the truck with commercial auto coverage, since it’s a business vehicle on the road, and carry general liability, which pays if a customer is hurt or their property is damaged. Add workers’ compensation, which covers an employee hurt on the job, once you hire. Insureon, an online insurance agency, puts general liability for food trucks at about $37 a month and commercial auto for food service businesses, trucks among them, at about $209 a month. Compare quotes from two or three small business insurance companies.

Customers can’t find a truck that moves unless you tell them where it’ll be. Set up free social media accounts before launch, and post the truck’s spot and hours every day you sell.

Catering sets the head count and the price before the day, so a rainy forecast doesn’t change what the event pays. Weddings, office lunches, and private parties can fill the weeks when street sales are slow. Check with the health department that your permit covers catering.

Start with one call to your health department’s mobile food program. Ask for its rules for the truck, its fee schedule, and its list of approved commissaries, then build the plan around them.
