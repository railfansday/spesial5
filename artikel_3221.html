Raft of heavy hitters: Qatar’s Doha Film Festival closes this evening after a second successful edition in its revamped format, having defied the complicated regional geopolitical tensions in the backdrop sparked by the U.S.-Israeli war on Iran. The mood around Doha’s Katara Cultural Village, which hosted the festival, and other hotspots like the Msheireb Downtown district and adjacent Souq Wafiq, was generally relaxed, with restaurants and other venues packed out. Big names passing through included James Gray with Paper Tiger, while Japanese Cannes regular Naomi Kawase was jury president. Program highlights included Sara Isaq’s opening film, The Station, set in a female-only gas station in a remote part of Yemen and giving a rare human insight into a country too long associated with conflict. As well as Ishaq and her scattered cast, the festival flew in several Yemeni figures such as Yemeni-U.S. coffee entrepreneur Mokhtar Alkhanshali, the subject of Dave Eggers Monk of Mokha, and influencer Nadir Nahdi. There were also strong Palestinian and Gulf region delegations. The Station is among 15 films in international competition and you can keep an eye on Deadline for the winners later this evening. The festival also hosted the second edition of the Film Committee at Media City Qatar’s Industry Days from November 19 to 21, which saw a raft of industry heavy-hitters stop by to discuss the region’s burgeoning film and TV sectors along with Joe Russo (pictured) and a very special Avengers trailer. Tricky Week For BAFTA Watch on Deadline

It’s Bad, It’s Bad (You Know It): A tricky week for BAFTA as it nervously prepares for the first Film Awards since the car-crash I Swear saga. It was reported over the weekend that the team behind one of the year’s biggest Box Office success stories, Michael Jackson biopic Michael, will not be submitting the Antoine Fuqua movie for consideration. At this point, motivations are unclear, but GK Films producer Graham King criticized the awards body plus BBC after the I Swear saga for “displaying cultural insensitivity and tone deafness.” This may have contributed to GK’s decision to wrench Michael away from the BAFTAs. There is also the fact that the Leaving Neverland documentary, which spotlighted two men alleging the deceased singer groomed then sexually abused them, won a TV BAFTA several years back. Either way, this was unwelcome news with the event less than three months away. Compounding the Michael reports was BAFTA losing its longtime headline film awards sponsor, EE. This decision was driven by EE’s “business strategy,” according to the awards org, but the timing could have been better. Nerves will be jangling at BAFTA’s Piccadilly HQ and executives will be looking to avoid any more of these stories before the curtain rises in late Feb.
