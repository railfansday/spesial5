Tomi Adeyemi Does Not Want to Talk About the Children of Blood and Bone Movie

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

“You are a once in a generation talent,” says Tomi Adeyemi, tears in her eyes. The author is not paying me a compliment; she is reading a line from one of her numerous fan letters.

Adeyemi is a New York Times bestselling writer, a feat she accomplished at twenty-four with her 2018 fantasy debut novel Children of Blood and Bone—the first in her Legacy of Orïsha trilogy, which marries old-fashioned American YA with West African mythology. Her new novel, The Siren—released in September—follows Emery, a freshman at Dartmouth desperate to find her place as she undertakes a series of trials to join the titular group of powerful girls on campus.

This is Adeyemi’s first time since childhood not writing fantasy; maybe her first time period, since she says the first book she ever wrote was a Parent Trap fan fiction. But it’s also an attempt to move past the widely successful young-adult fantasy with which she made her name—as well as the drama surrounding the film adaptation of Adeyemi’s debut novel, an experience she has called the worst thing she ever lived through.

In now-deleted TikToks, Adeyemi said that she’s distancing herself from the film, she won’t watch it, and that she blocked one of its leads, Amandla Stenberg, on social media. Sources indicated to VF this summer that Adeyemi got more creative control over the film than most first-time authors do, including a co-screenwriting credit; they also noted that she's switched talent agencies multiple times since striking an initial deal with ICM. “There must be something [going on] because she’s burned every bridge,” one insider who works in the book-to-film space said.

When we meet, Adeyemi says that she doesn’t want to talk about that.

Instead, she talks about how she used to exclusively write fantasy because the world wasn’t safe enough for her to write anything else. Now, though, she no longer feels like she has to hide behind magic. “I’ve become someone who can inhabit it fully,” she says. “When you allow a woman to grow her claws, I am more akin to a jungle cat than I am a house plant.” She breaks into a chorus of Frank Sinatra’s “My Way,” adding a Jackson 5-esque run: “Da-da-da, oh, baby, yeah-yeah.” She tells me that sometimes, when she connects with certain people—like me—she just can’t help herself from singing. “The stars, the moon!” she belts—lines from Florence + the Machine’s “Cosmic Love.”

Adeyemi’s a musical person. She saw Florence play at Lollapalooza: “Somehow, Florence wielded her magic so I was able to get to the very front row, and she’s singing to me. I’m like, Look at this wild woman, howling to the moon.” This reminds her of a scene from her new book: when Roux, the girl Emery idolizes, is introduced. Adeyemi writes Roux dancing on a table, kicking glasses, alive and wild. Emery wants to be like that; so does Adeyemi. She explains this before singing some more: “You woke up like that, you woke up like that.”

Adeyemi’s admiration for Roux is almost tangible. She smiles dreamily when she speaks about her creation, leaving you to wonder if there was a specific person this character was based on.

Though Adeyemi started working on The Siren in 2020, she says the novel’s published version was written entirely last year. She’s gotten used to trying, and trying again; while applying for fiction writing classes in college, Adeyemi says, she was rejected five semesters in a row. One professor actually told her he couldn’t teach her to write, an anecdote she’s happy to relate now: “Hello! So many bestsellers later.” She spent four and a half years writing her first, unpublished novel, which she says was rejected 63 times. Only then did she write Children of Blood and Bone.

Adeyemi says she writes to free herself—and while one can’t be sure exactly what she had to free herself from last year, the film adaptation of Blood and Bone was shot in 2025. That’s also when Adeyemi allegedly fell out with Stenberg. And while they’re spelled differently, it’s notable that Roux—The Siren’s charismatic villain—has the same name as one of Stenberg’s most popular characters, Rue in the first Hunger Games movie.

Female authors are often asked, unfairly, whether they are writing their own lives into a story. Yet it’s also impossible to ignore the parallels between Emery and Adeyemi herself. Both are Ivy-educated writers and self-proclaimed outsiders. Adeyemi also doesn’t shy from comparisons between herself and her most recent main character: she says that all authors write themselves, because doing so is a vehicle for understanding. Reading The Siren is like stepping into her consciousness.

So is interviewing her. Adeyemi has a tendency to go off on tangents about astrology, tarots, mystical energy. “Do you remember how it felt when the entire universe came alive? All these spindles, nerve endings rise,” she asks me at one point, holding out her arms. “If you could feel the stars and taste the wind?”

The novel is wrapped up in themes of belonging, identity, and loneliness—a narrative Adeyemi tells me she relates to. “I think if you don’t belong to yourself, you don’t belong anywhere,” she says—though Adeyemi also thinks that her Aquarius placements mean people like her will never fit in, because they are not designed to. Take “our most iconic Aquarius rising, Audrey Hepburn,” or SZA, who famously wears fairy wings onstage and on red carpets. “How painful must it have been when you were on land?” Adeyemi asks her fellow Aquarius rhetorically. “How painful must it have been to learn how to fly? How painful is it for the butterflies inside before they become their butterflies?”

Adeyemi herself appears on the cover of The Siren—something that “has never been done before,” she says. “But a lot of what I do has never been done before. I remember choosing the image because I saw the darkness, that power in Emery. I’ve never seen that look in anyone else’s eye.”

There was a time where having her books adapted for the screen felt like the epitome of Adeyemi’s work. The author no longer feels that way: “I am the epitome of my work,” she says. And as she said on those now-deleted TikToks, she really would rather not talk about the film adaptation of her first novel at all.

We avoid the topic during our initial conversation. When our time runs out, though, Adeyemi tells me she’d like to talk more at a later date. When I do eventually speak to Adeyemi again—and ask about the Blood and Bone film—she shuts down physically. Her smile drops; the question throws off the energy of the conversation, almost like a betrayal has occurred. I ask another question, and Adeyemi tells me that she is getting a call. She turns off her camera, and I sit in silence for seven minutes.

Eventually, Adeyemi returns and asks if I am still there. She tells me, “The Siren is number three, New York Times.” She’s feeling the hot flash version of goosebumps, she says, like she just finished a marathon.

Adeyemi says that all of this—her career, her work, her writing process—was inspired by extreme fear. She speaks about it in the second person: “An agonizing understanding that you’ve been taught to work so hard to build a better life, [but] you might not get to build that life. It could be snapped away from you like that. I live in that terror.” She’s gone through her own trials: not just the long journey to publishing Blood and Bone, but the internet’s scrutiny over the comments she made about the Blood and Bone adaptation. “I’m exceptionally proud of who I am today,” she says, though “life is terrifying.”

In our first conversation, Adeyemi had said The Siren might be her last book—that she might be finished with writing altogether. “You can depart from all these previous eras of your life with a gentle sort of ruthlessness,” she said, “and then tomorrow can actually be a new day.” Now, she seems reenergized by the idea of work, telling me that she’s met children’s literacy organizations that try to inspire kids with the work of James Baldwin, Octavia Butler, and herself. “I’m proud of writing this from a really hard time and place,” she says, “and I wanna continue Emery’s journey.”

I ask her what changed between that interview and this one. Her smile freezes; she laughs awkwardly before hesitantly replying, “I think it’s interesting talking to you in the summer and then talking to you post this tour. I don’t know how the piece will end up, since you’re getting two very distinct cross sections.”

She takes another beat. “So that quote felt more… there was more of a resonance the first time we spoke. As I’m sitting here with you now, I just have a vision of myself writing deeply.”

The time between our talks seems to have given her space for deep reflection. I remind Adeyemi what she’d told me about feeling more like a jungle cat than a houseplant, and ask if she felt she had to be a jungle cat during her journey with the Blood and Bone film. “I think all women have to be in tune with their power. I think Black women have to be in tune with their power, dark-skinned Black women especially,” she answers. “There’s a sense of gravity that wouldn’t have been there without knowing what it is to step into my power and voice. You owe it to yourself to feel safe, and if you’re the only one there to defend yourself, you have to have the claws.”

As the film’s January release inches closer, one has to wonder if Adeyemi has a desire to retract her claws—and her statements about the movie. Or at least whether everything that’s transpired has changed her relationship with her debut novel. “I think my relationship with myself has changed more than anything,” Adeyemi answers. When narrating the audiobook for The Siren, she noticed that Emery doesn’t recognize her own genius. That broke Adeyemi’s heart, because the character’s insecurities came from a personal place. The difference between her character and her own feelings also shows Adeyemi’s own growth: “You don’t feel that way anymore,” she told herself.

When asked whether she might try to make another movie someday—or adapt The Siren—she answers firmly: “I’ve already done that journey. It’s a lot of work, but it’s not work that is for myself. When you commit to doing something on screen, it’s more about everyone else on the other side of that screen. I think because I have such a directorial and cinematic approach to my writing, I do value having experienced everything.” Selling the rights to your novel means essentially giving it away to other people, she says. That was extremely difficult for her. “I don’t need anyone in this world.”

Maybe Adeyemi’s film future lies instead in front of the camera. She did film a cameo in Blood and Bone, and one in the second Devil Wears Prada film: “At the beginning of the year, I thought, Oh, I would like to try acting,” she laughs now. “By the end of the year, I had a cameo across from Viola Davis and another across from Meryl Streep.” In the future, she’d be interested in voice acting and animation. Or live-action—but maybe only if, say, Jon Chu asked her to star in a Bollywood rom-com with Dev Patel.

“SASUKE!” She yells the name of a Naruto character in a high-pitched voice, then adds in her normal voice, “I’m clearly a very animated person.”
