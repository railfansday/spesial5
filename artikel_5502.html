Antihypertensives Still Worth It for the Healthiest 10%, Large Study Suggests

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

— Drop in major cardiovascular events evident across spectrum of predicted risk

Across hypertensive individuals with varying predicted cardiovascular risk, blood pressure (BP) reduction was associated with a lower occurrence of real major cardiovascular events, according to a meta-analysis.

Using individual participant data from randomized trials, investigators found that major cardiovascular events were significantly less likely with every 5-mm Hg reduction in systolic BP from pharmacotherapy over 5 years (11.4% vs 13.0% with placebo or other control; HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.88-0.92).

This arguably covered people across the whole spectrum of cardiovascular health, as indicated by a study-specific risk model that put people with 5-year predicted risk <4% in the lowest decile and those with estimated risk >27% in the highest decile, reported Kazem Rahimi, DM, MSc, of the University of Oxford in England, and colleagues in the New England Journal of Medicine.

"BP lowering was associated with a decreased risk of major cardiovascular events across the risk spectrum, including a predicted 5-year risk below 4%," the researchers concluded.

The study adds to the limited literature on BP-lowering treatment in low-risk populations. Without better evidence, guideline recommendations for BP reduction in these individuals had been based on the assumption that the observed benefits of BP-lowering therapy in higher-risk individuals may be extrapolated to peers at lower risk.

"Many of the hypertensive trials, such as the SPRINT trial, have noted cardiovascular risk reduction in a high-risk group of patients with antihypertensive therapy. This meta-analysis shows that antihypertensive therapy reduces cardiovascular events across the risk spectrum and notably low-risk patients have a similar relative risk reduction as higher-risk patients," commented Matthew Sorrentino, MD, of University of Chicago Medicine.

Nevertheless, Rahimi's team found that the greatest absolute reduction in cardiovascular events occurred in the highest-risk group. The number needed to treat to prevent one major cardiovascular event over 5 years was 38 in the highest decile of predicted risk versus 255 in the lowest decile.

This is to be expected, according to Sorrentino. "The number needed to treat in a low-risk population will be significantly higher than in a high-risk cohort because of a lower incidence of events in the low-risk group, but the important finding is that risk reduction can be demonstrated in the low-risk cohort."

"Antihypertensive therapy will benefit anyone with consistently elevated BP regardless of calculated risk," he stressed.

Sorrentino said that the study supports the American Heart Association and the American College of Cardiology guideline on BP treatment, which does not suggest holding treatment in lower-risk individuals unable to achieve BP target with a lifestyle program.

Starting in 2025, the American groups said that low-risk individuals with an estimated 10-year cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk <7.5% (based on the PREVENT-CVD calculator) should initiate antihypertensives if average systolic BP remains at least 130 mm Hg (or average diastolic BP remains at least 80 mm Hg) after 3-6 months of lifestyle intervention, a class I recommendation.

Of note, Rahimi's team had an internal risk estimator for the meta-analysis, not PREVENT or another calculator used in clinical practice.

"This study is helpful to help us understand how BP lowering can be important even in lower-risk groups. While not all risk calculators are comparable, the overarching message is the same," maintained Sadiya Khan, MD, MSc, of Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine in Chicago.

"The key thing we know is that treating BP may be one of the most important things we can do to prevent cardiovascular disease even if someone may be at lower risk," she told MedPage Today. "If they are at higher risk, they will get much more absolute benefit from the treatment, so for individuals who have already had a heart attack or stroke, this could not be more important."

For their meta-analysis, Rahimi and colleagues relied on individual participant data from 51 randomized trials comparing antihypertensive treatment with placebo, an active comparator, or usual care.

The 357,440-person cohort had a mean age of 65 years and was 42% women. Mean baseline BP was 152.4/87.3 mm Hg, and the median predicted 5-year risk of a major cardiovascular event was 12.3%.

The primary outcome of interest was a first major cardiovascular event (i.e., stroke, ischemic heart disease, or heart failure resulting in death or hospitalization). Studies reached a median follow-up of 4.2 years in this report.

Patients of all predicted risk levels shared an increase in the risk of safety events with antihypertensive treatment.

"Because the absolute reduction in the 5-year cumulative incidence of a major cardiovascular event in lower deciles was smaller than in higher deciles and the relative risk of adverse events was similar across the risk spectrum, the balance between benefit and harm narrowed as the predicted risk decreased and is unlikely to be captured by any single risk or BP threshold," Rahimi and colleagues noted.

"Quantifying this balance would require methods that account for differences in the severity, duration, and irreversibility of the outcomes concerned, which was beyond the scope of this analysis," they cautioned.

Rahimi's group also acknowledged that event counts under trial conditions may differ from those in routine practice, where the duration of treatment is often shorter and typically characterized by lower adherence and less protocolized care.
