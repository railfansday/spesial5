Eight Guidelines for Brokerage Growth

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Above from left to right: Jackie Louh, Rob Cleapor, John Finn and Doug Smith, on stage at RISMedia’s 38th Annual CEO & Leadership Exchange in Washington, D.C. this past October. Not pictured, Amy Somerville.

Business owners are constantly on the look-out for the best ways to evolve and grow their firms, and real estate broker/owners are no different. However, in today’s unpredictable market environment, growth can be an elusive goal, and taking a brokerage to the next level requires clarity, commitment and creativity.

A group of brokerage leaders addressed the growth dilemma at RISMedia’s 2026 CEO & Leadership Exchange this past fall during the panel discussion “More Than One Way to Grow.” Here’s their top advice for achieving growth despite current challenges that can feel insurmountable.

1. Take a broad approach. According to Jackie Louh, chief operating officer of Lamacchia Realty, increasing business means diversifying your efforts to ensure you’re growing on a variety of fronts, such as: expanding the brokerage footprint, increasing agent productivity, increasing market share and growing agent count.

“To do that, we have different people focused on those different avenues,” said Louh “Anthony (Lamacchia, company CEO) and I, for instance, we focus primarily on what we call chunk growth—acquisition opportunities. And then we have our regional sales managers who are focused every day on organic growth. They work with recruiting in their markets. They work with helping the agents in their markets grow their business. It’s not just about being the biggest company. We want to be the best, and to do that and to grow, you have to hit all avenues of that growth.”

2. Support agents and franchisees. Rob Cleapor, CEO of the growing franchise Iron Valley Real Estate, admitted that early on, growth equated to agent count. He soon realized, however, that the number of agents was not as important as the quality of those agents.

“As we started growing rapidly, you start realizing that’s really not what’s important,” said Cleapor. “For us, the way we define growth is supporting our agents and our franchisees along the way. “Growth, like (Jackie) said, is agent count, it’s footprint. But more importantly for me, it’s the support that you give for franchisees and the agents, and making sure that we’re keeping that support at the top so that everybody can grow and grow the right way.”

Echoing Cleapor’s sentiments, Doug Smith, founder and CEO of Douglas Realty is zeroing in on productivity.

“Our growth this year from an agent headcount is about 12%,” he said. “Our transactions are up almost 45% in this crazy down market. So we focus a ton on training, a ton on bringing in good agents.”

3. Talk to teams…and tired brokers. As John Finn, CEO and senior managing broker of United Real Estate Richmond pointed out, the majority of brokerages in his market—and across the country—are small, many with 10 agents or less. That’s why his growth strategy has focused on bringing successful teams into the fold.

“We have people who had good teams and decided they wanted to be brokers, and then woke up one day and said, ‘Oh, I really don’t want to do this.’ We have had great success with that,” said Finn.

Finn also homes in on brokers that want to go back to being agents.

“We’ve had success with older agents who really want to get back to the business of actually selling real estate and not having to provide the back-office services, the payroll, the tech stack, AI…things they don’t want to deal with anymore,” he said.

4. Build the right infrastructure. As panel moderator Amy Somerville, COO of the Real REMAX Group noted, the definition of brokerage growth changes when you put the word “sustainable” in front of it.

“I want to talk about the internal infrastructure and what needs to be set up to support the growth that you plan for your business,” she said. “Technology systems, financial systems, general infrastructure.”

Finn discussed the benefit of tapping into the tech stack of a large parent company like United.

“I found that by the time I studied and researched state-of-the-art (technology) and developed state of the art and got the money together to buy state of the art and got the agents to use state of the art, it wouldn’t be state of the art anymore,” he explained. “When we’re out approaching other people about coming to join our team, I don’t have to worry about AI as many other smaller firms in my market do, because I’ve got United in my pocket.”

5. Work on your business, not in it. In the early days of Iron Valley, Cleapor quickly learned that sustainable growth meant putting the right team in place, then delegating accordingly.

“Early on, I was managing three offices. I was the director of marketing. I did all of our graphic design. I did our franchise sales,” he shared. “One of my favorite lines somebody said to me years ago was, ‘When you’re doing 10 things, 10 things are getting 10% of your effort. And when you only do one thing, it gets 100% of your effort.’ Putting the infrastructure in, the processes, and more importantly, the people on your team…you have to make that decision or everything that you’ve grown is going to collapse.”

6. Set acquisition criteria and stick to it. Growing a real estate brokerage by acquiring another brokerage can quickly expand a firm’s business—provided a specific strategy is in place and adhered to.

Louh, for example, says that focusing on firms located in contiguous states is usually the best path. She then targets legacy firms within those states.

“The (firms) that have been around a long time have really built a strong brand and they have agents that have been with them a long time,” she said. “That makes for a much smoother transition because the agents trust the owner. They’re going to do whatever the owner says. They’re going to go wherever the owner goes.”

When considering a firm to acquire, Louh also advised making sure the owner is focused on their agents, not themselves.

“If the owner is saying, ‘How is this going to benefit me? How much money am I going to make off of this?’ that is not a company we want to align with,” she said. “If the owner isn’t invested in their agents, if that acquisition does come together, the owner’s going to basically disappear. They’re going to collect their paycheck. They’re not going to work to retain the agents. They’re not going to work to recruit, and then we’re just going to end up losing those agents.”

7. Always be networking. Smith reminded everyone that real estate is a people business, and staying committed to building relationships with other brokers can lead to organic M&A opportunities when values align.

“Last year we acquired a 50-agent brokerage, and it kind of happened by accident,” he said. “The owner of that brokerage was president of our local Realtor board and he invited me to speak. We didn’t know each other, but it turns out we were very much in alignment on so many different things in business. And it just so happened that his franchise renewal was up and he pulled me to the side and said, ‘Hey, we should talk.’ So it started there.”

8. Grow horizontally. While firms traditionally want to grow vertically—more agents, more transactions—in the current market, brokerages need to take a closer look at growing horizontally by getting more out of the transaction through services such as title, escrow, mortgage, insurance and more.

“The customer is looking for an experience at one stop where they can get everything,” said Finn. “We as agents, we as brokers are going to have to start providing that for our people.”

“The future of our industry is you have to be involved in more than just one area of the transaction or else I think you will get shut out,” agreed Smith. “I think you have to have your hands in a little bit of everything. Provide a consumer experience, create that client for life, but even more and just as important, create that agent for life.”
