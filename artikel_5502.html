15 state tax measures on 2026 ballots could reshape property and income bills

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A wealth tax, property tax caps, and income tax changes appear among 15 of the 33 tax measures on state ballots this fall.

Most years, the tax bill that lands in your mailbox reflects decisions lawmakers made far from your kitchen table. This November, some of those decisions shift onto the ballot itself. Voters in a long list of states will write the rules on their own, settling how much homeowners owe, how high income tax rates can climb, and who covers the bill for local government. A yes or no on a single line can reshape a household budget for years. A constitutional amendment can lock the outcome in place long after the campaign signs come down. Anyone who owns a home, earns a paycheck, runs a farm, or rents from someone who does has a stake in what happens.

Property taxes sit at the center of many of these contests. Many exemptions, freezes, and caps would transfer part of the load from one group of taxpayers to another. A break for one category of landholders can leave everyone else carrying a larger slice of what localities collect. A ceiling on assessment growth can limit what governments take in as market values rise. The proposals differ in scale. Some would nudge an eligibility threshold or tweak a technical formula. Others would rewrite how an entire state funds itself. Those who want to know where the burden falls need to look past the headline promise to the mechanics underneath.

Tax Foundation counts 33 tax-related measures on state ballots this fall and features 15 of them in a results table on its tracker. The group's experts plan to follow live returns for the most notable contests as votes come in. Some of the tracker's summaries walk through how a change would work in detail. Others list a measure by name alone. The 15 measures below form a selection drawn from the ones the tracker discusses. The list overlaps with the featured 15 without matching it, and it covers fewer than half of the full 33. Together these picks span wealth, income, and property taxes across nine states, from the West Coast to the Southeast. Each shows how one ballot choice could alter what residents pay.

Proposition 40 asks California voters to impose a one-time 5% levy on billionaires' wealth, a move that would give the U.S. its first wealth tax. Only residents worth $1 billion or more would face it. Every other Californian, including households with large but smaller fortunes, would sit outside its reach.

Residency on Jan. 1, 2026, determines who owes, and the value of each fortune on Dec. 31, 2026, sets the size of the bill. Moving away does not cancel the obligation. A billionaire who counted as a California resident on New Year's Day would owe after settling in another state months later. The residency snapshot predates the November vote. No one could dodge the measure by relocating after seeing it on the ballot.

Choosing the year's last day as the measuring point ties the amount owed to how each fortune fares across 2026. A gain during the year would enlarge the payment, and a loss would shrink it. Billionaires would not learn their final figure until the calendar closes, weeks after voters decide.

Tax Foundation warns that the design raises serious legal, administrative, and economic concerns. Its objections span the measure's whole life, from whether it survives in court to how the state tallies and gathers it to what it does to the wider economy. No other state has run a wealth tax. California would build its enforcement machinery without a domestic model to copy.

The state stands to collect revenue in the tens of billions of dollars, spread over several years, according to an estimate by the Legislative Analyst's Office. The office projects that working out what each billionaire owes and bringing in the payments could cost the state tens of millions of dollars a year for multiple years. New wealth tax receipts would cover that expense. A one-off assessment would carry a collection tail lasting years. At those scales, the paperwork bill would consume a sliver of what the tax delivers.

California Proposition 41 would forbid the state from enforcing any new tax that exempts itself from the Gann Limit, California's ceiling on state spending. The measure entered the ballot as a rival to Proposition 40, the billionaire wealth tax, and it bundles several provisions that reach beyond that spending rule.

The Gann Limit clause reaches any future tax drafted to escape the cap, including levies no one has written yet. It would bind the Legislature and voter-led campaigns alike. Lawmakers or citizens could approve a tax designed to sit outside the cap, yet the state would lack the power to collect it. Anyone crafting new revenue would need to fit it inside the existing limit for it to carry any force.

Proposition 41 would also order regular state audits of new special taxes to judge whether the programs they fund deliver results. Taxes that originate in ballot initiatives would face an extra checkpoint, an audit before the election. The sequence moves scrutiny of a citizen-backed tax ahead of the moment Californians decide its fate.

Proposition 41 targets the wealth tax head-on through a vote-count clause. Passage with more votes than Proposition 40 would let Proposition 41 invalidate the billionaire tax. A simple majority would not save Proposition 40 on its own. Both measures could clear the bar, and the wealth tax would still die if Proposition 41 drew the larger tally.

The clause turns the wealth tax question into a race between two vote totals. Each voter who backs both measures adds one vote to each side and does nothing to widen Proposition 40's lead. A Californian who wants the billionaire tax to survive gains the most by voting yes on it and no on Proposition 41. Opponents of the wealth tax face the mirror-image choice, yes on Proposition 41 and no on Proposition 40. Ballots that skip one of the two questions could tip a close contest.

California Proposition 43 would require two-thirds of voters to approve any local special tax that citizens place on the ballot. Today a simple majority decides those measures.

The higher threshold reaches every citizen-initiated local measure that creates a special tax, extends an existing one, or raises one. A campaign to renew a levy would need the same two-thirds margin as a campaign to start a new one. A special tax that voters approved under the majority rule would face the steeper climb the next time its backers sought an extension. Proposition 43 would write the requirement into the state constitution as an amendment.

Local governments in California already need two-thirds of the vote for special taxes they propose, according to the California Budget Center. A general tax clears with a bare majority. Under current rules, citizens drafting a special tax face a lower bar than local officials crafting the same kind of levy. Proposition 43 would erase that difference and hold both paths to a single standard.

The supermajority rule reshapes the math of a local campaign. A simple majority asks organizers to outpoll the opposition by a single vote. Under a two-thirds rule, opponents can sink a measure by holding anything above one-third of the ballots cast. Supporters would need two yes votes for every no vote to prevail. A special tax that wins a clear majority yet falls short of two-thirds would fail.

The measure, as described, targets special taxes alone. Nothing in its summary touches the simple-majority path for general taxes. Approval would leave every local special tax facing one supermajority hurdle, whoever writes it, and would widen the gap between what it takes to pass a special levy and a general one at the ballot box.

## Florida Amendment 3 offers the largest homestead break

Florida Amendment 3 would exempt up to $250,000 of a homestead's value from most property taxes, the largest homestead exemption anywhere in the U.S. The break would begin at $150,000 in 2027 and rise to $250,000 in 2028. Owners would wait a year at the lower level before reaching the larger figure.

School district levies sit outside the exemption. Homeowners would keep paying school taxes on their home's full value, while cities, counties, and other local taxing bodies would lose the exempted slice of their base. Schools would keep their funding base whole, and other local services would absorb the cut.

Jacksonville Mayor Donna Deegan expects the amendment to erase one-third of her city's budget, a $300 million hit, according to CNBC. She warns of deteriorating roads, closed libraries, pools, and parks, slower public safety response times, worse housing affordability, and more homelessness. Estimates put the statewide revenue loss at around $46 billion by 2032, according to CNBC. Hallandale Beach Mayor Joy Cooper asked residents on Nextdoor which city services they wanted to cut, and most replies urged officials to accept lower salaries.

Support for Amendment 3 erodes once voters hear what it could cost local government. A University of North Florida Public Opinion Research Lab poll found 61% in favor and 32% opposed when respondents heard it would phase out non-school homestead taxes, according to CNBC. After learning of projected city and county budget shortfalls across the state, respondents shifted to 45% support and 47% opposition. The 16-point drop in support turned a lopsided lead into a narrow deficit.

Gov. Ron DeSantis considers the amendment too modest. He first proposed a far bolder rollback that in time would have wiped out all property taxes on primary homes, with no carve-out for schools. Florida voters also face a second property tax amendment this fall. Amendment 3 sits between a governor who wanted deeper cuts and mayors bracing for lost services, and the poll's swing suggests the budget argument could decide the outcome.

Georgia Amendment 1 would let a single landowner classify up to 4,000 acres as bona fide conservation use property, double the 2,000-acre ceiling in effect today. Farming, timber production, and conservation all qualify as eligible uses. Owners at the current ceiling would gain room for up to 2,000 more acres under the preferential rules.

Enrolled tracts carry a lighter assessment through Georgia's Conservation Use Valuation Assessment program, known as CUVA, which sets their value at 40% of current use value. The appraisal turns on what the land does at present, whether it grows crops, raises timber, or sits in conservation. Georgia intends the favorable treatment to keep owners from converting agricultural land into homes or commercial sites under the weight of property tax bills. A higher cap would stretch that safeguard across more ground held by a single owner.

Owners who enroll in Rockdale County must swear a formal promise to keep the land in its present use. The county attaches the same pledge to residential transitional land. Each acre added under the new cap would come with the same sworn obligation in that county.

Owners with more than 2,000 qualifying acres stand to benefit from the higher cap. An owner with 4,000 qualifying acres can enroll no more than half of them under current law, leaving the other 2,000 outside CUVA's valuation. Passage would bring all 4,000 acres into the program. Owners with 2,000 qualifying acres or fewer fit under the existing limit and would see no change in how many acres they can enroll. Holdings beyond 4,000 acres would leave some land outside the program even after passage. The amendment trims the excluded portion for the largest owners without erasing it.

Louisiana voters will decide whether homeowners earning as much as $150,000 can lock in the assessed value of their homes, up from a $100,000 ceiling today. The change would lift the income limit by 50%.

Today's program confines its assessment freeze to owners whose incomes total $100,000 or less. A household a few dollars above that mark gets no freeze at all. Two neighbors with matching homes can face different assessment paths over a small gap in pay. The rule draws a hard line, and the amendment would move it without erasing it. Households crossing $150,000 would meet the same cliff that households crossing $100,000 meet now.

A higher cutoff would admit households earning more than $100,000 and up to $150,000, a $50,000 band the program now shuts out. No current participant would lose access. Every income under the old limit also clears the new one. Owners in the new band who qualify would see their home assessments hold steady from year to year.

The break gains worth the longer an owner keeps it in a rising market. A frozen assessment stays put while comparable homes climb in value. Each year prices rise, the distance between the frozen figure and the market widens. The freeze fixes one input to the tax bill and leaves the rate free to move. Owners inside the program can see bills change when local millage rates shift.

The new threshold would not take effect until tax year 2028. Until then, the existing limit keeps deciding who can claim the break. Newly eligible owners would wait outside the program through tax year 2027, even if voters approve the change in November.

Louisiana voters face five amendments on this fall's ballot. Among the three Louisiana measures in this list, this one alone makes household income the test for a property tax break.

A Louisiana amendment would set each locality's maximum millage rate for the coming year from the previous year's maximum allowed rate, beginning in tax year 2027. Under current law, the cap tracks the rate a local government levied the year before, even when officials charged less than the law allowed.

Today's formula penalizes restraint. A local government that charges below its limit in one year inherits a lower ceiling the next, and the shortfall follows it into later years. One lighter year can shrink the room officials have to raise revenue afterward, whatever their reasons for holding back.

The amendment would sever the link between what a locality collects and what it may collect later. Localities could keep millage under the cap in a given year and still carry the full maximum forward. Their taxing capacity would survive a low-rate year intact.

Taxpayers face both sides of the trade. Under current law, a year of reduced millage drags the following year's limit down with it. Under the amendment, the maximum would hold at its prior-year level. A cut in one year would no longer guarantee a lower ceiling in the next.

The measure governs ceilings alone. A yes vote would not by itself raise anyone's bill. Nothing in it sets the rate a locality charges. Local officials would keep deciding each year how much of their allowed capacity to use. What shifts is the starting point for that decision.

The current rule gives any locality that wants to preserve its future room a reason to levy the full amount each year. The amendment would remove that incentive. Under today's rules, each lean year resets the base, and a long string of them could leave a locality's ceiling below where it started. Voters would swap an automatic downward ratchet for more freedom to offer temporary relief without forfeiting later revenue.

## Louisiana lets veterans' spouses move an exemption once

Surviving spouses of disabled veterans in Louisiana would gain a one-time right to carry the disabled veteran homestead exemption to a different home, with the transferred break capped at its previous value. The amendment would let the benefit follow the spouse after a move. Without the amendment, a spouse who sells the family home has no path to bring the break along.

The cap governs how much moves with the spouse. A widow or widower who buys a pricier home would bring an exemption no larger than the one the household held before. Any value above that amount would remain taxable. A costlier purchase enlarges the taxable portion of the bill, not the break.

The single-transfer rule draws a second boundary. A spouse who uses the transfer and later relocates again would lose the right to take the exemption to a third property. One move marks the full extent of the new flexibility. A spouse weighing a downsize now and a later move closer to family would need to choose which relocation carries the break.

Neither limit widens eligibility. The proposal adds no new class of veterans or spouses and raises no dollar amount. It changes only where an existing benefit can sit. Local taxing bodies would exempt no more than they did before the move. The value shifts from one property's assessment to another's. Under the cap, the burden shift this measure creates stays narrow next to the property tax rewrites elsewhere on the ballot.

Louisiana voters will see this proposal among five amendments on the state's ballot. Its two limits let a surviving spouse hold on to the break after a move while capping the cost to the tax base at what the state had granted the household.

North Carolina voters will weigh a constitutional amendment ordering the General Assembly to pass a cap on the total property tax levy that local governments can impose. The legislature itself referred the question to voters. The amendment names no dollar figure. Future legislation would set the cap's terms, and lawmakers could carve out exceptions.

Every layer of local government that collects property tax falls within the requirement's reach. Counties, cities, and towns would face the eventual cap, as would special districts and any other local unit with authority to levy the tax. No type of local taxing body would escape it.

An existing North Carolina statute holds local property tax rates to $1.50 per $100 of assessed value. A rate ceiling governs the price charged on each dollar of value. It leaves total bills free to climb as assessments rise. The same rate applied to a larger base produces more revenue. A levy limit targets the total dollars collected. The amendment would add that second guardrail and keep the first in place.

Rising assessments expose the gap between the two tools. Under a rate ceiling alone, a locality whose property values grow can collect more money without touching its rate. A locality taxing below $1.50 can lift its rate and stay within the law. Depending on its design, a levy cap could restrain both routes to higher collections. It counts the dollars raised, whatever produced them.

The two restraints would rest on different legal footing. The duty to enact a levy cap would live in the constitution, while the rate ceiling stays in statute. Later sessions of the General Assembly would control the cap's details, yet the constitution would bind them to adopt one.

Tax Foundation points readers to material on how a well-designed levy limit could benefit taxpayers. The qualifier "well-designed" carries the weight in that framing. Any relief for property owners would hinge on the bill the General Assembly writes if voters approve the measure.

North Carolina voters will decide whether to cut the ceiling on the state's income tax rate from 7% to 3.5%. A rate cap sets the highest rate the state may charge, so the amendment targets the outer boundary lawmakers work within.

The proposed limit sits 3.5 percentage points below the current one. Any future push to raise the income tax rate past 3.5% would run into a cap half as high as today's.

The amendment shares the ballot with a second North Carolina proposal aimed at local property tax levies. Approval of both would place new constitutional restraints on the state income tax and on what local governments collect from property owners, all in a single election.

Oklahoma State Question 847 would cut the constitutional ceiling on yearly growth in the fair cash value of most locally assessed real property from 5% to 4%. Oklahoma bases each owner's property tax liability on fair cash value. The ceiling governs the pace at which the foundation of each bill can rise. Lawmakers referred the measure to voters as a constitutional amendment, and it would shave 1 percentage point off the current limit.

Homes that qualify for a homestead exemption and land classified as agricultural would absorb a steeper reduction. Their annual limit would drop from 3% to 1.75%, a decline of 1.25 percentage points. Against the current allowance, permitted yearly growth for those properties would shrink by nearly 42%. Most other real property would face a 20% trim.

The amendment would enlarge the gap between the two tracks. Today the general limit sits 2 percentage points above the one for homesteads and farmland. Under State Question 847, the spread would grow to 2.25 points. The preferred ceiling would fall to less than half the general one, 1.75% against 4%, compared with three-fifths under current law.

Modest percentage changes compound into wider differences over time. A property whose value rose by the maximum allowed amount five years in a row could gain about 27.6% under the current 5% limit. The proposed 4% ceiling would hold the same five-year climb to about 21.7%. The five-year maximum for homesteads and agricultural land would fall from about 15.9% to about 9.1%.

A 10-year horizon widens the divide. Most real property could gain no more than about 62.9% over 10 years under today's limit and about 48% under the new one. Homesteads and farmland would slide from about 34.4% to about 18.9% over the same stretch. On either horizon, owners of homesteads and farmland would give up more than 40% of their potential growth in taxable value, about twice the share other owners would lose.

Oklahoma homeowners over 65 who pass an income test receive a full freeze on the fair cash value of their homestead, and State Question 847 would swap that test for a tiered scale tied to household income. The constitution draws a single line today. Seniors on the qualifying side see no yearly growth in the value that sets their property tax bill. Those on the other side get nothing from the senior provision, no matter how close their earnings sit to the cutoff.

Lower-income seniors would keep the complete freeze under the amendment. Their homestead values would stay fixed from one year to the next, as they do today. The measure leaves the bottom of the income scale alone and directs its change at households the existing test excludes. Stripping out the old eligibility requirements means no senior over 65 would stand outside the protection.

Higher-income seniors would gain a ceiling on valuation growth where they have no senior protection today. State Question 847 would hold their annual increases to between 0.35% and 1.75%, with household income setting each homeowner's exact limit. The top of that band is five times the bottom. A household's tier would decide whether its home value inches up by a fraction of a percent or climbs to match the cap the measure sets for other homesteads.

Household earnings would determine how much relief each senior gets, a shift from today's on-off switch. Seniors above the old cutoff would pick up a safeguard they lack now, and seniors below it would give up nothing. Lawmakers placed the change before voters as a legislatively referred constitutional amendment. Its tiers would live in the constitution itself. The overhaul turns Oklahoma's senior break from a benefit some households receive and others miss into one that tapers as household income rises.

Wyoming Initiative 1 would layer a 50% exemption on the value of an owner-occupied house on top of the homestead break lawmakers adopted in 2025. The add-on carries no value cap and applies to the structure alone. The land beneath the home gets none of the added benefit.

The 2025 law sets the baseline. It exempts 25% of the first $1 million in fair market value for owner-occupied single-family residences, a break that tops out at $250,000. Owners must live in the home at least eight months a year to qualify. Initiative 1 lowers that occupancy threshold to six months for its own exemption. The two-month cut would extend the new break to owners who live in their homes six or seven months a year, a group the 2025 law leaves out.

Eligible homeowners could claim both breaks at once. The first trims taxable value by a quarter of the property's entire worth, up to the $1 million mark. The second strips out half the house's value with no ceiling. Owners of homes valued above $1 million gain nothing extra from the 2025 exemption, yet the uncapped 50% break keeps growing with the value of the house. Properties where the building, not the acreage, makes up most of the worth would capture the largest share of the relief.

Wyoming's residential property taxes sit among the lowest in the country, according to Tax Foundation. Initiative 1 would trim bills that start near the bottom of the national scale.

Wyoming policymakers have worried for years that the state depends too much on unstable revenue from oil, gas, and mining. Tax Foundation argues that Initiative 1 would deepen that overreliance. Shrinking the residential tax base would leave a larger share of the state's income resting on those volatile sources. A slump in oil, gas, or mining would hit a budget left with less homeowner revenue to cushion it.

Colorado voters will decide this fall whether to drop the state's flat income tax. The question reaches the basic architecture of how Colorado taxes earnings, a deeper change than the assessment caps, exemptions, and vote thresholds that fill most of this list.

Colorado shares the income tax spotlight in this selection with North Carolina. North Carolina's amendment addresses how high the state's rate may climb. Colorado's measure asks whether a single flat rate should survive at all, which makes it the only question here aimed at the structure of an income tax itself.

Washington state voters will decide whether to keep a newly adopted 9.9% tax on millionaires. The question asks the electorate to judge a levy that already exists. Keeping it would leave the 9.9% rate in force. Rejecting it would undo a policy the state only recently put in place.

Millionaires would carry the levy's burden, while the choice to keep it rests with every voter. Its newness also sets this contest apart. California voters will settle whether to create a wealth tax from scratch. Washington voters will judge one the state has already adopted.

Massachusetts voters confront a related question about their own millionaire's surtax. Their ballot asks whether its revenue counts under the Commonwealth's revenue limit, a question that assumes the surtax stays. Washington's ballot goes further and asks whether its tax continues at all. Of the two states putting millionaire levies before voters, only Washington lets the electorate end one outright.
