10 travel-related diseases doctors see in returning travelers

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 10 diseases travelers bring home, from dengue and malaria to measles and giardiasis, show what returning symptoms can mean and how to reduce your risk

An infection picked up abroad often does not show itself until the suitcase is unpacked. Some germs take days or weeks to cause symptoms. The trip can end before the illness begins. A fever, an upset stomach or a sore throat that appears after landing can look like an ordinary cold or a bout of food poisoning. That resemblance is what makes the diseases travelers bring home easy to misread.

These infections reach people through a handful of routes. Contaminated food and water carry bacteria, viruses and parasites into the gut. Mosquitoes transmit viruses and parasites, and different species bite at different times of day. Respiratory viruses move through the shared air of airplane cabins, airports and crowded venues. In some regions, freshwater lakes and rivers harbor parasites that can enter the body through the skin.

Recent travel history changes how a doctor approaches a patient who is sick after traveling abroad. A clinician who knows where someone has been can test for infections that would not otherwise be on the list. Fever after a trip to an area where malaria spreads calls for urgent testing. It should not be dismissed as a passing virus. Mentioning the destination, the dates and activities such as swimming in lakes or eating street food gives a doctor useful information.

The 10 illnesses below are a selection of diseases that clinicians regularly see in returning travelers. They are not ranked, and the list is not complete. Four spread mainly through food or water, three through mosquito bites, two through the air and one through freshwater contact. Each entry covers how the disease spreads, what its symptoms look like and how it is treated.

Prevention differs from one disease to the next. Vaccines lower the risk of several of them, including hepatitis A, typhoid fever, influenza and measles. Others depend on avoiding mosquito bites, choosing food and drinks carefully or staying out of certain lakes and rivers. For malaria, preventive medication is available for travelers heading to risk areas. A travel clinic can review a specific itinerary before departure and advise on which vaccines and medications make sense. Knowing which illnesses can follow international travel also helps a returning traveler recognize symptoms worth reporting to a doctor.

Traveler's diarrhea is a gastrointestinal infection caught from contaminated food or water. Symptoms usually begin during the trip or soon after arrival. Most cases are mild and clear up without specific treatment.

Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli, known as ETEC, is a frequent bacterial cause. These strains release toxins that make the lining of the small intestine push water and salts into the gut. The result is the loose, watery stool that defines the illness. Cramps, nausea, bloating and an urgent need to find a bathroom often come with it.

ETEC is not the only cause. Other bacteria, including Campylobacter, Shigella and Salmonella, can produce a similar illness. Viruses such as norovirus also play a part, particularly in group settings like cruise ships. Without a stool test, the specific cause usually stays unknown.

Replacing lost fluid is the first priority. Water, broth and oral rehydration salts help restore both fluid and electrolytes. Oral rehydration packets contain a measured mix of salts and sugar. Pharmacies in many countries sell them. Young children and older adults are more vulnerable to dehydration and need closer watching. Dark urine, dizziness and a dry mouth signal that fluid intake is falling behind.

Over-the-counter medicines can ease symptoms. Bismuth subsalicylate, sold as Pepto-Bismol, can reduce the number of loose stools. It can also turn the tongue or stool black for a short time. Because it contains a salicylate, it is not suited to people with an aspirin allergy. Loperamide, sold as Imodium, slows the gut and cuts down on bathroom trips. Loperamide is not advised for anyone with bloody stools or a high fever.

Those two symptoms are warning signs. Bloody diarrhea, high fever or severe illness call for medical care rather than waiting it out. A doctor can decide whether an antibiotic is warranted. Diarrhea that drags on well after the trip also deserves a visit and a stool test.

Prevention starts with what goes into the mouth. Food that is cooked thoroughly and served hot carries less risk than dishes left at room temperature. Sealed bottled water or boiled water is safer than tap water where sanitation is uncertain. Ice made from that tap water carries the same risk. Washing hands with soap before eating adds another layer of protection.

Dengue is a viral infection spread mainly by the Aedes aegypti mosquito, which bites during the day rather than at night. A traveler who develops fever, a severe headache and pain behind the eyes after a trip to the tropics may have caught it.

The virus circulates across tropical and subtropical regions, including parts of Southeast Asia, South Asia, Latin America, the Caribbean and the Pacific islands. Aedes aegypti lives close to people. It lays its eggs in small collections of standing water, such as flowerpot saucers, buckets, old tires and water storage containers. That habit makes dengue largely a disease of towns and cities.

The daytime biting pattern shapes prevention. Protection against dengue cannot be limited to the evening, with repellent applied at dusk and a bed net at night. Aedes aegypti does much of its feeding in the hours after sunrise and before sunset, and it will bite indoors. Repellent, long sleeves and screened or air-conditioned rooms matter during sightseeing hours, not only after dark.

Beyond fever, headache and eye pain, many patients also have aching muscles and joints. Some develop a rash, nausea or vomiting. Many infections are mild, and some cause no symptoms at all. The signs overlap with flu and with other tropical fevers. A blood test can confirm dengue.

There are four dengue virus serotypes. Recovering from one gives lasting immunity only to that serotype. A person can therefore be infected more than once. A later infection with a different serotype carries a higher risk of severe dengue. Severe dengue can cause bleeding, plasma leakage and shock. Warning signs include severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding from the gums or nose, and unusual fatigue or restlessness. They often appear around the time the fever breaks, and they call for emergency care.

No antiviral drug targets dengue. Treatment is supportive, built on rest, fluids and close monitoring. Acetaminophen is the usual choice for pain and fever. Aspirin, ibuprofen and other nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are generally avoided because they can raise the risk of bleeding.

A travel clinic can advise on current dengue risk at a specific destination and on whether any dengue vaccine applies to an individual traveler.

Malaria is a parasitic infection spread by Anopheles mosquitoes, which bite mostly between dusk and dawn. Any fever after a trip to a malaria-risk area is a reason for urgent testing, even if the trip ended weeks earlier.

The disease is caused by Plasmodium parasites, not a virus or bacterium. Five Plasmodium species are known to infect people: Plasmodium falciparum, Plasmodium vivax, Plasmodium ovale, Plasmodium malariae and Plasmodium knowlesi. Plasmodium falciparum causes the most dangerous form. It can progress from a fever to severe illness quickly. Its complications include organ failure and cerebral malaria, which affects the brain.

Only female Anopheles mosquitoes transmit the parasite. Once inside the body, it travels to the liver and then infects red blood cells. Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium ovale can also stay dormant in the liver. They can then cause a relapse months after the first illness.

The early signs are easy to mistake for flu. They include fever, chills, sweats, headache, body aches, nausea and vomiting. Symptoms often begin a week or more after the infective bite. They can also appear well after a traveler is home, when malaria may not be the first thing a doctor considers. Diagnosis requires a blood test. That is usually a blood smear examined under a microscope or a rapid diagnostic test.

Risk is concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa, which carries much of the world's malaria burden. The disease also occurs in parts of South and Southeast Asia, Oceania, and Central and South America. People who grew up in a malaria-risk country and return to visit family can be at risk too. Partial immunity built up in childhood fades after years away.

Prevention has two parts. The first is preventive medication, taken before, during and after the trip. Options include atovaquone-proguanil, doxycycline and mefloquine. The right drug depends on the destination, local drug resistance and the traveler's health history. How long each regimen continues after leaving the risk area varies. A travel clinic can explain the schedule for a specific drug.

The second part is avoiding bites after dark. That means sleeping under an insecticide-treated bed net or in screened or air-conditioned rooms. It also means using an insect repellent such as DEET or picaridin in the evening and covering arms and legs at night. No preventive drug is 100% effective. Medication and bite avoidance are meant to be used together.

Influenza is a respiratory virus that travelers can pick up on planes, in airport terminals, at train stations and in other crowded places. The annual flu vaccine is the main protection against it.

The virus spreads mostly through droplets released when an infected person coughs, sneezes or talks. Close contact matters most. A long flight, a packed boarding gate or a crowded tour bus can keep a traveler near strangers for hours at a time. An infected person can pass the virus on about a day before their own symptoms appear. The passenger in the next seat may not look sick at all.

Flu tends to come on suddenly. Common symptoms include fever, chills, cough, sore throat, muscle aches, headache and fatigue. Symptoms usually begin one to four days after exposure. That gap means a traveler infected on the flight home often falls ill only after arriving back at home.

Timing adds a second layer of risk. Flu seasons in the Northern and Southern Hemispheres run at opposite times of year. In the Northern Hemisphere, activity typically builds in the autumn and peaks in winter. In the Southern Hemisphere, the season generally falls between about April and September. In tropical regions, flu can circulate year-round. A traveler leaving London for Sydney in July can meet flu at its height. That traveler can then carry it back to a city where it is out of season and few people expect it.

The World Health Organization issues separate recommendations each year for the strains included in Northern and Southern Hemisphere vaccines. A shot taken months earlier at home may not match what is circulating at a destination on the other side of the equator. A doctor or travel clinic can advise on whether an updated vaccine makes sense before a long trip.

Most people recover from flu in less than two weeks. Older adults, young children, pregnant people and people with chronic health conditions face a higher risk of complications such as pneumonia. Prescription antiviral drugs such as oseltamivir, sold as Tamiflu, work best when started within 48 hours of the first symptoms. For travelers in a high-risk group, that short window is a reason to contact a doctor promptly rather than wait out a fever.

Hepatitis A is a liver infection caused by the hepatitis A virus. It spreads through contaminated food or water, or through close contact with an infected person. A vaccine prevents it.

The vaccine is a standard travel recommendation for many destinations. That makes hepatitis A one of the travel-related diseases a traveler can plan against well before departure.

The virus passes from the stool of an infected person into another person's mouth. A meal prepared by someone who did not wash their hands can carry it. So can water or ice from an unsafe supply. Living with or caring for someone who is sick is another route. Shellfish harvested from polluted water is a further known source.

Symptoms often appear only after a traveler is home. The virus has an incubation period of several weeks. A person can feel well for the rest of a trip and fall ill long after the return flight.

The illness can bring fatigue, nausea, abdominal pain, dark urine and jaundice. Jaundice is a yellowing of the skin and the whites of the eyes. Some adults also lose their appetite or run a fever. Young children often have mild symptoms or none at all. They can still pass the virus to others.

There is no specific antiviral drug for hepatitis A. Care centers on rest, fluids and monitoring while the liver recovers. Most people recover fully, though the illness can last for weeks and sometimes months. In rare cases it leads to liver failure. Older adults and people with existing liver disease face a higher risk of severe illness.

Hepatitis A differs from hepatitis B and hepatitis C in two ways. It does not cause a lasting, chronic infection. It also does not spread mainly through blood. A person who recovers is protected against catching it again.

The vaccine is given as a series of shots. A travel clinic or doctor can advise on timing before a trip. They can also check whether a person is already protected through childhood vaccination or a past infection.

Handwashing with soap and water adds a layer of protection. Alcohol-based hand sanitizer works less well against this virus than against many other germs.

People who have had close contact with someone who has hepatitis A may still be able to get protection after exposure. The options include the hepatitis A vaccine and, for some people, immune globulin. A doctor can say whether either applies and how soon it needs to happen.

Typhoid fever is a bacterial infection caused by Salmonella Typhi. It brings a sustained high fever, weakness and stomach pain, and antibiotics treat it.

The bacterium lives only in humans. It leaves the body in the feces of people who are sick or who carry the infection without symptoms. It then reaches others through contaminated food or drinking water. Typhoid is most common where sanitation and water treatment are limited. South Asia is among the regions with the highest risk for travelers.

Some people keep shedding the bacteria long after they recover, and some carriers never feel sick at all. The best-known carrier is Mary Mallon, a cook in the New York area in the early 1900s. She infected many people while showing no illness herself. Newspapers called her "Typhoid Mary."

Symptoms tend to build gradually rather than strike all at once. The fever climbs over several days and can stay high. Headache, loss of appetite and either constipation or diarrhea can follow. Some patients develop a faint rash of rose-colored spots on the trunk. Illness often appears a week or more after exposure. A traveler may already be home when it starts.

Doctors usually confirm typhoid with blood, stool or other lab cultures. Untreated, the infection can lead to serious complications, including perforation of the intestine. Antibiotics shorten the illness and lower that risk.

Antibiotic resistance complicates treatment. Strains resistant to several common antibiotics have spread in parts of South Asia. An extensively drug-resistant strain was identified in Pakistan in 2016. For typhoid, the country a patient visited can shape which antibiotic a doctor is likely to choose.

Two kinds of typhoid vaccine have been used in the U.S.: an injection and an oral version taken as capsules. Neither gives complete protection, and protection fades over time. A travel clinic can advise which type fits a trip and when to get it.

The vaccines also do not cover paratyphoid fever, a similar illness caused by related Salmonella Paratyphi bacteria. Vaccinated travelers still lower their risk by choosing food that is cooked and served hot. Drinking water that is bottled, boiled or treated adds another layer of protection.

Giardiasis is an intestinal infection caused by the parasite Giardia, usually picked up from untreated water or contaminated food. It causes watery diarrhea, gas, cramps and bloating, and the symptoms can drag on for weeks.

The parasite goes by more than one scientific name. Giardia duodenalis is the name used in much of the current medical literature. Giardia intestinalis and Giardia lamblia are other names for it, and lamblia is still common in clinics and on lab reports. All three refer to the same organism.

Giardia spreads through cysts shed in the stool of infected people and animals. Swallowing only a small number of cysts can cause infection. The cysts survive for long periods in cold water. They also tolerate chlorine at the levels used in typical tap water treatment.

That durability makes untreated water the classic source. Hikers and campers who drink from streams, lakes or springs are at risk, even in remote areas where the water looks clean. In North America, the infection's link to backcountry water has earned it the nickname "beaver fever." Travelers $TRV abroad can also pick it up from tap water, ice or raw produce rinsed in contaminated water.

Food is another route. Fruit and vegetables eaten raw can carry the parasite. So can meals prepared by an infected person who did not wash their hands. Close personal contact spreads it as well. Outbreaks occur in households and childcare settings for that reason.

Symptoms typically appear one to two weeks after exposure. Besides watery diarrhea, gas and cramps, stools can be greasy and foul-smelling. Some people feel nauseated or lose weight. Others carry the infection with no symptoms at all but still pass cysts.

The long timeline sets giardiasis apart from a short bout of stomach trouble on a trip. An illness that starts after travel and lingers for weeks is worth raising with a doctor. It helps to mention where the traveler went and what they drank. Diagnosis relies on stool testing. More than one sample is sometimes needed to find the parasite.

Prescription antiparasitic drugs treat the infection. Metronidazole, tinidazole and nitazoxanide are the medicines most commonly used. A doctor chooses the drug and the dose. Some people develop temporary lactose intolerance after the infection clears.

Prevention centers on water. Boiling water kills Giardia. Filters rated to remove particles of one micron or smaller also trap the cysts. Alcohol-based hand sanitizer does not work well against them. Washing hands with soap and water is the more reliable option.

Chikungunya is a mosquito-borne virus marked by sudden fever and severe joint pain. For some people, that joint pain lingers for months after the fever has gone.

Two mosquito species carry the virus: Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus. The second is often called the Asian tiger mosquito. Both bite mainly during daylight hours. Aedes albopictus can survive in cooler climates than Aedes aegypti. That tolerance widens the range of places where the virus can circulate.

The disease was first described during an outbreak in southern Tanzania in 1952. Its name comes from the Kimakonde language and refers to the bent, stooped posture of people in severe pain. The virus has since caused outbreaks across Africa, Asia, Europe and the Americas. It reached the Caribbean in late 2013 and then spread through much of the Western Hemisphere.

Symptoms usually appear three to seven days after a bite from an infected mosquito. Fever and joint pain are the most common symptoms. The pain tends to settle in the hands, wrists, ankles and feet, often on both sides of the body. Headache, muscle pain, joint swelling and a rash can also occur.

Deaths from chikungunya are rare. Newborns, adults over 65 and people with conditions such as diabetes, high blood pressure or heart disease face a higher risk of severe illness.

There is no antiviral drug for chikungunya. Care focuses on rest, fluids and pain relief. The early symptoms look much like dengue, which the same mosquitoes spread. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention advises against aspirin and other nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs until dengue has been ruled out. Those drugs can raise the risk of bleeding in a person who has dengue.

A person who recovers from chikungunya is likely protected against catching it again. The lingering joint pain, not reinfection, is the main long-term concern for returning travelers.

Prevention centers on avoiding daytime mosquito bites. A chikungunya vaccine has been licensed in the U.S. Its current availability, and whether it suits a particular traveler or trip, are questions for a travel clinic.

Measles is a highly contagious airborne virus that travelers can catch in places with active outbreaks, including airports and crowded venues. Two doses of the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine give strong protection against it.

The measles virus spreads when an infected person coughs, sneezes or breathes. It can stay in the air of a room for up to two hours after that person has left. A traveler does not need face-to-face contact to be exposed. Sharing a departure gate, a waiting area or an indoor event with someone who is infected can be enough.

Symptoms usually appear seven to 14 days after exposure. That delay often means the first signs show up after the trip has ended. The illness tends to begin with high fever, cough, runny nose and red, watery eyes. Tiny white spots, known as Koplik spots, can form inside the mouth. A rash follows a few days later. It typically starts at the hairline and face, then spreads down the body.

People with measles can pass it on before the rash appears. The contagious window runs from about four days before the rash to about four days after it. A returning traveler who feels as if they have a heavy cold may already be spreading the virus to family, coworkers or other passengers. Anyone who develops these symptoms after a trip should call a doctor's office or clinic before arriving. Staff can then keep the patient away from others in the waiting room.

Measles can cause serious complications. These include ear infections, pneumonia and encephalitis, a swelling of the brain. Children younger than five, adults older than 20, pregnant people and people with weakened immune systems are more likely to develop complications.

The U.S. declared measles eliminated in 2000. That status means the virus no longer circulates continuously in the country. Cases still occur there. Many outbreaks begin when an unvaccinated person catches the virus abroad and brings it home. Gaps in local vaccination coverage then let it spread.

Checking vaccination status before departure is the main line of protection. Adults who are unsure whether they received two MMR doses can ask a doctor about their records or about a blood test that checks immunity. Parents of infants and young children traveling abroad can ask a pediatrician or travel clinic about timing. Recommendations for young travelers can differ from the routine childhood schedule.

Schistosomiasis is a parasitic disease caught by wading, swimming or bathing in freshwater where infected snails live. The larvae burrow through bare skin. The infection can go unnoticed for a long time while it damages internal organs.

The disease is also known as bilharzia. It is caused by parasitic flatworms of the genus Schistosoma, sometimes called blood flukes. Freshwater snails act as the parasite's intermediate host. They release free-swimming larvae into lakes, rivers, ponds and irrigation canals. Brief contact with that water can be enough for the larvae to penetrate the skin.

Risk is concentrated in parts of sub-Saharan Africa, where the disease is widespread. Lake Malawi is one freshwater site linked to infections in visitors. Schistosomiasis also occurs in parts of South America, the Middle East and Asia. Saltwater and properly chlorinated swimming pools do not carry the parasite.

Early symptoms are often mild or missing entirely. Some people develop an itchy rash where the larvae entered, within days of exposure. Others get a fever, cough, aches or chills weeks later, as the worms mature inside the body. Many travelers notice nothing at all.

The quiet early stage makes the infection easy to miss. Adult worms can live in the blood vessels for years. Their eggs lodge in body tissue. Over time this can damage the liver, intestines, bladder or kidneys, depending on the species. Schistosoma haematobium tends to affect the urinary tract and can cause blood in the urine. Schistosoma mansoni mainly affects the intestines and liver.

Anyone who had freshwater contact in a risk area should tell a doctor, even without symptoms. Blood, stool or urine tests can detect the infection. These tests may not turn positive until several weeks after exposure. A travel clinic can advise on the right timing.

The drug praziquantel treats schistosomiasis. There is no vaccine. Prevention depends on behavior instead. The most reliable step is to stay out of untreated freshwater in places where the disease occurs. Vigorous towel-drying after a brief, accidental exposure is not a dependable safeguard. Bathing water that has been treated or heated is safer than water taken straight from a lake or river.
