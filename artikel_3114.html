When To End A Technology Pilot: 19 Warning Signs

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Technology pilots give organizations an opportunity to test new solutions on a limited scale, evaluate their potential and identify challenges before committing to a broader rollout. But not every pilot warrants further investment, and knowing when to end one can be just as important as knowing when to expand it.

For tech leaders, the challenge is distinguishing between setbacks that can be addressed by adjusting a pilot’s scope or timeline and more fundamental issues that call its viability into question. Below, members of Forbes Technology Council share warning signs that indicate it’s time to end a technology pilot rather than extend it, redesign it or move it into production.

A pilot without success criteria is not a pilot. Defining boundaries and expectations, and, importantly, being transparent, are critical to a pilot’s success. Users should also share any uncertainty during the pilot instead of waiting for it to pivot or improve. - Arunava Sen, Tata Communications America Inc.

A clear sign is when the pilot team cannot tell you what the technology accessed, what decisions it made, or what data it touched. If you cannot answer those questions in a controlled environment (test group), you will not answer them in production. - Michael Nicosia, Salt Security

Forbes Technology Council is an invitation-only community for world-class CIOs, CTOs and technology executives. Do I qualify?

One sign a technology pilot shouldn’t progress doesn’t involve the new tech itself but the tools teams already work with. If teams are working in siloed workflows with tools that don’t collaborate with one another, introducing new technology can drive up costs, increase complexity and hurt productivity. Connected tools and workflows are integral to the success of any new tech deployment. - Usman Shuja, Bluebeam

End a pilot when its success depends on hand-curated data that won’t exist in production. If someone cleaned the dataset, resolved definitions and fixed edge cases for the pilot, and no one owns doing that at scale in production, extending or redesigning the product in production won’t help. In many cases, technology isn’t the problem; the foundation is. Ask not whether the pilot worked, but what it worked on. - Prakash Reddy, Okta

If the pilot only works while its champion is in the room or while the vendor quietly does manual work behind the scenes, you’ve tested enthusiasm, not technology. Hand it to a team that didn’t build it for 30 days. If results collapse, you’d be pushing a dependency into production, not a tool. End it and keep the learnings. - Damian Wasserman, BEON.tech

When the team stops asking what we learned and starts asking when we get to stop, that’s the sign. A pilot without defined success criteria is avoidance. If you can’t name the specific business result it was supposed to prove or disprove, extending it won’t help. End it, define the outcome you actually need, and redesign around that from the start. - Ali Alkhafaji, APPLY

Organizations should end a technology pilot when they have full visibility of the tool’s usage and cost metrics but still cannot connect either to a meaningful business outcome. Adoption alone is not enough without a clear path to measurable value, and extending the pilot risks turning experimentation into ongoing spend without a business case. - Conal Gallagher, Flexera

A clear sign is when the people who were supposed to benefit stop asking about it. Pilots drag on because the sponsors like the idea, not because users need it. If the pilot group has quietly gone back to their old way of working and nobody complains when the tool goes down, there’s nothing to extend. Name the lesson, thank the team and end it. - Hastimal Jangid, Coozmoo Digital Solutions, Inc.

A pilot should be reconsidered when it performs well in a demo but can’t prove that it solves a real business problem. AI makes it easy to add capabilities, but more features don’t always mean more value. Teams need clear measures of success and feedback loops to understand what’s working and what’s not before investing further. - Tony Grout, M-Files

A clear sign to end a technology pilot is when it repeatedly fails to demonstrate measurable business value despite validated assumptions and reasonable adjustments. If the solution cannot deliver meaningful ROI, operational efficiency or strategic impact, extending it only increases sunk costs. Strong leadership means knowing when to stop, not just when to scale. - Kameshwar Singh, Cohesity

End a pilot if no effort was spent defining the vision and how value will be realized at scale. That’s like building a bridge from both ends without deciding where it should meet. - Venky Ramesh, LatentView Analytics

A pilot should end when success depends on conditions that production will never have: handpicked data, expert users, manual fixes, privileged integrations or constant vendor support. If removing that scaffolding collapses the value case, extending the pilot only perfects the illusion. The real test is not, “Does it work?” but, “Does it still create measurable value when exposed to ordinary users, messy data, real constraints and an ordinary day?” - Nicola Sfondrini, PWC

End a pilot when making the technology operable, reliable and governable outweighs its business value. If protecting sensitive data, maintaining an audit trail and providing human oversight would cost more than the expected benefit, extending that particular pilot needs a stronger business case than the time and money already invested. - Brian Stimpfl, S-Docs

End a pilot when the evidence invalidates its core business premise. If users can complete the workflow but gain no meaningful time, cost or quality improvement over the current process, more engineering won’t create demand. Define that minimum benefit before launch, test it with real users, and stop when it remains out of reach. - Irina Shymko, Langate Software

End the pilot when the team cannot define a credible path from the experiment to a measurable business outcome. If success still depends on vague future improvements, heroic manual support or assumptions the pilot was supposed to validate, extending it usually just delays the decision. - Dhyey Mavani, Abridge

A pilot should end when the team cannot explain what new evidence another extension will produce. Every extra week should answer a specific open question about value, risk, adoption, cost or scale. Once the remaining time is only keeping the pilot alive, the experiment has already delivered its answer. - Sibasis Padhi, Walmart Inc.

I would end a pilot when it successfully completes individual tasks but repeatedly fails to deliver the full business outcome. Often, the underlying issue is the absence of a clear decision model for handling exceptions, approvals or unresolved work across teams. If nobody can authorize what happens next, scaling the technology only scales the problem. - Vamshi Noothi, Cisco

A pilot should be ended if nobody uses it unless someone reminds them. A pilot that only stays alive while its sponsor keeps pushing won’t survive production, and extending it just postpones that answer. Software nobody uses isn’t free; it still costs money to run, maintain and support. End it, write down what you learned about the problem, and put the team on something people actually want. - Jeremy Suriel, Kustomer

A clear sign to end a technology pilot is when no business owner is willing to take responsibility for implementation. Even if the technology performs well, success requires business ownership, accountability and commitment to change. Without a sponsor willing to champion the solution, allocate resources and own the outcomes, extending the pilot is unlikely to deliver value. Technology cannot create lasting benefits without clear business ownership. - Eranda Maldeniya, Enterprise Analytics
