Marketing Predictions for 2027: AI, Trust, Advertising, and the Future of Growth

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

November is a season of spooky movies, Black Friday sales and next-year trend reports. The data is in, and researchers are already turning it into projections. There’s just one thing about projections: they never align. Forrester says one thing, Gartner another, and each gets its own headline.

Some themes show up in almost every forecast, which is about as close to consensus as this industry gets. Others point in opposite directions.

So I went through 41 sources: 11 forecasts, 14 studies from the Stacy’s Brain library and 16 outside surveys, benchmarks and case studies (the full list is at the bottom). Then I did three things:

In 2026, AI was a fun experiment, and few companies seriously questioned its ROI. In 2027, novelty is no longer an advantage, so it’s time to make sure the math is on your side.

That’s the loudest message in this year’s forecasts.

Forrester’s B2C predictions put it bluntly: “exorbitant AI economics will force marketers to scale back a third of AI initiatives”. The reason is pricing. AI vendors increasingly charge per use, so every agent run and every generated asset now shows up on an invoice.

Leadership has noticed: in Forrester’s Q3 2026 CMO pulse, 82% of US B2C marketing executives expect to justify future AI spend more strongly than before. Forrester also expects AI agents to replace legacy marketing and insights tools (survey platforms, social listening dashboards, campaign reporting tools) at one in 10 companies.

Average monthly AI tool spend per marketer seat, US$ (bars), and number of AI tools per marketer (line), August 2024 – February 2026. Source: Visionary Marketing, AI tool spend benchmark 2026.

Gartner sees the same problem from the people side.

65% of CMOs expect AI to dramatically change their role within two years, but only 32% think the role needs major new skills. Gartner predicts that by 2027, a lack of AI knowledge will be one of the top three reasons large companies replace their CMO.

And I have an explanation for why the bill is arriving now.

And the mood is already turning. In CMI and MarketingProfs’ 2027 B2B survey, the share of B2B marketers planning to invest more in AI marketing tools fell from 45% for 2026 to 30% for 2027.

How much does it cost? In Visionary Marketing’s benchmark, a B2B marketer’s seat costs up to 1.6x more in AI tools than a B2C seat at a company of the same size. For scale, Gartner’s 2026 CMO Spend Survey puts AI at 15.3% of the average marketing budget.

Now for the part that looks like a contradiction.

Same year, same “AI”, but (!) opposite directions.

Imo, they’re not describing the same money. Forrester is talking about AI inside marketing: tools, agents and content generation, each billed per use. Kantar is talking about being visible to AI: showing up in ChatGPT’s and Gemini’s answers, and buying ads there.

So my reading is that 2027 cuts the first budget and grows the second. If your AI spend is one line in the plan, I’d split it before your CFO does it for you.

This is the strongest consensus in the whole set. 100% alignment.

Forrester expects human-made content to become a top priority again for 50% of brands, and a third of consumers to reject content that can’t prove its source. Its advice for 2027 is to win by being “the most trusted, not the most automated”

On the B2B side, Forrester’s B2B predictions go further: two thirds of B2B providers will lose deals because buyers’ AI tools find their claims weak or hard to verifyGartner adds that by 2027 brands will put half of their influencer budgets into content and creator authenticity.

Honestly, I felt this, and it’s one of the reasons I started building Stacy’s Brain. As long as marketers delegate market research to LLM models, the outcome they produce will have no moat and will keep relying on the same unvalidated vendor bullshit.

We’ve encountered it many times: “Cold emails are a top-ROI thing” is a claim that comes from vendors, not users. But LLMs do not care; if something is cited, it’s trustworthy — and here’s how a sales line from someone gets into your plan.

That’s a margin note, though; let’s get back to the topic.

“Trust” sounds soft, but I think the mechanism behind it is concrete. Buyers, and now their AI tools, check you against everything said about you. Proof you control (case studies, named authors, clearly labeled AI use) matters, and proof you don’t control (reviews, references, third-party lists) matters even more.

Another 100% aligned claim: shopping and buying are moving into chats and agents. They disagree on the speed, and the speed depends on who’s buying.

So… what does that actually mean for B2B?

A margin note on Forrester’s data. I don’t think we’re talking about full B2B purchases. Checking a vendor’s deck or proposal, comparing suppliers, drafting an RFP: yes, agents will definitely do that. But big contracts won’t be signed by an AI. They involve legal review, several stakeholders and trust that gets built between people.

Where agents already buy on their own is the small, routine, rule-based part of B2B spending:

Same pattern everywhere: low value per order, clear rules, and a human who sets the guardrails. So if you sell B2B, assume an agent will read and compare your offer before a person does, and make your claims easy to verify

All forecasters agree that budgets will follow proof of impact, and that most teams can’t produce it yet.

I can confidently validate this one from the agency side. We increasingly see that awareness and brand equity get bought only when they show a real path to money. Without it, most visibility initiatives get shut down. So yes, marketers need to start counting ROAS and make it their main KPI.

At the same time, from the marketer’s side, this is hell.

With AI search reshaping discovery, most people, especially in startups, don’t know which channels will work right now. And the ones that clearly will (SEO, AEO, founder-led growth) are long-cycle channels that won’t show ROAS in a quarter.

Gartner predicts that by 2027, over 40% of CMOs who push for bigger brand budgets will lose influence with the C-suite because they can’t show returns. Its survey describes a “doom loop” at 84% of companies:

Underfunded measurement leads to unclear impact, unclear impact leads to skepticism, and skepticism leads to tighter budgets. Source: Gartner, February 2026.

Everything in the previous sections says we need to build trust, and trust is basically brand equity. Yet companies are ready to fund only initiatives with a clear ROAS. So a smart marketer stops choosing between the two and builds combined funnels, where brand and sales tactics work in one plan.

Nielsen’s own analysis backs this up: campaigns that combined brand and sales tactics delivered 1.5x the sales lift of awareness-only campaigns. The CMO who loses the room in 2027 is the one defending brand as a category. The one who keeps it shows brand and sales working together, with a number attached.

Here two Forrester reports point the same way.

The productivity gains that justified the cuts are modest so far: the 14% in The CMO Survey, and McKinsey’s fewer than 10% of CMOs who’ve scaled AI. Meanwhile Nielsen finds that 34% of US marketers already act on AI recommendations without human review (oh no).

So if 2026 was “AI replaces people”, I expect 2027 to be “AI needs people to run it”: hiring for data, measurement, QA and editing.

Leaving AI aside, the channel forecasts point in one direction: money follows attention to screens where ads sit next to entertainment.

B2B SaaS already buys the biggest share of US ChatGPT ad impressions (19.9% in Graphite and Similarweb; 21.7% of early ads in Seer’s tracking). So the inventory exists but proof of return doesn’t .

Though we already have the first click data — and it shows how far it has to go.

In the US, ChatGPT ads have an all-time click-through rate of 0.73%, according to Graphite and Similarweb. For comparison, Google search ads averaged 6.42% between April 2023 and March 2024, just before AI Overviews launched in the US (WordStream by LocaliQ, 17,998 US campaigns), and 6.64% in April 2025 – March 2026 (LocaliQ).

TL;DR: assistant ads get clicked about ten times less often than search ads, at least for now.

AI search doesn’t answer the question you typed.

It breaks it into a set of sub-questions, searches for each, and builds one answer from what it finds. Google calls this “query fan-out” and its VP of Product for Search describes the system thinking of “a bunch of questions like great restaurants, great bars, things to do if you have kids” (Search Engine Journal).

In other words, the system already has a semantic kernel it’s ideating around, and it’s not really important where you rank for the main queries. Only ~12% of URLs cited by AI assistants rank in Google’s top 10 for the original prompt (Ahrefs).

What wins instead is being mentioned in many places that answer those sub-questions and having your brand as close to the topic as possible.

So the budget for AI visibility that Kantar’s marketers plan to grow (the net 75%) will flow to the old disciplines: PR, earned media, reviews, guest content and link building. My call: in 2027, PR and link building become core AI-optimization channels.

People learned to ignore banners, and they’re learning to ignore sponsored posts.

In a Gartner survey of 1,006 US consumers, 35% say they now rely less on influencers for product information because AI-generated content is spreading (Gartner). Ad load matters too: in a Journal of Marketing study of about 5,000 Instagram influencers, sponsored posts did worst for creators with a middling share of ads in their feed, the typical “sometimes sponsored” account.

I think the influencer bubble is starting to deflate into an efficiency zone. Short-term deals don’t have the strongest ROAS, and the return comes from long-term collaboration (there’s a great IPA’s analysis of 220 influencer campaigns to read if you’d love to dive deeper). My call: in 2027, brands cut one-off placements and move budgets to fewer, longer creator partnerships.

Building a product has never been easier. GitHub saw nearly 1 billion commits and 121 million new repositories in its 2025 reporting year, with commits up 25% year over year (GitHub Octoverse). And more of those products are built by one person: solo founders started 36.3% of new US startups in the first half of 2025, up from 23.7% in 2019 (Carta).

Making a product is easy; building distribution is hard.

A solo founder can change the product in a day but has little budget and no room for a wasted quarter. So the cheapest way to grow is to get the right early users in, talk to them and fix the product fast.

My call: demand for customer development, and for access to power users across categories (communities, panels, research networks), grows sharply in 2027.

Buyers want proof from people, not brands. In Optimizely’s UK survey, 72% of consumers say they’re more likely to engage with content that feels human rather than automated (Optimizely). So I see the media presence of companies shifting to people who don’t sell anything directly and simply talk about the products they build: founders first, then the team.

A strong X, Instagram or Substack account becomes part of a professional’s value, an asset that raises salary and that companies will pay extra for.

The backlash is already measurable. Over a third of web pages published since ChatGPT’s release show signs of AI authorship (Pew Research Center), and audiences are noticing:

My call: “made by humans” becomes a visible positioning in 2027, with brands labeling human-made work the way they label organic food.

When thousands of small products compete for the same users, swapping audiences is cheaper than buying them. Growing companies start offering each other’s products inside their own: as a perk, a bundle or a recommendation.

My call: with the rise of solo founders, in-app partner placements become a common growth channel for small products in 2027.
