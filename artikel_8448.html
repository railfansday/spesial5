Foothills Trail Shakedown

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

In November 2026, I took on the Foothills Trail (FHT) eastbound as my shakedown hike to test my gear, mental toughness, and physical strength. Stretching 77 miles through North and South Carolina, the trail challenges hikers with rugged terrain. My watch had tracked 16,132 feet of ascent and 16,584 feet of descent. My main goal was simple: focus on what gear worked and what didn’t. It turned out to be the exact wake-up call I needed before January. Some of my systems passed with flying colors, while others forced an immediate rethink.

Let’s talk about the gear I will NOT get rid of, my MVPs! You can find a gear list breakdown in my profile. My tent is my favorite piece of gear by a long shot. Because it’s semi-freestanding, the poles give it structure, meaning I can pitch it practically anywhere with just a few stakes. It also weighs in at just under two pounds, which is a huge advantage. My second favorite piece of gear is my new down quilt. I’m naturally cold, so having a 20°F quilt hits the spot for chilly Florida winter temps and the late-season cold snaps of southern Appalachia. It has a closed footbox to keep my feet warm and pad straps, which clip underneath my sleeping pad to keep the quilt secured and trap warm air inside.

The nights were cold with the lowest temp at camp being 28°F one night. At this time I had a 30°F quilt with an open toe box, no quilt straps, and by night two I had already decided this quilt wasn’t going to work even with my cold weather clothes. I sent many texts to my husband each night about how cold I was and even spent 45 minutes one night looking for a bail-out point for the following day which he wouldn’t learn until after my hike. Little did I know, he was one step ahead of me, as always, and had purchased a 20°F quilt for me after my first text night one. My first gear swap was complete before my shakedown trail had even really started.

More than any single piece of gear, the Foothills Trail taught me how important it is to manage my body and timing. Taking time to stretch at the end of each day made a massive difference in keeping my muscles from locking up after hours of steep climbs and descents. I also realized that smart pacing isn’t just about hitting daily mileage goals. It’s about getting into camp with enough daylight to pitch my tent, eat dinner, and get into my warm layers before the temperature drops for the night. Walking away from those 77 miles, I feel way more confident in my gear.
