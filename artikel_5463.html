VC Bradley Tusk decided to offer startups services instead of cash. How’s that working out?

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Good morning, it’s Finance Editor Jeff Roberts in for Allie. In the course of my career, I’ve interviewed hundreds of venture capitalists, and I have an observation: Many VCs regard themselves as unconventional thinkers, but when it comes to the norms of their industry, they are highly conformist. Bradley Tusk is not one of those VCs.

Brash and outspoken, Tusk made his name as the chief of staff for former New York City mayor Mike Bloomberg, and by helping Uber in its early battles with city officials and the taxi industry. Soon after, he leveraged his reputation as a political fixer to launch Tusk Ventures, which helped startups like Lemonade, Bird, FanDuel, Ro, and Coinbase navigate various regulatory minefields.

In 2025, Tusk made another bold bet. Despite a successful run as a conventional VC, which saw Tusk raise $300 million over three funds, he decided to abandon the model in favor of something novel: taking equity in exchange for services rather than cash.

“I took half a step back and asked, ‘What do I and don’t I like about this process?’ I like two things: I like legalizing disruptive shit, and I like betting on and working with founders who are interesting. What I did not like was raising money, dealing with LPs, taking board seats, and portfolio construction. I also didn’t like giving someone else 80% of my profits,” Tusk explains.

In practice, Tusk says the pivot means his firm spends almost all of its time on a few core areas. Those include helping startups obtain licenses and navigate regulations, and turning up on fundraising calls to assure would-be investors that the government won’t cripple an up-and-upcoming sector. Tusk Ventures also provides comms advice that allows startups to avoid blowing cash on pricey PR retainers, and makes introductions to other CEOs.

This model suits Tusk on a personal level, but it also looks like a shrewd choice in light of a challenging era for conventional VC firms. As Allie noted last week, venture firms are being buffeted by a scary confluence of slow exits, nosebleed valuations, and climbing interest rates. In this context, Tusk’s pivot may have been driven as much by necessity as by choice. The question is whether that pivot is working.So far, it appears it is. Tusk says his firm is currently supplying its equity-for-services arrangement to around 21 startups. “Our fees are between $50,000 and $200,000 a month in preferred equity, scaled to whatever the valuation of the company is. Engagements are usually about 18 months,” he explains, adding that the equity is typically paid out of a pool set aside for employees, and is less than 2% of the overall supply. One upside of this arrangement, and it’s a big one, is that the firm can cut its losses if a startup hits the skids. As Tusk observes, the firm will have expended time and internal resources for naught, but that’s a better outcome than watching $5 million or $10 million in cash go down the drain.

This model, which Tusk describes as an “equity-for-services-at-scale fund” (he admits it’s a terrible name), may be a tempting one for other VC firms given the current venture climate. The catch, of course, is that it requires a hefty supply of capital to give it a go.

“If we can generate, say, $20 million to $30 million a year in earned equity over a 10-year investment period, that’s $200 million to $300 million,” Tusk said. “That’s like the equivalent of a $200 million fund, but times five, because you keep all of it. Obviously I have costs a normal venture fund doesn’t have, because I have to pay everyone every two weeks without management fees to offset it.”

Even as Tusk makes these large early outlays, he is also devoting some of his fortune to a number of civic-minded ventures. Those include a bookstore on Manhattan’s Lower East Side called P&T Knitwear, in homage to a schmatta store run in the same neighborhood by his grandfather, a Holocaust survivor. Tusk admits he is losing a bushel of money on the venture, but that he views the bookstore as a form of philanthropy.

In the coming year, Tusk says he plans to move part-time to Los Angeles, where his firm has a satellite office. I suggest that, when he arrives, he employ his considerable political skills to solve one of the city’s most intractable problems: completing a monorail that has been fully built around LAX, but has been unable to start operating due to ongoing problems related to L.A.’s dysfunctional government. Tusk says he’ll consider it.
