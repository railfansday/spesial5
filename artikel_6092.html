TSA PreCheck Touchless ID: How Facial Recognition Replaces Your Driver’s License

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Through 2026, the new TSA PreCheck Touchless ID has been rolling out at an increasing number of airports nationwide, making flying and security checks a little more convenient. The technology uses facial recognition to identify travelers passing through security without a TSA officer having to manually check photo ID (such as a driver's license or passport) and match it with the boarding pass and the passenger's face.

The TSA PreCheck Touchless ID is a new program that could be easily misunderstood, and at this stage it remains optional. Many passengers passing through US airports will not qualify and will need to have their passports/other photo ID and boarding passes checked manually as before. Even for those who do qualify, it remains important for all passengers to continue bringing eligible physical ID (e.g., passport) when flying at all times. Here is what to know about the new TSA PreCheck Touchless ID, what it means for passing through security, and which documents you need to bring when flying.

The TSA PreCheck Touchless ID is a program that lets enrolled travelers confirm their identity at the security checkpoint via a facial scan instead of handing a physical ID to a TSA officer. It doesn't replace the boarding pass itself; passengers are issued an updated boarding pass. Instead, when going through the Touchless ID TSA line, passengers pause for a facial scan and then keep moving.

The TSA's description of the program is: "TSA PreCheck Touchless ID enhances the security screening process with facial comparison technology for faster, more efficient identity verification. TSA PreCheck travelers with a valid passport can opt in with participating airlines or Google Wallet to move through dedicated lanes with ease, enjoying a smoother and more convenient airport experience—an effortless way to start your travels."

The program was initially launched in 2021 with Delta at its major Atlanta hub. By January 2026, the program was available at 28 airports with five US-based airlines (Alaska, American, Delta, Southwest, United). Hawaiian has since joined, and it has been expanding rapidly. By June, it was at 65 airports, with prioritization given to cities hosting the Football (Soccer) 2026 World Cup. Passengers are still required to carry physical ID.

The program is not automatic for passengers. Passengers can choose to opt in to the TSA PreCheck Touchless ID program through their airline profile. They need to update their Known Traveler Number (KTN), passport number, and country. After that, passengers are able to select 'opt in' on their profile or at the flight check-in stage. Their boarding pass will automatically be updated with the TSA PreCheck Touchless ID indicator.

After opting in, it can take up to ten minutes for the change to be reflected. American Airlines requires that, to opt in on their system, passengers need to be AAdvantage members and over 18. Delta adds, "With the help of facial matching, eligible SkyMiles Members can use dedicated bag drop and security lines to breeze through the airport without having to present a traditional photo ID."

Another way to opt in is with Google Wallet. Passengers who already have a Google ID pass saved in their Google Wallet can opt in without being an airline mileage member. Travelers can also opt out of facial recognition at any time and revert to the previous method of using standard ID checks. The TSA states that it "collects a live photograph of the passenger, passport number, known traveler number, transactional metadata (e.g., transaction ID, timestamps, quality scores), and the match results."

In practice, the program is mostly restricted to US citizens, permanent residents, and foreigners already enrolled as trusted travelers. The TSA says, "The TSA PreCheck Application Program is only open to U.S. citizens, U.S. nationals and lawful permanent residents." The TSA PreCheck Touchless ID is not a standalone nationality-based program, but it requires passengers to be eligible for other programs that are nationality-limited.

Passengers need to have TSA PreCheck eligibility or a DHS Trusted Traveler Program that includes TSA PreCheck, such as Global Entry, NEXUS, or SENTRI. The TSA notes, "Global Entry, NEXUS, and SENTRI allow some foreign citizens to apply." Global Entry is for expedited US customer/immigration arrival by air and is open to citizens of 23 partner countries.

NEXUS is for expedited U.S.-Canada air, land, and marine border crossings and is open to Canadians and some Mexicans. SENTRI is for expedited US-Mexico land border crossings and is open to all nationalities. Foreigners outside of these programs generally can't apply. The Google Wallet opt-in route is not available to foreigners as it requires a Google ID pass backed by a US passport. Foreigners need to use their airline loyalty profile.

While Touchless ID is new in the United States and highly limited for foreigners, it is already common practice in much of the world. Several countries operate airport biometric checkpoints that verify identity by face (or iris) without requiring the passenger to build an airline loyalty profile or apply for a trusted traveler-equivalent program. Two different systems operating around the world relate to how passengers' faces are used.

Some countries already have their citizens' faces in a government database, and so the airport matches the face to the existing national ID, passport, or immigration arrival database. Another method is to use a same-day 'one-ID' token, where passengers scan their passports once at check-in or bag drop. This creates an encrypted, temporary biometric token that subsequently opens security, boarding gates, etc., for that single trip.

Examples of countries with government databases matching faces include China, Singapore, and the UAE. In China, Chinese citizens' faces are matched to the national Resident Identity Card database. This allows passengers to pass check-in, security, and boarding with zero physical ID or boarding pass. Foreigners scan their passports at security and are then often recognized at the boarding gate. China has started rolling out a program for international flights.

There are various examples of same-day 'scan once at the airport' systems that do not require loyalty profiles or pre-vetting. Airports (sometimes with an opt-in requirement) include select airports in Japan, Thailand, Hong Kong, Italy, and the United Kingdom. India has introduced its Digi Yatra program that allows passengers to go through security and boarding gates without physical identification.

That said, I have flown through India (domestically and internationally) many times, and passengers can expect to have their passports and boarding passes checked many times at a bunch of places (e.g., midway on the boarding bridge). For me, it's sometimes been at least eight times. On another note, different countries operate in very different cultural contexts with very different societal expectations.

But while the United States appears relatively resistant to auto-IDing unvetted passengers, it has some other distinctive features. One of the most notable is passing through Preclearance at some Canadian and other participating airports to the United States. This essentially means entering the US in Canada and allows some flights to go to otherwise domestic-only airports like New York LaGuardia.

The Customs and Border Protection says, "Today, CBP has more than 600 officers and agriculture specialists stationed at 16 Preclearance locations in 6 countries: Dublin and Shannon in Ireland; Aruba; Bermuda; Abu Dhabi in the United Arab Emirates; Nassau in the Bahamas; and Calgary, Toronto, Billy Bishop Toronto, Edmonton, Halifax, Montreal, Ottawa, Vancouver, Victoria, and Winnipeg in Canada."

Another notable feature about US airports and immigration is that there is generally no emigration counter. US authorities work with airlines to track when travelers leave the country, which largely eliminates the need for emigration checks. The US generally doesn't care too much about people leaving. As passengers do not pass through an exit booth, they do not get an exit stamp. Overstaying travelers find out the consequences of their overstay when they try to return.
