Traces of insecticide in cord blood raise new questions about childhood development

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

A Japanese study linked insecticide exposure before a child's birth to lower scores on certain thinking tests at age nine.

Two compounds linked to widely used insecticides turned up in umbilical cord blood in a Japanese study.

At age 9, children with higher levels of one compound at birth scored lower on a test of processing speed, while those with higher levels of the other scored lower on a test of visual reasoning.

Earlier tests, given between 10 and 32 months, yielded no clear links after researchers accounted for the number of comparisons they made.

The researchers wrote that any association may hinge on the age of the child and the skill examined. Just 200 children took part. Proof that the insecticides caused the weaker scores cannot be drawn from this work.

Abir Nagata and Kenji Tsuchiya, a professor, headed the investigation. Both are based at the Research Center for Child Mental Development at Hamamatsu University School of Medicine.

They are also affiliated with a graduate school run jointly by The University of Osaka and four other Japanese universities.

The growth of a child’s brain and abilities is referred to by scientists as neurodevelopment. Children in earlier work on these insecticides were seldom followed beyond early childhood.

“Previous studies have largely focused on neurodevelopmental outcomes at a single time point,” Nagata said.

Nicotine served as the chemical model for neonicotinoids. Their target is a receptor on nerve cells that normally binds one of the body’s chemical messengers.

Insects carry these receptors and so do humans. Within a fetus, the authors wrote, the receptors assist nerve cells in maturing and connecting.

Harm to bees is what the insecticides are chiefly known for. Over a quarter of global insecticide sales are now attributed to them.

Food and drinking water have both been found to contain residues. Nationwide monitoring in Japan has picked up neonicotinoids in tap water throughout the year.

Animal experiments have been cited as grounds for concern. Altered brain structure followed exposure during brain development in those experiments. Impaired movement, learning and attention were documented as well.

Links with early development have been reported in several studies of children. Agreement between those studies has been poor, however.

All of the children are enrolled in the Hamamatsu Birth Cohort, an ongoing project on child development. The project recruited 1,258 babies. Their births took place in Hamamatsu, Japan, between December 2007 and March 2012.

Urine from pregnant women was the usual source of insecticide measurements in earlier research. What actually reached the baby in the womb is not shown directly by urine. Umbilical cord blood drawn at birth offers a direct measure.

Limited resources meant that cord blood from only 200 randomly selected children could be analyzed. Serum, the liquid fraction of blood, was screened by a laboratory for insecticides and their breakdown products.

Of the ten compounds measured, just two turned up often. Dinotefuran was detected in 104 of the 200 samples, or 52%.

A breakdown product of acetamiprid, a second insecticide, was the other. Its detection rate was 62.5%, or 125 samples.

The team split the children into low, middle and high groups for each of the two chemicals. Detection was seldom possible in the low group, which was used as the comparison.

Quantities were minute. A typical reading in the high dinotefuran group was 0.07 billionths of a gram per milliliter of serum.

The researchers scheduled five rounds of testing between 10 and 32 months of age. Depending on the visit, 185 to 195 children had enough data to be included in the analysis.

Toddlers with higher levels scored below the low group in a handful of comparisons. Large body movements at 10 and 14 months were weaker in the middle dinotefuran group.

The reverse was seen once. Hand and finger skills at 18 months were stronger in the middle group for the acetamiprid breakdown product.

The team ran 50 comparisons per chemical over the toddler period. Some results will appear meaningful purely by chance when that many comparisons are run.

Statistical significance was lost for every toddler result after the researchers corrected for this. Across ages and skills, no consistent pattern emerged.

A standard four-part intelligence test was completed by 178 of the 200 children at age 9. Differences on two of the four parts survived the same correction.

Processing speed was lower among children in the middle dinotefuran group. The measure captures how fast a child gets through simple tasks.

The shortfall against the low group came to roughly 7 points. An average score on this test is 100.

Perceptual reasoning was weaker in the high group for the acetamiprid breakdown product. Visual puzzles and pattern spotting are what this part of the test relies on. The gap on this part was almost as wide.

Overall IQ scores were lower in those same two groups as well.

Rising insecticide levels did not bring a steady decline in scores. Processing speed was also lower in the high dinotefuran group. That difference was smaller, though, and chance could account for it.

Tsuchiya said the contrast with the toddler findings may reflect links specific to certain skills and ages. On that reading, no single lasting pattern runs through childhood.

The children were only observed as they grew up, and nothing about their exposure was controlled. A link can therefore be identified by the study, whereas a cause cannot be proven.

A single blood sample taken at delivery supplied each child’s insecticide level. The timing of exposure during pregnancy cannot be learned from one sample. Neither can the degree to which exposure varied.

Adjustments were made for each child’s sex and age, premature birth and the education of both parents.

Diet went unmeasured, as did lead, other heavy metals and the parents’ own thinking skills. Part of the gap in scores might be explained by such factors.

Dinotefuran levels at birth were also higher in children who took the age 9 test than in the rest. The results could be skewed by that difference.

Too few samples contained the eight other compounds for them to be analyzed, alone or combined.

Only 178 children contributed to the age 9 results. The authors wrote that estimates from a sample of this size are imprecise. Weaker links may also have gone undetected.

Whether other groups of children show the same links is the next question. Why the links would surface at age 9 and not sooner also remains unexplained.

The authors called for larger studies. Exposure would need to be measured several times during pregnancy in such studies. Testing of the children would continue through middle childhood.

For now, a single group of children in one Japanese city is the source of the evidence.

The full study was published in the journal JAMA Network Open.
