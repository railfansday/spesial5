School playgrounds may have shade in all the wrong places

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Researchers found that only one of nine playground areas met standards for shade, comfort, and play, raising questions about schoolyard design.

A shade sail stretched over a school playground looks like a job done. But children play where the fun is, not where the shade is.

When the two don’t line up, the shade covers an empty patch of ground while the children play in the sun.

Researchers in New Zealand have built a way of grading playgrounds that treats that mismatch as a design problem.

It scores every part of a schoolyard on whether it blocks the sun, whether it’s comfortable, and whether a child would want to play there.

On its first test, at a primary school in Christchurch, New Zealand, one of nine play areas passed.

Anupriya Sukumar of the School of Landscape Architecture at Lincoln University in New Zealand led the work. Her co-authors are at Lincoln and in the Department of Landscape Architecture and Urban Planning at Texas A&M University.

Sun protection is especially important in New Zealand. The country and neighboring Australia have the world’s highest rates of skin cancer, with about 100,000 New Zealanders affected each year.

Roughly a third of a person’s lifetime exposure to the sun comes before age 18.

In an interview with Earth.com, Sukumar said the schools she has mapped tend to put their shade in the wrong place.

“While shade was available in many schools, it was most commonly located around the perimeter of playgrounds, particularly along school boundaries,” she noted.

Those shaded strips often went underused, she said, because they weren’t where the children played.

Sukumar’s team starts by dividing a playground into activity zones, each one a place for a particular kind of play.

The Christchurch school had nine, from a sports field and a tennis court to a sandpit and a covered outdoor classroom.

Around each zone with a fixed purpose, the team drew a buffer of about 6.5 feet (2 meters) for the children waiting their turn.

Shade was measured for the worst moment of the school day. The team built a 3D model of the school and simulated its shadows for 1:30 p.m. on February 1.

That is the end of the summer lunch break, close to solar noon.

A zone passed on sun protection only if at least 80% of it was in shade dense enough to block most of the sun’s ultraviolet light for about an hour.

A thin tree canopy, which lets 70% of that light through, doesn’t count.

The surface underfoot reflects ultraviolet light back up at a child’s face.

Grass reflects about 2% of it and dry sand 20%, so a sunlit sandpit reflects ten times as much as a sunlit lawn. Asphalt and concrete fall in between.

The team assessed comfort separately, using five years of records from a nearby weather station to identify prevailing wind patterns.

They then mapped the areas sheltered from wind by buildings and trees.

Running children need shade with a breeze to stay comfortable, while children sitting down to eat need shade out of the wind. So each zone is scored against what happens there.

The third test is the simplest. A zone counts as attractive if it holds something that draws children in, such as climbing equipment, sand, or balls.

Only the covered outdoor classroom met all three tests. Four zones met one of them, and four met none. On sun protection alone, one zone was adequate, four were moderate, and four were low.

The sports field, the tennis court, and the basketball court all failed the attraction test.

School policy restricts balls at break time, so these large spaces lacked the equipment needed for their intended activities.

Asked what surprised her most, Sukumar singled out those big sports areas.

“One of the most surprising findings was that large portions of school playgrounds were dedicated to sports fields and hard courts, yet these spaces often lacked access to sports equipment, such as balls, due to lunchtime restrictions or school policies,” she said.

Smaller spaces such as sandpits and nature play areas took up much less of the playground, she said, and were often used more.

The grades lead to a repair plan the authors call the three Rs. A zone that nearly passes gets a retrofit, meaning a simple, low-cost change.

More serious failures call for a longer-term redesign. Schools can relocate or repurpose areas that fail every test and see little use.

A school can work through that list as money allows instead of rebuilding everything at once.

Sukumar told Earth.com that schools with funding for only one project this year should improve their busiest play area first.

The method was tried at one school, and the team hasn’t yet measured whether the children there spend more time in protected zones or get less sun.

“At this stage, we have not directly measured how much time children spend in shaded or sun-protected areas,” Sukumar said. “However, this represents an important next step for future research.”

The authors wrote that the attractiveness scores rest on what a zone contains rather than on watching children use it.

The pass marks come from earlier research, not from measurements at this school.

The comfort rules were set for Christchurch’s temperate climate and would need adjusting elsewhere, and the authors called the whole process complex and potentially time-consuming.

What would settle the open question is a count of where children actually go during outdoor play.

Sukumar said direct observation, or tracking children’s movements across a playground, could show whether the protected zones are used as intended and where shade is needed most.

The full study was published in the journal Cities & Health.
