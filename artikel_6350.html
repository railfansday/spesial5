Clean indoor air has become more important than ever. While allergens like dust, dander, mold, and bacteria are all teaming up to infiltrate the air you breathe, the widespread wildfire smoke affecting much of the U.S. right now is also proving just how quickly outdoor pollution can make its way inside. According to the EPA, Americans spend about 90 percent of their time indoors, where pollutant concentrations can sometimes be even higher than they are outside. An air purifier protects your airways from airborne irritants while providing clean, fresh air indoors (which is why we think everyone should invest in an air purifier).

We got our hands on air purifiers from brands like Coway, Levoit, and GermGuardian and put them to the test. Filtration was our main concern, so we could determine the air purifiers’ efficiency. Then we moved along to other factors such as noise while running, how well they work in a given room, and convenience features. After our in-home testing, these are the air purifiers that came out on top.

We tried out several air purifiers with a range of filtration systems, noise levels, and features. We tested these models in multiple different apartments, placing them in various rooms of the home to measure their efficiency. We considered the different uses of an air purifier, like if you’re an allergy sufferer or have a home filled with furry friends, and tested special features like light-up displays and automatic shutoff to determine their ease of use and effectiveness. We considered the amount of time it took to set up the different purifiers and install their filter systems.

For units that we haven’t tested, we combed through dozens of potential products and their specifications, reviews, and user complaints to identify what we thought were the best air purifiers. We settled on the following list, which includes options that are budget-friendly, sleekly designed, and high-performing.

Air purifiers vary in filtration range, so it's important to first consider your room size before buying. To find a model that accommodates your space, the EPA recommends that you first multiply the length and width of the area where you intend to operate the air purifier to calculate the square footage.

Most air purifiers have a maximum coverage area listed, so just compare to your calculations to find an air purifier that is the best fit for your home.

If you have an open floor plan or high ceilings, opt for an air purifier with more coverage. You should also place the air purifier in the room you use most regularly, and make sure it’s in an area where the airflow isn’t obstructed.

The filter system of your air purifier might differ depending on the model and issue you're trying to target such as allergy relief, odor elimination, or germ protection. You’ll often see three-stage filtration (or four-, five-, or six-stage), which includes all three of the filter types detailed below.

Pre-filters are typically the first line of defense in an air purifier and trap larger particles like dust, hair, and fur, which helps extend the lifespan of the main filter and improves the overall efficiency of the unit.

All of our selections on this list involve a multi-step filtration system to target a majority of common pollutants and allergens in your household.

Purvi Parikh, MD, an allergist and immunologist with NYU Langone Health, recommends selecting an air purifier that comes with a HEPA filter. “Make sure it's a HEPA air purifier since these filter down to 0.3 microns and have a robust amount of studies done showing their efficacy.” HEPA filters remove at least 99.97 percent of airborne particles like dust, bacteria, and pollen, and are the gold standard for air purification.

Activated carbon filters are more porous and help get rid of gases and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that are commonly found in household items like surface cleaners and candles. These filters absorb molecules onto carbon and are best used in environments with strong odors or smoke.

With laundry machines, dishwashers, and fans all emitting their own noise, adding another loud appliance into the mix could be overkill, especially since air purifiers are meant to run the entire day.

Look for models that range from 10 to 30 decibels. A whisper is around 30 decibels, so expect air purifiers in this noise range to be almost virtually silent.

“Air purifiers need to be kept clean, and filters should be changed every six months,” Parikh says. Since air purifier filters are designed to collect and trap different-sized particles, contaminants, and hair, they get gunky pretty easily. This buildup could even reduce the efficiency and the air quality of the purifier if not cleaned often enough. Most air purifiers come with reusable filters, so just rinse them off in between replacements.

App compatibility, savvy control panels, remotes, check filter indicators, and variable settings are beneficial features that make it easier to use your air purifier. If you want to monitor the air quality levels in your home or keep tabs on filter life, look for an air purifier that pairs with an app. If you’re easily overstimulated by loud noises, consider an air purifier that has different modes for adjusting the speed of the internal fan.

Air purifiers use a fan to draw air into the unit, then pass it through a series of filters to remove particles and contaminants. Said filters vary in effectiveness and normally target a specific concern like odors, smoke, and viruses. Once the air has passed through the fan and all of the appropriate filters, it is recirculated back into the room for a cleaner and healthier environment.

“Air purifiers filter out pollutants, improve air quality, remove infectious agents and pollen,” says Parikh. “They limit your level of exposure and reduce lung damage and flare-ups of underlying chronic conditions such as asthma attacks, COPD exacerbations, and heart issues.”

“It can affect everyone’s health by causing inflammation and lung damage over time that may be either reversible or irreversible,” Parikh says. “It is especially dangerous for those with underlying heart disease, lung disease, pregnant women, children, and immunocompromised individuals.”
