Peregrine falcons are much smarter than we thought, using fake attacks to tire out their prey

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

A new study reveals that peregrine falcons deliberately use false attacks against their prey, making them much easier to catch later on.

When a peregrine falcon dives at a flock of birds and comes away with nothing, it looks like a failed hunt.

At one bay in British Columbia, some of those failed hunts may never have been meant to succeed. A study of peregrines and small shorebirds called Pacific dunlins suggests that early attacks send the flock into the air too soon.

A few hours later, the dunlins are often back on the ground, and that’s when the falcons make the most kills. If the researchers are right, the early attacks make the dunlins easier to catch later.

Ron Ydenberg and two colleagues at Simon Fraser University’s Centre for Wildlife Ecology published the analysis in October 2023. Their case rests on the timing of flights and kills at a single bay.

Predators are “imagined as clever in novels and movies, like the velociraptors in Jurassic Park,” Ydenberg said. But biologists who work from evidence, he added, tend to doubt such ideas.

Between 20,000 and 40,000 dunlins spend the winter at Boundary Bay, feeding on mudflats that the tide covers twice a day.

As the tide rises, the dunlins have to feed closer to the shoreline, where a falcon can ambush them from cover.

Like other shorebirds, dunlins used to rest on shore at high tide. During the 1990s, as more peregrines wintered at the bay, the dunlins largely stopped.

Now they often spend daytime high tides circling in a loose flock high over the bay. Almost every dunlin the falcons were seen to kill was outside that flock.

But circling takes energy. On days when the dunlins made these flights, they averaged 3.1 hours in the air.

By the team’s estimate, the three most dangerous hours for a dunlin on the ground run from one hour before high tide to two hours after.

On those days, the dunlins spent 89% of the hour before high tide in the air and 95% of the hour after.

But in the second hour after high tide, the dunlins were in the air only 44% of the time. The falcons did best in that hour, making 43 of the 139 kills recorded in 905 hours of watching.

The dunlins had done a lot of their flying earlier, more than an hour before high tide, when the danger was low. Compared with the safest schedule, their flights started early and ended early.

To explain the early flights, the researchers used a 2007 model inspired by killer whales hunting sea lions.

In that model, a predator can gain by making attacks that have little chance of success. The prey still has to flee, then make up the lost feeding time by taking more risks later.

At Boundary Bay, that would mean falcons attacking before high tide to send the dunlins up early.

The team couldn’t test the idea, because even experienced observers can’t easily tell a serious attack from a half-hearted one.

In data the team hasn’t published, peregrine hunts at the bay peak two to four hours before high tide, hours before the kills do.

The records come from Dick Dekker’s winter visits to Boundary Bay between 1994 and 2012.

Falcon attacks were recorded on 151 days. The dunlin flocks were timed from dawn to dusk on 34 days, and they made their high-tide flights on 23 of them.

The authors wrote that Dekker became “the world’s foremost watcher of wild falcons.”

The authors offered two other explanations.

Less skilled falcons might be the ones attacking early, while skilled falcons hunt just after high tide. Or the dunlins might land early because feeding is especially good on a falling tide.

To test those explanations, researchers would have to track individual falcons closely, Ydenberg said. That doesn’t look practical at Boundary Bay, where the falcons weren’t marked.

He added that the idea could be tested in other predators and their prey, where the same logic should apply.

The full study was published in the journal Frontiers in Ethology.
