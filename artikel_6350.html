The rise of oxygen on Earth may have unfolded differently than we thought

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Ancient rocks suggest major rises in Earth’s atmospheric oxygen occurred earlier than thought, while the deep ocean took longer to catch up.

For most of Earth’s history, the planet lacked enough atmospheric oxygen to sustain wildfires.

Charcoal preserved in rocks indicates that oxygen had reached levels capable of supporting wildfire by about 420 million years ago – roughly three-quarters of today’s atmospheric level.

Haiyang Wang and Chao Li, geochemists at Chengdu University of Technology, reviewed evidence suggesting that major increases in atmospheric oxygen preceded corresponding changes in the ocean.

Their review suggests that two major atmospheric oxygen increases occurred earlier than traditionally thought.

Many widely used clues to ancient oxygen levels come from marine rocks, including their minerals, isotopes, and trace metals.

These records primarily reflect ocean chemistry, providing indirect evidence of atmospheric conditions.

Ocean chemistry is highly complicated. It answers to nutrient supply, biological productivity, how water circulates, even the shape of the basin.

Sulfate offers a more direct connection to the atmosphere. Atmospheric oxygen carries a distinctive isotopic signature that can pass into sulfate when sulfide minerals oxidize.

Preserved in sedimentary rocks, that signature helps researchers reconstruct ancient atmospheric conditions.

The Great Oxidation Event, 2.4 to 2.1 billion years ago, still holds. Cyanobacteria had been quietly making oxygen in shallow coastal waters for ages before that.

Oxygen concentrations in these shallow waters may have reached levels sufficient to support oxygen-using microbes. Reactions with minerals and gases consumed much of that oxygen before it could accumulate in the atmosphere.

Then production finally outran consumption and the atmosphere flipped.

Steps two and three are where the textbooks take the hit. Wang and Li date them to 1.3–1.0 billion and 0.44–0.41 billion years ago.

Those dates precede the conventional timings of the Neoproterozoic Oxygenation Event, around 800–500 million years ago, and the Paleozoic Oxygenation Event, around 420–360 million years ago.

But according to the sulfate record, those weren’t the atmosphere oxygenating.

They were the ocean catching up to atmospheric changes that had already happened – about 200 million years late in the first case, and 30 million years late in the second.

Between about a billion and 410 million years ago, the carbon isotope record is peppered with sharp excursions that generations of scientists read as oxygen rising.

The sulfate evidence suggests that some ocean oxygenation events temporarily lowered atmospheric oxygen. As oxygen entered the ocean, reactions with stored organic carbon and sulfur compounds consumed it.

The “boring billion” suddenly looks less boring. Modeling pegged to the sulfate data puts mid-Proterozoic atmospheric oxygen at 0.1–1% of today’s atmospheric oxygen level.

At those low atmospheric levels, local photosynthesis likely supplied much of the oxygen in shallow waters, while the deep ocean remained largely oxygen-free.

Two oxygen modes coexisted through most of this history. The first was local: cyanobacterial mats producing oxygen in shallow, sunlit patches.

The second was global: oxygen dissolving from the atmosphere into surface water and being shoved deep by circulation.

The local mode is older than three billion years. The global mode becomes more influential as atmospheric oxygen levels rise.

Widespread oxygenation of the deep ocean developed around 410 million years ago.

Oxygen-poor zones persisted, however, and episodes of widespread ocean oxygen loss occurred later.

Strangely, the ancient local mode still runs today. Redox-stratified lakes in Antarctica and Venezuela sustain oxygenated pockets above anoxic bottoms.

There, they are powered by the same benthic microbial photosynthesis that kept tiny patches of Earth’s ocean alive for two billion years.

Low oxygen availability initially restricted the habitats available to oxygen-dependent life.

During the Proterozoic, fluctuating oxygen levels helped shape opportunities for more complex organisms.

Eukaryotes appeared, and multicellularity got its start. Organisms also evolved ways to respond to low oxygen levels.

Later increases in oxygen helped expand the habitats available to large, active organisms.

Major increases in atmospheric oxygen came before widespread oxygenation of the deep ocean. In the long interval between them, life evolved amid uneven and changing oxygen supplies.

The study is published in the journal The Innovation.
