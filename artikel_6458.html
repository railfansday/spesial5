A bizarre seahorse relative found in South Australia in 2006 was actually a new genus that has never been seen since

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

A seahorse lookalike dredged off South Australia in 2006 belongs to a new genus known from eight specimens, none recorded in the wild since.

On February 11, 2006, a dredge working near the Gambier Islands, at the mouth of South Australia’s Spencer Gulf, brought up a small orange seahorse lookalike from about 164 feet (50 meters) down.

The dredge was sampling brachiopods, small animals with shells, for the Waterhouse Club Expedition. The club ran that survey with the South Australian Museum.

The expedition mainly targeted marine invertebrates such as sponges and soft corals. But the paper says it recorded little locality data.

It now has a name, Australyichthys aegis, and the common name helmethead sea muppet. A team led by Graham Short of the Australian Museum reports that it and two relatives are not seahorses. Also, none of the three has a verified sighting in the wild.

The paper lists eight known specimens of the genus, collected over nearly 80 years. Seven were examined for the study, at Museums Victoria, the Australian Museum, the South Australian Museum, the CSIRO fish collection and the California Academy of Sciences.

H. Howell collected the oldest in 1927, about 8 miles (13 kilometers) east of Eden in New South Wales. J.A. Tubb took one off Eden in October 1941. R.J. Slack-Smith caught another there on December 30, 1960, in a Danish seine trawl at 210 to 240 feet (64 to 73 meters).

Martin Gomon of Museums Victoria used the 1960 fish in 1997 to describe a new seahorse, Hippocampus minotaur. D. Gledhill added a male from east of Disaster Bay, south of Eden, on December 10, 1996, from 407 feet (124 meters).

On July 26, 1995, S. Hageman, P. Bock and Y. Bone collected a single female southwest of Esperance, Western Australia, at 335 feet (102 meters).

Foster and Gomon named it Hippocampus paradoxus in 2010. Ralph Foster works at the South Australian Museum, and both he and Gomon are co-authors of the new paper.

The three sites lie across more than 1,550 miles (2,500 kilometers) of coast. Trawls or dredges caught all the fish by accident, between 164 and 407 feet (50 and 124 meters) down. Since 2006, no new one has turned up.

The specimens are irreplaceable, so the team did not dissect them. Short ran the 2006 male, the 1996 Disaster Bay male and the Esperance female through a Bruker SkyScan 1173 at the Karel F. Liem BioImaging Center, at the University of Washington’s Friday Harbor Laboratories.

Each one went in wet, in a 15-milliliter plastic tube of ethanol with damp cheesecloth packed around it. The machine ran at 65 kV through a 1 mm aluminum filter. The image stacks then came out at 2,048 by 2,048 pixels.

Seahorses carry a bony crown on the head, the coronet, and plates fused into a tube around the belly. On the scans, however, Australyichthys had no coronet and no fused belly plates.

Its belly rests on three groups of small bones, front, back and middle, and the authors found that setup in no other member of the family.

In that Disaster Bay male about nine embryos showed up inside the pouch. The Esperance female had round, dense bits in its gut, probably pieces of tiny crustaceans, which is the only clue so far to what any of these fish eat.

The 2006 male was the only one of the seven kept in ethanol (the rest were fixed in formalin, which ruins DNA), and so the genetics come from that single fish.

A. King at the Australian Centre for Wildlife Genomics in Sydney took DNA from tail tissue. J. Bishop at the University of Oregon prepared the library, and an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 produced 101,723,675 reads, now filed at the U.S. National Center for Biotechnology Information as BioProject PRJNA1494570.

Susan Bassham and Clayton Small ran the comparison across 271 stretches of DNA from 23 species. The new genus sat outside the seahorses.

Instead, it fell in a group of Indo-Pacific pipefishes and pygmy pipehorses that is the seahorses’ sister group. By that result, the bent head, gripping tail and closed male pouch arose at least twice in one fish family.

That family, the Syngnathidae, is the one where males carry the eggs and give birth. It also holds tiny reef species that lost genes and changed shape to hide in coral.

The 2006 male measures 2.2 inches (56 millimeters). A photo taken when it was fresh shows an orange to pink-orange body with blotches of pink, white and pale yellow, and in ethanol it has faded to yellow-brown.

It has a very large, domed head and a row of sharp, triangular skin flaps along its back and tail. The name aegis refers to the shield of Greek mythology.

The genus name joins Latin for southern and Greek for fish, and Amanda Hay of the Australian Museum suggested calling the group “sea Muppets.”

In 2022, Long and Rodríguez proposed a cutoff for lost species. Under it, a species counts as lost after ten years with no confirmed record in the wild.

Short and his co-authors then apply that rule to the whole genus. They call Australyichthys a lost genus, a term they introduce in the paper.

The label “does not imply imminent extinction,” they wrote. Their conservation statement puts the 20-year gap down to how little anyone samples the mid-depth reefs off southern Australia.

The paper also says no one has made a targeted, non-destructive survey there. The conservation group Re:wild keeps its own list of 25 most wanted lost species.

The paper names bottom trawling as a risk for small fish that live on the seafloor. Ocean warming, lower oxygen and silt from the coast are listed too, especially for semi-enclosed waters such as Spencer Gulf.

The habitat of the 2006 fish was never recorded. The authors blame the “limited locality data recorded during this non-scientific expedition.”

Only one species has DNA, so the team placed the other two in the genus because of their bones. So far, nobody has found a female helmethead. The team did no formal IUCN Red List assessment and calls the genus data deficient.

So the paper proposes deep diving off Eden, in the Great Australian Bight and around the Gambier Islands. It also suggests eDNA tests built on the new genome.

Co-author David Harasti of the Port Stephens Fisheries Institute, who has worked on seahorse hotels in Sydney. He reported that a remotely operated vehicle has spotted pygmy seahorses in the wild.

Trawls and dredges have caught males carrying young in the austral spring and summer, so the team suggests searching then.

The Gambier Islands Group Marine Park, with no active trawling, is on its list. It lies close to where the dredge pulled up the helmethead in February 2006.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Lost Species.
