Would Alaska-Hawaiian 12-Year Widebody Captains Really Earn $493.41 An Hour Under The Tentative Pilot Contract?

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Tada Images | Shutterstock, Wikimedia Commons, Simple Flying

After more than 600 days of negotiations, Alaska Airlines and Hawaiian Airlines came to a tentative Joint Collective Bargaining Agreement with the Air Line Pilots Association International (ALPA). The terms now dictate that a 12-year widebody captain will earn an eye-popping base rate of $493.41 an hour starting in early 2027. Most mainline airline pilots credit between 800 and 1,000 flight hours per year, meaning a $493.41 rate translates to roughly $395,000 to $493,000 in base flight pay.

That rate is only the starting point for 2027 and is scheduled to rise annually, reaching $539.16 per hour by 2030, according to Aero Crew News. The Black Box Aviation Podcast reported that rates will be adjusted annually to match the average of American, Delta, Southwest, and United on comparable aircraft, or increase by at least 3%, whichever is greater. Alaska Air Group will also contribute 18% directly to 401(k) accounts, alongside a performance-based program with 5% annual payouts.

The terms ALPA agreed to on November 10 will be put to a vote on December 1 for the 4,600 union membersto collectively ratify, according to Airline Geeks. If approved, the new pay scales take effect on January 31, 2027. The new contract represents an immediate raise from $86.41 to $102.41 per hour, or a 21.2% to 26.2% pay bump.

The agreement is a long-awaited resolution following Alaska Air Group’s $1.9 billion acquisition of Hawaiian in September 2024. While the Federal Aviation Administration approved the financial merger and granted a single operating certificate in October 2025, aircrews remained divided. Formal joint negotiations launched in February 2025, aiming not only to set uniform pay rates but also to establish an operational framework to reorganize the pilots and jets of the combined fleet.

ALPA negotiators built the new agreement on Alaska’s existing contract framework while preserving legacy Hawaiian protections. Until the unified labor contract is official, management cannot freely assign pilots across both of the newly combined networks or readily swap aircraft to new airfields based on changing demand.

Captain Drew Coyle, the Alaska Airlines MEC chair, commented on the negotiations.

“Our Joint Negotiating Committee remained focused on the priorities of both pilot groups while working through some of the most complex issues facing our combined membership. Nothing about this process was easy. Now it’s our pilots’ turn to review the agreement, ask the tough questions, and decide whether it meets their expectations.”

The tentative agreement also clears a major labor hurdle to Alaska’s long-haul expansion and Hawaiian's $600 million modernization plan. Alaska is expecting up to 17 new Boeing 787 Dreamliners that will launch 12 new overseas routes. Meanwhile, Hawaiian’s fleet overhaul in Honolulu will retrofit all 24 Airbus A330-200 widebodies with new Leihōkū lie-flat suites and premium economy cabins. Still, flying two dozen midlife A330s alongside new 787s forces the airline to maintain separate supply chains, training programs, and maintenance programs.

Passing this contract is only half the battle because Alaska and Hawaiian pilots still cannot fly each other's jets until their seniority lists merge. Under federal law, a neutral arbitrator must combine both pilot rosters into a single master list. Because seniority controls everything from captain upgrades to holiday schedules, pilots must stay separated on their legacy fleets until that process finishes.

Before seniority talks even wrap up, all 4,600 member pilots must approve the four-year agreement itself. Union voting runs from December 1 through December 22. If a majority votes yes, the new pay rates and scheduling rules officially take effect on January 31, 2027, locking in labor stability through January 30, 2031, while eliminating the old pay gap between both workforces.

Captain Larry Payne, the Hawaiian Airlines MEC chair, also remarked on the agreement.

“This joint collective bargaining agreement represents an important milestone for both pilot groups. The decisions made throughout these negotiations will shape our pilots’ careers and quality of life for years to come. If approved, this agreement will establish the foundation for future contracts as we move forward together as one pilot group.”

Once seniority lists finally combine, Alaska Air Group can unleash the full scheduling flexibility it bought with the merger. Management will be able to shift crews seamlessly between Hawaii's refreshed narrowbodies and Seattle's growing Dreamliner fleet as seasonal travel shifts. That operational freedom was impossible after the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) granted a single operating certificate in October 2025, merging safety rules but leaving cockpits divided.

Ultimately, locking in $493.41 an hour for widebody captains proves Alaska is serious about competing alongside major legacy airlines on global routes. Yet paying top-tier wages across both fleets raises the stakes for every flight across the Pacific. The carrier's long-term financial success now hinges on whether it can fill those upgraded cabins at profitable fares against tough domestic and international rivals.

Executives cite disproportionate overhead and geopolitical fuel shocks as they realign post merger operations and roles.

As part of the merger, Alaska took over Hawaiian's 787 Dreamliner orders and rebranded its existing 787s to construct an international gateway at its historic Seattle-Tacoma International (SEA) base. To make the increased $493.41 widebody pay rate financially viable, Alaska is dividing its twin-aisle jets across the combined fleet's two unique hubs. Thus, Hawaiian maintains its legacy A330-200s in Honolulu to meet established island leisure and regional Pacific demand.

Hawaiian originally sought the 787-9 in 2018 to upgrade its fleet and increase capacity on long-haul Pacific routes. Hawaiian even canceled a prior order for six A330neos because the Dreamliner had superior fuel efficiency, longer range, and more business-class seating in densely populated markets such as Tokyo and New York. Hawaiian eventually increased its commitment to 12 Dreamliners before merging with Alaska.

Alaska now has widebody jets, experienced pilots, and foreign operating certificates, which it lacked as a primarily narrowbody airline before acquiring Hawaiian. From Seattle, Alaska deploys the 787-9 on ultra-long, high-demand routes to Tokyo Narita, Seoul, London, Rome, Paris, and Athens. The Dreamliner’s range and fuel efficiency are an ideal fit for these business and luxury travel corridors where higher ticket prices offset premium pilot wages.

In January, Alaska placed a historic order for 105 737 MAX 10s and five more 787-10s, pushing its total Boeing order book past 240 planes. This international push fits naturally with the airline's identity as a fiercely loyal, all-Boeing mainline operator based in Boeing’s backyard. By folding Hawaiian’s Dreamliners into an all-Boeing fleet, Alaska avoids the high cost of training crews and stocking parts for Airbus widebodies.

Back in Honolulu, Hawaiian’s A330-200 fleet anchors core flights to Tokyo Haneda, Osaka, Sydney, Papeete, and Pago Pago, alongside major West Coast cities. Starting in 2028, Hawaiian will upgrade all 24 jets with new Leihōkū lie-flat suites and premium economy cabins. Although older A330s lack the Dreamliner’s range and fuel efficiency, their lower ownership costs bring costs down to make economy vacation airfare more affordable.

Apart from this change, today will also see the airline finally join Alaska Airlines in the oneworld alliance.

The narrowbody crews of the combined Alaska-Hawaiian fleet are about to undergo a significant fleet makeover, much like the widebody pilots who fly long ocean trips. Hawaiian is retiring its outdated 717s, which are frequently used for interisland flights between Honolulu and nearby islands. As part of the expanded narrowbody business in the continental US, Alaska Air Group is moving some 737-800s to Hawaii and bringing its own new 737 MAX 8 and MAX 10 aircraft.

Under the new deal, 12-year narrowbody captains are paid $401.09 per hour. That's $92.32 less than widebody captains. Despite the lower hourly pay, narrowbody pilots perform significantly more takeoffs and landings every shift. This is especially true for crews doing 30-minute island hops. Replacing the 128-seat 717 with larger 737s will add dozens of additional seats per flight while also updating the flight deck, saving fuel on each sortie, and decreasing fleet maintenance. The pilots will have to train on a larger airframe, but the airline will be able to transport more passengers without recruiting additional workers.

Switching to an all-737 narrowbody fleet simplifies pilot training and spare parts across both airlines. Instead of maintaining separate cockpit ratings for 717s, A321neos, and 737s, pilots can eventually fly a single narrowbody type across West Coast and island routes. This standardization lets management smoothly shift crews between mainland and island flying whenever seasonal travel demand spikes or dips.

Still, operating larger 737s on short interisland routes brings clear economic and mechanical trade-offs. Because the 737-800 is much heavier than the 717, it burns more fuel per trip and puts extra wear on brakes and landing gear during frequent landings. However, the higher seat capacity lowers the cost per passenger, helping the airline offset those added baseline expenses.
