The 8 Best Fantasy Book Authors of All Time, Ranked

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Fantasy has been present in stories for just about as long as human storytelling has been a thing, but it didn't become an actual marketing label for the publishing industry until around the 19th century. Since then, we have gotten thousands of authors who can be called full-on fantasy authors, and there is many an element that makes up a great one. Without boundless imagination, deep technical craft, a purposeful eye for world-building, and the ability to write grounded characters, no fantasy author can be truly great.

But even though there have been hundreds upon hundreds of great fantasy writers over the years, there are eight in particular who can be considered the very best to ever grace the genre. These are authors who aren't just phenomenal from a technical or artistic standpoint, they are also of vital historical importance to the genre, still influencing countless fantasy authors the world over to this day. Their work is as foundational as it is essential, and their names belong on the bookshelf of all fantasy fans.

After graduating with a Bachelor's degree in Philosophy from the University of Manitoba, Canadian author Guy Gavriel Kay began writing poetry before turning to prose. Soon enough, he became one of the greatest—and most underrated—fantasy authors of his generation, penning many forgotten books that have aged like fine wine. His work has been particularly important in revolutionizing the mixture between fantasy and settings strongly resembling real historical places and periods, even though Kay himself rejects the label of historical fantasy.

Kay was hired by Christopher Tolkien to help assemble and edit J. R. R. Tolkien's chaotic mass of posthumous manuscripts into the legendary The Silmarillion.

Tolkien's chaotic mass of posthumous manuscripts into the legendary The Silmarillion, and that alone should already be proof enough of his quality. After that, he became a true pioneer of inserting historical elements into the high fantasy genre, delivering masterpieces of the caliber of Tigana and The Lions of Al-Rassan.

Robin Hobb is the pen name of Margaret Astrid Lindholm Ogden, who's undoubtedly best known for her iconic Realm of the Elderlings megaseries. It wasn't until she turned 30 that she published her first book under the name of Megan Lindholm, proving that it's never too late to join the pantheon of history's greatest fantasy authors. She began writing as Robin Hobb 12 years later, and her debut under that legendary pen name was Assassin's Apprentice, the first book in the Farseer trilogy from the Realm of the Elderings series.

Exploring themes like otherness, gender, and queerness across her incredible work, Hobb has crafted some of the fantasy books with the best world-building and the best character writing in history. Shifting the genre's focus from earth-shattering spectacle to deep psychological intimacy and philosophical complexity, she proved just how character- and theme-driven the genre could be when written by a gifted enough author.

If there's any one author worthy of being called the most prolific and widely celebrated fantasy writer currently working, it's almost undeniably Brandon Sanderson. His debut novel, Elantris, was published in 2005, by the time Sanderson had already written about a dozen unpublished books. He has been nothing but a writing machine since then, best known for his iconic Cosmere universe. Composed of multiple subseries and standalones, it's one of the best ongoing fantasy book series.

From the Mistborn series to the Stormlight Archive, Sanderson has written several of the most ambitious high fantasy books of all time, all the while building the biggest multi-decade meta-narrative that fantasy literature has thus far seen. Off the page, he has also trained and influenced countless authors over the years, and coined the distinction between hard magic and soft magic systems, being the undisputed master of the former.

British author, literary scholar, and Anglican lay theologian C. S. Lewis also wrote some incredible science fiction, but he's obviously better known for his fantasy work, particularly The Chronicles of Narnia, one of those fantasy series where every book's a masterpiece. He began writing fiction as a child, inventing stories with his brother and fellow author, Warren Lewis; and soon enough became one of the most important English writers of his generation.

Alongside his good friend Tolkien, Lewis still stands as one of the major pillars of modern fantasy. His work not only revolutionized and popularized portal fantasy, it also made fantasy as a whole more widely accessible, more culturally ubiquitous, and more deeply rooted in Western mythology and philosophy than ever before. Without authors like him, who's to say whether fantasy would have ever been fully legitimized as a genre worthy of being taken seriously?

Another instance of a fantasy literature legend who's best known for one particular series, but whose contributions to the genre extend far beyond that, Robert Jordan is the must-read author of some of the most perfectly-written fantasy books in history. He decided that he wanted to write books while recovering from his time as a Vietnam War veteran, and began writing while working as a nuclear engineer for the US Navy.

The most prominent work of such an iconic career is The Stormlight Archive, far and away one of the greatest and most influential fantasy book series since the '90s. High fantasy had been very heavily inspired by Tolkien (or directly aiming to imitate him) for decades before Jordan came about, and it was authors like him who proved that the genre could start doing things differently. Darker, more mature, and more complex than most other fantasy novels from his time, Jordan's work broke the mold and has continued aging like fine wine.

Dramatic fantasy is of vital importance, and it's always a delight to read an author who actually takes the genre seriously and builds something deeply compelling out of it. However, comedic fantasy is arguably every bit as important, and as far as that subgenre goes, you don't get any authors more foundational than Sir Terry Pratchett, fantasy's most beloved satirist. He's best known for his Discworld series, composed of a whopping 41 fantasy comedies.

What makes Pratchett's work so brilliant is that even though his satirical fantasy works are undeniably hilarious, they're also surprisingly profound. On top of providing one of the best soft magic systems in history, Discworld proved that even comedic fantasy could be legitimately serious in its own way. Thematically complex and richly human, Pratchett's work interrogates human nature, institutions, and morality in all sorts of enrapturing ways. His books are brilliantly meta and quite diverse, making Discworld a must-read series for anyone who thinks that fantasy can't be hilarious and profound at the same time.

In the entire history of speculative fiction, few authors have ever been quite as important as Ursula K. Le Guin. Indeed, the American master could also very reasonably be counted among the eight most important and essential sci-fi authors of all time, but there are many who prefer her work in fantasy. Le Guin wrote her first fantasy story at age nine and submitted her first sci-fi story to a magazine at age 11, and from there, there was nowhere for her to go but up.

The daughter of an anthropologist and with a deep academic interest in mythology, Le Guin wrote masterpieces like Lavinia and the Earthsea Cycle, many of them among those books that are a masterclass in high fantasy writing. By introducing elements of Taoist philosophy, psychological realism, and non-Eurocentric anthropological and cultural depth to her work, Le Guin completely rejected many of the tropes and traditions that had ruled fantasy literature for years. She genuinely revolutionized fantasy, pioneering many new tropes of her own in the process.

You'd be hard-pressed to find anyone unwilling to call J. R. R. Tolkien the single greatest and most influential fantasy author of modern times, and that's for good reason. Never has the history of speculative fiction seen anybody quite like Tolkien, and it likely never will again. Sparked by the Professor's love for philology, ancient mythologies, and original myth-making, the world was treated to an authorial career that completely revolutionized fantasy. Indeed, many would divide the genre's modern history between the pre-Tolkien and the post-Tolkien eras.

Yes, Tolkien wrote The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings, his most celebrated works and perhaps the most influential fantasy novels of the 20th century. However, his contributions to the genre extend far beyond. He wrote several masterworks outside Middle-earth, he contributed to the academic study of fantasy, and he completely laid down the blueprint for modern high, epic, and heroic fantasies. Author of some of the most memorable characters, the most fascinating lore, and the best battles in the history of fantasy books, Tolkien was the greatest of all fantasy writers. It will take someone so special that he's truly out of this world to ever dethrone Tolkien.
