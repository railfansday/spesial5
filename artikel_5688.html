A Pediatrician, a Pastor, and a Paralympian Just Did Wonders for Kids’ Mental Health

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

— The midterm elections offer lessons in decency and difference

My son is 32. Early Wednesday morning he forwarded me a New York Times story about the new faces Tuesday's midterm elections sent to Washington and to statehouses.

He added a note: "Some themes for your next article, like the Knicks one. A message about being decent human beings. A message about how what makes us different brings us together." Then he quoted one line from the Times article: "There's a pediatrician, a pastor, a Paralympian and a former Navy helicopter pilot."

In June, I wrote that the Knicks' championship handed parents a teaching moment. My son was right: Tuesday handed us another.

Our kids and pediatric patients need the lesson. They have lived through a full campaign season. Attack ads. Comment sections. Adults yelling at screens. Before the last presidential election, 77% of American adults named the future of the nation a significant source of stress. Children absorb what we carry home. In the Trevor Project's 2024 national survey, 90% of LGBTQ+ young people said politics had hurt their well-being in the last year. Let's call that the un-teaching.

First, start with the line my son quoted. The pediatrician is Annie Andrews, MD, of South Carolina. She ran for the Senate on a platform focused on children's health after a 2025 measles outbreak sickened some 1,000 children in her state. Andrews, a pediatric hospitalist, saw harm to kids and asked for the job of stopping it.

The pastor is Adam Hamilton, who leads the largest United Methodist congregation in the country. In January he was a registered Republican. Now he is the first Democrat to win a New Hampshire Senate seat in Kansas since 1932. He campaigned on his faith and promised to work across the aisle.

The Paralympian is Josh Turek of Iowa. Two gold medals in wheelchair basketball. A working-class kid. A man who once voted for George W. Bush. He is headed to the Senate.

The helicopter pilot is Rebecca Bennett. She flew for the Navy, ran a healthcare business, and now heads to the House from New Jersey.

Then come the firsts. Keisha Lance Bottoms of Georgia will be the first Black woman ever elected governor in this country. Chris Pappas of New Hampshire will be the first openly gay man in the Senate; he told the Times he is living a life he could not have pictured as a boy. Abdul El-Sayed, MD, DPhil, of Michigan, a physician who ran Detroit's health department, will be the first Muslim senator. Melat Kiros, 29, becomes the first Gen Z woman elected to Congress. James Talarico, a former middle-school teacher who quotes Scripture in his stump speech, will be the youngest member of the Senate at 37.

Ask a child what these people share. Different faiths. Different bodies. Different races, ages, loves, hometowns. Each one walked into rooms full of strangers and asked for their trust. The strangers said yes. What makes each of us different brought them together.

Talk about Amy Acton, MD, MPH. A physician, Ohio's former health director, now its governor-elect. She has spoken openly about a traumatic childhood and sexual abuse at the hand of her stepfather. Late in the race, an ad from her opponent put Acton's estranged mother on camera to call her a liar. Acton held her ground. She said she told her story so other people might see a way out.

As a child psychiatrist, I treat children with histories like hers. Most of them believe the worst thing that happened to them is the truest thing about them. Let's tell these patients Acton's story.

Adolescents build an identity partly by borrowing one. They pick role models whose life stories connect to their own, and they gravitate toward role models who resemble them. For some kids that is a matter of life and death. LGBTQ+ youth who have an older LGBTQ+ role model report lower rates of considering and attempting suicide. In an earlier Trevor survey, more than 80% said positive public role models made them feel better about who they are. A gay teenager in Manchester watched a gay man win a New Hampshire Senate seat Tuesday night. A kid in a wheelchair in Des Moines watched a Paralympian win one in Iowa. That registers.

Difference, met up close, heals. Cross-group friendships rank among the strongest known antidotes to prejudice in childhood, and they support children's well-being. Civic life protects too. In a national study that followed more than 9,000 adolescents into adulthood, those who volunteered and voted went on to have better mental health and engage in fewer risky behaviors. Community engagement in adolescence predicted fewer depressive symptoms years later.

My son also pointed out another crucial point: Kids across the country just witnessed an incredible underdog story. Talarico and El-Sayed were up against hundreds of millions of dollars in opposition spending. Several of the winning candidates represent groups historically left out from Congress, including gays, Blacks, Hispanics, and women. There is something immensely powerful about marginalized candidates running campaigns built around taking care of their communities and defeating the wealthiest individuals in the country. It doesn't matter how powerful you are, compassion for others and belief in self is what can change the world. That's a story our young patients need to witness.

Of course, half of the country may have woken up disappointed Wednesday. But even for that half, the lesson holds. Hamilton was a Republican 10 months ago. Turek voted for Bush. A child can admire a person and still argue with the platform. Disagreement is healthy. Contempt is the toxin. After the last election, the head of the American Psychological Association warned that pulling away from people over politics can cost us the relationships we need most.

Decency. Service. Faith worn in the open. Grit in a wheelchair. Honesty about one's own pain. A hand across the aisle. Strangers saying yes to someone unlike themselves.

There's the message. Everyone in this country has a reason to bring it up this week, and a son or daughter or patient will be ready to hear it.

My son is 32. He still sends me lessons. That's how it's supposed to work. Don't let the moment pass.
