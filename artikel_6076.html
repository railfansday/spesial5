Can Venezuela Build a Different Kind of High-End Tourism?

Adriana Cisneros sees an opportunity for Venezuela to build a distinctive, conservation-minded tourism industry before the international hotel brands arrive. Whether that vision becomes reality will depend on political stability, security, and crucially, the choices made by the country's tourism leadership.

Venezuela is hardly an obvious candidate for the next great luxury travel destination. After decades of political and economic turmoil, the country faces deteriorating infrastructure, significant security risks, and an uncertain political future. The U.S. State Department advises Americans to reconsider travel, with stronger warnings against visiting several regions, including parts of the Amazon.

But Adriana Cisneros, the Venezuelan businesswoman whose family group has interests spanning media, real estate, and hospitality, believes the country has an unusual opportunity to build something different.

She estimates it could be eight years before Venezuela sees a Four Seasons, Rosewood, or Capella. Rather than waiting for the conventional luxury hotel industry to arrive, she imagines a network of four or five base camps connected by small aircraft and boats, offering access to the country's remarkable natural environments.

Adriana Cisneros, whose family group has interests in media, real estate, and hospitality, told Skift that Venezuela could skip the conventional luxury hotel playbook and build a network of four or five ultra-high-end tented camps linked by small aircraft and boats. She estimates it could be eight years before a Four Seasons, Rosewood, or Capella opens in the country, and she sees aircraft such as Cessna Caravans and Pilatus PC-12s serving remote areas rather than waiting for roads and airports to be rebuilt. This is not an announced development program. The obstacles are substantial: Venezuela's political transition is unresolved with no confirmed election timetable, the U.S. State Department advises Americans to reconsider travel and applies its highest warning to some of the remote regions central to the concept, and reliable electricity, water, healthcare, and emergency response cannot be taken for granted. Cisneros anticipates more risk-averse wealthy travelers could take another two or three years to feel comfortable visiting. The country is not starting from zero: independent operators, family-owned posadas, and lodges including Waku Lodge and Campamento Ucaima have kept tourism alive through decades of underinvestment. Cisneros points to her Tropicalia project in the Dominican Republic, where a forthcoming Four Seasons sits within a conservation-focused development, as her training ground.
