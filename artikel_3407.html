What Happens If We Break Letterboxd?

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Earlier this month, the Trylon in Minneapolis gave people six chances over three days to see “Kanał,” Andrzej Wajda’s 1957 film about Polish resistance fighters retreating into the sewers of Warsaw. After opening night, programmer John Moret saw enthusiastic reviews on Letterboxd.

The next evening, he remembers, ticket sales picked up.

Moret could imagine the calculation for somebody still on the fence. “Oh, these people I trust went to go see this movie, and they loved it. And it’s just playing for two more days.”

On November 5, the New York Times reported that its parent company was in exclusive talks to buy Letterboxd for roughly $330 million. Under the deal being discussed, the film-logging site would likely keep operating on its own and use the Times Company’s advertising technology. For theaters like the Trylon, what matters is whether people who went on opening night can still reach those who might go tomorrow. In 2026, that handoff is working remarkably well.

Five days before “Kanał” opened, Ken Russell’s “The Devils” sold out at the Trylon on election night. Moret also co-founded the national Programmers’ Jam with Bret Berg, theatrical sales director at the American Genre Film Archive, and Dan Halsted of Portland’s Hollywood Theatre. When the Jam met in Los Angeles in May, Berg told IndieWire that programmers had access to more restorations than ever before. They also had more places to show them and a new audience coming through the doors.

When we spoke again this month, Berg said Letterboxd drives “the FOMO of needing to be conversant in a movie of the moment.” For a few days in Minneapolis, that movie was “Kanał.”

Berg can see why a press company might be a good home for Letterboxd. He also remembers what the last sale made plain to him. Something people had spent years helping build was “just an asset to be bought and sold.” In the r/Letterboxd thread about the talks, one commenter saw an upside to a newspaper owning a site that rates movies. They worried about paywalled features and ads, but liked the prospect of a ratings system free of “studios putting their fingers on the scale.”

You come home from a movie and write what you thought. Your friends get a tip, the theater gets a little local press, and Letterboxd gets something to put an ad next to. Years later, someone who’s never been to Minneapolis can land on the same page and read what it was like to see that movie with a crowd.

Moret keeps his own movie diary in a paper notebook. He stopped posting what he watched for a while because people kept asking if it meant he was going to program it. Sometimes a guy just wants to watch a movie. He still checks Letterboxd after a screening. “I can see that night exactly how they responded,” he said. He’s seen patrons meet Trylon volunteers and follow them afterward. He’s also heard from people who knew one another by their Letterboxd handles before they started going to movies together.

Moret did have an ulterior motive for December’s Dede Allen series. He wanted to show “Mike’s Murder,” James Bridges’ 1984 mystery starring Debra Winger as a bank teller trying to find out who killed her tennis instructor. A Letterboxd user could make a pretty good list out of movies you didn’t know shared an editor like Allen. Moret gets to put them on a calendar. If you like what Allen does with a bank full of hostages in “Dog Day Afternoon” on December 11, you can come back for “Mike’s Murder” on December 18. Both play in 35mm.

Berg reads the reviews from one town with the next town in mind. A joke review with thousands of likes might tell him very little about who’d pay to see a movie. Five people leaving a one-off screening at a DIY microcinema and feeling compelled to write about it might tell him quite a lot. Their reviews can tell him what got a reaction in the room or whether people were excited to see a particular restoration. For someone deciding which films in the archive’s catalog deserve another look, that’s “some interesting free data.”

Even Berg comes across movies he’s never heard of in his own catalog. Three and a half stars on Letterboxd is enough to make him give one a closer look. Then, he wants to know what kind of time people had. “How many audience moments are there? That’s what I care about.”

For a horror movie, he might be counting jump scares. With Herzog, he’s thinking about how many iconic images there are. (I personally suggested that the nation craves the sight of a steamship being pushed up a mountain. “Oh, it’s true,” Berg said.)

Berg named Spectacle in Brooklyn and Whammy! in Los Angeles as places where he could count on hearing from a few people whether a movie made an impression. And he can look for the same things he would on a research viewing, with the added benefit of a crowd. Was everybody there to see a new restoration? How full was the room? Somebody who mentions how many other people turned up has given a distributor in another city information he couldn’t get by sitting down and watching the film himself.

Berg also thinks about who’s had the chance to write film books, or to be taken seriously as a critic on a national stage. He singled out trans critics among the writers who can find an audience on Letterboxd without first persuading an editor to commission a review. The person who finally gets you to watch a movie might write three sentences half a century after the original reviews ran. They might be the only person you follow who’s even heard of it.

If the people writing those reviews scattered across half a dozen sites, Berg could still go looking for them. It would take longer. If Letterboxd imploded altogether, he’d expect “a return to gut feeling.”

Berg has some experience with the decision to leave. He still uses Vimeo for his day job, but the price helped persuade him to move his own project, Museum of Home Video, to a cheaper service. Finding another place to put a video was manageable. The Letterboxd equivalent would mean finding another place to put a review, then finding the person whose reviews got you to sign up in the first place.

In that same Reddit thread, one person was already looking for the exit. “Any other apps people know about with similar list-building functionality?” they asked.

Andrew Masyk, the founder of rival tracking site Simkl, says its importer can bring over your watchlist, ratings, and the full text of your reviews. Custom lists can come over using a separate tool. The comments and follows stay behind. “People have to find each other again, and that’s the hardest part of moving,” he wrote.

Masyk has every business reason to make switching easy. Even so, he figures people who have spent years keeping a movie diary are inclined to stay put. “What moves people is the product changing under them: core features going behind a paywall, heavier ads, or getting your data out becoming harder.” His test for a buyer is whether it keeps the exit open. “So the export button matters more than who owns the company,” he wrote.

The export button can save a review. The friend who used to see it in their feed still has to find it. And that can get harder long before anyone leaves. If a buyer gave sponsored posts more room in the feed, you could still log every movie you see and miss the three sentences from a friend that would’ve gotten you to the Trylon.

If Letterboxd breaks, the Trylon loses one way to fill a seat, and a programmer in another town loses one way to tell whether “Kanał” might fill a few seats there, too. Hear about “Kanał” on Monday, and you can still go. Hear about it on Wednesday, and you’ve missed it.
