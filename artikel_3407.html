‘Filipiñana’ Could Be the Philippines’ First-Ever Oscar-Shortlisted Feature — Even as the Film Was Dealt an Unexpected Tragedy

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

A posh, Philippine country club’s colonial secrets are exposed in Rafael Manuel’s evocatively creepy, grimly funny, and austerely constructed social satire, “Filipiñana,” set outside Manila.

Writer/director Rafael Manuel’s film, which won a World Cinema special jury award at the 2026 Sundance Film Festival, also stands a chance at being his country’s first representative on a Best International Feature Film Oscar shortlist. While the Philippines was among the eight countries that submitted during the category’s inaugural year in 1956 (with Lamberto V. Avellana’s “Child of Sorrow”) along with France, Italy, and others, it’s never been nominated or shortlisted. Executive-produced by Manuel’s former mentor Jia Zhangke, “Filipiñana” follows a tee girl who discovers something is off (and downright sinister) about the club’s administration, specifically president Dr. Palanca (Teroy Guzman). She’s played by Jorrybell Agoto, who, at just 32 years old, died earlier in November after a short battle with lymphoma.

IndieWire’s interview with filmmaker Rafael Manuel took place a week before the news, though he shared in a later statement, “The tragedy of this loss cannot be put into words. The world has just lost one of its brightest stars. We have just lost a great friend. But our thoughts and prayers go out especially to Jorybell’s family, who have just lost a loving ate [the Filipino word for older sister] and a devoted daughter.”

An elegaic air, too, hangs over the film in retrospect and even before Agoto’s passing, as “Filipiñana” dwells in ghosts of the Philippines’ imperial past and Spanish occupation.

“No one explains it to you beforehand,” director Manuel told IndieWire of touring with his first feature for nearly a year. (We spoke over Zoom while he smoked a cigarette in his Amsterdam apartment.) “What I’m starting to realize is the biggest difference between short and feature is that with short festival, it’s kind of the end goal. When you premiere at your festival, you’re so happy, and it’s done; it’s like people get to see it finally. But with a feature film, it’s just the beginning, and no one explained this to me.”

Manuel joins the ranks of Lav Diaz, Brillante Mendoza, the late Lino Brocka, and many others to represent the Philippines at the Academy Awards — a concept, he said, that was never really on his radar.

“When we were coming up with this film and the genesis of the film, you don’t really think about an Oscars race,” said Manuel. “You just think about making the best film you can. And especially coming from Southeast Asia, access to the American market, even getting a U.S. theatrical release, it’s not something that’s very common. Usually, your usual route is via the European arthouse market. So it’s been really surprising and in the best way possible, seeing how well received the film is in the States, in North America, and in the Anglo world in general.”

The process was a “very challenging one,” he said, as the Philippines is taking the Oscar push very seriously from a governmental perspective. “And to be the first film that’s seriously backed by the government means a lot to us,” he said, especially for a film that’s critical of government and social hierarchy.

And there are hat tips not only to Jacques Tati, but also to Wes Anderson and Michael Haneke, at least in reviews, in terms of the movie’s flattened remove from violence and its dryly observational approach where everything in the frame is meticulously arranged.

“I get why people are saying Michael Haneke and Wes Anderson,” he said. “I mean, Jacques Tati, obviously, he’s a really big influence for me, but for example, with Haneke, I feel like we tackle violence very differently from how Haneke tackles violence. And I think with Wes Anderson, I see it because of the colors, because of the symmetrical framing and all of that, but I think this is very much a satire of that. And so, yes, we use a lot of Western codes and Western aesthetics in the film, but because it’s a film that deals with Western influence in the Orient, it was a film that deals with colonization. So the form and the aesthetic of the film is very much part of the satire. It’s the form.”

He added, “It’s a polarizing film. I think it’s either you love it or you hate it, and I think that’s the same whether you’re in the West or in the Philippines.”

The distribution of world cinema in the Philippines also helps to explain the divided palate for “Filipiñana,” as audiences have very little exposure to arthouse cinema from the outside world.

“In the Philippines, distribution of world cinema, it’s almost nonexistent. In Metro Manila, we have basically one or two arthouse cinemas, independent cinemas. The rest of the cinemas are all in shopping malls, and all they play are local rom-coms or Marvel movies,” he said. But issues in the West of audience fatigue over shopworn, recycled I.P. and familiar material also apply in the Philippines.

“It’s been a hot topic lately in the Philippines and consumers and audiences complaining about formulaic Filipino filmmaking. They’re tired of the formulaic rom-coms. But then the thing is that in the Philippines, I would say 90 percent of people have never seen a Lav Diaz film, even maybe 99 percent of people haven’t seen a Lav Diaz film,” Manuel said of the slow cinema master behind last year’s submission, the Gael García Bernal-starring “Magellan,” which also dealt in the Philippines’ colonized history. (Diaz and Manuel share producers, including Bianca Balbuena and Bradley Liew, though they’ve never actually met.)

“Filipiñana” marks a break from tradition in terms of its Westernized style — and chatter has been flying on Letterboxd over the film since the Sundance premiere. (“I look through Letterboxd sometimes. Well, I used to, I stopped. And then the unhealthy thing with Letterboxd, for me especially, is that I’ll skip all the good comments and I’ll just read all the bad comments,” he said.)

“When you’re faced with something different with arthouse cinema, like Filipino arthouse cinema, when Filipino audiences are faced with Filipino arthouse cinema, and you get some violent reactions, then it begs the question, ‘When you’re complaining about being tired of formulaic Filipino filmmaking, what exactly do you mean, and what exactly is it that you want?’ Do you just want Hollywood films but with brown faces in them? Because I don’t think that’s the answer either.”

“Filipiñana” is now streaming on Kino Film Collection.
