A touchscreen MacBook has been on the wishlist of many Mac users (and media commentators) for years, and now it’s finally here. While Apple hasn’t gone all the way and produced a 2-in-1 MacBook with a stylus, there are some applications for the touchscreen tech in creative apps, and the switch to OLED means there's incredible brightness on offer. The big drawback is that it’s even more expensive, especially as it only comes on top-end machines, but one day every MacBook might be like this.

There's a lot of ‘pro’ in this laptop. It’s a MacBook Pro. It has an M5 Pro CPU inside it. And it has Apple’s new PROLED XDR screen, which is a touchscreen OLED to everyone else. Is there any way that more pro-ness could have been squeezed in? Perhaps one of the Radeon Pro GPUs that graced the ‘Darth Vader’s dustbin’ generation of the Mac Pro?

That aside, we might be looking at the future of the MacBook line. While for now the new screen is only available on a few top-end models, the benefits it brings - not only improved brightness but a thinner and lighter chassis - are likely to trickle down through the range.

The MacBook Pro remains pro-fessional, has a powerful pro-cessor, and Apple’s hardware pro-wess means we shouldn’t get any pro-blems from the PROLED display. Let’s see. It could be heading to the top of our best laptop for animation guide.

• Slightly slimmer than before• Not a 2-in-1

First things first, the PROLED screen makes the 16in MacBook Pro a little slimmer and lighter. It’s also a stunner in terms of colour and brightness, but Apple is so late to this party it’s a little hard to get overexcited. We’ve had OLED touchscreens on Windows PCs since at least 2016 and Lenovo’s Yoga 14, and while the display tech is magnificent it does mean you end up with fingermarks all over your screen, especially if you’re using it after lunch.

Then there's the debate about what touchscreen laptops are actually for. In 2-in-1 designs you can bend the screen back and whip out a stylus for some sketching, but in a traditional laptop you’re more likely to use it for zooming and scrolling. Apple’s announcement event for the laptop showed someone using the touchscreen to play piano keys directly on its screen, and while this somehow seems unlikely, as surely your hands will be in entirely the wrong position, it can also be used to click and drag files, audio tracks, and to do a little precision brushwork with your fingertip if you need to. Happily, it’s optional, and the MacBook trackpad is as good as ever. The touchscreen supports most trackpad gestures, if you’re the sort to let their fingers dance, and as ever you can pay extra for the Nanotexture coating that cuts reflections.

Elsewhere, you get the usual MacBook Pro build. They’re really well made, and this one’s no exception. The switch to OLED has allowed for a millimetre to be shaved off the laptop’s thickness, and a few hundred grams to be shed from its weight. The weight reduction is the more noticeable of the two, making Apple’s largest laptop a touch more portable. By far the most important thing, however, is that the Apple logo on the MacBook’s lid once again lights up when the laptop is in use, and it’s the right way up.

• Thunderbolt 5• Excellent colour reproduction

There are three Thunderbolt 5 ports on the MacBook Pro 16, all of which are compatible with USB4 peripherals, and as there's a full-size HDMI 2.1 port and a MagSafe 3 for charging, you won’t have to bog them down connecting to monitors and chargers. This opens them up for use with external SSDs, and given the extra cost of upgrading the storage in a modern Mac when you order, one of those will probably be on your shopping list.

The screen is the big new feature for this 2026 release of the MacBook Pro 16, and this new OLED is an absolute beauty. It’s a ‘tandem’ OLED, which stacks two light-emitting layers on top of one another for a brightness boost, and it shows: Apple promises up to 2,000 nits of brightness if you feed it HDR content, a figure that rivals even its immensely bright Studio Display XDR. Colour is excellent too though you’ll need to manually activate the PROLED XDR P3 + AdobeRGB colour profile in the Settings app to get the most out of it. Do this, and you can expect 93% of Adobe RGB and 94% of DCI-P3 alongside 100% of sRGB.

Apple has also fiddled with the MacBook Pro’s hinge for a better touchscreen experience. Push too hard and you can find a laptop’s screen leaning away from you, but the hinge on the PROLED is noticeably stiffer than that on a non-touch MacBook. It’s also incredibly smooth, almost like there's some sort of hydraulic aspect to it, as it moves up and down.

On the front you’ll find Apple’s usual keyboard, which is pretty good, and a Force Touch trackpad that can take a bit of getting used to, especially if you’re used to holding the pad in to drag files about. This can trigger a deeper click that brings up file information, which is less useful than you might think, but it doesn’t take long to get the hang of, and you can change what it does in the settings.

• Very fast CPU• GPU can't beat descrete chips

The PROLED MacBook is a premium product, and so comes with a choice of premium processors (and a premium price, but more on that later). It can be fitted with an M5 Pro, which is what we’ve got here, or an M5 Max that piles on the processing cores. There's no option for anything else, so you won’t be picking one up with a vanilla M5 in it. The M5 Pro and Max both use Apple’s Fusion Architecture to weld CPU dies together into one mega-chip, and the core counts make it look very much like the Pro is two normal M5s stuck together, with two cores disabled. It’s split into six Super cores and 12 Performance cores - the old Efficient cores are missing from this generation - and it can turbo up to 4.6 GHz.

Apple’s claim that the M5 offers the fastest single-core performance out there remains unbeaten in our tests. In the Geekbench 6 single-core test, the M5 Pro comes within a hair’s breadth of the M5 Max in a Mac Studio, a remarkable feat for a laptop. The Studio pulls ahead a bit in the multi-core test due to having more cores, but the Pro is right up there, actually beating the M5 Max from when we tested it in a MacBook Pro and leaving the M4 Pro behind.

The fastest non-Apple single-core result in our tests, the Asus Zenbook A16 powered by a Qualcomm Snapdragon X2 Elite, now rests in tenth place in the Geekbench charts, and you have to go to 13th place before you find an X86 CPU, which is in a desktop gaming PC. Apple is completely dominant in this arena at the moment, and it’s only when you switch to the GPU test results that the gaming PCs rise to the top and the Apple chips get some real competition.

In real-world applications, the M5 Pro isn’t as fast at doing tasks such as denoising raw files in Adobe Camera Raw as an Nvidia GPU, but it’s way faster than trying to do it using the integrated graphics on most Intel or AMD chips.

The battery life of all the M5 generation MacBooks has been exceptional, but the OLED screen has tipped the 16in Pro over the edge into a machine that can be relied on for two whole days of use. Testing how long it could play a video until exhausted took a lot longer than on most other laptops, with a final figure in excess of 21 hours. Push it really hard and that will come down, of course, but it’s a remarkable achievement nonetheless.

This is the thing that’s going to keep these first touchscreen Macs out of the hands of many creatives. The 16in model we’ve got here starts at £3,399 for one with 24GB of RAM and 1TB of storage, and just climbs and climbs as you add more stuff to it. You want 64GB of RAM and 4TB of storage? You can have it, but it’ll cost you £5,899 and you can go higher than that if you switch to the M5 Max. The touchscreen is a premium feature, and the same laptop with the old display starts from £2,999. The old M5 model no longer seems to be available, probably to make way for an M6, which is already there on the 14in version.

The fact this laptop even exists means Apple thinks there's a market for it, and if you’re a touchscreen laptop lover it may be the machine you’ve been dreaming of. There's no denying the expense, however, which means you’re really going to have to love it to consider it over other, better-value, choices.

Excellent CPU performance, GPU can't fight Nvidia.

Closer in performance to the MacBook Pro than to the MacBook Neo, the M5 edition of the MacBook Air is the bargain in Apple's range.

The frist generation of Nvidia's RTX Spark hardware looks promising for creatives. This 14-incher from Asus is powerful, well built, beautifully thin and light, and will last all day on a charge.

A mighty gaming laptop with a powerful GPU can make a good buy for a video editor or designer who likes to work on complex documents. This one from HP is slightly more subtly designed than many, and though its battery life is short it can surely speed up your workflows.
