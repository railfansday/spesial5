Latinx Art’s New Visionaries

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Arlene Dávila on the rallying cry of contemporary Latinx artists, Stephanie Wambugu on Mary Lovelace O’Neal, and Martin Puryear’s quiet revolution.

Optimism is a rare sight in this day and age — which is why scholar Arlene Dávila’s unabashed excitement about Latinx art right now is so invigorating. These artists draw from vernacular traditions, community histories, family memories, and more to reframe the narrative, despite facing a barrage of attacks in recent years. “The work of these new visionaries is a rallying cry,” she writes in an opinion piece today. “Now is the time to write, create, and make art boldly — together.”

Read on for a pulse on the rest of the art world, from novelist Stephanie Wambugu on Mary Lovelace O’Neal’s first posthumous exhibition, to Martin Puryear’s quietly revolutionary sculptures, to a new film reevaluating Frida Kahlo’s legacy and commodification. And it wouldn’t be the art world without some auction drama. Today, heirs of the Jewish publisher Samuel Fischer call on Sotheby’s to withdraw a Van Gogh painting it valued at $180 million, alleging that the work was sold under duress during World War II. Meanwhile, at Christie’s, a Willem de Kooning painting that entered the Whitney’s collection just a few years ago is headed under the hammer as Marquee Week kicks off in NYC.

Latinx communities are facing one of the most aggressive series of attacks in recent history, with many families enduring the pain of separation, persecution, and dehumanization. But if you’ve been paying attention to Latinx arts and culture, you’ll see that as we are being told to hide and disappear, artists are stepping into newfound confidence, showcasing the full value and complexity of our identities. | Arlene Dávila
