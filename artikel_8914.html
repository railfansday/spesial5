I Rode My Bike in a Climate Chamber That Can Recreate Almost Any Weather on the Planet—Here’s My Biggest Takeaway

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Inside Italy’s Terra X Cube, a Tour de France simulation became a real-world heat test.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

I looked into the giant chamber filled with strategically placed bike trainers and couldn’t help but question what I signed up for. The chamber we were walking into is called the Terra X Cube located in Bolzano, Italy, and is designed to replicate conditions from anywhere on the planet. The chamber can recreate a snowstorm on Mount Everest or a heat wave in the Sahara.

Variables like temperature, humidity, wind, precipitation, and altitude can all be controlled from a small observation room just one door over. I knew immediately when I crossed into the threshold of the chamber and the heat hit me in the face that this would be a rough trainer ride.

I was in Bolzano, Italy for an opportunity to participate in this testing in the Terra X Cube as well as tour the apparel brand Q36.5’s headquarters based in Bolzano. The apparel from Q36.5 quickly became my top choice for cycling apparel after getting the chance to test some pieces from their kit for the last few months. Our hot and humid summer in the Northeast gave a perfect testing ground for the Q36.5 Dottore jersey and bibs—and they shined. When a chance to learn more about this brand by visiting their Italian headquarters came up, I jumped on a plane—the opportunity to experience riding in a climate chamber was a special bonus.

Here I’m going to talk about the unique experience of riding in a climate chamber with the heat turned up and my biggest takeaway that you can apply to your trainer riding this season.

The test course and climate were designed to replicate the last section of Stage 2 from the 2026 men’s Tour de France ending in Barcelona. We rode with a custom workout in Zwift and our data was being tracked across the board: SRM cranks to track power, heart rate data to validate fatigue, and CORE sensors to track our heat strain throughout the workout. The room was set to a balmy 97 degrees Fahrenheit.

There was even a tester from Q36.5 in the room with us with a custom jersey. The jersey he wore was effectively two jerseys cut in half at the zipper and straight down the back. He had multiple CORE heat sensors attached around his body to track which jersey design and material was more effective at thermoregulation. One of those jersey designs will end up in a future product.

The ride was supposed to last for about 45 minutes, but our group was the first of the day and ran into some technical difficulties getting the trainers set up. In one sense this was good because we were able to have an extended warmup on the trainers, but the reality meant we spent an extra 45 minutes warming up in the already heated up chamber.

Thankfully, over the last few months, I’ve been doing heat training protocols with my CORE training sensor. I dove straight in with six weeks of active heat training, then I pivoted to strictly passive heat training with my home sauna. While I was absolutely heating up I was relatively comfortable rolling into the ride.

Once we got through our extended warmup we began the proper ride. Effectively we were shooting for an over-under workout that emulated the end of stage two at this year’s Tour de France. I had my CORE temperature data up on the screen and saw that before we were starting the ride my internal temperature was at about 99 degrees. The giant fans at the front of the room would change power to replicate the airflow during climbs versus descents, but at those temperatures it felt like sitting in front of a massive hair dryer.

As we began the official warmup my temperature steadily climbed—according to my CORE sensor I peaked just over 102 degrees. In the middle of the test I looked around the room and everyone looked like they were putting out high effort, even when we were supposed to be sitting in our recovery phase. The heat was taking its toll and our views did not inspire a boost in energy. Staring at the metallic walls while pedaling on our hamster wheel bikes did not give inspiration for an extra push for our final sprint. But as we finished up our not-so-cool cooldown I couldn’t help but think I would do it again.

After our group left the chamber the second group queued up for their turn on the bikes. The technical difficulties were overcome with our group, so they were able to jump straight into the ride after a five-minute warmup. That night we all regrouped to go through the data and we were able to see our data versus the data from some of the professionals in the stage we recreated. My biggest takeaway from the comparison to the professionals was their core temperature data. The professionals we were comparing against showed very little heat strain compared to even the fittest in our group. It’s worth noting the professionals had also already ridden over 60 miles before our route even started—those professionals were extremely heat adapted.

My biggest personal takeaway was how I could recreate this at home. When I talked to someone else in my group we discussed the similarities to this experience and heat training at home. When I do an active heat session at home I layer up in three layers top and bottom, a painter’s suit with the shins and feet cut off (so it doesn’t get caught in the cranks), and a hat or beanie for good measure. My active heat session numbers at home are surprisingly similar to this experience: typically a 45-minute warmup gets me into zone three of the heat strain index in the CORE app and then I spend about 45 minutes in this zone. This shows you don’t need a massive climate chamber to get the training benefits of heat training. Some warm layers, a painter’s suit, a CORE sensor, and a bike trainer can give you the benefits you need to develop this component of training.
