The lower price per ounce on a bulk pack saves you money only on the part your household uses before it goes bad. Cooking oil and nuts turn rancid, spices go stale, and sunscreen and medicine expire, so buy those in a size you’ll finish in time, or split the big pack with a friend.

The unit price on a shelf tag assumes you’ll use every ounce in the package. The tag doesn’t account for oil that turns rancid at the bottom of the tin or sunscreen that expires in the closet.

Say a 3-liter tin of extra virgin olive oil costs $30 and a 1-liter bottle of the same oil costs $15, and the last half of the tin goes rancid before you reach it. You paid $30 for the 1.5 liters you used, or $20 a liter, against $15 a liter for bottles you finish.

That tin was a third cheaper per liter, so it stopped saving money once more than a third of it went to waste. Any bulk buy works the same way. A pack that’s 20% cheaper per ounce stops saving money once more than a fifth of it gets thrown out.

Every one of the 12 buys below can go bad before a household finishes the warehouse club size, and each comes with a smaller size to buy or a way to make it last. Storage times for food come from the USDA’s FoodKeeper app unless an item names another source, and the app lists how long hundreds of other foods keep in the pantry, fridge, and freezer.

A multipack or a big tin can still pay off if you split it with a friend or neighbor. Pay half each and divide it the day you buy it, so each household takes home only what it will use up in time.
