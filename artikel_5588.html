An Insurer Paid Me $6.73 to Save a Teen's Life

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

— The No Surprises Act allows me to keep saving others

In October 2023, a 19-year-old came into the emergency department with a perforated appendix. By the time I got him to the operating room, his abdomen was full of pus. A surgery that normally takes me 15 minutes took an hour and a half due to the amount of inflammation distorting his anatomy. I rounded on him in the hospital every day for a week until he was stable for discharge, then followed him for another 2 months until he had fully recovered.

For that surgery, the week of inpatient care, and 2 months of post-op follow-up, the insurance company sent me a check for $6.73.

This wasn't a clerical error -- it was the contracted rate the insurance company had set for the procedure. Laparoscopic appendectomy, like most operations, falls under a 90-day global period, which meant I couldn't bill separately for any care delivered in the 90 days after surgery. As far as the insurer was concerned, $6.73 was full and final payment for treating a teenager with sepsis from a ruptured appendix.

This is precisely this kind of payment that makes the federal No Surprises Act's (NSA) independent arbitration process so important to physicians like me. The law gives us a way to challenge inadequate insurer payments through a neutral process, helping independent physicians keep our practices open so we can continue caring for patients and saving lives.

I opened my own practice in 2023 after several years in academic medicine, where I had learned exactly how little say a physician can have over her own patients, her own schedule, and her own worth. I wanted to make medical decisions based on what my patients needed, not on what a hospital system's spreadsheet required. I did not expect that the fight for that independence would move from the hospital administration to the insurance companies.

When I first opened my doors, I kept a part-time position at a county hospital to make ends meet while I built a patient base. When that wasn't enough, I added emergency general surgery call at another hospital for $40 an hour. For about 6 months I was working four jobs. My savings dwindled. I started drawing from my 401k. As a single mother and the sole provider for my two daughters, I was doing the math every month to make sure I could pay my staff, office rent, medical malpractice insurance, and my mortgage. For 2 years I did not pay myself, and I wondered whether staying in medicine was still something I could afford to do.

Lately, I find myself wondering whether I can keep practicing independently, because that $6.73 check was not an outlier; it is part of the pattern. A few months after the teen with the perforated appendix, another patient came in with severe anemia and a bowel obstruction due to a large colon tumor. I performed a robotic colectomy to remove the cancer, relieve the blockage, and stop the bleeding. The insurer denied the claim, stating the operation was "not medically necessary."

This is where the NSA arbitration process became critical to my practice. When an insurer and a physician can't agree on payment, each side submits an offer to a neutral arbitrator, who picks one. It isn't a settlement. It's one of the only checks that exists on an insurer's ability to set whatever price it wants for care the patient has already received.

Physicians win these disputes more than 80% of the time, and some critics have pointed to that number as evidence the system is broken. It isn't. It's evidence of how low insurers' offers actually are. An analysis of nearly 160,000 arbitration cases found insurers offered $0 to the physician in more than 20,000 cases. In the fourth quarter of 2024, roughly a quarter of all disputes were resolved by default, seemingly because the insurance company provided incomplete information or failed to make an offer at all. When one side offers nothing, or next to nothing, an arbitrator does not need to search hard for the fair number.

I did not go into medicine and open a solo practice in a saturated Houston market because I thought I could get rich. Sixteen years of education and training to be paid $6.73 for emergency surgery is a terrible way to build wealth, it turns out. I built my practice so that I could provide accessible surgical care while maintaining autonomy and agency over my life and professional choices. Every solo and small-practice physician I know who now uses this process was doing the same math I was doing at my kitchen table over the last 3 years, wondering if it was truly possible to practice medicine independently, outside of large institutions and private-equity-owned medical practices.

Arbitration is one of the only tools we have that puts a solo surgeon and a multibillion-dollar insurer on anything resembling equal footing.

The numbers show what happens when that tool disappears or is weakened by suggestions that doctors are the ones driving up costs. The share of physicians in independent practice fell from 37.8% in 2019 to 22.4% in 2024, with more than 75% of physicians now employed by hospitals or corporate entities. Every one of those doctors who left independent practice took something with them: continuity with patients they'd known for years and the ability to make a clinical decision without checking it against a corporate metric.

No physician is asking for sympathy. We are asking that the next time someone suggests that physicians are "gaming" the NSA, she instead asks why an insurer's opening offer for a week of care after a ruptured appendix was $6.73, and why it took a federal arbitrator and an 8-month process to make that number make sense.

The law was written to protect patients from surprise bills. What's often left out is that it also protects physicians who are trying to practice independently, because we are not interested in 5-minute visits and being told to double-book patients. Take this protection away and let end-stage corporatization and consolidation of healthcare finish the job, and we will witness the end of the independent practice of medicine in America.
