In Viral Stunt, Hundreds Try and Fail to Move Anish Kapoor’s ‘Bean’ Seven Inches to the Left, To Help the Man Supposedly Trapped Inside

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

“We need to move the bean 7 inches to the left. First push @12:47 pm 11/8/2026.”

So read a cryptic flyer that appeared in Chicago in recent days, referring to one of the world’s most popular pieces of public art. Hundreds heeded the call, gathering on a sunny Sunday in a benign flash mob. In the end, the crowd was no match for a 110-ton stainless steel sculpture.

The subject of the viral campaign was, of course, British Indian artist Anish Kapoor’s silvery sculpture Cloud Gate (2006), which stands in downtown Chicago’s Millennium Park. Inspired by liquid mercury, the 66-foot-long, 33-foot-high public artwork is nicknamed the Bean in reference to its shape, which resembles the kidney bean. A 12-foot-high walkway underneath the sculpture forms the titular “gate,” where countless visitors have marveled at their reflections and posted selfies. You might ask: What? Why? Well, the IRL attempt to shift the sculpture results from a viral rumor that has long circulated online, which says that a man is trapped inside. The event’s Partiful page, created by a mysterious “gs,” says that “new findings suggest that there might be [a] latch door under the right side of the bean. If we move the bean about 7 inches to the left we might be able to access that door and give the man inside the bean resources.” The sculpture did not move; no latch door was discovered; no supplies were delivered.

According to the authorities, there is not a man trapped inside the Bean. Alderman Brendan Reilly of the Windy City’s 42nd ward, which includes the site of the sculpture, told Book Club Chicago that his office was inundated with calls about it at one point last year. “I am happy to confirm that a man has not been trapped inside ‘Cloud Gate’ (a.k.a ‘the Bean’) for the past 21 years,” Reilly wrote in a statement.

Kapoor did not immediately respond to ARTnews’s request for comment, sent to his gallery, Lisson.

The sculpture has been exploited for stunts of a different kind in the past. Border Patrol agents used it as a background for a photo shoot amid the agency’s aggressive crackdown in the city a year ago. “I am deeply horrified and saddened that US Border Patrol has chosen to rally in front of Cloud Gate for their self-congratulatory photo-opp,” Kapoor told ARTnews in an email at the time. “In my view they are no different to SS Nazi troops, intimidating citizens and the people they deem to be immigrants.”

Cloud Gate is so popular that the Chinese city of Karamay erected one that looks suspiciously like it in 2015; it’s so beloved by tourists that the National Rife Association saw fit to include an unauthorized image of it in a promotional video, to Kapoor’s outrage; and it’s all over postcards, sweatshirts, and travel posters.

Even Kapoor himself has tried to capture lightning in a bottle and bring the magic to Gotham, creating an oblong sculpture in stainless steel for a residential building in Manhattan’s Tribeca neighborhood. Unveiled in 2023, the 19-foot-high, 40-ton version sits at the foot of the so-called Jenga tower, designed by Herzog & de Meuron (where Kapoor himself bought a $13.5 million apartment), which appears to be compressing the silvery blob to the point of rupture.

But the magic seems not to be portable, and the sculpture hasn’t drawn sustained attention. ARTnews’s Alex Greenberger wrote after its unveiling that “this sculpture is no Cloud Gate, and personally, I wouldn’t mind if the building above it made good on its promise and crushed the thing altogether.”
