Riot Sells More Than 20,000 Bitcoin as AI Data-Centre Contracts Reach $38B

TL;DR: Riot Platforms says it sold 20,264 BTC in the first nine months of 2026, raising about $1.5 billion primarily for data-centre development. Its latest quarterly release puts long-term contracted data-centre revenue at around $38 billion, underlining how far the miner’s business model is shifting.

Riot Platforms has made a striking choice with its Bitcoin holdings: sell a substantial amount of the asset it mines to finance the infrastructure it hopes will deliver future revenue. That strategy is clearer than ever in its third-quarter results, released November 4.

In its earnings filing, Riot said it sold 20,264 Bitcoin year to date for approximately $1.5 billion, mainly to fund data-centre construction. The company also announced a 15-year powered-land lease for 910 critical IT megawatts at its Corsicana campus, representing about $28 billion of contractual value over the initial term.

Riot recorded $181.4 million in third-quarter revenue, barely above the comparable quarter a year earlier. Bitcoin mining contributed $116 million, down from $160.8 million, even though production increased to 1,637 BTC from 1,406 BTC. The company attributes much of the decline in mining revenue to a lower average realised Bitcoin price during the quarter.

At the same time, engineering revenue rose to $47 million and data-centre revenue was $18.4 million. Those lines make the transition visible in the income statement, although the extraordinary long-term lease figures are not the same as cash already earned. They depend on delivery milestones, counterparties, the terms of each agreement and years of operations.

Riot says it has now leased 1,151 MW of critical IT capacity, with roughly $38 billion of long-term contracted revenue. Investors should distinguish that total contract value from current profit, and consider what future capital expenditure may be required to bring the facilities online.

The Bitcoin sales create resources that can fund land, power connections, construction and specialised infrastructure. They also reduce direct exposure to future Bitcoin appreciation. It is a deliberate decision to exchange part of a liquid treasury asset for a business with potentially steadier contracted revenues, but one that carries construction and customer concentration risks.

Riot said it ended September with $950.2 million of cash and Bitcoin, including 2,418 BTC. It reported no equity raised during 2026, a detail relevant to shareholders concerned about dilution as build-outs expand.

Mining economics are still important. Riot reported an average cost to mine Bitcoin of $39,265 excluding depreciation, influenced by power-curtailment credits. Such credits are valuable, but energy arrangements and pricing can change. Our earlier look at Bitcoin miners’ growing AI commitments explored the same industry-wide tension between holding coins and building something new.

The milestones that matter most are construction progress, delivery of contracted megawatts, tenant payments and conversion of headline lease values into recurring revenue. A business can accumulate an impressive contract pipeline while facing sizeable financing, permitting or execution obstacles.

Riot’s disclosure shows that Bitcoin mining companies are not necessarily behaving like Bitcoin holding companies. One of the industry’s biggest operators is increasingly using the mined asset as a funding source for a different kind of data-centre business. That may eventually diversify earnings, but the timing and profitability have to be proven in cash flow rather than headlines.
