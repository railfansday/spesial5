AI hasn't killed the joy of coding, but it has moved the craft up the stack, and that's where the problem-solving now lives.

Does coding still feel joyful, or does it just feel like output? That question's become a real conversation among developers over the past year. It's not two people trading opinions, but a pattern showing up across YouTube and Reddit, from veterans a decade or more into their careers to developers just breaking in.

Alex Kondov, a software engineer, gave that feeling one of its sharpest articulations: after twelve years of programming, he described AI as having compressed the part of the job he loved most—sitting with a hard problem, reading documentation and GitHub issues, trying and discarding solutions until one finally fit. In its place, he found himself reviewing AI-generated answers and reasoning through their second- and third-order effects.

It’s the same loss many engineers are describing. They didn’t fall in love with software to supervise an AI that writes code for them. They loved the craft of it: weighing two or three ways to solve a problem and picking the one that actually fits, writing something clean enough that it's still readable a year later, tracing strange behavior through a system, testing a theory, and finally understanding why something worked.

AI-assisted coding has changed where that craft lives. But the joy isn't gone—it's relocated to a level most of the current conversation hasn't caught up to yet: the system, not the line of code.

The engineers who find their way back to it are figuring out where the interesting problems are and working on them there.

AI coding tools are making engineers dramatically faster at implementation. Features get scaffolded in minutes. Refactors that once took hours now happen almost instantly.

And yet, teams still feel overloaded. According to recent research by Atlassian, 68% of developers now save more than 10 hours a week using AI tools. Still, half of them lose 10 or more hours a week right back to digging for context they don't have: tracking down who owns a service, re-explaining an incident to the third person who's picked it up, chasing a config change through a system no one fully documented.

In other words, coding itself is no longer the primary bottleneck.

This isn't the first time the ground has shifted under engineers. Machine code gave way to assembly, then high-level languages. Next came frameworks, cloud infrastructure, and sophisticated tooling that steadily removed low-level implementation work from developers' workflows.

When higher-level languages became mainstream, some feared that developers would stop reasoning deeply about computers. When frameworks automated more of the stack, others argued that engineering would become little more than assembling prebuilt systems.

AI-assisted coding has triggered a similar reaction. If machines can generate implementation details, what meaningful work is left for engineers to actually do?

However, abstraction has never eliminated engineering value. Each shift simply changed where that value lived. Engineers became responsible for broader systems, larger tradeoffs, and more operational complexity. The craft evolved alongside the systems being built.

Traditional programming creates a tight feedback loop: engineers explore the system directly, test assumptions in real time, wrestle with implementation details until the architecture finally clicks into place. For a lot of engineers, that loop was where the satisfaction actually lived—writing something rough, debugging it, fixing it with something slightly less rough, and getting the hard-won rush of finally understanding a problem. Remove that struggle, and the work starts to feel a little more automatic and a little more lifeless.

What makes AI-assisted coding feel emotionally different is the workflow itself. More of the interaction shifts from exploring a problem firsthand to evaluating, steering, and refining generated solutions. The workflow is faster, but it creates more distance from the process of arriving at an answer.

Maximilian Schwarzmüller, a developer and instructor with 3 million students, reached a similar conclusion in a video pointedly titled, "It was more fun before AI." Fundamentals and understanding the code still matter, but as he puts it, "the writing code part is definitely going away." Not gone entirely, but a fundamentally different job than it was even a few years ago.

That emotional shift is real. It doesn't mean engineering skill is disappearing. But the abstraction is arriving faster than many teams can absorb.

AI-generated code increases output, but also increases the amount of software that engineers are responsible for maintaining, debugging, and operating in production.

More generated code means more dependencies, more interactions between services, more edge cases, more regressions, and more operational complexity distributed across systems.

That burden doesn't land evenly. Senior engineers become the team's unofficial system model, the people who know which services misbehave under load, which deployment patterns have caused regressions before, which customer environments behave differently, and why. None of that lives in a document. It builds up over years of being the one who got paged.

AI-generated systems move faster than documentation and coordination can keep up with, and that’s where today’s AI copilots fall short. Copilots can help ship a change in minutes, but every fast, uninspected change is a little context debt that someone else may have to pay off later. They're built to help write the next thing, not to help whoever's on call in three weeks understand what shipped today.

For a growing number of individual contributors, that shift has an uncomfortable side effect: less time spent doing the engineering they signed up for, more time spent managing an AI agent that gets the interesting part of the job and leaves them the rest.

The biggest misconception in the current conversation is that problem-solving is disappearing. It isn't—it's moved.

For decades, engineering craft lived close to the implementation layer—mastering syntax, navigating tricky code paths, hand-stitching systems together. Today, the center of gravity has shifted upward. Isolated functions or repositories don't define modern systems; they're shaped by the interactions between services, infrastructure, deployment pipelines, and now the AI-generated code running through all of it.

Here's what that looks like in practice. A product team wants to let customers undo a bulk action after it's already synced to three downstream systems—something that sounds simple until it’s actually worked through. The obvious approach is a soft-delete flag, but that breaks two other features that assume "deleted" means "gone". The right answer turns out to be an event log the system can replay backward, something none of the existing services were built around.

That's the part no AI tool gets to on its own: sitting with the shape of the problem, turning over two or three approaches, understanding why the simple one breaks and the harder one holds. Once that design is real—once the shape of what’s being built and why is clear—handing the scaffolding to an AI agent isn't a loss. It's the same relationship a senior engineer already has with a junior one: “Here's the shape, here are the constraints, go build it, and I'll review it against the design I understand.”

That moment—seeing the real shape of a hard problem before anyone writes a line to solve it—is the rush Kondov described missing: the one that used to come from sitting with a problem long enough to actually understand it. ThePrimeagen, a developer with a large following among engineers, described something similar when responding to Kondov's reflections: not the typing itself, but the thinking that used to happen before and during it, which hasn't been showing up in prompt-and-review work the way it used to.

Engineers aren't doing less meaningful work, but the joy in problem-solving has moved somewhere harder to see. And right now, most people are stuck skipping straight to the prompt instead of sitting with the problem first.

Many engineers aren't frustrated by hard problems. They're frustrated by work that stands between them and the problems they actually care about solving: the hour spent reconstructing what changed before starting debugging, the back-and-forth over who owns an issue, re-diagnosing a failure that’s already been fixed this quarter.

Most AI tooling stops at the code layer, so it doesn’t reduce overhead. Code assistants are excellent at writing and refactoring code faster, inside the file already open. But the answer to why something's breaking in production usually isn't in that file.

What's missing isn't more help writing the next thing. It's something built to make sense of what's already running. That's not a permanent condition of the job. It's a tooling problem.

Shared production context removes that overhead, opening space for higher-value work on the architecture and systems thinking that's actually interesting, not ticket triage.

When engineering craft lives at the systems layer, shared production context is what makes that craft possible at scale.

When the signals from code, telemetry, incidents, runtime behavior, and deployment history are connected in one place, engineers spend less energy jumping between tools to reconstruct basic context. PlayerZero automates away the worst parts of engineering toil—production defects, firefighting, and debugging loops—by handling the heavy lifting of signal correlation and root‑cause tracing. Engineers investigating an incident don’t have to rebuild context from scratch every time something breaks. They can spend more energy on the parts of the hunt they love: reasoning about the system and testing better solutions, even if they’re writing fewer individual lines of code by hand.

For example, at Higher Logic, support teams depended on engineering for the context needed to investigate most customer-reported issues. After adopting PlayerZero, support gained shared visibility into production behavior and the codebase. Instead of escalating, they investigate issues, validate findings, and resolve customer-facing defects before they reach engineering queues. As a result, engineering reclaimed 250 hours in two months and reduced delivery time on eligible fixes by 60%.

Instead of being pulled into every unfamiliar ticket to “take a quick look,” senior engineers had stretches of time again to make changes they trusted to hold up under real‑world conditions. The numbers show reclaimed capacity; the day‑to‑day reality was that problem-solving started to feel like the job again, not AI supervision.

Skill is quietly being redefined. Being fast with a keyboard or fluent in a language's syntax matters less than it used to. What's replacing it is the ability to mentally hold a system's behavior, to look at a strange symptom and know, or find out quickly, which of a dozen moving parts is actually responsible.

That's not a smaller kind of expertise. It's the same instinct that made debugging satisfying in the first place, tracing a problem back to its real cause, just aimed at a bigger, more interconnected target.

The engineers who enjoy that kind of work are the ones who'll find the job more engaging under this shift, not less, once they're pointed at problems that size.

What many engineers are mourning right now is real, and it deserves to be taken seriously rather than argued away. But that feeling can make it seem like the meaningful part of the job is disappearing, when what's actually changing is where it lives.

Getting it back doesn’t require waiting for new tooling to show up in the stack. At the individual level, a few simple habits bring the thinking loop back into the job. For example, follow one incident all the way across, not just through one layer. When something breaks, don't stop once the immediate piece is fixed. Trace it upstream and downstream, what triggered it, what it touched, what would've caught it earlier. The muscle that the old debugging loop used to automatically build now has to be chosen.

Write the hypothesis before checking the logs, and guess what the dashboard will show and why. This is the fastest way to recreate the "thinking loop" AI-assisted work compresses. Being wrong and then finding out why is what made debugging satisfying in the first place.

Volunteer for the ambiguous ticket. An agent can handle scoped tickets with clear repro steps as long as a human is in the loop. The vague ticket with no clear repro—"it's slow sometimes," "customers are seeing something weird"—is where systems-level reasoning gets exercised.

Map how a feature actually behaves in production, not just how it reads in code. For an existing feature, spend twenty minutes tracing its real runtime path, what it touches, what depends on it, and what breaks if it's slow. For new ones, sketch the events, services, and edge cases first, then use AI to scaffold the code against that design. This builds the same kind of system intuition that used to come from writing every line by hand. The systems thinking and tradeoffs stay with the human, while AI handles the implementation.

Individual habits can bring an engineer’s joy back, but the right tooling makes it easier and faster at scale. What’s more difficult is making deep, systems‑level investigation the default for the entire team, even when under production pressure.

That's the harder problem AI-assisted coding hasn't solved on its own. It's also the subject of our companion piece, AI-Assisted Coding Is Great. It's Just Solving the Wrong Problem.
