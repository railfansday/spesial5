Yankees should cap a David Bednar deal at two years

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

The Yankees can live with paying David Bednar $23.15 million for one season. The mistake would be treating that number as the starting point for every year of a new contract.

Bednar has until 4 p.m. Eastern on Tuesday to take the one-year qualifying offer. An acceptance gives New York a closer without tying up future seasons. A rejection gives the Yankees a different decision: how much guaranteed money to put on the table to buy a second year.

I'd offer two years and $38 million if he turns it down. That's a suggested ceiling, not a reported offer. It guarantees Bednar $14.85 million more than the qualifying offer while bringing the average annual salary down to $19 million. If his market takes him beyond that, I wouldn't add a guaranteed third year simply to win the negotiation.

The one-year price buys certainty now. Bednar saved 35 games, posted a 2.47 ERA and pitched 69 1/3 innings in 2026. That last number matters. The Yankees wouldn't be paying for a pitcher they have to hide until three outs remain; they would be retaining someone who handled a career-high workload.

The second year buys more control but introduces another season of injury and performance risk. Atlanta already signed Evan Phillips for three years and $35 million. That's a useful check on overall dollars, not a direct price match. Phillips logged only 26 innings last season after missing time. Bednar can make a better case for a higher salary per year; that doesn't mean New York needs to match Atlanta's term.

Brian Cashman described the hole to MLB.com's Bryan Hoch: "Two of the three pitchers going into our eighth and ninth inning are gone." He was referring to Bednar and Paul Blackburn reaching free agency. Getting one back still leaves decisions to make about who handles the run of dangerous hitters an inning earlier. That's why I care more about the third guaranteed year than whether a two-year deal comes in at a tidy annual number.

The fallback if Bednar rejects the offer is to spend on two established late-inning arms and let the better fit close. That gives the Yankees another pitcher they can use when the ninth isn't the hardest inning, but it also leaves Aaron Boone without the proven closer he had last season. Neither a draft pick nor two contracts automatically makes up for losing him.

Nobody outside the negotiation knows what another club will guarantee, and a proposed two-year deal does nothing if Bednar wants a third year. If he takes the qualifying offer, New York should accept the expensive season and work on the rest of the bullpen. If he declines, the Yankees can pay him more total money without assuming the same pitcher will be there three years from now.
