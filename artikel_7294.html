This week’s Future of TV Briefing looks at the latest call-to-action for the CTV advertising industry to tackle its transparency problem.

You may have heard that connected TV advertising has a transparency problem. So what’s anyone gonna do about it?

Well, the Coalition for Innovative Media Measurement has some ideas. Having surveyed 50 programmatic marketers about CTV’s signals situation, the industry organization has come up with a couple of recommendations.

First, companies need to agree to a minimum viable set of trading signals. The study’s proposed list of baseline signals includes standard parameters like IP address (as questionable a signal as it is), video placement and supply chain object (or “schain”).

These are already pretty standard signals. According to FreeWheel, one of the CIMM study’s sponsors, 95% to 100% of U.S. CTV and set-top box bid requests through its platform in February 2026 contained IP address, video placement and schain. However, other proposed baseline signals were slightly less common. For example, auction type was missing from 5% to 20% of FreeWheel’s sampled bid requests but would be table stakes under CIMM’s proposed signal set.

This gets at the underlying issue. Ad tech intermediaries don’t always support the signals that buyers and sellers want to be passing back and forth. Per the CIMM study, only 8% of the surveyed programmatic marketers — 4 out of 50 — said the demand-side platforms they use “always” are able to handle the buyers’ required signals.

To be fair, 58% of buyers said their required signals are “often” able to be handled by DSPs. But “often” is not “always,” which is the problem.

“The biggest asks that I got were from buyers and sellers that we want to understand which signals are making it all the way through the path and are actually operationalized versus used for reporting or just unusable,” said Emily Palmer, an industry consultant and chair of CIMM’s programmatic working group, who was one of the study’s authors.

Agreeing to a set of baseline signals would be one way to address this, though that would mean companies actually adhering to that agreement.

FreeWheel already requires certain signals like IP address in order to validate traffic, said Alex Strickland, head of programmatic supply relationships at the Comcast-owned ad tech provider that manages demand-side and supply-side platforms. But asked if FreeWheel requires all of the signals listed in the proposed trading baseline set, he said “we don’t require them.”

OpenX, the study’s other sponsor, takes signals into account when selecting which publishers’ bid requests to send to which DSPs, said Danner Close, vp of operations at OpenX. “We constantly engage publishers with, ‘Hey, here’s what is missing from a high-fidelity standpoint that’s preventing your request from going further or from garnering greater bid density’,” he said.

Supporting the table-stakes signals is one thing. Knowing which signals — beyond the baseline requirements (or would-be requirements) — an ad tech firm supports is another. This is where the CIMM study’s other recommendation is meant to come in. Ad tech firms would be asked to publish capability statements declaring which signals they support.

“Buyers and sellers would love to have a centralized resource [containing these capability statements]. That would make a big difference. That sounds somewhat simple; it sounds like it would be a work-in-progress; it sounds like each platform would have a product person occasionally update it; it would have to be an ongoing effort to be accurate and useful,” said Palmer. “The devil’s in the detail.”

Asked if FreeWheel already offers a form of capability statement, “I don’t think we have anything outside of our internal documentation that our customers have access to on our hub,” Strickland said.

OpenX did not provide information on whether the company offers a form of capability statement as of this writing.

Both FreeWheel’s Strickland and an OpenX spokesperson said their respective companies are committed to participating in that centralized resource containing the capability statements, though. Eventually.

“We are all actively, with OpenX as well, working to build a more efficient and competitive marketplace where our premium video content can be recognized and monetized appropriately, and we are willing to do whatever it takes to make that happen,” said Strickland.

Maybe that work will start soon. CIMM is looking to organize a voluntary pilot program for companies to adopt the proposed baseline signals set and implement capability statements. When will that pilot start, though? TBD.
