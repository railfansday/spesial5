Opdivo, Jardiance and Xtandi: 3 drugs facing copies on different clocks

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Patent expiry dates rarely mark when sales fall. 3 blockbuster drugs, Opdivo, Jardiance and Xtandi, show how copies arrive

A patent lets a drugmaker sell a best-selling medicine without competition from cheaper copies, and for several of the industry's biggest products, that protection is running out at about the same time. The 20 largest biopharma companies stood to lose $180 billion in revenue to expiring patents through 2028, according to a 2024 EY estimate reported by CNBC. That covers only the biggest companies and only a few years, and it's still a number every major drugmaker has to plan around.

It matters because drugmakers use revenue from today's best-sellers to fund the research behind tomorrow's. So for investors, patients and the companies themselves, the real question is how fast that money disappears once protection ends.

Drugmakers aren't sitting still. They tweak their drugs and fight to defend their exclusivity, pushing the loss further out. But they can only delay it, not stop it. "While you can tack on some years and extend revenues, there's only so much time you can add," Mike Perrone, a biotech specialist at Baird, told CNBC in 2024. Those extra years buy time to launch new products, not a permanent escape.

The losses also don't arrive all at once. Copies can hit one country before another, and the type of drug affects how quickly rivals can win patients over. That's why a patent's expiration date is a poor guide to when a blockbuster's sales will actually fall.

The Wall Street Journal identified the top-selling drugs about to lose patent protection, from Astellas' Xtandi to Bristol Myers Squibb's cancer drug Opdivo. They don't share a single deadline. Each faces its own fight over how long sales hold up, in courts, before regulators, in pharmacies and country by country. For each one, the question is what decides how fast sales fall.

Bristol Myers Squibb will lose patent protection on its cancer immunotherapy Opdivo this decade, The Wall Street Journal reports, with the drug's key patent expiring in 2028, according to CNBC. A lot is riding on it: Opdivo brought in $8.25 billion in 2022. With that much revenue at risk so soon, the question isn't whether Bristol Myers loses Opdivo's sales, but how fast.

That depends on what kind of drug Opdivo is. It's a biologic, and copies of biologics, known as biosimilars, have been slow to steal business from the originals. AbbVie lost only a small share of the market for Humira after biosimilars launched in 2023, according to Bioepis figures reported by CNBC, and biosimilars overall were still slow to catch on in 2024, William Blair analyst Matt Phipps told CNBC. That's no forecast for Opdivo, but it suggests Bristol Myers is unlikely to lose most of the drug's revenue the year its patent expires.

A slower decline still ends with a much smaller drug, though. Opdivo could bring in $3.18 billion in 2032, according to a 2024 Leerink Partners estimate reported by CNBC, far less than its 2022 sales. Being a biologic buys Bristol Myers time, not protection.

Boehringer Ingelheim's diabetes drug Jardiance is one of the industry's biggest sellers headed toward the end of patent protection, according to The Wall Street Journal. The patent on its active ingredient, empagliflozin, runs until November 2029, according to The Pharma Letter. That gives Boehringer time to line up replacements, assuming rivals wait for the patent to expire before selling copies. In Russia, one hasn't.

Russian drugmaker Grotex has launched its own generic version of Jardiance, according to The Pharma Letter, and Boehringer says it infringes the patent. No ruling has been reported, so for now that's an accusation, not a proven violation. But the dispute was public by early September 2026, years before the patent runs out, which means Boehringer may already be losing Jardiance sales in one country.

Its other big drug, the blood thinner Eliquis, is under pressure too. Eliquis makes up 30% of the company's revenue, according to The Wall Street Journal, and it was among the first 10 drugs picked for Medicare price negotiations. The negotiated price took effect in January 2026, two years before Opdivo's key patent expires.

To fill the gap, Bristol Myers is buying new products, including through its acquisition of Karuna Therapeutics. JPMorgan analyst Chris Schott told CNBC in 2024 that he expected those products to "ultimately position the company for growth." But that growth would come only after much of Opdivo's revenue is already gone.

Bristol Myers has a few years to prove its new drugs can replace an old one that copies will slowly eat away.

Boehringer is treating the copy as a fair-competition issue as well as a patent fight. It has brought in Russia's antitrust regulator, the Federal Anti-Monopoly Service, giving it a second way to press its case.

Jardiance faces pressure in the U.S., too. Like Eliquis, it was one of the first 10 drugs picked for Medicare price negotiations, and its negotiated price took effect in January 2026. Boehringer is defending Jardiance one country at a time, and it can't count on untouched sales until the patent expires in 2029.

For most blockbusters on this list, losing patent protection is still a future worry. For Astellas' prostate cancer drug Xtandi, it's already started. Xtandi's compound patent in South Korea expired on June 27, 2026, according to Dailypharm, and generic copies hit the market the next day. That date applies only to South Korea, so it isn't the moment Astellas loses exclusivity everywhere. But it offers an early look at how generic makers move in and how a brand fights back.

On paper, Astellas had a backup. A second patent, covering how the drug is formulated, runs until September 2033. But Korean drugmakers including GL Pharma, Hanmi, Chong Kun Dang, JW Pharmaceutical and Alvogen Korea won rulings that let their versions get around it. The backup bought Astellas no extra time, a reminder that the secondary patents a brand counts on can be cleared away while its main patent is still in force.

With no extra years to work with, Astellas changed the product instead. It got new Xtandi tablets listed for reimbursement in April, ahead of the generics, which launched only as capsules. It also priced the stronger 80mg tablet so a month of treatment costs about 25% less than the same dose in 40mg capsules, which means fewer pills to swallow and a smaller bill.

That edge may not last. Generic makers have already applied for approval of their own tablet versions.

The question now is whether a more convenient pill and a lower monthly cost can keep Korean patients on the brand once cheaper copies come in every form.
