‘Wolf of Wall Street’ Jordan Belfort on his AI salespeople who are better than humans: ‘What if you have a bad apple that says stuff?’

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Just months after Jordan Belfort’s court-ordered restitution obligation ended, leaving his victims far short of the $110 million he was ordered to pay them, “The Wolf of Wall Street” is selling over the phone again, this time with AI bots trained on his sales techniques.

Belfort’s new AI sales-voice startup, Tone, has brought in about $1 million in revenue so far, and he’s aiming for between $20 million and $30 million in recurring revenue within the next year.

Belfort said Tone’s technology is more reliable than humans because companies can keep a firm grip on what it says. This contrasts with salespeople who can stray from approved scripts and could potentially cross a line with customers.

“What if you have a bad apple that says stuff—maybe inadvertently even, right?” he said.

Tone launched publicly on Nov. 16 with about $10 million raised, and Belfort said its API and a content creation platform goes live in about a week. It arrives as big companies move to automate customer calls—Verizon CEO Dan Schulman told Fortune this month the telecom giant is preparing to automate much of its routine customer service work—and two years after the FCC put AI-generated voices under federal robocall rules. A study by international cloud voice software company AVOXI found in September that just over half of organizations are already using AI voice agents, and a majority are planning to adopt them over the next two years.

Tone’s offerings include 16 different male and female artificial voices ranging from higher to lower-pitched that are trained on Belfort’s tonal sales techniques. Unlike other AI voices, he said they adjust their intonation and volume, among other factors, to try to better influence listeners.

This includes phrasing declarative statements as questions to show the listener you’re engaged, or speaking in a low voice to convey scarcity and the importance of the information, Belfort told Fortune. Those subtle vocal techniques, he argued, can make the difference between someone listening to a sales pitch or hanging up and will help the company compete against an increasingly crowded field of voice AI startups. The tech is not meant to replace salespeople but merely to expand the number of contacts they can reach, he added.

Of the $10 million the company has raised (TechCrunch first reported the figure), 70% of it came from friends and family and the rest from investors, he estimated.

Yet Belfort also noted that Tone still has limitations, especially when it comes to navigating complicated conversations.

To make conversations sound natural, Tone’s models have to respond within about 500 to 600 milliseconds. But that leaves only about 100 milliseconds for the AI to process a question and formulate an answer, which means the company is limited in the complexity of the models it can use to get the fastest response.

As AI models improve and computer chips become faster, however, Belfort believes that trade-off will become less significant.

“I would think in the next six months, at the most a year, that gap will be eliminated,” he said.

Belfort pleaded guilty and was later sentenced in 2003 for fraud and money laundering, spending 22 months in prison before being released in 2006. Since then, he has focused on motivational speaking and consulting. His memoir, “The Wolf of Wall Street,” also inspired the 2013 film by the same name starring Leonardo DiCaprio.

Belfort was ordered by a federal judge in 2003 to pay $110 million in restitution, yet his court-ordered restitution obligation ended earlier this year, with TMZ reporting that his victims ultimately received a much lower amount than expected. As of 2018, Belfort had paid about $13 million of the total sum, according to court documents.

The prospect of AI sales bots trained on the techniques of someone convicted of fraud raises ethical questions. Belfort’s cofounder, the venture capitalist Shervin Pishevar, has also faced sexual misconduct allegations, which he denies.

There are also regulatory concerns. The Federal Communications Commission ruled in 2024 that AI-generated voices fall under existing restrictions on robocalls under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, a federal law aimed at protecting consumers from unwanted telemarketing calls. The rules include exemptions for some noncommercial polling and survey calls to residential landlines.

Belfort previously told TechCrunch that Tone makes outbound sales calls only to opted-in leads or existing customers. The company always recommends disclosing when customers are speaking to AI, he told Fortune.

“I think it’s important that it should be disclosed, and I think the worst thing you’d want to break trust with someone—if they do at some point realize it, that would be, I think, a really bad thing,” he said.

For now, Tone offers two languages, English and Spanish, but Belfort said it plans to add two to four more languages each month. In the future, he said Tone will develop AI-powered avatars to add virutal body language and facial expressions to its portfolio of persuasion tools during video calls.

Despite his prior conviction, Belfort said he has spent the last two decades working to rebuild his reputation and is now working 14 hour days to build Tone.

“I think that the company and the technology should speak for itself, and I think people need to make their own decision,” he said.

Role: Reporter Marco Quiroz-Gutierrez is a reporter for Fortune covering general business news.
