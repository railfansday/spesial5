Pending, posted or failed? How to read the status of your payment

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Pending vs posted vs failed payments explained, covering ACH timing, wire cutoffs, card holds and app labels that show whether a bank transfer went through

A bank transfer can leave one account and take hours or days to appear in another. The money has not vanished. It is usually sitting in an intermediate stage. The label your bank or app attaches to it tells you which stage that is.

Every electronic payment passes through a sequence. It is requested, recorded, processed and settled into an account. Banks compress that sequence into a few words on a transaction screen, and those words carry specific meanings. A pending item has been recorded but not finished. A posted item is final and part of the account's official record. A failed or declined payment never moved any money. A returned payment is a separate case: it can look complete before it is sent back.

The difference has practical consequences. It affects whether you can spend money that appears in your account and whether a rent payment has reached the landlord. It can also show whether a charge that looks wrong may still change before it posts. The status shapes the next step, too. A pending transfer usually needs more time. A failed one needs fixing before a retry.

Timing depends on the payment rail behind the transfer. In the U.S., ACH transfers are processed in batches through the Automated Clearing House network. That network is operated by the Federal Reserve and The Clearing House. Standard ACH transfers are processed on business days, which exclude weekends and federal holidays. Wire transfers are sent individually and are generally treated as final once sent. Card purchases go through an authorization step before the merchant submits the charge for settlement. That is why the amount on a pending card transaction can differ from the final one. Peer-to-peer apps such as Zelle, Venmo and Cash App use their own status labels and their own rules on reversals.

This guide to pending vs posted vs failed payments breaks the topic into 10 parts. The first three slides explain what each status means. The next five cover how card, ACH, wire and app payments behave, including temporary holds that can tie up part of a balance. The final two explain why transfers fail or get returned and how to confirm whether a payment went through. They also cover what information to have ready when contacting the bank about a stuck transfer.

A pending payment is a transaction your bank has recorded but not yet finished processing. The money may already be set aside from your available balance, but the item is not final.

That unfinished state is why a pending entry looks different from the rest of the transaction history. Many banking apps and websites list pending items in their own section, apart from completed activity. Others mark each entry with a "pending" label. Either way, the bank is signaling that the payment has started but has not reached the end of the process.

The clearest effect of a pending item shows up in the account's two balances. The available balance is the amount the bank currently lets you spend or withdraw. The current balance, also called the ledger balance, reflects only transactions that have fully completed. While a payment is pending, the bank may reduce the available balance right away. The ledger balance can stay where it was until the item finishes.

This gap between the two numbers is the main reason people think a transfer has disappeared. Money leaves the available balance, yet nothing matching it appears in the ledger balance or on the statement. The funds are usually in a holding position while the payment works its way through.

A pending status applies in both directions. An outgoing payment can be pending on the sender's account, and an incoming deposit can be pending on the receiver's account. In each case, the bank has acknowledged the item without treating it as complete.

Because a pending entry is provisional, its details are not locked in. The amount shown can differ from the amount that eventually settles. The entry can also drop off without ever completing — for example, if a merchant or sender cancels the payment before it is finalized. Until the status changes, the pending amount works as a placeholder rather than a final record of what moved.

A posted transaction is one the bank has finished processing. It has been added to the account's ledger balance, sometimes called the current balance. Once an item posts, its amount no longer shifts, and it will not drop off the account on its own.

Posting marks the point at which a payment stops being provisional. For a card purchase, the merchant's charge has settled. For a deposit, the incoming money has been credited to the account record. For an outgoing transfer, the debit has been entered on the bank's books.

Posted items usually carry a posting date. That date can differ from the day the purchase or transfer was made. A purchase made on one day may post a day or more later. The gap reflects how long the payment took to finish processing. It does not mean the purchase itself was delayed.

The statement is the formal record of an account. Posted items are what a monthly statement lists. They are also what reconciliation, budgeting software and tax records rely on. Pending items generally do not appear there.

The word final has a narrow meaning in this context. It means the bank has completed its processing and the entry is part of the account history. It does not mean the account holder has run out of options. A posted credit card charge can still be disputed as a billing error under the Fair Credit Billing Act. Errors in electronic transfers to or from a bank account fall under the Electronic Fund Transfer Act and its implementing rule, Regulation E. Each law sets its own dispute process. The original entry typically stays on the record, and a correction shows up as its own new line.

A posted deposit is not always fully spendable right away. Banks can hold some deposits, particularly checks, under the Federal Reserve's Regulation CC. That hold governs when the money can be used. It does not change whether the deposit has been recorded.

On the sending side, a posted debit is the clearest sign the bank completed its part of a transfer. It does not by itself show that the receiving bank has credited the recipient. That step happens on the other institution's books.

A failed or declined payment never completes. No money leaves your account, and none reaches the recipient. A returned payment is different. It can look finished before the money comes back.

The labels "failed" and "declined" describe a payment that stopped before any funds changed hands. A card declined at a checkout counter is a common example. The purchase is refused on the spot, and the balance stays where it was. A bank transfer or app payment can end the same way. Instead of a refusal at a register, it shows up as failed or declined in the transaction history.

With nothing moved, a failed payment usually leaves little trace on the account. There may be no entry at all, or a line showing the attempt with a status label rather than a deduction. The bill, rent or invoice the payment was meant to cover remains unpaid until a new payment goes through.

A returned payment leaves a longer trail. The original entry appears first, and it may even post. A second, offsetting entry appears later, when the money is sent back. Someone who checked the account only once could believe the transfer succeeded. A later look at the account shows the reversal.

A return usually carries a reason code. The code is a short label that identifies why the payment came back. In the U.S. ACH system, return reason codes start with the letter R followed by two digits. The code typically sits in the transaction details or in a message from the bank, not on the main account summary.

Timing is another way to tell the outcomes apart. A decline or failure usually surfaces at the moment of the attempt or shortly after. A return arrives after the payment has already started moving. That gap means it can appear after the sender has stopped watching.

The difference matters on the receiving end, too. A failed payment never shows up for the recipient. A returned payment can appear in the recipient's account and then be taken back out.

A card payment goes through authorization first and settlement second. The bank approves the purchase at checkout, and the merchant submits the charge for final payment later. The amount approved at the first step is not always the amount that posts at the second.

Authorization is the quick check at the register or online checkout page. The payment terminal sends a request through the card network, such as Visa $V or Mastercard $MA, to the bank that issued the card. That bank confirms the card is valid and that the account has enough money or available credit. It then approves or declines the request.

An approval reserves the amount, but it does not complete the payment. No money has reached the merchant at this point. The transaction exists only as a commitment that the funds are there.

Settlement is the second step. The merchant gathers its approved transactions and submits them for payment, often as a group rather than one by one. The issuing bank then sends the money to the merchant's bank through the card network. Once that happens, the item on the cardholder's account changes from pending to posted.

The gap between those two steps is where amounts can shift. A sit-down restaurant is the clearest example. The server runs the card for the bill before the diner writes in a tip. The authorization covers only the pre-tip total. When the restaurant submits the charge, the tip is included. The posted amount then comes in higher than the pending one.

Online orders can push the two steps further apart. A retailer may authorize the full order at checkout and settle the charge only when the goods ship. If an item sells out, the settled amount can be smaller. If items ship separately, the charge can arrive as more than one posted line.

Purchases made in a foreign currency can change as well. The conversion into dollars is applied when the charge is processed for settlement. If the exchange rate moves between authorization and settlement, the posted figure can differ slightly from the pending one.

A merchant that never submits an approved charge never collects on it. In that case the authorization does not become a payment. It drops off the account after a period set by the card issuer. This often happens when an order is canceled before it ships.

A pending charge that does not match the receipt is not, on its own, a billing error. The posted amount is the one to check. If the posted charge differs from the final receipt, the card issuer's dispute process is the route for challenging it.

A standard ACH transfer does not move instantly. It travels in batches over the Automated Clearing House network and advances only on business days, which leave out weekends and federal holidays.

The ACH network is the U.S. system behind many routine bank-to-bank payments. Paychecks sent by direct deposit commonly run on it. So do automatic bill payments and transfers between your own accounts at two different banks. Two operators process the traffic. The Federal Reserve runs one. The Clearing House, a company owned by large commercial banks, runs the other.

Batch processing explains much of the wait. Your bank does not send each payment on its own the moment you tap the button. It gathers payments together and submits them as a group. The network sorts them and passes them along to the receiving banks. Those banks then apply them to customer accounts. Each handoff happens on a set schedule rather than continuously.

ACH payments flow in both directions. An ACH credit pushes money from the sender to the recipient, as an employer does on payday. An ACH debit pulls money from an account with the account holder's authorization, as a utility or lender does with autopay. Both types follow the same business-day calendar.

That calendar decides when a transfer actually moves. A transfer started on a Saturday does not begin moving until the next business day. When a federal holiday falls on a Monday, that weekend transfer waits until Tuesday. Banks also set their own daily cutoff times for ACH requests. A request submitted after the cutoff is handled as part of the next business day's work.

This is why a payment can sit in pending status through a long weekend without anything going wrong. The bank has accepted the transfer, but the network has not processed it yet.

Same Day ACH is a faster option within the same network. It lets eligible payments settle on the business day they are submitted instead of a later one. It still follows business-day rules. A Same Day ACH request made on a Sunday does not settle that Sunday. Whether you can choose it, and on what terms, depends on the bank or service handling the transfer.

For a payment that has to arrive by a specific date, business days matter more than calendar days. A loan installment or tuition bill due the day after a holiday weekend has fewer processing days available than the same payment due midweek. Starting the transfer earlier leaves room for the batches to clear before the deadline.

A wire transfer submitted before your bank's daily cutoff time usually goes out that business day. Once it is sent, it is generally treated as final.

Each bank sets its own wire cutoff time. No single nationwide deadline applies to every institution. The cutoff can also differ between domestic and international wires at the same bank. It can differ between a request made at a branch and one made online, too.

A wire requested after the cutoff typically waits until the next business day to go out. Weekends and federal holidays push a late request forward in the same way. A wire started on a Friday evening may not move until the following week.

For U.S. domestic wires, the main system is Fedwire, run by the Federal Reserve. Fedwire handles each payment individually and settles it in real time. That one-at-a-time handling is why a domestic wire can reach the recipient's bank on the same day it is sent.

International wires usually pass through more steps. Banks commonly exchange the payment instructions over the SWIFT messaging network. The money may also move through one or more correspondent banks before it reaches the recipient. Each extra stop can add time, and each bank along the way follows its own processing schedule.

Finality is the trade-off for that speed. A sending bank can ask the receiving bank to send a wire back. Whether the money returns usually depends on the receiving bank and the recipient cooperating, or on the funds still sitting in the account. There is no simple cancel button once a wire has moved.

That finality makes wires a frequent target for fraud. A common scheme involves criminals impersonating a title company, a vendor or a relative and sending new account instructions by email or text. Calling the recipient at a phone number already on file, rather than one in the message, is a direct way to check those instructions before sending.

U.S. consumers sending money abroad have one narrow protection. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau rules for remittance transfers generally let a sender cancel within 30 minutes of paying. That window applies only if the recipient has not already picked up or received the funds.

Before a wire goes out, check the recipient's full name, account number and the bank's routing number or SWIFT code. Asking the bank for its cutoff time on the specific type of wire being sent avoids an unexpected one-day delay.

A temporary card hold is money a merchant reserves on your card before it knows the final charge. The hold lowers the funds you can spend right away. It is a placeholder rather than the payment itself, and it is replaced or released once the real charge goes through.

Fuel pumps show how this works. When you swipe or tap at the pump, the station does not yet know how much fuel you will buy. It first asks the card issuer to approve a preset amount, which is called a pre-authorization. That amount reflects the station's own setting, not your actual purchase.

After you finish filling the tank, the station submits the true total for settlement. The final posted charge reflects the fuel you pumped. The pre-authorization should then fall away rather than add to that charge.

During that gap, the account can look confusing. The hold may show as a pending item for one amount, while the real charge appears for a different amount. To someone scanning the transaction list, that can look like a double charge. In most cases it is one purchase shown in two stages.

The effect depends on the type of card. On a debit card, a hold reduces the cash available in the linked checking account. On a credit card, it reduces available credit instead. With a debit card, the held amount is the account holder's own cash. That money cannot cover other bills while the hold remains.

That can cause knock-on problems. A large hold on a debit card can leave too little available balance for another payment. A payment that would otherwise go through may then be declined.

Fuel stations are not the only merchants that work this way. Hotels commonly place a hold at check-in, and car rental companies commonly do the same at pickup. In both cases the business is reserving funds against a bill that is not yet final.

Paying a cashier inside the station for a set amount of fuel avoids the open-ended pre-authorization. The charge is known up front. If a hold stays on the account after the final charge has posted, the merchant or the card issuer can confirm whether it has been released.

Zelle, Venmo and Cash App each use their own status labels. A label describes where a payment sits inside that service, not necessarily where the money sits in a bank account. The same status word can mean different things in different apps and on a bank statement.

The difference starts with how each service is built. Zelle is operated by Early Warning Services, a company owned by a group of U.S. banks. It appears inside many banks' own mobile apps. A Zelle payment moves money from one bank account to another, with no separate balance held in between.

Venmo, owned by PayPal $PYPL, and Cash App, run by Block $SQ, work differently. Money sent through either app usually lands first in the recipient's balance inside the app. It stays there until the recipient spends it or moves it out.

That structure creates two separate events for Venmo and Cash App users. The first is the payment between two people. The second is the transfer from the app balance out to a bank account. A payment can show as finished in the app while the move to the bank is still in progress on its own timeline. Both apps offer a standard transfer to a bank and a faster instant option that carries a fee.

Zelle payments between users who are already enrolled are generally described as arriving within minutes. That description is not a guarantee. A payment sent to someone who has not yet enrolled works differently. The money cannot reach the recipient until that person signs up with the email address or mobile number the sender used.

Reversal rules also vary from one service to the next. A payment that one app lets a sender cancel may be impossible to cancel in another. The conditions for cancelling differ as well. Some services treat a payment as final once the recipient has it. Under that rule, money sent to the wrong person may not be recoverable through the app itself.

A bank statement shows only money leaving or entering the bank account itself. A payment from one Venmo or Cash App balance to another, funded without touching a linked bank account, does not appear on that statement.

Six common causes explain why a bank transfer fails or gets returned. The sender lacks funds, a number is wrong, an account is closed or frozen, a fraud or compliance hold intervenes, the details don't match, or a limit is exceeded.

Insufficient funds means the sending account did not hold enough money to cover the payment when the bank processed it. A balance that looked adequate when the transfer was set up can fall short by the time it runs. Pending card purchases, scheduled bill payments and other debits can draw the account down in between.

A wrong account or routing number points the payment at an account that does not exist or cannot accept it. The routing number identifies the bank. The account number identifies the specific account at that bank. One transposed digit in either can stop the transfer.

A closed account cannot receive money. A transfer sent to one is rejected or sent back. This can happen when someone switches banks and an old account number stays saved in a payroll system, a bill-pay list or an app. A frozen account may still be open but restricted. That restriction can block incoming or outgoing payments until it lifts.

A fraud or compliance hold stops a payment while the bank or app reviews it. Activity that looks unusual for the account, such as a large transfer to a new recipient, can prompt that review. A held payment may go through once the review clears, or it may be canceled.

A mismatch in the recipient's name or other details can also cause a rejection or return. Some banks and apps compare the name the sender entered with the name on the receiving account. A nickname or a personal name entered for a business account can be enough to trip that check.

Exceeded limits are the last common cause. Banks and apps cap how much a customer can send, often per transaction and per day. A payment over that cap can be declined outright. The caps vary by institution and by service. The sender's account terms list them.

The quickest way to confirm a transfer is to open the transaction in your banking app or online account and read its status. If it still looks stuck, contact the sending bank with the date, amount and reference number.

The transaction detail screen usually holds more than the one-line entry in your account history. Tapping or clicking the item can show the status label, the date the payment was initiated, the account it went to and a reference code. That screen is the first place to look before assuming something has gone wrong.

Compare the two balance figures your bank displays. If only the available balance reflects the payment, the transfer is still pending. If both the available and current, or ledger, balances reflect it, the payment has most likely posted. This comparison shows where the money sits on your side. It does not show whether the money has arrived.

A confirmation number or trace number identifies one specific payment. Banks and payment apps typically generate these identifiers when a transfer is submitted. Save it, screenshot it or write it down. The receiving bank can use the same number to locate the payment on its end.

The recipient's account is the other half of the picture. Ask the person or business you paid to check their own transaction list, including any pending items. A payment can be complete on the sending side while it still waits to be credited on the receiving side.

Read alerts and emails about the payment closely. Banks and apps often send a notice when a payment is submitted, completed, declined or returned. A message about a decline or return may include the reason, which narrows down what needs fixing. Check spam folders as well. Confirm that any message came from your bank before acting on it.

If none of those steps settles the question, contact the sending bank or app directly. Have three details ready: the date of the transfer, the exact amount and the reference or trace number. Those details let a representative pull up the specific payment instead of searching through your history. Use the phone number printed on your card or listed on the bank's official website, not one from an unsolicited message.
