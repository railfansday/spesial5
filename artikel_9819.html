The Microsoft 365 outage on November 12 and 13 2026 knocked out Exchange Online, which sits behind Outlook, for about 24 hours. Microsoft Teams, SharePoint and other services were hit hard as well. Microsoft says it wasn't one mistake but rather a latent defect from a July fix, a routine Teams failover and nearly 14% of gateway capacity already out that all lined up at peak US traffic.

Press reports citing Microsoft's preliminary report show the disruption lasted from 4:20 PM UTC on November 12 to 4:10 PM UTC on November 13, and users in 74 countries and territories reported problems to Downdetector within about an hour.

Microsoft has said the outage was a technical issue and was not related to hackers, cyber activity, or to AI, and I'll take that at face value because it isn't what this post is about.

What caught my attention is how Microsoft describes the cause: not one mistake, but three ordinary things lining up.

In an abridged root cause posted on November 16, Microsoft's Charles Lamanna described three factors behind the failure of a networking gateway that fronts many Microsoft 365 services.

Together, the last two pushed the gateway into the latent bug during peak US traffic, and rerouting then sent load to the remaining servers until more than 80% were unresponsive at the worst point. Microsoft says bulkheads between partitions didn't stop the cascading failure, due to a separate issue it hasn't yet explained.

Normal load testing missed the defect because the test labs leaned toward weaker, lower-core hardware, and the contention gets worse on newer machines. Exchange Online couldn't fail out of the gateway the way other services could, and Microsoft's normal rollback and feature flags couldn't quickly revert a months-old change. Engineers wrote an update that returned the code path to its previous known-good state.

Look at each ingredient separately: a fix that passed staged deployment, a failover that Microsoft describes as normal, a capacity shortfall with enough headroom to absorb it, load tests built around weaker hardware, and rerouting that does what rerouting is supposed to do. Each one is routine, and in most large engineering organizations, each would sit with a different person or team, such as performance engineering, platform networking, service owners, operations or release engineering.

I don't know how Microsoft is organized, and I don't know whether anyone there had the full picture of the change risk, or whether the picture existed anywhere at all, which is an important distinction. Knowledge can be fragmented, meaning someone knew and it never reached the decision, or it can be missing, meaning nobody knew the problem existed. Shared context clearly helps with the first. It helps with the second only when joining existing records surfaces a contradiction, such as "production runs on hardware your tests don't cover."

In my experience, failures like this are hard to see from any individual seat, and they sit in the part of reliability that dashboards don't capture because the relevant knowledge isn't a metric. It's the relationship between a change, the environment it runs in, the state production is in today, and what happened the last time something similar broke.

The defect ran for more than three months without causing a problem, and it's tempting to read that as evidence the change was safe. But my read is that it shows something narrower: the conditions that trigger the bug hadn't happened yet.

Staged rollout was used here, so "roll out gradually" isn't the lesson: staging verifies the conditions that occur during the rollout, but it can't verify the ones that haven't occurred yet, like a failover landing on a gateway that is already 14% short during peak traffic.

Metrics like change failure rate have the same limit because they count failures that have already happened, and tests have it too. Tests encode assumptions, and assumptions go stale when the fleet or traffic mix changes, so a test suite that is green for a hundred days only says the assumptions held for a hundred days.

Engineering teams we talk to describe the same shape of problem regardless of scale. A software development director at an enterprise software company described what happens when experienced people leave:

"We're losing knowledge, I think, faster than we're gaining it."

An engineering practitioner at a mid-market software company described the gap between a developer's view and the whole system:

"They test it in their isolated environments. It goes into the large pre-production environment that has lots of changes flowing in. And we're kind of bottlenecked on like human review at this point."

Neither is describing Microsoft's failure, but both describe the same underlying condition: the knowledge needed to judge a production change, and assess the risk of that change, is spread across people, environments and history. Nobody holds all of that mission-critical knowledge when the decision is made.
