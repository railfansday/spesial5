The true power of humans and AI working together as a team is being heavily underestimated right now

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

A reanalysis of 74 human-AI studies finds people and AI did best together when AI explanations came with feedback that let humans learn.

A person with a good AI assistant should beat either one working alone. Two years ago a tally of 74 experiments said otherwise: on average, the human-AI pair did worse than whichever partner was better by itself.

A new recount of those same experiments says they left out something that people need in order to get good at anything – finding out whether they were right.

Only 10 of the 74 studies ever told participants the correct answer after a decision. In the few that did, and that also let the AI explain itself, the pair tended to come out ahead.

Julian Berger and Stefan Herzog of the Center for Adaptive Rationality at the Max Planck Institute for Human Development in Berlin led the reanalysis. Colleagues at the University of Copenhagen and elsewhere in Europe joined them.

They didn’t run new experiments. They went back through every study in the 2024 review and coded what each one had let its participants learn.

“Learning effects in human-machine interaction remain virtually unexplored,” Berger said.

The 2024 review, published in Nature Human Behaviour, pooled studies in which people worked with an AI system on tasks such as sorting images, predicting house prices, or writing code.

Across 370 experimental conditions, the human-AI pair scored below the stronger partner on average.

The pair came out ahead only when the human was already better than the AI. When the AI was the stronger one, the pair did worse than the AI by itself. Neither the AI’s stated confidence nor its explanations changed that.

Berger’s team found that the typical study was a single session, with no practice trials, a median of 30 decisions, and no word on whether any of them was correct.

Only 10 studies, 14 percent of the 74, included a condition with outcome feedback. Just three of those looked at whether people’s use of the AI changed over the trials, and none varied feedback on purpose.

Feedback matters because it is how a person learns when to trust an AI assistant and when to overrule it. Without it, a participant who sees the AI’s answer has no way of telling whether the AI, or they themselves, tend to be right.

The team re-ran the comparison, splitting the 370 conditions by whether people got feedback. With feedback, the pair’s edge over the better partner was slightly positive. Without it, slightly negative.

By the team’s statistics there was an 84 percent probability the gap ran that way, which isn’t enough to be sure of. The team saw a clearer result once it sorted the conditions by AI explanations too.

In the 24 conditions where people got both an explanation of the AI’s reasoning and feedback on the outcome, the pair tended to outperform the better partner alone.

In the 148 conditions with explanations but no feedback, the pair did clearly worse. Without explanations, feedback made no reliable difference either way.

The authors’ reading is that an explanation helps mainly when a person can check it against results and learn how far to trust it.

Because no study varied feedback within an experiment, the team could only compare studies with it against studies without it. Other features of those studies could explain the differences.

In 12 further models that added each of the original review’s other factors, the direction held. Still, they wrote, experiments that switch feedback on and off are needed before anyone can call it a cause.

Most studies also reported only average performance across all trials, which hides any improvement late in a session.

In the three studies with trial-by-trial data, people learned to go along with an AI that was outperforming them when the task gave them something to learn from.

The authors wrote that their estimate may, therefore, understate how much learning helps.

Whether any of this carries over to chatbots is open. Herzog said a conversation with a chatbot could make tailored feedback and explanations easier to deliver.

“But for that to happen, people also need to find out what is actually correct; a chatbot’s answers can be wrong and do not constitute independent feedback,” he said.

He added a second worry. “At the same time, new risks arise from cognitive ‘offloading’ – that is, delegating mental work to AI. In the medium and long term, this could jeopardize learning success or even lead to loss of skills.”

The team called for experiments that vary feedback and the task itself, and for sharing trial-level data so that learning can be measured directly.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
