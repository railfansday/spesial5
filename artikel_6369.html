Simple fungi can recognize shapes, like circles and crosses, when looking for wood to eat

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Scientists found that a brainless wood-rotting fungus can recognize shapes. It grows differently on circles versus crosses, suggesting these simple organisms can process spatial patterns.

A fungus has no brain, no eyes, and no nerves. Yet a wood-rotting fungus, given a cross of wood blocks to grow on, attached more of its threads to the outer blocks than to the inner ones.

Given a circle to grow on, it showed no such pattern and left the center bare. The researchers suggested the difference may be a form of pattern recognition.

Yu Fukasawa of Tohoku University in Japan led the 2024 study with colleagues at Tohoku and at Nagaoka College.

The fungus they used lives on forest floors and builds its networks by sending thick strands of thread, called cords, from one piece of dead wood to the next.

Its name is Phanerochaete velutina, and Fukasawa’s team had earlier found that it remembers the direction to a piece of wood.

“You’d be surprised at just how much fungi are capable of,” Fukasawa said. “They have memories, they learn, and they can make decisions. Quite frankly, the differences in how they solve problems compared to humans is mind-blowing.”

Even so, the team phrased its own conclusion as a question, not a verdict.

The mushroom in the video above is a pink oyster, not the fungus in this study. Pink oysters, named for their salmon-pink caps, grow on dead hardwood and are farmed on straw as food.

The study’s fungus grows as a thin crust on wood, with no cap or stem. Both break down dead wood with a network of threads.

Fukasawa’s team cut beech wood into cubes less than half an inch (1 cm) on a side, weighed them, and let the fungus grow into them for six weeks.

The team then set nine cubes on each tray of damp soil, in a circle or in a cross, with 2 inches (5 cm) between neighboring blocks. Each tray was about 9.5 inches (24 cm) square, and there were ten of each shape.

Everything but the shape was the same from tray to tray. The trays stayed in the dark at 68°F (20°C) for 116 days, nearly four months.

Once a week the team misted them with sterile water and moved them around the incubator. A camera 18 inches (46 cm) above the trays photographed each one monthly.

At the end, the researchers counted the cords attached to each block, then dried and weighed the blocks again to see how much wood the fungus had broken down.

After 116 days, cords ran from block to block across every tray, though a few blocks ended up with no cord attached at all.

In the cross, the four blocks at the ends of the arms had more cords than the four middle blocks and the one at the center. The count ran from zero cords on some blocks to nine on others.

In the circle, no position in the ring had reliably more cords than another. The cross result was the opposite of what the team had predicted.

They had expected the center block, the one with four neighbors, to be the most connected, and instead the blocks at the ends of the arms were.

The team suggested the outer blocks may have worked as outposts, where the fungus absorbed water and nutrients from the soil. The inner blocks, with fewer cords, may have become little more than a path for cords passing through.

The more cords a block had, the more of its wood the fungus had broken down. Blocks in the circle lost between 18% and 65.5% of their dry weight, and blocks in the cross lost between 16.7% and 62.7%. Overall, the cross blocks lost a little less than the circle blocks did.

The researchers also reran the analysis without the extreme blocks, the ones with no cords or with nine. Without them, the difference between the two shapes faded, while the link between cords and decay remained.

The fungus also spread roughly the same amount of thread over the soil in both shapes. So the cross blocks didn’t decay less because the fungus grew less. The network was built differently, and the wood decayed differently.

The recognition argument rests on that difference. In a human brain, different images activate different groups of neurons, and that contrast in activity is what lets the brain recognize an image.

A network of fungal threads that builds itself one way for a cross and another for a circle may be doing something similar, Fukasawa’s team wrote.

Every block started the same way, with threads growing outward in all directions. Then the fronts from neighboring blocks met and fused into one body.

In the circle trays, the merged fungus kept growing outward, and by day 34 a large bare patch had formed at the center.

Fukasawa’s team compared this to fairy rings, the circles of mushrooms that widen year after year because the fungus beneath them grows outward from its edge. Why the fused network left the center empty is something the team can’t yet explain.

One possibility they raised is crowding: fungal growth is known to stop once the threads get dense enough, and the threads inside the ring may have reached that point.

Whether the fungus is conscious is irrelevant, the team wrote, because cognition can run without consciousness even in a brain.

Slime molds, which also have no brain, have already solved a maze and linked food sources with networks about as efficient as a real railway.

The team raised one possible explanation, and it involves rhythm. The amount of calcium at each growing tip rises and falls in a cycle, and so do the flow of material and the electrical activity through the fungus.

When separate colonies fuse, those rhythms tend to synchronize, and synchronized rhythms may be how the network carries information about where things are.

The rhythm idea is a hypothesis, and this experiment didn’t measure it. The team also can’t yet say whether the tray lids affected the empty center of the ring.

The lids were loose and came off every week, so the researchers doubt the middle ran short of oxygen. The way to settle it is a tray with no lid, and that’s the experiment they said would be worth running.

The full study was published in the journal Fungal Ecology.
