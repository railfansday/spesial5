North America’s oldest footprints tell a story of drought and survival

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Ancient footprints at White Sands reveal how people returned for nearly 2,000 years as drought and warming transformed an ice age lake.

The last ice age can sound like a time when landscapes barely changed across generations. But ancient footprints at White Sands tell a different story.

At White Sands in New Mexico, the footprint makers watched a lake shrink into a flat, salty mudflat called a playa, and a pine forest give way to sagebrush.

A severe drought came and went within about a century. Through it all, people kept walking the same shoreline for nearly 2,000 years.

A team led by Kathleen Springer and Jeffrey Pigati of the U.S. Geological Survey dug a new trench at the footprint site. Jessica Tierney of the University of Arizona reconstructed the climate from its mud.

The layers at White Sands National Park record conditions in steps of about 50 years, roughly a human generation.

“Even though these footprints have been buried in the gypsum mud for more than 20,000 years, they look like someone walked out on the playa yesterday,” Tierney said.

“This is an incredibly rare opportunity to be able to do paleoclimate reconstruction and combine it with archeology in the same exact place.”

The original trench, dug in 2020, was 36 feet (11 meters) long. The team extended it to 82 feet (25 meters), and the number of footprint layers rose from seven to 20.

Most of the measured footprints were consistent with children’s feet.

Of the 152 measured footprints, 72 were consistent with children around six years old, and another 12 with children around 12.

Eight came from toddlers under four, and 60 from adolescents or adults.

Mammoths, ground sloths, members of the dog family and possibly bears left tracks in the same layers, beside drag marks from people hauling loads on poles.

There was no sign of a camp, so the team read the site as a place people passed through rather than settled.

The first dates for the White Sands footprints, published in 2021, came from seeds of a water plant from the lake.

Critics argued those seeds could have absorbed old carbon from the lake water and so look older than they are.

Pine pollen doesn’t have that problem, because the trees took their carbon from the air.

Earlier pollen dates had carried uncertainties of more than 2,000 years, so they couldn’t settle the question.

The USGS lab sorted up to 75,000 pollen grains out of each sample with a cell-sorting machine and measured how much modern carbon the laboratory process introduced.

The uncertainty on each pollen date dropped fivefold.

The pollen dates came out younger than seed dates from the same layers, by up to 1,330 radiocarbon years.

The corrected dates placed the footprints between 23,500 and 21,700 years ago.

The oldest mud in the trench formed under standing water between about 24,200 and 23,600 years ago.

Lake Otero covered the site, and pine, fir and spruce made up 40% of the pollen blown in from the mountains.

The team estimated air temperature from microbial fats in the mud, whose makeup changes with temperature: about 54°F (12°C), roughly 7°F (4°C) colder than the area today.

Then the lake began to shrink. From about 23,600 years ago the sediment switched from lake clay to silt and sand washed in over land, and oak and sagebrush replaced the conifer forest.

Warming sped up around 22,400 years ago, and the upper layers filled with gypsum and salt left behind by evaporation.

The White Sands warming was the opposite of the trend across the rest of the Northern Hemisphere, which kept cooling until about 18,000 years ago.

Ocean records off California show the same early warming, which the authors suggested may have been widespread across the Southwest.

Between 24,200 and 23,600 years ago the basin was wet, and hydrogen in preserved leaf wax points to winter rain off the Pacific rather than summer monsoon rain.

Then, 23,200 years ago, a severe but short drought hit.

The water turned saltier, drought-tolerant piñon and juniper spread, and the shallow lake turned permanently into a mudflat wet only in patches.

The oldest footprint layer dates to that drought, when newly exposed ground became walkable.

The same moment shows up in Greenland ice cores as a sudden warming, one of a series of abrupt climate swings during the ice age.

The White Sands footprint dates carry smaller errors than the ice core’s, so the authors wrote that their record may date that event more sharply.

“This tells us that local changes in aridity at White Sands were quite large and followed global climate shifts,” Tierney said.

Another wet spell around 22,700 years ago left clay from a short-lived pond, and the water plant’s share of the pollen rose to 60% in some wet layers.

People kept walking the shoreline through every swing. The authors read that as a sign of how much the waning lake still offered.

Springer said early humans in the region “were real-time witnesses to a rapidly changing environment discernible within a human lifetime – that is really food for thought.”

Ed Jolie, a curator at the Arizona State Museum who is Oglala Lakota and Hodulgee Muscogee, noted that the findings “humanize the deep Indigenous past of the Americas.”

He said they support Native nations’ oral traditions of having been here since time immemorial.

The footprint counts are rough. Some excavated patches are small and crowded, so the team cautioned that its estimates of how many people walked each layer can’t be used for population figures.

Ages from foot length are approximations too. A larger question concerns the connection between Greenland’s warming and drought at White Sands.

Tierney and her colleagues don’t yet know why a warming spike over Greenland would dry out a valley in New Mexico. Working out that mechanism is their next step.

The 50-year resolution of the White Sands record gives them something to test ideas against.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
