Why some city blocks stay cooler may come down to their neighbors

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

A study of 101 cities links larger clusters of buildings with similar uses to cooler surfaces, revealing an overlooked factor in urban heat.

Cities run hotter than the countryside around them, and the usual suspects are pavement, missing trees and the heat given off by buildings and their air conditioners.

Another factor may be how buildings are grouped by use.

Across 101 cities, larger clusters of homes, factories or other buildings serving the same purpose were associated with cooler ground surfaces during both day and night.

Researchers at Sun Yat-sen University in Guangzhou and Ghent University in Belgium studied 101 cities, from New York and London to Doha, Shanghai, and Libreville.

Locations 330 feet (100 meters) farther inside a same-use cluster had daytime surface temperatures about 0.28°F (0.156°C) lower, roughly 4.5% of a typical urban heat island.

At night, the figure was 0.15°F (0.085°C).

The effect is modest per block, and it is an association rather than a proven cause. But it held after the team accounted for building height, tree cover, pavement, wind and a dozen other factors.

The authors argue it gives planners a lever they haven’t been using.

First author Xucai Zhang, who is also at Imperial College London, and colleagues started with building footprints and heights from OpenStreetMap, Microsoft, Google and two research datasets.

To each building they added the businesses and services mapped at that address.

A computer model then sorted every building into one of seven types: residential, commercial, industrial, medical, education, administration and other public services. It got the type right about 87% of the time.

From there, the team drew the city at a resolution of 33 feet (10 meters) and merged touching cells of the same type into clusters.

Their measure, which they call the urban homogeneous scale, is simply how far a spot is from the edge of its cluster.

A house deep inside a large housing estate scores high. A shop wedged between a hospital and a warehouse scores low.

Surface temperatures came from a NASA satellite for the summer of 2023, or the 2023-24 summer south of the equator.

The team measured each block’s heat island by comparing its surface temperature with the median surface temperature of the surrounding countryside.

Each block measured 1,640 feet (500 meters) across. That gave 233,844 blocks to compare.

On every continent, residential areas formed the largest same-use clusters, with industrial zones second.

Commercial, medical, education and government buildings sat in smaller, more broken-up patches.

Libreville in Gabon stood out for its wide stretches of informal housing, and Shanghai for its industrial belts. South American cities had the smallest clusters of all.

The measure is not density or greenery under another name.

Across the 101 cities, cluster size showed no consistent relationship with pavement, population density, road density, building density or tree cover.

Among the factors the team tested, wind did the most to lower daytime surface heat, and the share of paved ground did the most to raise it.

Cluster size ranked seventh by day and fourth by night, behind pavement, surface brightness and tree cover.

The relationship between cluster size and surface temperature was not linear.

Below about 360 feet (110 meters) from a cluster edge, the surface temperature barely changed. Past that, it fell steeply, and the decline kept going out to about 870 feet (265 meters).

At night, the benefit arrived at smaller cluster sizes and flattened sooner.

Inland cities gained slightly more by day than coastal ones, and coastal cities gained about twice as much at night.

The authors tie that to the sea breeze, which mixes the air by day and fades after dark. Humid cities also gained more than dry ones, which matters because the body struggles most to cool itself in humid heat.

Middle-income cities saw the biggest daytime benefit: surface temperatures were about 0.30°F (0.168°C) lower for every additional 330 feet (100 meters) from a cluster’s edge toward its interior.

At night the benefit rose with income, from 0.09°F (0.051°C) in low-income regions to 0.19°F (0.107°C) in high-income ones.

In low-income cities, bigger and bulkier buildings weakened the cooling more than anywhere else.

The authors point to the larger share of industrial land there, where clustering concentrates waste heat.

Low-income cities also have shorter buildings, less building volume and more tree cover, which may leave less room for additional cooling from clustering.

Tree cover worked in the opposite direction. In low- and middle-income regions, more trees strengthened the daytime cooling of large clusters, with the biggest gain in low-income cities.

The authors wrote that development adding building density in low-income regions should be tied to compensating tree planting.

The temperatures here are those of roofs, roads and ground as seen from orbit, not the air a person breathes at street level.

The researchers noted that the daytime pattern in particular should not be extended to air temperature without new measurements.

The data also cover one summer, and the study can show that bigger clusters and cooler surfaces go together, not that one causes the other.

The authors offer a few reasons why they might go together.

Buildings of the same type may share heights and shapes that allow wind to move through more smoothly.

Similar operating schedules could also create quieter periods with less waste heat, while mixed-use blocks may release heat over more hours of the day.

Satellite data cannot reveal whether the air above large housing clusters is also cooler. Answering that question will require direct measurements of air temperature.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
