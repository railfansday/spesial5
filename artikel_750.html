IceCube detects ghost particles with 10 billion times the energy of solar neutrinos

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

An enormous number of invisible particles are constantly traveling through Earth, passing through our bodies and continuing into space as though almost nothing stands in their way. Known as neutrinos, these mysterious particles are among the most difficult objects in the universe to detect. Yet scientists are finding ways to capture evidence of their passage, opening new opportunities to investigate the cosmos and its most extreme environments.

Neutrino research has played a remarkable role in modern physics. Discoveries involving these particles have contributed to five Nobel Prizes in Physics. This year, physicist Francis Halzen received the prestigious award "for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin."

George Fuller, a Distinguished Professor of Physics at the University of California San Diego, has studied nuclear physics, particle astrophysics, and cosmology for decades. He also collaborated with Halzen on an early scientific paper about IceCube. His expertise helps explain what makes neutrinos so unusual and why scientists built an enormous observatory deep within the Antarctic ice to study them.

Neutrinos belong to a group known as elementary particles, which scientists believe have no smaller components. They come in three varieties, called electron, muon, and tau neutrinos. Unlike electrically charged particles, neutrinos have no electric charge. They do have mass, but it is extraordinarily small, potentially around 10 million times less than the mass of an electron. Their exact masses remain unknown, as does the mechanism responsible for producing them.

Another remarkable characteristic is their ability to travel through matter with almost no resistance. Because neutrinos interact so rarely with other particles, they can cross vast distances at speeds approaching the speed of light.

Neutrinos originate from many different places, including the Sun, distant regions of space, and sources scientists have yet to identify. They are also astonishingly abundant. Estimates suggest the universe contains roughly 1089 neutrinos, equivalent to approximately one billion for every atom in existence.

"Neutrinos are truly ghost-like particles," stated Fuller. "It's hard to stop them. It's hard to catch them. How do we even know they exist or where they are?"

Ghost Particles That Pass Through Almost Anything

Despite their elusive nature, neutrinos occasionally interact with matter through a fundamental force known as the weak interaction. This force is approximately 20 orders of magnitude weaker than electromagnetism. Such interactions can cause neutrinos to scatter or be absorbed, providing scientists with a way to detect them. However, these events are so uncommon that observing them requires exceptionally sensitive equipment.

Their ability to move through dense material also makes neutrinos valuable messengers from otherwise inaccessible regions of the universe. They can escape from environments surrounding collapsing stars and black holes, carrying information that other forms of radiation might not reveal.

The scale of this invisible activity is staggering. Approximately 100 billion neutrinos pass through every square centimeter of your body each second, almost entirely unnoticed.

"Because the interaction is so weak, neutrinos can get through almost anything," said Fuller. "Light years of lead -- depending on their energy, they would go right through it."

How Scientists Learned to Detect Neutrinos

The search for neutrinos began long before technology existed to observe them. During the 1930s, physicist Wolfgang Pauli proposed that an unknown particle might explain why energy appeared to be missing during certain forms of radioactive decay. He initially used a different name for the hypothetical particle (he called them "neutrons" at the time).

Pauli understood how difficult it would be to verify his idea. He famously acknowledged the problem by saying, "I have done a terrible thing, I have postulated a particle that cannot be detected."

For decades, neutrinos remained beyond the reach of experiments. That changed in 1956, when physicists Clyde Cowan and Frederick Reines successfully detected them in an experiment known as "Project Poltergeist."

The breakthrough established that neutrinos were real, but detecting them remained an enormous challenge. Scientists needed increasingly sophisticated instruments to investigate the rarest and most energetic neutrinos arriving from space.

Why Antarctica Is Ideal for Hunting Cosmic Neutrinos

During the 1990s, Halzen recognized that the South Pole offered an extraordinary natural environment for neutrino research. Far below Antarctica's surface, immense volumes of ice are exceptionally clear, with relatively few bubbles or contaminants to interfere with the detection of faint flashes of light.

His vision eventually led to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory, a gigantic scientific instrument embedded within roughly one cubic kilometer of Antarctic ice. To construct it, researchers drilled deep holes and lowered long strings of sensitive light detectors called photomultiplier tubes.

These instruments search for brief flashes of blue light produced through a phenomenon called Cherenkov radiation. The light appears when a neutrino interacts with matter in the ice and produces a charged particle traveling faster than light can move through that material.

By recording these flashes, researchers can identify evidence of neutrinos that would otherwise pass through Earth without leaving a detectable trace.

IceCube is particularly important for studying neutrinos that arrive from distant cosmic sources with extraordinarily high energies. Unlike the enormous quantities of relatively low-energy neutrinos produced by the Sun, these powerful cosmic visitors are extremely rare. The observatory detects only a few dozen of the most energetic examples each year.

When one of these neutrinos interacts with the ice, it can produce a rapidly moving electron or muon. A muon, for example, generates a cone of Cherenkov light as it travels through the ice. Scientists analyze the resulting pattern of light to estimate the neutrino's energy and reconstruct the direction from which it arrived.

For Fuller, these exceptionally energetic neutrinos are among IceCube's most exciting discoveries because their origins and the physical processes responsible for accelerating them remain poorly understood.

"Some ultra-high-energy neutrinos detected at IceCube have roughly 10 billion times the energy of a typical neutrino from the Sun. One detected by a similar experiment has an energy 200 times higher than that. We don't really know where that neutrino came from," he stated. "The fact that it got here at all may be saying something profound about the universe. In that sense, Halzen really is at the frontier of experimental fundamental particle physics."

Neutrinos Could Reveal Secrets of the Big Bang

The scientific effort to understand neutrinos extends far beyond Antarctica. Researchers at UC San Diego are also involved in another major observatory that could offer new insights into these elusive particles and the universe's earliest history.

Located high in Chile's Atacama Desert, the Simons Observatory is gathering information about the cosmic microwave background, the ancient radiation left over from the early universe. Often described as the oldest light in the cosmos, this radiation provides a valuable record of conditions that existed shortly after the Big Bang.

UC San Diego helped establish the observatory, and Brian Keating, a Distinguished Professor of Physics at the university, serves as its principal investigator.

Although the observatory primarily studies ancient light, its measurements can also reveal information about neutrinos from the earliest stages of cosmic history. The project could provide an enormous amount of new data related to these ancient particles, potentially offering insights that other experiments cannot obtain.

Fuller believes the anticipated observations could improve scientists' understanding of the Big Bang, the early evolution of the universe, and other fundamental questions about how the cosmos developed.

"Halzen's work, and the work of those he inspired with his vision, was foundational in giving humanity a new window on the universe and on the mysterious and notoriously slippery neutrino," he stated.
