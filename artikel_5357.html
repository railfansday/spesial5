The end of October 2026 was marked by the largest incident in the history of the crypto market — the $6.3 billion hack of the Binance exchange. This case exposed the vulnerability of the instant exchange (swaps) infrastructure.

While the automated systems of the North Korean group TraderTraitor withdrew $242 million via the Tron blockchain in the first two days, decentralized bridges and liquidity aggregators de facto became the main tool for legalizing the stolen funds.

Against this backdrop, the head of NEAR Intents Alex Shevchenko issued a warning to cryptocurrency wallet developers. His position is pragmatic: focusing exclusively on finding the best rate for the user turns services into unwitting accomplices in cybercrimes.

Since around $3.5 billion still remains under the hackers’ control after a wave of emergency freezes, regulatory pressure on the Web3 sector will inevitably increase. For wallet developers, the problem is structural. A single swap often passes through several protocols at once — an aggregator, a bridge and multiple liquidity pools — and each hop dilutes responsibility.

If any link in this chain processes tainted funds, the wallet through which the transaction was initiated shares the reputational damage and, increasingly, the legal exposure.

This is why due diligence on integration partners is shifting from an optional step to a core product requirement.

Investments in compliance and integration security are no longer a boring formality. The market has reached a point where the reputational and legal cleanliness of a service is valued far higher than saving a few cents on fees.
