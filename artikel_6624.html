Welcome to Wonder by Josh Aronson

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Vizcaya is a fantasy, even by Miami standards. I found myself wondering who belongs to it. Here was a forest in the middle of Miami, and beyond it, a house that seemed to have arrived from another time and place. I've always found that meeting a little magical. But I've come to understand how quickly the Florida landscape can change. The longer I spent at Vizcaya, the more I wondered how a photograph might hold some of that feeling, and what it might ask us to protect.

I grew up in South Florida. My family moved here from Toronto when I was three, and we didn't have roots here before us. I've often felt both at home in Miami and curious about my place within it. Vizcaya was part of the city's imagination long before I was invited to make pictures here. To contribute to its story as an artist felt almost difficult to believe.

Photographs have helped shape the way we see Vizcaya from its earliest days. In 1917, Mattie Edwards Hewitt photographed the estate for an issue of Architectural Review devoted to the house and gardens. Her images offered a vision of the world James Deering, Vizcaya's founder, and his collaborators had brought into being on the shore of Biscayne Bay. Today, people come here with cameras for quinceañeras, engagements, weddings, and family portraits. Those pictures find their way into albums and onto walls, carrying a little piece of Vizcaya into homes across Miami.

For Welcome to Wonder, I made a new body of photographs featuring people, animals, and landscapes across Florida. At Vizcaya, the images appear in circular frames around trees and on large banners suspended overhead, along paths that are free to walk seven days a week.

While preparing for Welcome to Wonder, I spent time looking through the estate's archive. In many of the images, people of color appeared working on or caring for the property. I began thinking about what other photographs could exist alongside them: images of young people from Miami's many communities at rest, in play, at ease in the landscape. Who gets to be seen enjoying a place like this? Who gets to imagine that it belongs to them, too?

These questions have been with me throughout my practice, from Tropicana to Florida Boys. I've traveled the state with groups of young men, making photographs in springs, swamps, forests, and along backroads. Many of us grew up in Miami and were encountering these places together for the first time. I would arrive with an idea for a scene, but the pictures were shaped by the people who came along and what the landscape offered us. I've returned to places I'd scouted and found a body of water had dried up or a shoreline had shifted. Those changes stay with me. The places that feel so expansive and enduring can be fragile, too.

I think often about Naomi Fisher's Cibachromes and Hernan Bas' paintings, the tension and theatricality their subjects seemed to find together in the landscape. Those photographs and paintings helped me imagine what it might mean to create a new world with other people, and make an image of it. With Florida Boys, I wanted to picture young men who could be tender with one another, who could rest, who could be curious. Making the photographs gave us a reason to go out and experience something together. What began as an imagined scene became part of our lives.

At Vizcaya, that world began to grow. I found myself wondering what might have happened if, after Deering died, a band of young people had moved into the estate. Who would they have loved? What would their days have looked like? What would they have discovered in the trees and along the water? I began to see the photographs as a kind of fictional archive, evidence of encounters that might have taken place here. Some pictures were made at Vizcaya; others were made elsewhere in South Florida. Together, they allowed me to imagine a landscape without the boundaries of a single property or moment in time.

For this project, I also photographed young mothers, young fathers, and young families. I thought about the lives a photograph can have after it is made. A child might one day look at an image and see their parent as a young person. A mother might see something of her own family in the gesture of someone she has never met. The photograph becomes a kind of mirror, reflecting something different depending on who we are and where we stand in our lives. I hope these pictures hold a little of that possibility for the people in them, the way they do for me.

I thought, too, about Deering. He never married, and questions about his sexuality have followed him long after his death. The historical record doesn't give us a conclusive answer. As an artist, I'm interested in the space between what a life contains and what the archive preserves. We do know that Paul Chalfin, Vizcaya's artistic director, lived here with his partner, Louis Koons. I wonder about the other private worlds that might have existed here, leaving traces we may never find.

On the Hammock Paths, photographs printed on acrylic are held in circular frames that appear to float around the trees. Along the Metrorail route, larger images hang in the canopy, printed on mesh that lets the light filter through. I was thinking about the symmetry of Vizcaya's gardens, but also about the way a circle has no beginning and no end. You can walk around a ring and find a photograph on either side. You can enter the path from the city or come upon it as you leave the museum. The images can be discovered, passed by, returned to, seen differently on any day.

Both the Hammock Paths and the Metrorail site are free and open to the public seven days a week. You don't need a ticket to spend time with the work. In a city where so much can feel out of reach, that matters. I think about the people who walk these paths every day, on their way home or with a dog, and the people who might encounter the photographs without knowing anything about Florida Boys or my collaborators. The possibility that an image could become part of someone's daily life feels like a gift and a responsibility.

Across the city, at MOCA, my commission A Sudden Leap rises from the plaza's reflecting pool. Four photographs of Florida's natural world have become sails, suspended above the water. Some of these quieter images grew out of the same journeys that gave me Florida Boys. There are no people in the pictures, but there are people moving around them, bringing their own histories and experiences to the work. I think about the sails and the journeys that have brought so many of us to Miami, including my own family. I hope the photographs might make someone want to see these places and care about what happens to them.

To have work at Vizcaya and at MOCA, my neighborhood museum, at the same time is something I'm still trying to take in. Both have given me a way to look at the place that raised me, and to offer those images back to the people who live here. The photographs at Vizcaya will eventually come down, and people will continue to walk these paths. I hope someone remembers an image the next time they pass a tree, or carries a little of the feeling of this place back into the city with them. What we choose to see, I've come to believe, has something to do with what we choose to hold dear.

Josh Aronson's Welcome to Wonder is on view at Vizcaya Museum and Gardens, Miami through May 24, 2027. A Sudden Leap, Aronson's MOCA Art on the Plaza public artwork commission, is on view at the Museum of Contemporary Art, North Miami through January 17, 2027.

Josh Aronson (b. 1994, Canada) is a Miami-based artist whose work explores masculinity and landscape in the American South. His photographs have appeared in The New York Times, The New Yorker, The Paris Review, Frieze, and Apartamento, among others. His first solo museum exhibition, Welcome to Wonder, is on view at Vizcaya Museum and Gardens, Miami, and his first museum-commissioned public artwork, A Sudden Leap, is on view at the Museum of Contemporary Art, North Miami. www.josharonson.us
