Whether you're avoiding penetration, looking to switch things up with a partner, or just realizing you've been skipping over some genuinely excellent options, outercourse deserves a little more attention. Outercourse is an umbrella term for sexual activities that don't involve penetration, although the exact definition can vary. “The details of what this means is subjective from person to person based on their boundaries,” says Tyomi Morgan, certified sexologist and pleasure coach. “Some people consider outercourse everything but penis in vagina. Others exclude penetration of any kind including fingers, sex toys, and anal sex.”

Basically, outercourse can include everything from making out and sensual massages to dry humping, external sex toy play, and certain BDSM activities, says Queen.

And just because penetration isn't involved doesn't mean orgasms are off the table. Outercourse can be just as pleasurable as intercourse (if not more so...), and taking penetration out of the equation might even help you discover new ways to get off.

The two terms are sometimes used interchangeably, but there's an important distinction. “I am not crazy about the term 'foreplay,' because it implies that you are doing activities that precede the real main event,” says Queen.

The thing is, plenty of people have great sex without ever having intercourse. As Queen puts it, “Penetration is an option, not the definition of sex.”

Calling something outercourse rather than foreplay acknowledges that it can be the entire sexual experience, not just a lead-up to penetration. There's no requirement for anything to come before or after it.

Generally speaking, outercourse can carry a lower risk of pregnancy and certain sexually transmitted infections (STIs) than penetrative sex, but that doesn't mean it's completely risk-free. The level of risk depends on what you're doing, whether you're using protection, and the type of contact involved.

For example, massages and touching over clothing generally carry little to no STI risk, while kissing, oral sex, and direct genital contact can transmit certain infections. Some STIs, including herpes and HPV, can spread through skin-to-skin contact, even without penetration. And while outercourse can reduce pregnancy risk, pregnancy is still possible if semen gets on or near the vaginal opening.

Talking with your partner about STI testing, using condoms or dental dams when appropriate, and practicing good hygiene can help reduce potential risks.

And, of course, consent is just as important during outercourse as it is during penetrative sex. “It's no less important to make sure your partner wants to do the things you're planning to do when engaging in external touch,” says Queen.

The easiest way to explore outercourse? Start doing more of the things you might normally rush through on your way to penetration. Take your time making out, explore different kinds of touch, and don't feel like you need to move straight to genital stimulation. Talk about your fantasies, give each other sensual massages, or see if you can bring each other to orgasm without penetration, recommends Queen.

And if you're looking to build even more anticipation, consider edging. “Try edging,” suggests sexologist Shamyra Howard, author of Use Your Mouth. “During mutual masturbation, attempt to bring each other to orgasm but stop right before orgasm occurs. Repeat this at least three times for an intense, mind-blowing climax.”

Ultimately, there's no right or wrong way to explore outercourse, as long as everyone involved is comfortable and consenting. Whether it becomes a regular part of your sex life or something you turn to when you're not in the mood for penetration, the point is to enjoy the experience without treating intercourse as the inevitable next step.
