A June sighting in Anchorage went viral in November. Two witnesses, one camera, a rainbow, a military base somewhere behind the treeline, and far more questions than verified answers.

At about 10:13 p.m. Alaska Daylight Time on June 15, 2026, Sara Jane Osborn was photographing a rainbow from a car on Sixth Avenue in Anchorage when she noticed a dark, black-and-silver shape above the trees. Her husband, Pete, pulled into an empty downtown parking lot so she could shoot unobstructed. Osborn described dangling appendages that seemed to move independently as the shape turned side to side, and said it was visible for roughly three minutes before it "disappeared almost as quickly as it came," according to IBTimes UK, which drew on a November 10 Daily Mail interview.

The couple then searched a nearby park for a kite flyer and drove toward Arctic Valley, looking for about 30 minutes without finding an operator or another witness. That fruitless search is part of their testimony. It is not proof that a kite was impossible. Asked who knows what she saw, Osborn offered: "Only God and the government." She also allowed, "maybe it's a drone-type thing."

Two eyewitnesses are named: Sara Jane and Pete Osborn. Only Sara Jane is credited as the camera operator. Coverage that says the couple "captured" the object does not establish two independently operated cameras.

The defensible public count as of November 12: one witness, one phone, several still photos taken in quick succession, followed by one video, per IBTimes UK. No outlet we reviewed published the video's length or the total number of stills. The clips circulating on X and Instagram, including an X post by Mario Nawfal and a screengrab WION credits to X user @digijordan, appear to be reposts, crops or excerpts of the same encounter. Counting reposts as independent sightings would be misleading.

No verified second-angle recording was located. Nor was an original uncompressed file, complete EXIF metadata, camera specifications, verified geolocation or an authenticated radar track. WION says plainly that it "cannot independently verify the authenticity" of the footage.

Tech editor's note: five months between capture and publication is a long time for a file to travel through messaging apps and social platforms, each of which strips metadata and recompresses video. Provenance standards like C2PA can, in principle, ride along with a file, but provenance metadata is only useful if it survives, and as Paul Krause argues on HackerNoon, records attached to a file can simply be stripped. The single most valuable artifact in this story is still the original file on Osborn's phone.

The couple believed the object sat toward Joint Base Elmendorf-Richardson (JBER). Pete estimated it was within about three miles. IBTimes UK notes that neither the distance nor any link to the base or military airspace has been verified. An object visually aligned with military land may be much closer to the camera. Without a reliable range, azimuth and camera position, no one can say whether it entered restricted airspace.

The published still adds a useful constraint. In the frame, the object hangs in front of a sunlit, forested mountainside, not against open sky (see the feature image). Whatever it was, it was nearer to the camera than that slope. That fits a modest-range object at least as well as a giant distant one.

JBER is large. A U.S. Air Force installation document puts it at about 73,157 acres, including roughly 13,375 acres on the Elmendorf side, while an Alaska environmental record rounds the installation to 74,000 acres. Neither figure tells us how much of the actual sightline crossed military land.

Construction at Elmendorf Field began June 8, 1940, and Elmendorf and Fort Richardson were consolidated as JBER in October 2010. The land has a far longer history of Indigenous presence; the base points readers to a cultural-resource history and story map. JBER hosts F-22 Raptor operations, as IBTimes UK notes, but the sighting has no demonstrated connection to any mission.

"10:13 p.m." sounds like night. In Anchorage in mid-June, it isn't. Sunset that week falls around 11:39 p.m. at an azimuth of about 328 degrees, and civil twilight runs all night. Using the standard NOAA solar-position approximation, we estimate the sun sat roughly 6 degrees above the horizon in the northwest at the time of the sighting.

Two things follow. First, a rainbow always forms opposite the sun, so Osborn was facing generally east to southeast, toward the Chugach front range, which is consistent with a sightline toward the eastern reaches of JBER and Arctic Valley. Second, a low sun behind the camera front-lights everything in view. That helps explain a "silver" sheen on a dark object. Reflective fabric, foil or Mylar would flash brightly in exactly that light.

It also rules out one famous lookalike. A "space jellyfish" is a rocket exhaust plume lit by the sun high above a ground observer who is in twilight darkness. Osborn was standing in daylight, and the object was in front of a mountainside, not 100 miles up.

Visually, yes, at a descriptive level. A domed upper mass with thin dangling projections evokes a jellyfish drifting through water, and the resemblance strengthens when the appendages flex and the body rotates. But resemblance is not taxonomy. Helium decorations, show kites, balloon bundles, fabric, debris and compression artifacts can all produce organic silhouettes.

Real jellyfish move by contracting a bell to push water. The Anchorage object has not been shown to pulse in a matching locomotion cycle, and the available clips do not establish its speed, altitude or any controlled acceleration. "It looked alive" is a witness impression, not a biological or aerospace finding.

Kite or line laundry. Long fabric streamers and wind-driven rotation fit the silhouette. Inflatable octopus and jellyfish show kites are commercial products, sold in sizes like an 8-meter octopus. A visible tether, an identified operator or a weather-consistent trajectory would strengthen the case. Note that under the FAA's Part 101 rules, kites flown high or near an airport require notice to air traffic control, and Anchorage has several airports, including JBER and Merrill Field. The couple's failure to find a flyer does not eliminate the explanation, and Osborn's own reasoning ("why would you only fly it for, like, three minutes?") is a fair question rather than evidence.

Balloon cluster or novelty balloon. A buoyant object can drift and rotate while ribbons swing on their own. Close-ups showing seams or strings, or a reconstruction against wind data, would test it. This is the explanation that resolved the most famous previous "jellyfish" (see below).

Drone carrying material. Mechanically possible, but there is no evidence of a drone, operator, motor noise or flight record. Since March 2024, the FAA has fully enforced its Remote ID rule, which requires most drones to broadcast identification and location. A compliant drone would have been detectable by any nearby receiver at the time; no one has reported such a capture.

Digital manipulation or AI generation. Possible in principle, but no forensic analysis of the original file has been published either way. As HackerNoon contributor Matthew of Technology News Australia observes, despite ubiquitous cameras no one has produced one clear, independently verified photograph of an alien craft. Blurry phone footage keeps both the believers' and skeptics' cases unfalsifiable.

Perspective and apparent size. The couple recalled an aircraft that looked much smaller than the object. That comparison cannot establish size unless both distances are known. The Pentagon's AARO paper on forced perspective and parallax explains why apparently huge or fast objects routinely mislead observers.

As of November 12, no aerospace engineer, meteorologist, imaging-forensics specialist or AARO investigator has publicly authenticated or solved this recording. Claims of expert confirmation should be treated with caution. IBTimes UK states explicitly that the object's identity, location and link to the base remain unverified.

The base rate is worth knowing. AARO's FY2025 annual report, summarized by New Space Economy, counted 319 new reports. Of the 114 incoming cases it closed, 60 were balloons and 44 were satellites. The Pentagon's 2024 historical UAP review found no verified evidence of extraterrestrial technology and noted that many unresolved cases simply lack adequate data. Neither is a determination about this video.

Conversely, speculation about "sentient plasmoids" and nonhuman intelligence, aired in a Post Disclosure World episode summarized by UAPDrop, is conjecture, not evidence that the Alaska object was alive or alien.

Al Taqaddum Air Base, Iraq, October 23, 2017. The infrared "jellyfish UAP" that Jeremy Corbell released in January 2024 was filmed from a force-protection aerostat over the base. In September 2025, AARO assessed with high confidence that it was a cluster of fully and partially inflated balloons drifting with the wind at roughly 4 to 14 mph and 850 to 2,200 feet, with no heat signature from motors. It is a separate case with a different sensor and does not corroborate Anchorage, but it is the closest precedent: dangling strings, apparent shape-shifting and a military backdrop, all resolved as balloons once the full footage and geometry were analyzed. The Metabunk forum thread documents how independent analysts reached the same answer.

Stroud area, England, February 10, 1989. Drivers near the A46 reported a jellyfish-like aerial object, according to a recap citing the Swindon Star's front page. The account rests on old newspaper reports, with no comparable imagery.

Fresno area, California, April 2023. A tentacled object drew comparisons to sci-fi creatures until zoomed footage showed strings consistent with a novelty kite, per the same recap. A useful cautionary counterexample, not a debunking of Anchorage.

The useful next steps are concrete: obtain Osborn's original phone files and full photo burst; check capture timestamps and GPS metadata; pin down the exact Sixth Avenue and parking-lot positions; reconstruct the rainbow and mountain sightlines (the solar geometry above is a start); identify the lens focal length; pull wind profiles at several altitudes; check FAA records, Part 101 kite notices and any Remote ID logs; request a JBER public-affairs response; and look for other cameras pointed east that evening.

The open question is not whether the silhouette is strange. It is. The questions are whether the object was near or far, how it moved relative to the wind, whether its disappearance was real acceleration or just loss of visibility, and whether anyone else filmed it. Until those are answered, the most precise description is "an unidentified object in one witness's video," not an alien vehicle over a military base.

The Anchorage jellyfish is real as a reported encounter: a named woman and her husband say they saw something unusual, and her imagery surfaced five months later. The viral claims, including giant size, flight directly over JBER, impossible motion, multiple independent films and extraterrestrial origin, remain unverified. The one still in public view places the object in daylight, front-lit by a low sun, in front of a mountainside. The last famous jellyfish turned out to be balloons. This one deserves the same patient analysis, starting with the original file.
