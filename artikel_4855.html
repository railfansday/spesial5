Why It's Difficult to Be a Good Leader

When I conduct a workshop or extended program on leadership development, I often start by asking participants to identify the various characteristics of “good leaders.” Regardless of the audience, their lists are typically similar. In other words, the characteristics that define good leaders are not a secret, and there is general agreement as to what those characteristics are. I then follow with a question: “If this isn’t a secret, why don’t we see more actual leaders who exhibit these characteristics?”

Of course there are several viable answers to that question, but one important answer that gets overlooked is polarities that are inherent in leadership within an organization. What’s a polarity? Imagine a continuum, anchored at each end by a competing goal. As you invest more in reaching one of the goals, it’s like a pendulum swinging toward that end of the continuum, and you risk failing to reach the other goal. That is a polarity, and the trick is to continually and intentionally manage where you are on the continuum, recognizing that focusing more at one end tends to starve achievement of the goal at the other end.

As an example, a common polarity that many employed individuals face is the work-family polarity. The more time and energy devoted to work tends to result in negligence of one’s family, and vice versa. In a sense, “work-life balance” implies maintaining a midpoint position on the continuum. In reality, work or family often receives more than 50 percent of one’s time and energy due to what is needed in each domain at the time. Perhaps the ultimate goal of “life success” rests on not neglecting work or family for too long.

I imagine there are additional polarities, and perhaps some that are specific to each setting, but managing even this set listed here represents a monumental achievement. Leaders frequently notice a deficit in some area, perhaps followers regularly working in ways that do not align with the organization’s priorities. We might say that the leader’s pendulum had swung too far toward allowing autonomy. Because it’s now a problem, the unfortunate tendency is to swing too far in the other direction. In this case, the leader may take a heavy hands-on approach, which ends up frustrating followers as they complain of being micromanaged.

Just as leaders commonly focus on instances where a pendulum swung too far in one direction within the polarity, followers do as well. In the example of too much autonomy, at least some followers likely felt as though they did not receive enough direction or supervision. Just like Goldilocks and the Three Bears, the trick is finding and maintaining the area within the polarity that feels “just right.” Unfortunately, what constitutes “just right” varies across individuals.

So what can leaders do? Identify the prominent polarities they must navigate, and work on resisting the temptation to swing too far in one direction when trying to rectify a situation in which the other end of the continuum had been overly emphasized. I also suggest being explicit in explaining to followers the concept of polarities and the inherent tension the leader faces in each one. The hope is that followers begin to notice when a polarity is becoming out of balance and can alert leadership.

Perhaps you’re not a leader, and instead feel as though you are at the mercy of polarities over which you have little (if any) control. In that case, you now know about polarities and have that construct with which to better understand at least some of the problems you encounter at work. That knowledge doesn’t remove the negative effects of being too far toward one end of a continuum, but hopefully it provides the ability to recognize that leaders face difficult polarities. Because leaders frequently have not heard of the concept and the dynamic that polarities explain, it may be helpful for you to share this post with the leaders you think may benefit from it.
