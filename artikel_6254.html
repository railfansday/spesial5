On Tour: Australian Indie Darling Radio Free Alice on Tour Life, Guerrilla Gigs, and American Motels

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

This article is part of a new series, On Tour, in which we talk to touring artists about life on the road.

On Halloween, a bouncing crowd of skateboarders, witches, ghosts, and ghouls slowly coalesced in New York City's Tompkins Square Park. They were there for a quintessentially East Village pop-up concert headlined by the Melbourne-based post-punk band Radio Free Alice (followed by an after party at Niagara). The band's debut album, What’s In Between, was just released the day prior, blending the high energy of ’80s new wave with biting social satire and a healthy dose of cheek. Having picked up a following in Australia and Europe, the four-piece group has built an underground reputation on guerrilla gigs like this one—held everywhere from beneath London railway bridges and Melbourne’s former Pentridge Prison to suburban backyards in Austin, Texas.

That’s what makes the album title so apt: The young Aussies are caught between cities and countries, trying to make sense of everyday life while the road itself starts to feel like home. They’re in the thick of their own coming-of-age story, and one that's unfolding on an international stage. Fresh off the first dates of a nearly sold-out European lineup, tickets are now on sale for their What's In Between North America tour, which will kick off in San Francisco on February 24, 2027.

Earlier this month, vocalist and guitarist Noah Learmonth picked up my call from a Sprinter van somewhere in Germany, on the road from Copenhagen to Berlin. With the wit, gumption, and on-stage vocals of a Gen-Z Morrissey, his devilish grin fills the screen as he tells me he spends 99% of his time in the window seat behind the van’s little table, facing forward. His preferred seat is currently occupied by guitarist Jules Paradiso. He gives me a 360-degree tour of their ecosystem, where everyone has settled into their own version of life on the road. Drummer Lochie Dowd has claimed a small sleeping nook near the back. Michael Phillips, bassist and resident reader, sits with his Kindle, having just finished Christos Tsiolkas’s The Slap. As for who’s running the show? “Fuck, no one,” the still-grinning Noah says. “There’s a man up front called Ben, our tour manager, who’s a crazy man. He’s the one who leads the blind. We’d all be fucked otherwise.”

What’s everyone’s travel personality on tour? Who’s running the show?

Yeah, [I'm] type D. I’m sort of in my own world when I’m traveling—headphones on, that kind of vibe. Jules is really easy company. He’s a valuable asset to have in the world. Michael’s pretty chill, too. He’s looking at me now as I summarize his personality. Lochie likes to sleep in this little nook. That’s a big part of his tour experience: finding that little nook.

When we met Ben, we just fell in love with him and his crazy methods, his madness. The thing about Ben is he gets our sound right every time. It’s really annoying when someone else does the sound and you watch the videos back and go, ‘Oh my God, what the fuck? Why is there no bass in the synth? Why do we sound like Duran Duran? What’s going on? I sound like Sonic Youth.’ That’s not at all what we were going for. Two of my favorite bands, though. But Ben gets exactly the sound we’re going for, and he’s a real empath. We’ve spent so much time on the road together. He’s our guy.

Where do you stay while you're on the road?

When we’re touring Europe, we’ll stay in budget hotels. Either an Ibis or a Travelodge. In America, it’s more motel-type places—Motel 6 kind of things. It’s very cliché, but kind of sick in a way. Each morning when you wake up, you set your alarm for half an hour before you have to leave, get up, have a shower, smash your clothes into your backpack, and run down to the lobby so you’re not late and Ben doesn’t give you a verbal spanking.

Terrible. I just had McDonald’s. I don’t even like McDonald’s. You literally don’t have an option. You pull into a service station and the only food there is McDonald’s. Jules is a Big Mac guy, though. I know that.

I’m personally notoriously light. I just have this yellow North Face backpack that I’ve had since I was about seven years old. My auntie gave it to me for my birthday. My trusty yellow backpack. Lochie has a big suitcase, Michael has a smaller suitcase, and Jules has a big, backpacker-style backpack.

I’m wearing the same jeans I’ve been wearing every day for the past year. I got them from a thrift shop in Melbourne. So my essentials are my jeans. Lochie’s are his headphones—we all use Sony. Jules’s is…video games. He’d hate that I just said that. Let’s say his laptop. Michael’s is his Kindle. He’s the best reader. Let’s see what he’s reading…ah, he just finished The Slap.

What's your favorite city that you've played in so far?

New York. I mean, the obvious answer is New York. New York’s the best city in the fucking world. It’s amazing.

The first few times we were there, we partied a lot. We just said, ‘This is a fucking adult amusement park.’ It’s ridiculous. We were staying with this band called Been Stellar, too, who are great. When we’re back we like going out on the Lower East Side, especially to this bar called Treasure Club. I personally also love the diners. I think the atmosphere in an old-school, family-run diner might be the best atmosphere of any place in the world.

A gig is just people playing music, and it can happen literally anywhere. There are no rules. You can do it anywhere. Shaking up the gig setting—rethinking what a gig is and what it can feel like—is really fun, and it keeps it important. The Melbourne set at Pentridge Prison [a former 19th century prison that's been transformed into a residential area] was sick. It was an incredible visual.

Zurich. So random. I just remember having a strangely amazing time. I remember leaving that gig thinking, ‘Actually, there was something incredibly awesome and sweet about that.’ And it was the people. They were just these thoughtful, sweet people.

After a show like Zurich, we’ll be in the green room after. We’ll probably crack open a beer, and Lochie will be funny. If it went really, really well, we might go out somewhere nearby, to a pub or a bar or something. But generally, what happens is we go back to the green room, we’re really buzzed, we’ll have a beer and we’ll be pretty hyped up. Then Ben will come in and say, ‘All hands on deck. We need to load up the van. We’ve got to get to the next city.’ Zurich, though—I’d love to do it again.

What's your impression of the United States so far?

I mean, there’s one thing about America: the difference between, say, New York and LA, and then LA and Minneapolis or somewhere, is worlds apart. You could be in completely different countries.

Middle America can feel like an absolute time warp. When you’re on the road, there’s a sort of repetitiveness to the long stretches of road that all seem to look exactly the same. You arrive at a petrol station that’s identical to the last one you stopped at. There’s that stereotype that Americans are big talkers, but I’ve found that’s almost underrated. I can’t believe how chatty Americans are. They love just talking, going on and on. Coming from Australia, it’s the opposite. I love it.

Any memories you've made on the road that you’ll never forget?

The first time we played in LA, this very sweet girl called Alice randomly made us all these gifts. She gave us all these handmade things: embroidered bracelets with band-related names and glasses with our names on them. I find something quite American about that. It’s just so sweet. In Europe, people tend to be a little less expressive in that way. It’s still just amazing to me.

Glasgow had this sort of “rara”-ness to it, which was really fun and funny for us. It just felt so boisterous. We’d play a slower song, and they’d still find a way to just absolutely lose their minds. It was cute.

When you're not on tour, where’s home for you guys?

Nowhere, not really anywhere. I’m not exaggerating. I feel disingenuous calling anywhere our home at this point. But if there were one, it would be London. It’s making a big impression on us. What’s cool is that, a year before we moved to London, we played to maybe one person at one of our gigs there. Then, the following year, we played to this really big, sold-out room. It was an incredibly high-energy, adrenaline-fueled, amazing, crazy show. That was special. I’ll never forget it. It was probably my favorite show.

There are so many young indie-rock bands coming up in London that I’m seeing, and they’re all ambitious and exciting. I’m really excited by London. It’s lived up to the hopes I had for it. Because of that we’ve found ourselves hanging out at venues like the Shacklewell Arms and the George Tavern, that sort of thing.

While you're on tour, what do you miss the most?

You know, this isn’t a very cool answer, and I kind of wish it were, but the truth is I really miss having an exercise routine. In Melbourne, I was really big on swimming every morning, doing laps or going to the gym. I find it puts me in a really good place for the day. I’m chill and focused, not neurotic, and I just find it’s really good for me.

What's a band touring right now that you are personally excited about?

Lime Garden. They’re an all female lineup. The English rock scene is a bit of a sausage fest, so they’re a breath of fresh air.
