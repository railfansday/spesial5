GM Just Switched Stock Exchanges. Here’s Why That Matters for Investors

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

The automaker’s move to the Nasdaq could put its stock in front of hundreds of billions of dollars in index funds.

General Motors (NASDAQ: GM) first listed its stock on the New York Stock Exchange in 1911. Apart from a break after its 2009 bankruptcy, it traded there for more than a century.

As of today, Nov. 17, it trades on the Nasdaq. The ticker is still "GM," and shareholders don't need to do anything. From a stock trading or stock ownership perspective, nothing is really changing. But the switch could matter more than it seems, because it puts GM in line to join one of the most widely tracked indexes in the world.

The Nasdaq-100 is composed of the 100 largest nonfinancial companies listed on the Nasdaq, and only on the Nasdaq. That's why GM could never be in it before. Funds that track the index, including the popular Invesco QQQ Trust (QQQ -0.49%), hold more than $600 billion combined and must buy every stock in the index.

GM says the move reflects its "technology-driven strategy," and that may be true. The company has emerged as one of the clear leaders in EVs, and its software and services business is growing rapidly. But it's hard to ignore the timing. Nasdaq ranks eligible companies by market value on the last trading day of November, and the annual changes are typically announced in mid-December. GM's switch comes two weeks before this year's cutoff.

Walmart (WMT +0.06%) made the same move last year, but it switched on Dec. 9, after the November cutoff. It missed the December changes and had to wait until January to join the index, a change that analysts estimated would bring about $19 billion in buying from index funds. There are new rules in place since Walmart joined the index; it's now a quarterly review process, but the timing of the switch matters.

GM is much smaller than Walmart, with a market cap of about $72 billion. That's still big enough to rank in the top 100. Alnylam Pharmaceuticals was the largest eligible company outside the index heading into last year's reconstitution, with a market cap of about $60 billion, and it was added. Of course, nothing is official until Nasdaq announces the changes.

If General Motors is added to the Nasdaq-100, index funds will have to buy its shares, which often gives a stock a short-term boost. But to be perfectly clear, that's a one-time event and is likely to be a short-term catalyst for the stock at best. It doesn't make GM's vehicles sell better or change its profits. Over time, GM's stock will still depend on what it always has: its earnings, its electric-vehicle and software plans, and how much stock it buys back.

In short, this is a good reminder that where a stock trades can decide which funds have to own it. I wouldn't buy GM for a potential index bump, but if you already like the business, it's a potential tailwind worth keeping an eye on.
