File your late tax return now. If you’re owed a refund, there’s no penalty, but file within three years of the deadline or lose it. If you owe, the penalty is 5% of the unpaid tax a month, up to 25%, though the IRS waives it automatically if you filed and paid on time the three prior years.

Say you don’t qualify for that waiver, pay nothing until you file, and owe $4,000. File five months late, and the penalties reach $1,000, a quarter of the bill, before interest.

Of that, $900 is the late-filing penalty. Paying late costs 0.5% of the unpaid tax a month, and when both apply, the IRS trims the filing penalty by that amount, so together they come to 5% a month. File within a month of the deadline instead, and the penalties come to $200, plus $20 for each month the tax then stays unpaid.

Once you’re more than 60 days late, the filing penalty is at least $525 or the full tax due, whichever is less, so on a small balance the penalty can match the tax itself.

The waiver is the IRS’s Automatic Exemption from Penalty. On a 2025 or later return, the IRS doesn’t charge either penalty as long as you filed and paid on time for the three prior years. It applies when the IRS processes the return, and a letter confirms it, so there’s nothing to request. Without that record, you can still ask the IRS to remove a penalty for reasonable cause, such as a fire, a serious illness, or a death in the family. Neither one removes the interest, which runs on unpaid tax from the April deadline and builds daily until you pay.

So file now, even if you can’t pay it all. Filing stops the 5% penalty from growing, and interest stops on every dollar you pay.

Pay what you can through IRS Direct Pay, which draws the payment from your bank account for free, and set up an IRS payment plan for the rest.
