The Average Social Security Check Will Increase $75 in 2027, but You'll Beat That if Your Current Benefit Is More Than This

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Your new checks are still a few weeks away, but it's not too early to start planning for them.

By Kailey Hagen, CFP® – Nov 19, 2026 at 9:00AM EST

Social Security benefits are slated to increase by 3.6% in January, thanks to the 2027 cost-of-living adjustment (COLA). This will add roughly $75 per month to the average check. But COLAs are percentages, so you could get more or less than this, depending on how your current benefit stacks up to the average.

Here's a closer look at how you can estimate your 2027 Social Security benefit, how it compares to the average, and what to do if you don't think your checks will go far enough.

The typical senior claiming a Social Security retirement benefit in October 2026 received $2,092 per month. This is a record high, and with the COLA, the average check will surpass $2,100 for the first time in January. But not everyone will earn this much.

If your current Social Security checks are below the $2,092 average, you'll still get a benefit boost next year, but it'll be less than $75 per month. On the other hand, if you currently receive more than $2,092 per month, you'll receive an above-average boost.

You can get a rough idea of how much your checks will grow by adding 3.6% to your current benefit amount. But if you're on Medicare, keep in mind that rising Part B premiums will eat up some of this increase, so you may wind up with a little less than what you've calculated.

The Social Security Administration will send out personalized COLA notices to all beneficiaries in early December. This will give you your exact benefit amount for next year, including Medicare Part B withholding if you're enrolled in this program.

Once you know how much the Social Security COLA will add to your checks next year, you can start building your 2027 budget. Compare your new benefit amount to your average monthly retirement expenses to see how much you'll have to cover on your own.

Then, work out a plan for how you'll cover the rest. You might be able to make up the difference by withdrawing money from personal savings or relying on your paychecks, if you're still working. If those aren't options, you might have to look into full- or part-time employment or other government benefits to cover the difference. You could also use a combination of these strategies.
