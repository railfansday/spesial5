Darius Acuff is showing flashes of NBA stardom, but he has one massive problem

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Flip on a Sacramento Kings broadcast and you're likely to be inundated with graphics, statistical queries or fun facts framing Darius Acuff Jr. in an illustrious ensemble.

Names like Michael Jordan, LeBron James, Oscar Robertson and Luka Dončić are frequently mentioned alongside the rookie guard, who is averaging a robust 26.2 points, seven assists (3.9 turnovers) and 3.6 rebounds through the first 14 games of his career. It's an esteemed neighborhood for Acuff to reside in and certainly an encouraging omen as he progresses further into his NBA tenure.

Skeptics, however, will peek beyond the box score and point to Sacramento's 1-13 record, Acuff's relative scoring inefficiency (53.3% true shooting, 24th percentile, per Dunks & Threes) or his ball-dominant tendencies to downplay his raw production. They'll note how much of the Kings' 27th-ranked offense is channeled through him and posit his true impact pales in comparison to those All-Star-level statlines by citing a host of metrics and data.

Stretching back to his sensational freshman season at Arkansas a year ago, Acuff has divided the masses with his play. The first month of his rookie campaign has done little to assuage that polarization, a reality existing independent of anything he's responsible for but rather captures the cantankerous discourse enveloping the sports world more than ever these days.

Nonetheless, there is plenty to examine, praise and critique about Acuff's first 14 games. He's flashed loads of promise and indicated various areas of concern as the Kings presumably build around him moving forward. So, what about his skill-set may suggest future stardom and what may offer reasons for worry in an NBA ecosystem increasingly cruel and unforgiving to smaller guards?

Every star player needs a superpower. Some are more consequential or prominent than others but they all have one. Though not a star at this stage, Acuff's superpower is his ability to maintain a live dribble wherever and whenever he desires. That may sound rudimentary. There are a horde of guards who can haphazardly dribble the air out of the ball. But Acuff makes it immensely practical.

As the league continues to evolve and grow sharper fangs defensively while also upsizing its offensive hubs, dribbling — especially inside the arc or against pressure — is a bit of a lost art. So many players are unable to initiate offense while enduring full-court physicality or slalom through gap help. Acuff can.

He's already one of the most dextrous dribblers in the NBA, equipped to prime screens however necessary, vary the height of his dribble to best suit his intentions at any given moment, wind through treacherous limbs and preserve his handle in the face of point-of-attack pestering. If he wants to avoid stunting defenders or insulate the ball from pressure, he'll keep it low. If it's time to rise for a jumper or make a delivery, he pounds the rock to speed up its arrival into his shooting/passing pocket. The toggling between them is seamless, too.

Acuff is not solely a modern Curly Neal, he also touts an incredibly elastic upper body. Such a trait is evident in his dexterity — he can dribble and pass from a variety of arm and hand placements — but spans beyond it, too.

It empowers him to duck low underneath stiffer defenders or coil around them to find opportunistic angles. The dude gyrates, and it's integral to his offensive signature. Any potential stardom will hinge, in part, on his rare intersection of ball-handling and upper-body flexibility that fuels his playmaking and bucket-getting.

That superpower grants Acuff access to virtually any part of the court. The broader the scope a player can access on the court offensively, the better. It renders him a grander threat to defenses as a scorer and passer. Yet right now, Acuff is not an omnipresent threat. His wondrous ball-handling unlocks doors for him he cannot utilize.

He lacks the burst and vertical explosion to create separation as a driver or aptly elevate as a finisher, issues borne through insufficient lower-body strength that prevent him from generating force downhill or through the air.

He is not an imposing scorer around the rim, consistently reduced to attempts well below the basket or unable to discard help inside, even if momentum is in his corner. His handle and upper-body pliability mitigate some of these troubles but not all of them, which rear their head enough to disrupt his game and narrow the avenues where he's a daunting offensive conductor.

According to Cleaning the Glass, 17% of his field goal attempts occur at the rim (30th percentile among point guards) while he's converting just 58% of those attempts (35th percentile). Similarly, he's currently unable to overwhelm defenders and reliably draw fouls. Despite touting the league's sixth-highest usage rate (34.7%) and having taken the third-most shots, he's 39th in free throws attempted (68).

A lean diet of shots at the rim and charity stripe makes for a tough life in building an efficient scoring repertoire. Until or if Acuff adds significant lower-body strength (and gradual increments could facilitate this, it's not all or nothing), the path toward efficient high-volume scorer is to embrace the 3-ball.

He's already doing so off the bounce, launching the league's third-most pull-up triples per game at 6.9. Although he's only netting 32% of those, it's clearly a shot he's hunting and cozying up with, largely against drop coverage around Domantas Sabonis' bludgeoning screens. But he'll also dance into them over switches and still hoist when a trailing point-of-attack defender is re-entering the play.

Thirty-two percent cannot suffice long-term but it's far from a disastrous launching pad. As he approaches his prime, it's entirely plausible the newly minted 20-year-old levels up as a shotmaker to better suit the volume with which he's evidently found comfort.

If Acuff ascends to a requisite level as a long-range pull-up shooter that warps defenses into dissuading those looks, it should benefit his interior pursuits. Deep drop coverage is an arduous tactic for him to attack inside because of aforementioned physical limitations. But seeing more bigs switch or play around the level — and away from the paint — could allow him to weaponize his handle and flexibility into drives without the fear of a rim protector lurking.

Ideally, the Kings will also add another bona fide creator alongside Acuff, whose off-ball game has yet to be spotlighted much because of the rickety ball-handling carousel surrounding him. Among starting guards, his 49.8% on-ball rate ranks third behind Jalen Brunson and Andrew Nembhard, the latter of whom has played much of the year without Tyrese Haliburton (per databallr).

In the rare moments he's been afforded to operate off the ball, his instincts locating space, traversing screens or curling into dribble handoffs lend credence to the notion his sprawling ball dominance is a product of Sacramento's cumbersome infrastructure more than his individual nature.

Even in the small sample he's shared the floor with Malik Monk — who's struggled mightily this year but is still the Kings' most viable non-Acuff creator as someone who can dribble, initiate possessions and operate a ball-screen — Acuff's efficiency spikes without suppressing his volume. Whether it's spotting up, being deployed as a screener or piercing a tilted defense, he's yielded the rewards of defenses fixating on someone else.

Through 14 games, Acuff is shooting 41.7% (15-of-36) on catch-and-shoot threes. An environment promoting more of those chances and opportunities for his quick-witted, malleable decision-making — Acuff adapts his shooting and passing processes on the fly quite well — would simplify his responsibilities without dimming his light.

That doesn't mean Acuff is ill-suited for an on-ball role, he's exhibited reasons to believe in him there. But the extent to which Sacramento relies upon him for it seems detrimental to himself and the collective offense.

Between occasional minutes next to Monk and his screening/handoff camaraderie with Sabonis, the Kings do present a few helping hands for their rookie point guard. That all evaporates defensively, though. There's hardly any safety net.

They're a horrendous rim protection team; opponents shoot a league-high 75.6% at the basket against them, per Cleaning the Glass. They don't have established point-of-attack stoppers or slippery screen navigators. They switch the majority of actions 1-4 because most of their rotation players cannot fight around picks and that makes Acuff a moving target for opposing offenses.

Despite a litany of debilitating factors, Acuff does himself no favors either. He is, thus far, a very, very poor defender. He applies scant physicality on the ball, gets trapped in screens like a never-ending maze, hardly ever forces turnovers or grabs rebounds and is often ill-positioned off the ball without the tools to recover punctually.

Habits like these gleaned from the eye test are reinforced by various datapoints. Per Cleaning the Glass, with Acuff on the floor, opponents take 6.9% more of their shots at the rim, log a 2.1% higher offensive rebounding rate and turn the ball over 3.6% less of the time. His STOP% (steals, blocks recovered, offensive fouls drawn per 100 possessions) is 1.6 and ranks in the 6th percentile, according to databallr.

Playing for the league's 30th-ranked defense exacerbates the magnitude of Acuff's woes but he is culpable for the vast majority of his shortcomings and must improve in the coming years. He does not make offenses feel his presence and appears to simply drift through possessions instead of committing to sustainable defensive efforts.

As defensive-minded players blossom into loftier offensive duties, we sometimes see them regress defensively. Maybe, through an optimistic lens, the opposite can unfold for Acuff. He shoulders a titanic offensive load and has displayed the capacity to function off the ball in a lower-stress role.

For that to ignite meaningful defensive growth, though, Acuff probably has to infuse substantial lower-body strength. It'll make him sturdier and more liable to anchor against contact, which he's mostly shied away from altogether, and accelerate his lateral mobility, where he concedes breakdowns because he cannot slide quickly enough to cut off ball-handlers.

Achieving this could address glaring limitations on both sides of the ball and amplify his superpowers — the very ones propelling him into graphics of glory alongside Hall of Famers. Maybe someday, Acuff, too, will be the sort of complimentary company rookie guards hope to join if they burst onto the NBA scene.
