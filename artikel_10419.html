Thinking of quitting your job? Don’t until you consider this factor

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

You know that stinging sensation you get when your coworker gets the promotion that you thought was yours? Or that sinking feeling of doom when you realize that a new hire could take over your role? Most likely, that voice within will tell you: “You’re falling behind.”

You are, however, likely ignoring an important emotional experience happening at the same time. It’s envy: simply wanting something that someone else has. Because of the stigma and shame attached to this “deadly sin,” we tend to want to suppress envy without even knowing it. It’s understandable, but also a huge inadvertent mistake.

If you’re a conscientious high achiever, or someone smart and caring, it is easy to see someone else’s success and start questioning your situation. Envy is a universal emotion. It is also normal to wonder if it’s time to make a big change, like looking for a new job or even a new career. But examining your envy first can help you avoid costly career pitfalls.

Beware of ‘Premature Game Switching’

A common reaction to workplace envy that I see among my coaching clients is to make a change. This feels like being proactive, but it is giving up on the current situation too soon, or what I call “premature game switching.” You see someone getting ahead or getting what you want in the game you’re playing. You jump the gun and assess that you can’t obtain what you want or win at this game, so you opt out and choose a different contest.

Doing something feels better than doing nothing. When we avoid taking a beat to study our envy, however, we ignore critical data.

All negative emotions have what is called “signaling value,” demanding that we pay attention to what is important. Fear helps us see danger. Grief occurs when we lose someone dear. Envy helps us see what we truly desire and avoid “desire confusion.”

Thanks to savvy advertisers and social media, we are inundated with opportunities to feel what the philosopher and critic René Girard called “mimetic desire.” We tend to want things that those around us want. While “keeping up with the Joneses” used to include only neighbors or people we encounter in real life, the blast radius for envy has widened. Now we can see many more things that many more people have. We don’t often see how these cloud our judgment.

Envy gives us a chance to see what we really want.

Heed the SIReN

To examine envy, start by looking at its four key components, or “SIReN.”

First, S is “similar target.” According to the social psychologist Abraham Tesser’s Self-Evaluation Maintenance model, we tend to compare ourselves to those who are just ahead of us, our peers or our coworkers. We envy them, while we admire those far beyond us. This provides hope, telling us that we’re not as behind as we think we are. You feel envious of the promotion, for example, because it was within your reach.

Second, I is “intangible desire.” What we truly want is not the thing itself but the feelings we believe we will experience once we have it. It isn’t really the promotion, but what it represents to you, which could be a variety of things: financial security, recognition as an expert, or the opportunity to mentor others.

Third, Re stands for “relevant comparison.” We envy in domains or areas that are important to us. If we care about our careers, we’re likely to experience career envy. It pinpoints more specifically what matters to us and where we may want to focus more energy. I, for example, don’t envy people who have podcasts, but I do envy other nonfiction authors.

Finally, N is “negative emotion.” The unpleasant feeling is a gauge. If we’re able to close the app and forget about what we saw, the envy was fleeting and perhaps not worth dwelling on. If it lingers long after you put down your smartphone, you may want to take another look at why you’re feeling that way.

Next, Channel Envy in the Right Direction

Social scientists, including Jens Lange and Jan Crusius, have shown there are two kinds of envy: benign and malicious. Malicious envy tends to occur when you’re preoccupied with the person who has what you want, while benign envy is when you focus on what it is that you want. The Right Reverend Allen Shin, Bishop Suffragan of the Episcopal Diocese of New York, recently said that with malicious envy you cede your agency to the person you envy. But when you try to figure out how to get what you want, you keep your agency.

After examining your envy and figuring out the intangible desire, the next step is to figure out some “skill-building microactions.” Ask yourself: “What tiny activities should I test out to see if I can get closer to what I want?” If, for example, you realize that the intangible desire from wanting a promotion is financial security, then quitting your job will likely become a costly error. Instead, boosting your personal finance acumen might be a better option. Or, if you’re realizing that a promotion to you means mentoring others, then perhaps learning how to get reengaged with your alumni association and finding ways to mentor younger alumni might be a way to get more immediate satisfaction.

Finally, when we can direct our envy toward meaningful work, deepening relationships and serving others, we can feel more satisfied with our lives and do some good.
