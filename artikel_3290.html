The way we test the best dehumidifiers is pretty straightforward; we use them. We bring them home, set them up and let them pull moisture from the air to see how they perform. That's the core of what we do, but we pay attention to other areas too.

Since you might want to move a dehumidifier to several rooms in your home, we look at how heavy it is, whether there are handles for easy carrying and if the unit has wheels. Then there are the often overlooked aspects, like how long the power cable is and, if the unit can drain, does it come with a hose and of what length.

You can find dehumidifiers that have buckets to empty, hoses to expel the water into a drain and even a pump for improved drainage. Depending on which setup the model uses, we test it out and see how easy it is to get rid of the water. And for bucket units, is the bucket easy to carry when full?

Almost all dehumidifiers have a digital display or buttons on the top of the unit so you can adjust settings or set target humidity levels. We check how easy those are to program, but also install any companion apps and play around with the features and connectivity to see if it's straightforward and reliable.

We try to standardize our testing, and Dan tests most of our dehumidifiers. He runs the shower in his basement bathroom to get the humidity up to around 90%, then he tests how quickly the unit can bring it down to 35%. For accurate readings, Dan compares the humidity against an Amazon Alexa air quality monitor.

Although all dehumidifiers take moisture out of the air, there are differences between models. Before you decide on the best dehumidifier for your home, it's worth thinking through exactly what you need it to do, the type of technology it uses, and what it does with the wastewater extracted from your home.

There are three main types of dehumidifiers: desiccant (water-absorbing material), thermoelectric (air passes over electrically charged plates) and condensers. The last style is generally the most effective, and the only one we recommend. These cool the air to turn the moisture into liquid and put it in a tray for disposal.

Because the water needs somewhere to go, you have a choice to make between a storage tray and a model with automatic drainage or a hose. The latter models mean you can feed the machine directly into a drain and can theoretically leave the dehumidifier running for as long as you need.

That's not always practical, though, like if you need it in your bedroom. So then you'll need a machine with a large enough capacity to store the water you need without having to empty it too frequently. Most models have an auto-shutoff for when the tray gets full, so it won't start leaking back out into the room.

Dehumidifiers generally come in three sizes: large (50-pint capacity), medium (30-pint capacity) and small (20-pint capacity). Often, as the size increases, so does the price. This tank size is different from the capacity you'll see listed on the machines in pints; that relates to the amount of water it can theoretically extract in a day.

If you’ll be placing a dehumidifier in a quiet room, such as a bedroom or home office, where you need to fall asleep or concentrate without being disturbed, consider the noise level of the dehumidifier before you buy. Aim for a dehumidifier with a decibel level of around 55 decibels or below; anything above this level will be audible.

You may need a dehumidifier if a room regularly feels damp, suffers from condensation or mold, or stays humid after showering, cooking or drying laundry indoors. It can also improve comfort in muggy conditions by lowering indoor humidity. It is most useful where moisture cannot clear easily, such as a basement or a poorly ventilated room. Before buying, rule out leaks and serious ventilation problems, too.

Over time, water can cause damp, rot wood and degrade electrical equipment. Plus, it can make breathing harder, which could worsen health conditions like asthma. The longer the moisture hangs around, the more likely it'll create mold buildup, which can be dangerous to breathe in and damage your home.

Adding a dehumidifier to your home can help prevent all of that. The machine extracts the water from the air, condenses it and places it in a tray for you to pour away. Some even have hose outputs so you can send that water directly to the drain. Many models are portable, so you can wheel them around the house as needed.

Choose a dehumidifier based on both the size of the space and how damp it is, rather than room area alone. Larger or persistently damp rooms generally need a higher-capacity model, like the GoveeLife and Frigidaire, while a small bedroom or lightly humid room may be better served by a smaller unit, such as the Midea.

As you'll find, 20-pint, 30-pint and 50-pint models are common size bands. Along with checking the size of your room, consider how badly it's affected by damp. If it's a lot, even in a smaller space, it's best to get a larger capacity.

Dehumidifiers can add a lot to your electricity bill when they run for long periods, but the cost depends on the model’s power draw, how many hours it operates and the humidity setting. A larger or harder-working unit will not necessarily be the cheapest to run simply because it removes moisture faster.

Look for an appropriately sized unit and use a realistic target humidity rather than running it continuously without need. Energy-efficiency certification can be a useful comparison point, but check the current specification and local rebate eligibility rather than relying on older labels alone.

Yes, you can use a dehumidifier in a bedroom, but noise, heat output and tank capacity should shape the choice. A quieter model and an appropriate capacity are preferable if it will run while you sleep; we suggest considering a noise level of around 50 dB or below for quiet rooms.

Place the unit according to the manufacturer’s clearance guidance and use the target humidity or timer controls where available. If you cannot run a drain hose safely, choose a tank that will not need frequent emptying overnight and rely on its automatic shut-off when full.

In 2025, the Trump administration proposed scrapping the Energy Star program, which standardizes energy efficiency ratings. If a device meets the standards, it receives an Energy Star certification, and federal agencies, states and utility providers often offer rebates on certified products.

In early 2026, it was confirmed that the program wouldn't be scrapped, but would move from the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) to the Department of Energy (DOE). This took effect in 2026 and, so far, it looks like it'll operate as it did before. But while its future was up for debate, many providers pulled rebates.

There are fewer opportunities now to claim money back on certified products, and most providers will now only offer rebates as part of larger home energy efficiency projects. Still, if you buy an Energy Star-certified device, you know it hits an efficiency standard that'll keep your energy bills down.

Many of the best dehumidifiers we've tested were certified under Version 5 of the standard. In October 2025, Version 6 was released to the world, and this effectively resets the list of certified devices. Anything validated under Version 5 is no longer actively certified, and so wouldn't be eligible for rebates.

But if you buy one of these previously-certified products, you'll still find it labeled with the Energy Star badge, as companies are allowed to 'sell-through' existing stock without removing these labels. So although all the dehumidifiers we've tested are efficient, they don't necessarily hit the most up-to-date standards.
