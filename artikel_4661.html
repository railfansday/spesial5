As a therapist with over forty years of experience in the mental health field, I have come across clients presenting with anxiety on countless occasions. From education, workshops, conferences, and direct client contact, I’ve always felt I had a strong understanding of what their experiences were like.

A recent return of my own generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), the first instance being several years back preceding my cancer diagnosis, has reminded me of how little one can ever truly understand of the experience unless they've had the disorder themself.

Clinically, GAD is defined as “an anxiety disorder characterized by persistent, excessive, and difficult-to-control worry about multiple areas of life, such as health, work, family, finances, or everyday responsibilities.” In layman’s terms, GAD is the mother of all worries, which can weave its way into every aspect of life

For myself, and many of the clients I’ve seen, there is a gradual buildup to these symptoms rather than all of them simply showing up one day. This downward spiral, also seen in many who struggle with depression, adds to the tension as one senses what is happening but feels helpless against its gravitational pull.

Early theories of anxiety pointed to excessive stress as the culprit. We now know that it arises from a complex series of experiences, genetics, and learned behavior that turns on the stress alarm system in the central nervous system (CNS) even when no threat is present. Over time, the CNS becomes dysregulated and needs very little to set it off.

To those of us who suffer from this condition (and suffer is truly the correct word), the search for relief can become a full-time experience. While cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), with or without medication support, is offered as an evidence-based best practice, it seldom has the quick relief that an anxious person desires. The wait for medications to build up to therapeutic levels and the wading through one’s psyche to find the sparks that lit the anxiety fire can feel exhausting.

To assist with the patience necessary to treat something as insidious as GAD, many like me turn to alternative methods of comfort and relaxation. Breathwork, yoga, Qi Gong, Reiki, prayer, mantras, or worry stones rubbed into a fine powder are just some of the techniques employed to stay afloat while being flooded with worst-case scenarios.

During my first go-round, my car was essentially a new age shop on wheels. I had worry stones, aromatherapy items, and printed reminders that “this too shall pass” stuck on my dashboard, prayer cards, and, harkening back to my Catholic upbringing, a rosary. I sought assistance through a trusted psychiatrist for medication, underwent hypnotherapy, read any book that seemed to promise an end to a racing mind, practiced mindfulness, yoga, and the not-so-subtle art of doing ordinary things even when my anxious mind said I couldn’t pull it off.

I wish I could say with certainty which of these techniques pulled me through and brought an end to the unending whirring of both body and mind—that way I would simply repeat the process and be typing these words with much calmer fingers.

I’ve met many clients through the years who, thinking they had conquered their inner demons, returned to counseling with the simple words, “It’s back.” The despair was palpable, and the first order of business was to remove any sense of guilt or shame for having brought this back into his or her life. Compassion is the key element for a wound that is already open and needs time to heal.

Equally important is to seek out those professionals who have a working knowledge of how a dysregulated nervous system responds to the world and who can act as a guide down the path of recovery—a recovery that is not about controlling the mind but retraining it to set off alarms when helpful and turn them off when not.

Much of GAD work takes place outside the provider’s office as we repeatedly practice the art and science of reconditioning our fight, flight, or freeze responses. Sometimes the task isn't to convince yourself that nothing bad will happen. It's learning that you don't need absolute certainty to keep living your life.
