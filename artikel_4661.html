Journaling Can Get Anxiety Out of Your Head

Sometimes the mind won’t stop looping and replaying. Anxiety has a way of showing up at the most inconvenient times. You're at a birthday party, hanging out with friends, sitting in class, trying to finish homework, or even lying in bed trying to fall asleep. Suddenly, there’s that thought or stream of thoughts. The “What Ifs.” "What if something goes wrong?” The constant stream of negative thoughts runs through your head, and there is no ‘stop’ button

In my work with people of all ages, I see how easily anxious thoughts can hijack our attention. They interrupt our ability to be present, interfere with concentration, and sometimes make even the simplest tasks feel overwhelming. The problem isn't always the thought itself. It's what happens when we get stuck in the cycle of thinking, rethinking, analyzing, and overanalyzing. What do we do with all of that mental noise? Sometimes, the answer is surprisingly simple: Put it on paper.

There's Something Powerful About Putting Pen to Paper

Writing out our thoughts can be therapeutic, but we don't always need an audience. We live in a world where just about everything happens on a screen. We text, scroll, type, swipe, and communicate in abbreviations. Somewhere along the way, we seem to have lost the art of slowing down and writing by hand with pen and paper rather than texting or typing on a keyboard.

Journaling is more than writing down what happened during your day. It's an opportunity to slow your brain down long enough to process what's happening inside of it. The neuropsychological component of writing: When we journal, we're engaging multiple areas of our brain. We have to organize our thoughts, retrieve information, put feelings into words, sequence our ideas, and translate them into written language.

We're also engaging executive functioning skills, including working memory, organization, planning, and self-monitoring. There’s an emotional component. Sometimes we don't even realize how we're feeling until we start writing. A thought that seemed so big in our heads might look a little different once it's on paper. We may begin to recognize patterns, identify triggers, or realize that what we're worried about isn't necessarily what we're upset about. Writing allows us to pause, reflect, and look at our thoughts from a different perspective. It's not about having perfect handwriting, perfect grammar, or writing something profound. It's about permitting yourself to be honest.

We can remind ourselves, especially as parents, that we spend so much time filling our days. Our children's days are filled with school, homework, sports, activities, social commitments, and screens. Our own days are filled with work, responsibilities, errands, family obligations, and the never-ending to-do list. We're constantly moving from one thing to the next. But when do we stop? When do we give ourselves (and our children) the chance to slow down, reflect, and reconnect?

We talk a lot about helping children develop emotional regulation skills, but we forget children need opportunities to practice identifying and expressing what they're feeling. Journaling can be one of those opportunities. For younger children, it might mean drawing a picture, writing a few words, or having a parent help them put their feelings into sentences. For adolescents, it might be a private notebook where they can work through friendships, school stress, social pressures, and all those complicated feelings that come with growing up.

Journaling isn't a magic cure for anxiety, and it doesn't mean that every worry will disappear once you put it on paper. But it can help create some distance between you and your thoughts. It can help you recognize patterns, process emotions, organize your thinking, and feel a little more in control. Most importantly, it gives you something we don't make enough time for anymore: uninterrupted time with yourself. So, put down the phone. Close the laptop. Grab a notebook and a pen.

You don't need to write a novel, sound intelligent, or even make sense. Just start writing because sometimes the best way to quiet the noise in your head is to give those thoughts somewhere else to go.
