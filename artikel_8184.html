Six Feet of Snow Trapped 29 People in the Himalayas. Their Survival Came Down to the Details

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

(Photo: Screenshot via Aquaterra Adventures)

The mountains around Auden’s Col, an 18,010-foot high-altitude pass in India’s Garhwal Himalayas, looked deceptively calm. It was the afternoon of September 25, 2026; the sky was clear, and the 29-member expedition team had reached base camp at approximately 16,400 feet, preparing to cross one of the region’s most challenging passes the next morning.

But within hours, the weather began to change. A fast-moving depression from the Bay of Bengal swept across the Indian subcontinent towards the Himalayas. The Indian government issued a red alert only after the expedition had reached base camp. From the evening of September 25 through September 27, more than six feet of snow fell.

“There was no advance warning, and forecasting services failed to predict the severity of the storm,” Vaibhav Kala, the founder of Aquaterra Adventures, the Indian adventure outfitter that organized the expedition, tells Backpacker. “The team was caught in a very rare, heavy weather event.”

The group included six clients, three guides, and 20 porters. It was led by Jetendra Singh Rana, a mountaineering guide who had crossed the pass nine times over his 22-year guiding career. Aquaterra Adventures helped open this 10-day route to commercial departures nearly two decades ago.

“This is not a casual trek as the route traverses steep slopes, glacier terrain and moraine, to descend onto the heavily crevassed Khatling Glacier,” adds Kala.

Rana stepped out of his tent at midnight on the 25th and saw clouds stacking up over the col that hadn’t been there earlier.

“Something just did not feel right,” he says. At 2 a.m. on September 26, the sky still hadn’t improved, so he made the first critical decision to call off the next morning’s scheduled ascent to Auden’s Col. By 3:30 a.m., snow had started falling.

“No one questioned [Rana’s] decision, and that was the single most important moment during the entire trek that saved our lives,” Preeti and Raj Chauhan, guests from Bangalore, India, say. Had the group left on schedule, they would have been on the face of the col when the snow arrived. By the end of the day, winds had reached around 62 mph, and temperatures had dropped to about -4 degrees Fahrenheit (-20 degrees Celsius).

Informed by two decades of guiding experience, Rana’s choice that morning came from a place of pragmatism: “In the mountains, confidence doesn’t mean ignoring danger. It means understanding the situation, trusting your preparation and making responsible decisions,” he says.

By the afternoon of September 26, the group’s small sleeping tents were no longer safe because of the snow that continued to fall. At around 4 p.m., Rana moved everyone into a The North Face 2-Meter Dome Tent, which served as the group’s dining tent.

Through the night, the wind rose to near-blizzard levels, and the team felt the tent’s metal poles straining. For about two hours, they all took turns pressing on the tent poles from the inside to prevent snow from accumulating.

“We are not sure it helped,” Chauhan says. “But it made us feel useful and not panic.”

Another guest, Vikram Badhwar, describes a quieter fear.

“It is tough to be cooped up in a tent for 48 hours, but no one lost their heads,” he says. “We were joking, laughing, talking and, of course, silently praying for our safe homecoming.”

Outside, porters and staff worked through the night with little equipment, shoveling snow off the tent and cutting a perimeter around the base to prevent the rising wall of snow from crushing it.

The only communication available to the team was through a satellite phone, which Aquaterra requires all its guides to carry. Rana used it to report the snow and relay his decision to cancel the ascent. By September 26, they were having pre-scheduled status-update calls every few hours.

That’s when headquarters concluded that the group could not walk back. Kala called the Uttarakhand tourism secretary, who escalated to the Uttarakhand Disaster Management Authority. The additional district magistrate and divisional forest officer also got involved, along with the Civil Aviation Department and the Indian Army. All six agencies collaborated to requisition nine helicopters to rescue 136 stranded trekkers in the valley, including the Aquaterra team.

On the morning of September 27, during an eight-minute phone call, Kala outlined a plan to airlift the entire team. Rana and his staff were instructed to clear a 10-by-10-meter area for a landing pad. The rescue was scheduled for sunrise the following morning.

On September 28, the weather cleared for a brief four-hour window. The crew dismantled their safety dome and used the flat surface underneath as a landing pad. Because of the continuing storm, Kala guided the pilots over base camp using Google Earth Imagery from his office in Delhi.

The first sortie lifted off about 8:45 a.m. with two passengers. “Departure order was set in advance with the guests so there was no scramble for the aircraft,” says Rana. Aquaterra Adventures was the only group that had given the pilots exact coordinates and a prepared landing zone, keeping them on track. By 11 a.m., all 29 hikers were safe.

The first day’s window evacuated 38 people in total from the valley before the weather closed in again. Thankfully, the remaining stranded hikers were evacuated over the next few days with no fatalities.

According to Kala and Rana, this rescue was one of the most successful mountain operations in Uttarakhand in recent years, thanks to an unprecedented coordination between state governments, regional municipalities, and private agencies.

Kala subsequently submitted a detailed report to the Uttarakhand Government documenting the actions of his team, Rana, and other guides and stakeholders, along with lessons for future expeditions. His key takeaways include focusing on small details like packing a central shelter, shovels, portable power banks, spare satellite-phone charging cables, and ensuring trekkers are always prepared to be rescued.

“Our objective was to cross Auden’s Col, but our responsibility was much bigger than reaching a mountain pass,” Rana says. “The mountains will always be there, and there will always be another opportunity to try again. But the safety of every person on an expedition comes first.”
