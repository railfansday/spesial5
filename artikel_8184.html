The Glacier Died—And the Trail Runners Started Coming

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

I’m standing with Samuel Mendoza on top of Chacaltaya Mountain, 17,785 feet above sea level. Under our feet is a sharp slope of scree, a jumbled descent of rocks with patches of snow. The air is thin, and the vast Bolivian landscape surrounds us.

Directly ahead, the Altiplano—the high plain—stretches from the foot of the mountain. El Alto, the chaotic concrete and red brick city that has grown to roughly 900,000 people, lies at the far edge of the plain at 13,000 feet of elevation, crammed against a canyon’s edge. About 1,500 feet below and connected by a series of cable cars, La Paz, Bolivia’s seat of government, sprawls.

Behind us rises the Huayna Potosí massif. The nearly 20,000-foot summit towers above, its glaciated peak passing in and out of fast-moving clouds. About 45 miles to the northwest, Lake Titicaca—the central motif to Inca creation stories—shimmers on the horizon.

It’s here, on this high mountain of almost-bare rock cradled in the Andes, that Mendoza learned to ski.

Located roughly 20 miles north of La Paz, Chacaltaya was once the world’s highest-altitude ski resort. It began in the 1930s when Raúl Posnansky, an engineer and ski enthusiast, recognized the mountain’s potential, according to 2023 reporting by “Radio Ambulante.” Chacaltaya’s glacier, though relatively small in area at roughly 55 acres, was a solid tongue of ice descending the slope. Temperatures up high stayed low, and snow fell heavily and often during the wet season. The ice was there, had always been there, and would always be there, by all indications at the time.

Posnansky worked to materialize his skiing fantasy: He hired laborers to build an access road, haul materials, construct cabins, and install the first rope tow at Chacaltaya—powered by a truck engine housed in a cabin hanging over the base of the ski run.

Posnansky, along with other mountain athletes, founded the Club Andino Boliviano, a group of dedicated skiers and enthusiasts. To fund the growing ski scene, the club drew involvement from La Paz’s middle and upper classes, and Posnansky and the club persuaded Bolivia’s Congress to pass tax legislation dedicating 1% of the country’s hotel revenue to Bolivia’s Ski and Mountaineering Federation. This funding would help transform Chacaltaya from a passion project into the hub of Bolivian skiing—a hub that, while retaining its roughshod, backyard roots, would grow to host a national ski team, crowded weekend races, the first South American ski championships, and athletes who would represent the country at Winter Olympics.

When Mendoza was a young boy in El Alto, his family wasn’t connected to La Paz’s urban elite who would drive across the Altiplano to ski. Mendoza’s father, however, worked as the caretaker of Chacaltaya, often living for weeks at a time at the resort to maintain the facilities.

When he was 15, Mendoza accompanied his father to the mountain for the first time. He spent his first night at the base racked by altitude sickness.

“My dad gave me coca leaf tea,” he says. The next day, “the sun came out; I felt better. I got acclimated, and I learned to ski.”

Fifty years later, standing once again on the summit, Mendoza is transported back to the resort’s heyday.

“The rope would drop us off here,” he says. “And then …” He puts his hands on bent knees and waves them back and forth in a slalom. He raises his hands over his head and lets out a high yell, feeling the old rush of racing in the cold air.

Mendoza is in his 60s now. He has a harder time seeing, looking out through thick glasses, and his knees sometimes bother him.

Around us the glacier has completely vanished, leaving behind only a mountain of rock.

Infatuated with the world of snow and slopes, Mendoza learned to ski on gear borrowed from Club Andino. He worked long, physical days with his father, tasks ranging from construction and upkeep to manning the rope tow—including rebraiding the wire cable whenever it snapped, a job that left Mendoza with thin white scars across his fingers. From his father, he learned to manage the mountain, the two often sleeping in the lodge for weeks at a time.

Meanwhile, his love for skiing only deepened. Mendoza remembers Stanley Shepard, an American diplomat posted in Bolivia, who taught him advanced techniques and shared the joy of the mountain.

“Stan would drive up this road so fast,” he says as we drive the same route from the city to Chacaltaya some 50 years later. He smiles with the memory of the adrenaline rush.

Mendoza’s ski story exists in a split reality: In one, he is the caretaker’s son, surrounded by a country’s elites who lived near, but not next to, families like his. The socioeconomic divide paralleled the geography: La Paz’s wealthy neighborhoods in the valley, El Alto on the cold outcropping of the Altiplano above. Despite the divide, Mendoza was a vital member of the Chacaltaya scene; the members of Club Andino were his friends. The nights were full of music whenever others would sleep alongside him and his father at the refugio. The period is marked in Mendoza’s mind by shared meals in the cabin and shared runs on the mountain.

By the 1970s, with Chacaltaya entering its golden period, Mendoza was both employee and skier. He tells me he used to set up for the weekend slalom races—ones he’d often compete in as well. I ask about the slalom poles, curious about infrastructure. He tells me they were made nearby. “But the first times,” he says, “we used hollow canes, like cornstalks.”

In a 1976 article in Ski Magazine, American writer Jeff Frees described his visit to the ski hill. There were some two dozen weekly skiers sharing the piste and “a slightly doglegged swath of snow” that was “lined with rocks from top to bottom,” although there were no lift lines, he writes.

The chalet, perched on a rocky precipice, was “so frightening and insecure-looking,” that “some skiers refused to enter it,” Frees says. With the altitude nearing 18,000 feet, other visitors didn’t even bother trying, exiting the minibus up from La Paz only to climb into the truck heading back down the road.

This was the electric, eclectic golden period. One 1,000-foot run served by a single rope tow prone to snapping and powered by a truck engine. A handful of foreign visitors and the dedicated group of Club Andino members. A small, warm refugio built on a cliffside, filled with coca tea, quinoa soup, and music. Weekend races and training events for young locals, supporting ski teams who would go on to participate in four consecutive Olympics starting in 1980. And behind this incredible scene was Samuel Mendoza.

“It was frozen all the time,” Mendoza says. “The people said, ‘This is forever. It will never recede.’”

“But little by little, it started to thaw.”

Scientists had been documenting Chacaltaya for decades, but a systematic study of the glacier began in 1991, led in part by Edson Ramírez of the Universidad Mayor de San Andrés in La Paz (UMSA). Glaciologists’ observations began to echo the concerns skiers had voiced for years: Chacaltaya was melting—and fast.

“Snow would fall at night,” Mendoza says, “but it would be gone by midday.” As temperatures rose, the glacier retreated, exposing more and more of the rock underneath.

While the retreat was evident in some of the earliest photos from the 1940s, Ramírez and his team found that, in just six years from 1992 to 1998, the glacier’s melt had accelerated dramatically, losing more than 40% of its surface area and roughly two-thirds of its ice volume. The team’s prediction: The glacier would disappear within 10 to 15 years.

By 2009, the glacier was completely gone.

“The entire glacier of the world’s highest ski slope had turned to water,” Mendoza reflects.

As the ski run melted under them, various last-ditch efforts were implemented to keep the sport alive. Artificial snow production was attempted, but ultimately proved too costly. Moving the resort to other nearby mountains was considered, but transportation and access were constant hurdles. Even traditional Andean rituals were offered to the mountain gods.

But the glacier, once gone, would not come back.

Without their ski hub, the Club Andino eventually unraveled. A photo hangs in the abandoned refugio of smiling members piled outside the club. Mendoza tells me their names—a running list of friends moved abroad or passed away. Even their headquarters in La Paz is now shuttered.

While sooner than expected, Chacaltaya’s glacier death was not unique within the broader scope of Andean glaciers. Research published in 2026 by Giuliana Adrianzen and Andrew Malone of the University of Illinois Chicago found that Bolivia’s Cordillera Real lost a stunning 42% of its glaciated area between 1992 and 2024.

This trend is widespread across the Andes, which are home to 99% of the world’s tropical glacier area—particularly vulnerable ice fields located near the Earth’s equator.

Glaciers in this latitudinal band are much more susceptible to small temperature changes than those outside of it. Kept frozen primarily by extreme altitude, tropical glaciers already exist close to the temperature threshold to sustain permanent ice; they also don’t experience the long, cold winters of glaciers nearer the poles. Andean glaciers are now thinning 35% faster than the global average, according to a 2025 international report co-authored by researchers at the University of Sheffield. As global warming pushes the freezing level higher, more precipitation falls on these glaciers as rain instead of snow, accelerating the melt.

Over the 60 years between 1960 and 2020, Peru lost 56% of its glacier area. In 2024, Venezuela was declared glacier-free with the death of the Humboldt Glacier (named, perhaps ominously, after the German naturalist who pioneered the notion that everything in the natural world is connected). Colombia is following the same trajectory, having lost more than 90% of its glaciated area since the mid-1800s.

According to UNESCO, the Andes could lose 78 to 97% of their tropical glacier volume by the year 2100.

Of course, more than skiing is at risk—including the water supply risk of some 90 million people across the Andes. These glaciers serve as water reserves for the millions of people who live downstream, and their disappearance foreshadows looming ecological disasters. Many Andean municipalities also rely on hydroelectric power, which depends on steady dry-season meltwater from the glaciers. As the glaciers shrink, that flow is dwindling, increasing the severity of droughts.

While Chacaltaya’s lift has long since stopped spinning, a handful of trained guides and foreign clients continue to practice ski mountaineering in Bolivia, climbing high peaks and bombing descents on still-intact glaciers. While the chalet has ceased to function, the mountain remains an active scientific base. The Chacaltaya Observatory, run by UMSA’s Laboratory for Atmospheric Physics—with multiple international collaborators—continues to measure the atmosphere, climate, and cosmic rays.

And although skiing has abandoned the mountain, Mendoza has not.

Since his father died on Chacaltaya over 30 years ago, Mendoza has served as the mountain’s main caretaker, a position he continues to hold. He spends more time in El Alto with his family now, but he and his brother Adolfo still make multiple trips a week to Chacaltaya, welcoming the handful of tourists who come to visit the abandoned chalet or use it as an acclimatization hike. With the small collected entrance fees, he pays for repairs and maintains his memories of the resort.

Many of Mendoza’s days, however, are spent in solitude, standing alone at 17,000 feet on Chacaltaya’s rocky remains.

“When you’re alone it’s a little sad,” he says. “With only the birds, the condors, the Andean foxes.”

Since the collapse of the glacier, a new group of athletes has taken to Chacaltaya: trail runners—whose arrival is directly tied to skiing’s disappearance. Paolo Choque is the director of skyrunning for the Bolivian Ski and Mountaineering Federation. One of the “last people to learn to ski in Bolivia,” Choque witnessed the collapse of skiing at Chacaltaya, and he has since helped cultivate trail running to take its place.

In 2001, when Choque learned to ski, the main chunk of the glacier was already “ruined,” he says. Against the backdrop of the dying mountain, Choque became involved in promoting skiing and snowboarding in Bolivia, including searching for alternative mountains to host competitions.

In 2011, the federation found another suitable slope at nearby Charquini and hosted a Bolivian Ski Championship. Choque says that snowboarding grew in popularity over a handful of years with the promotion efforts, but participation numbers remained low—with just 25 racers in the snowboard event in 2015. Amid melting ice and low participation, he made the decision to pivot. “I said, ‘No, this isn’t going anywhere. We don’t even have a ski slope.’”

Inspired by the sport’s explosion in Spain, Choque organized a pilot skyrunning race on Chacaltaya in 2016, which was met with a strong reception. Athletes had returned to the mountain. The federation’s championship now draws about 200 participants each year, according to Choque. Racers gain points at multiple events across the country, which take place over otherworldly terrain—from the world’s largest salt flats in Uyuni to the folds of the Sajama stratovolcano, the shores of Lake Titicaca, and, of course, Chacaltaya Mountain.

“Skiing may have died,” Choque says, “but mountain sports have not.”

While private trail races have also increased in popularity in Bolivia over the past decade, the federation aims to develop skyrunning and its next generation of athletes through accessible competition. Race entries, Choque emphasizes, are kept inexpensive, and youth competition categories give athletes the opportunity to qualify for international competition.

“For me, sport must come first, and it must be accessible to everyone,” Choque says.

Despite challenges with funding, Choque says the federation has seen success in sending participants to compete in the South American Championships, where they have earned podium finishes, including three medals in 2025.

As one sport fades and another grows, Choque defines what success could look like for Bolivian skyrunning.

“What I want to achieve is to bring a South American championship here, and if possible, even a youth world championship to Bolivia,” he says. “We have the potential.”

Paolo and the federation hope that Chacaltaya will continue to be an important mountain hub for decades to come—with or without the glacier.

For all of the uniqueness of Chacaltaya’s history, it does mirror a larger global skiing trend: most of the participants were wealthy. Mendoza remembers well the divide caused by the cost of equipment. “From the city of El Alto there weren’t a lot of skiers,” he says.

Through the Ski and Mountaineering Federation, Choque is determined that the next iteration of Chacaltaya’s mountain sports—trail and sky running—do not follow the same pattern. Mendoza, meanwhile, has seen some of the runners who are taking to the mountain to train where he once skied. I ask him if he would ever run one of the races, and he tells me he enjoys running. His knees, though, keep him from doing too much of it. But when a German tourist wanted to race from the summit to the lagoon below, Mendoza happily said yes.

“Replacing skiing at Chacaltaya in the future is possible,” he says.

Looking across the dry slope where he learned to ski, the rusted remains of his rope tow, the faded chalet and the memories contained, Mendoza takes a deep breath. At the end of the day, as we drive the winding dirt road down to the city with Chacaltaya shrinking into the distance behind us, he looks ahead.

“It makes me happy when people visit the mountain,” he says.
