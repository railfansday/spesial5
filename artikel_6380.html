Pumping hydrogen in and out of a deep fault may steady it rather than shake it loose

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Computer models of a natural hydrogen deposit in Albania suggest that pumping hydrogen in and out each year could leave its fault steadier.

Pumping gas in and out of a fault deep underground sounds like a good way to make it slip. For a natural hydrogen deposit under a mine in Albania, a new modeling study suggests the opposite.

With the right injection depth, each year of filling and emptying left the fault less likely to slip than the year before.

That points to a new job for hydrogen pockets too small to be worth mining. They could become storage sites for hydrogen made somewhere else.

Lei Hou, an associate professor at the China-UK Low Carbon College of Shanghai Jiao Tong University, designed the study with his colleague Zhengxin Zhang. Fengshou Zhang of Tongji University and Derek Elsworth of Penn State were among the co-authors.

Everything they report comes from computer simulations – no hydrogen has been pumped into this fault yet.

An energy system that runs partly on hydrogen needs somewhere to keep huge amounts of it for months at a time. Tanks at the surface can’t do that at scale, so the gas has to go underground.

The usual candidates are caverns cut into thick salt, porous rock soaked with salty water, and old oil and gas fields. None of them, the authors wrote, comes with direct proof that it can store hydrogen in particular.

A natural hydrogen deposit does. If the gas has built up there over geological time, the rock has already shown it can keep hydrogen in.

Many such deposits are too weak to sell. Their natural flow typically falls at least ten times short of what commercial production needs, and the authors expect most future finds of natural hydrogen to fall short too.

Hundreds of these sites are already on record worldwide, including a mine in northern Ontario, and the list keeps growing.

The deposit the team modeled lies under the Bulqizë chromite mine. In 2024, other researchers reported in Science that the mine releases at least 220 tons (200 metric tons) of hydrogen a year, one of the largest natural flows ever recorded.

Gas sampled there is 84 percent hydrogen. The gas collects in a fault zone inside slabs of ancient ocean crust. The fault tilts at 80 degrees, almost straight down, and reaches as deep as about 3 miles (5 km). Temperatures along it run from about 104°F to 320°F (40°C to 160°C).

That heat helps. The main microbial routes that consume hydrogen underground work best below about 104°F (40°C), and the methane in the mine’s gas appears to come from rock chemistry rather than microbes.

Other teams have looked for large deposits beneath mountain ranges. Bulqizë is one of the best-studied deposits, and its steep, deep fault makes it one of the hardest places to store hydrogen safely.

Stored hydrogen can disappear in three ways. It can dissolve into underground water, microbes can consume it, or it can react with carbon in the rock to make methane.

The team gave each one generous assumptions, so real losses should be smaller. In the simulations, about 1 percent of the stored gas dissolved into water, and most of it came back out when the gas was pumped up again.

The reaction with carbon took less than 0.3 percent, and microbes took even less.

Added together, the losses stayed under 2 percent. That fits the deposit’s own history. It holds an estimated 5,500 to 55,000 tons (5,000 to 50,000 metric tons) of hydrogen and has kept releasing gas with no sign of running dry.

Underground oil and gas work has been linked to earthquakes before, so the team tested where along the fault to inject. They ran 72 combinations of depth, pumping rate, and how easily gas moves through the rock.

In the model, injecting near the top, where the fault meets the rock layer sealing it, pushed the fault toward slipping every time. Shallow and middle depths were safe only at the slowest rate, which stored too little. The very bottom caused trouble too, because gas piled up in the tight rock there.

Injecting at about 14,500 feet (4,400 meters) worked best. Under the best plans, the team estimates the fault could hold roughly 12 to 17 billion cubic feet (350 to 480 million cubic meters) of gas, depending on the rock.

That’s about as much as the fault held naturally, and in some setups more.

The team then ran ten simulated years. Hydrogen went in from April to September and came out from October to March, at about 26 million cubic feet (750,000 cubic meters) a day.

That pace held for the full decade. And the fault’s slip risk during each filling season fell further every year, because the moving gas evened out the pressure inside it.

In an interview with Earth.com, Hou said the team had started from the opposite worry, since any engineering in a fault zone calls for caution. “What surprised us was that the cycling itself helped.”

“This stabilizing effect is not automatic – it depends on choosing the right injection depth,” Hou added.

Some processes are missing from the model. It doesn’t capture how shifting stress changes the rock’s ability to pass gas, and its grid may miss fine detail near the wells.

It also leaves out rock fatigue from repeated pressure swings, which could change the fault’s behavior beyond ten years.

Hou was asked when a site like this could store hydrogen commercially. “I cannot give you a date, and I would be cautious of anyone who does,” he told Earth.com.

Field trials and engineering costs will set the timeline, he said. The appeal, in his view, is economic. An explored deposit too weak to develop currently leaves its operator two choices: suspend the project or abandon it. Storage would add a third.

What the fault would do after a decade of cycling remains an open question. Until hydrogen is pumped into a real fault in a field trial, the steadying effect exists only in simulations.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
