‘Hell chicken’ dinosaur had a bizarre jaw and an unexpected appetite

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

A rare skull reveals how the “hell chicken” dinosaur’s unusual sliding jaw may have helped it mash fruit, much like a living parrot.

A small dinosaur nicknamed the “hell chicken” had an unusual way of using its beak.

Its lower jaw could slide back and forth, potentially pressing soft food against the roof of its mouth. That movement may help explain what it ate.

The dinosaur, Infernogallus jamesi, appears to have worked its jaw much like a living parrot that feeds on fruit pulp.

The similarity offers one of the strongest cases yet that some dinosaurs had adapted to eat fruit.

Fruit-bearing plants spread late in the age of dinosaurs, but direct fossil evidence of those adaptations has been missing.

Gregory Funston, an assistant professor in the Department of Anatomical Sciences at Stony Brook University, led the study.

His team built its case from the skull’s shape and comparisons with living animals. No food preserved inside the fossil confirms the animal’s diet.

Scientists have debated what this dinosaur’s relatives ate for a century.

Early researchers thought they ate eggs. Later proposals included shellfish, meat, seeds, fruit and food extracted from cracks.

“For more than 100 years, we didn’t know what the skulls of these animals looked like,” Funston said.

“Now, we know they were even weirder than we could have predicted.”

Infernogallus belonged to the caenagnathids, a family of toothless, beaked dinosaurs that lived mostly in North America.

These animals had long, slender legs and air-filled bones that rarely survive intact in the rocks of the American West.

Researchers have had to piece together their lives mostly from scattered, broken bones. The new fossil gives them the family’s first complete skull.

David Evans is the chief curator of natural history at the Royal Ontario Museum (ROM) and the paper’s senior author.

“Complete remains of small dinosaurs like Infernogallus are very rare in the fossil record,” said Evans.

Collectors found the skeleton in Garfield County, Montana, in 2011. It lay in sandstone about 51 feet (15.5 meters) below the rock layer marking the end of the Cretaceous.

The team also studied jaw and skull bones from Montana and the Dakotas, including a set held by the North Dakota Geological Survey.

All the specimens came from the Hell Creek Formation. That explains the “hell” in the dinosaur’s name. Its small, birdlike body supplied the “chicken.”

Growth lines in its leg bones show that the animal had finished growing. It was at least six, and probably seven, years old when it died.

At about 26 pounds (12 kilograms), the adult weighed roughly one-fifth as much as a larger relative found in the same rocks.

Infernogallus jamesi belonged to the same broad branch of the dinosaur family tree as T. rex.

In most dinosaurs on that branch, the main jaw muscles pulled the lower jaw upward to close the mouth.

CT scans helped the researchers rebuild the Infernogallus skull on a computer and see how its jaw differed.

Bones in its palate, or the roof of its mouth, curved sharply downward. Large muscles attached there would have pulled the lower jaw mainly forward and back. The sides of the skull also had less room for biting muscles.

An opening in the skull behind the eye measured about 11% of the skull’s length. In Citipati, a well-known relative, that figure was 29%.

Together, these features suggest that Infernogallus had a much weaker bite than its relatives.

Its jaw hinge also allowed an unusual amount of movement. A rounded ridge on the lower jaw fitted into a saddle-shaped groove on the skull.

When the team fitted these bones together digitally, the lower jaw could slide back and forth by as much as 0.6 inch (15 millimeters). That is about 14% of the skull’s length.

Inside the mouth, two bones met to form a large knob pointing downward. When the jaw rested, this knob sat in a groove along the middle of the lower beak.

Ridges and grooves lined both jaws. As the lower jaw slid, these surfaces could have sheared, mashed or crushed soft food, much like a mortar and pestle.

A large attachment point for a tongue muscle also suggests that a strong tongue helped move food around.

The team found fewer signs that the dinosaur could handle tough food.

Other plant-eating dinosaurs on its branch used teeth or swallowed stones to break down fibrous plants. The stones ground food inside the gut.

Infernogallus had no teeth. Although its belly ribs survived complete, researchers found no stones in its body cavity while preparing the fossil.

It also lacked the sturdy jaw hinge and flat crushing surfaces that animals use to break nuts and hard seeds. These clues pointed toward soft food that still needed mashing before the animal swallowed it.

The researchers compared the direction of jaw-muscle pull in 19 dinosaurs and birds with different diets.

Most of the other dinosaurs grouped together because their muscles pulled mainly upward. Infernogallus stood well apart from them.

Its closest match was Pesquet’s parrot, which lives in New Guinea’s mountain forests. This bird depends on fruit, eating the soft pulp and discarding the seed.

Most parrots, including African greys, use powerful bites to crack seeds and eat what is inside. Their jaw muscles mainly pull upward.

Pesquet’s parrot also has a longer, lower skull and jaw than other parrots, with more pronounced ridges at the jaw hinge. Infernogallus differs from its dinosaur relatives in similar ways.

Thomas Dudgeon is a postdoctoral research fellow at ROM and a co-author.

“It’s pretty rare for animals to become specialized for a diet of fruit, so when it happens, it comes with some remarkable adaptations,” said Dudgeon.

“The anatomy of Infernogallus is bizarre among dinosaurs, and we quickly realized that we were dealing with something special.”

The comparison suggests that Infernogallus may have stripped flesh from fruit and left the seed behind. The researchers think it probably ate other soft plant parts as well.

The team also explored whether these dinosaurs appeared at a time when fruit could have supported them. They rebuilt the group’s family tree and estimated when its branches arose.

Their results place the family’s origin more than 100 million years ago, shortly after flowering plants began producing fruit.

Those fruits included stone fruits, which surround a single large seed with flesh, as a cherry does.

Microvenator, the family’s earliest known member, already had early versions of the palate and beak features found in Infernogallus.

The branch with the most advanced jaws appeared around 84 million years ago. After that, the family split into many more lineages, while stone fruits and other fleshy fruits were getting bigger.

Earlier studies found no broad match between the diversification of dinosaurs and flowering plants.

The team suggests that the closer timing between this dinosaur family and fleshy fruits may reflect a more specific ecological connection.

The skull makes a case for fruit eating, but it cannot settle the question on its own.

The team measured the jaw’s sliding range using bones alone. Soft tissues probably limited that movement in the living animal.

The skull had also been crushed, so researchers used better-preserved relatives to estimate where some muscles began.

Living fruit-eating birds add another complication. Some swallow fruit whole, some eat the seeds and others eat the flesh. Those different habits make it hard to identify a fruit diet from a skull alone.

The researchers also do not know whether these dinosaurs helped fruiting plants spread.

Their possible dependence on fruit raises a question about their extinction, too.

Small body size is thought to have helped birds survive the mass extinction 66 million years ago. Infernogallus was small, but its family did not survive.

The team suggests that diet may have mattered. Fruit flesh is seasonal and does not last like seeds, so relying on it could have left these dinosaurs vulnerable.

Fossilized stomach contents would provide a more direct test of what they ate.

The researchers also want to build more detailed models of their chewing movements and learn more about how living birds handle fruit. A full description of another specimen is still to come.

The study was published in Nature Communications.
