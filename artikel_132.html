Shot in the face at 15: The lives shattered by Cameroon's decade of conflict

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Median Bah was shot several times in the face by unknown gunmen

Median Bah was just 15 when her life changed forever.

She remembers the date vividly - 7 April 2018. She was ill and confined to her bed.

Armed men stormed her family's compound in Cameroon's Southwest region, one of the country's two English-speaking areas at the centre of a separatist conflict.

Median, now 23, says she was fast asleep when her brother woke her, panicked by the approach of a group of armed men who were shooting indiscriminately.

He escaped through a window. But for Median, fleeing was not so easy.

"Since I was sick, I had no strength to jump through the window with him, so I had to pass through the door," she tells the BBC.

That decision would have devastating consequences.

She says she came face to face with two of the attackers in the backyard, who opened fire at her.

They first shot her in the thigh before firing several more rounds, which severely damaged her face.

"I kept pleading with them not to kill me, that I wasn't feeling well. However, I found myself on the floor," she recalls.

Everything went dark. She lost consciousness and could neither see nor speak.

Median initially thought she was dreaming.

The attack left her with devastating facial injuries. Her jawbone was damaged, most of her teeth destroyed and much of her mouth severely injured.

The Anglophone crisis began in 2016, when English-speaking Cameroonians took up arms over alleged marginalisation by the French-speaking government (file photo)

Over the past eight years, she has undergone at least four reconstructive surgeries in Cameroon, Ghana and India, with more procedures expected next year.

Parts of her face have been reconstructed using tissue taken from other parts of her body.

She also lost the ability to breathe normally through her nose and mouth. She underwent a tracheostomy, a surgical procedure that creates an opening in the neck to allow air into the windpipe.

For eight years, she relied on a tube to breathe. It was only recently removed, allowing her to breathe normally again.

A teenager left with devastating facial injuries, a mother forced to flee her home and a student whose education was disrupted. Nearly a decade of fighting in Cameroon has left countless lives shattered.

The crisis began in October 2016, when lawyers and teachers in Cameroon's English-speaking regions went on strike over the alleged marginalisation of Anglophone communities by the Francophone-dominated government.

They were concerned about the appointment of French-speaking judges and teachers to courts and schools in the English-speaking Northwest and Southwest regions.

Initially, the protest was peaceful, with thousands demanding greater recognition of the English-speaking minority.

But human rights groups accused security forces of using excessive force to suppress the protests. The crackdown, critics say, helped fuel an armed separatist movement.

By 2017, a dozen armed groups had emerged, according to the International Crisis Group, seeking independence for the two English-speaking regions and the creation of a breakaway state known as Ambazonia.

Since then, separatist fighters have engaged in a prolonged and often brutal conflict with government forces.

Rights groups have documented serious abuses by both sides.

Separatist fighters have been accused of targeted killings, kidnappings for ransom, torture and attacks on schools and other public and private facilities.

Government forces, meanwhile, have faced allegations of arbitrary arrests and detention, torture and unlawful killings.

Both sides have denied the accusations, although in some instances they have acknowledged responsibility for abuses.

The violence has devastated communities and forced nearly half a million people from their homes, according to data published by the UN this year.

At least 6,000 people have been killed since the conflict began, according to Human Rights Watch.

The UN refugee agency, UNHCR, says more than 70,000 Cameroonians have sought refuge in neighbouring Nigeria.

Gloria Akom is among 15,000 people who fled Cameroon for Nigeria's Cross River state

Glory Akom, 44, lives with a disability after surviving severe burns in an incident unrelated to the conflict.

The mother-of-two now lives in a camp in Ogoja, a town in Nigeria's south-eastern Cross River state, near the Cameroonian border.

Ogoja hosts about 15,000 refugees across three settlements, Richard Effiong, from the UNHCR, told the BBC.

The UNHCR has provided refugees with brick houses topped with tin roofs.

For many, however, uncertainty remains over when, or whether, they will ever return home.

Glory says her life in Cameroon was far better. She ran a business and was able to provide for her family.

But everything changed when fighting intensified in 2017.

She said soldiers deployed to her area in the Southwest region pursued residents into the bush, forcing them to abandon their homes and flee into Nigeria.

"We were sleeping in the bush, as we were always running," she said.

"If you slept in your house, they could kill you there."

Initially, she settled in a neighbouring village before moving to Ogoja, where she was eventually allocated a permanent home.

Like many other refugees, Glory depends on $67 (Â£51) monthly cash assistance from UNHCR to support her children, one of who suffers from sickle cell and requires regular medical attention.

The money is barely enough to cover food, medical bills and other necessities, she says.

Her sadness at having to leave home is shared by other Cameroonian refugees in Ogoja who spoke to the BBC.

Maurine Mangha, 21, said she and five other members of her family fled Akwaya in the Southwest region after separatist fighters opened fire during celebrations marking what they described as their independence on 1 October 2017.

"They shot a lot of people, and we had no choice but run into the bush," she tells the BBC.

Education in Cameroon's Southwest region has been severely disrupted by the conflict (file photo)

Maurine had stopped attending school months earlier because of a separatist-imposed education boycott.

The 2017 boycott campaign, which sought to pressure the government, involved threats and attacks against schools and those who defied orders to stay away.

The consequences for children have been severe.

More than 200,000 children are currently out of school in the conflict regions, while nearly 2,500 learning institutions remain non-operational in the two regions, according to the UN's children's agency, Unicef, citing Cameroonian education authorities.

Despite the disruption, Maurine managed to secure a scholarship through a higher education programme for refugees managed by the UNHCR.

She is now studying microbiology at a local polytechnic.

But Maurine says she cannot help thinking about friends who remained in Cameroon.

"I feel very bad for my friends as they did not have the opportunity [to further] their education because of the crisis," she says.

Maurine Mangha has managed to get a scholarship to continue her education

After nearly a decade of fighting, the Cameroonian government wants the separatists to abandon their armed struggle.

In September, the authorities launched a community dialogue initiative aimed at finding grassroots solutions to the conflict.

"It is you, all of us sitting here, who have the capacity today to organise to talk to those boys [separatists], to tell them, the time has come for them to leave," Prime Minister Joseph Dion Ngute said during the launch in Buea, capital of the Southwest region.

He repeated a similar message in Bamenda, the main city in the Northwest region and one of the epicentres of the conflict.

At both events, nearly 60 people described as former separatist fighters were presented as having abandoned the armed struggle and surrendered to the state.

A similar initiative in 2019 failed to end the fighting.

As of November 2026, almost 1,500 former separatist fighters were undergoing rehabilitation at Disarmament, Demobilisation and Reintegration (DDR) centres in the two English-speaking regions, a Cameroonian official from the agency overseeing the programme told the BBC.

"A lot of Amba boys [separatist fighters] have been killed, others have surrendered, and many of them have abandoned the ideology of independence," says Clovis Naseri, a former separatist commander.

President Paul Biya, who has been in power since 1982, has faced criticism over his government's military crackdown on Anglophone separatists

Clovis spent about two years with the White Tigers of Ribbons, an armed group operating in the Southwest region, before abandoning the group in 2019.

He was subsequently admitted to a reintegration centre, where he underwent rehabilitation for several years.

He is now a vocal critic of the separatist movement and advocates peace and national unity.

Despite defections and government efforts to encourage fighters to surrender, separatist groups continue to carry out attacks across the two regions.

Some analysts argue that the conflict has persisted for so long partly because of the government's failure to address the grievances that triggered it.

Roland Fru, a researcher on the Anglophone crisis, says the authorities are yet to demonstrate a clear understanding of the issues driving the conflict.

"I've not heard even once, the government explain things in such a way that helps me understand that they even understand the problem," he says.

The BBC contacted several government officials for comment but did not receive a response.

Cameroonian authorities have frequently rejected criticism of their handling of the crisis.

Last November, President Paul Biya said the reconstruction efforts, supported by international partners, were helping restore normality in the affected regions.

"These efforts will continue steadily," he said during his swearing-in ceremony, while praising security forces for their "effective actions" and "professionalism" in improving the situation.

For survivors such as Median, Glory and Maurine, however, lasting peace may remain elusive until the fighting comes to a permanent end.

As they try to rebuild their lives and hold on to hope, Median, who still lives in Cameroon, says she remains grateful to have survived.

"I have defeated death," she says confidently. "I don't think there's anything that can stop me."
