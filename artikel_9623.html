Your cutout passes on white and fails on dark: a 10-line edge test before you ship product photos

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Most product-photo pipelines are tuned on a white background, because that is what marketplaces ask for. White is also the most forgiving background a cutout can be tested on. Every mistake a matte makes — a contaminated edge, a one-pixel ring of the old background, a bite taken out of a hair strand — disappears against white and reappears the moment the same PNG lands on a dark card, a coloured hero, or a comparison table.

That is not a rendering bug. It is what the file actually contains.

A straight-alpha PNG stores colour and opacity separately. An edge pixel is usually the original photo pixel — background included — with partial alpha. When the viewer composites it:

the leftover background colour in src is multiplied by whatever a happens to be. Against white, that residue is white on white: invisible. Against #101010 it is a light rim. The alpha value was never wrong; the colour under it was.

Composite the same RGBA image over white and over a near-black, then compare only the pixels whose alpha is strictly between 0 and 250 — the edge band. Fully opaque pixels and fully transparent pixels are identical in both composites by definition, so they contribute nothing but noise.

I ran it on a synthetic 600×600 test image: a blurred bottle shape with 120 one-pixel hair strands, a cream background, and two candidate mattes — one soft (1 px erosion plus a small blur, roughly what an over-eager segmentation threshold produces) and one hard (a 128 threshold, no partial alpha at all).

The soft matte changes colour by more than half its range depending on what sits behind it — that is the fringe, quantified. Fifty-five percent of its edge pixels read light on white, which tells you the residue is concentrated in the background's colour: exactly the failure the dark composite exposes.

The hard matte scores zero on both, and that is not a win. It removed the partial-alpha band by deleting it, which is also how the hair strands disappear and the silhouette turns into a staircase. A clean threshold looks perfect on paper and worse on screen.

On a 2,000 px image, run it on a downscaled copy or move the loop to numpy; the shape of the numbers does not change.

We do ask for clean edges: our edit prompt says "clean, precise edges with no halo or fringe", keeps every flyaway hair strand, and forbids reshaping the subject. Prompts are requests, not measurements. A generative edit can honour the instruction and still return a one-pixel white rim, because the rim is not something the request can see. The audit is what makes the claim checkable — run it on every output and hold the pipeline to the number.

A cutout is not finished when it looks right on white. It is finished when the edge band has nothing left to reveal on black — and that is a measurement, not an opinion.

I build CleanWhiteBG, a browser-based background remover for product photos: transparent cutouts, white marketplace exports, and batches of up to 50 images. The audit above is the check I run on its output before I trust it.
