7 Psychological Thrillers From the 2020s That Are 10/10 Masterpieces

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

A New York native, Joe graduated from an avant-garde theater school upstate, where he was obsessed with sketch comedy. There, he and his lunatic friends founded The Sketchies — which still lives on at Skidmore College today. Living in New York City, a screenwriter friend of his then taught him how to write for film. This awakened a new passion in Joe. Since then his scripts have garnered over 200 festival selections, nominations, and wins all over the globe (including the BlueCat Best Feature award). Several of his screenplays have been made into feature films — some of them not that bad! Overall, he loves to create unique, hilarious, touching, and bizarre stories.

While there are a ton of sub-categories of films in the thriller genre, the most beloved by movie fans just may be the psychological thriller. Every decade has produced a plethora of mentally stimulating, enthralling, deliriously anxious works, and the 2020s are no exception; even if we’re only about 60% done.

Here are seven thrillers produced from 2020 until now, that are all 10/10, stunningly bold works of cinematic art. Some of these explore the pitch-black criminal underworld of various parts of the country, others delve into the eerie recesses of the fractured human mind, while others just go all-out and take the audience down a rabbit hole of surreal imagery and intellectually opaque theories. Regardless of their topics, all these films thrill in their own peculiar and uniquely clever ways.

Sometimes, it’s pretty rough out there. There have been a handful of films made in the 2020s that showcase their protagonist going through absolute hell, and then having them (and the audience) learn an unforgettable lesson. Director Benjamin Caron’s Night Always Comes (based on the Willy Vlautin bestseller) exercises these familiar tropes, but in a refreshingly brutal way. Chocked to the max with increasingly harrowing and dangerous set pieces, this ferocious film shows the world that Vanessa Kirby is a lot more than just an elegant member of the royal family.

Lynette (Kirby) is a battle-scarred Oregonian woman who’s been through quite a lot in her relatively short amount of time on earth. She lives with her (on the surface, initially flippant) mother, Doreen (Jennifer Jason Leigh, reliable as always) and brother, Kenny (Zack Gottsagen, sweet and incredibly genuine), who has special needs. The set-up is that they are about to lose their home to the bank unless they drum up enough cash to put forth a down payment in the amount of 25k. Doreen has apparently squandered some of their collective cash on a new car, and Lynette is justifiably pissed. She then embarks on a wild ride of an expedition to score the requisite amount of dough. Over the course of the night, we learn that while Lynette has a job at a bar, she is also a sex worker for more substantial earnings. Trying her absolute best to hit up all the people in her sphere who owe her in one manner or another — including her escort friend, Gloria (Julia Fox), and ex-boyfriend/abuser-groomer, Tommy (Michael Kelly). This is one of those movies where everything that can go awry for the main character does, but she perseveres anyway — including meeting up with the walking definition of a sleaze ball, Blake, played convincingly by Eli Roth (he really loves to make cameos as dirt bags). Without spoiling the finale, the film ends on a moving, bittersweet note — as a long shot captures a Portland highway at dawn, as it conveys people both coming and going; an endless sea of human dramas.

While Aubrey Plaza has taken on a bunch of challenging roles in the last few years, none may be as fierce, provocative, and earnest as the lead character in writer-director John Patton Ford’s psychological thriller/crime drama Emily the Criminal (and that’s including her delightfully unhinged performance in Ingrid Goes West). The titular role here goes through such a grueling journey in this one — where she does everything in her power to “do the right thing” but nothing ever seems to work out — that, as a viewer, you’re really rooting for her to even come out of this one alive.

Emily Benetto (Plaza) is a talented artist who was forced to drop out of art school because of, well, money (now she’s in serious debt). Eking out a meager existence in Los Angeles (a place with a notoriously high cost of living), Emily snags low-paying catering gigs to sustain herself. As evidenced by an awkward job interview gone horribly wrong, she can’t obtain employment in the legit world because of a couple smears on her record (a DUI and an assault charge). Emily eventually gets suckered into working a phony credit card scam, introduced to her by her co-worker, Youcef (Theo Rossi). It’s a tidy little scheme, and the allure of quick, real cash is too much for Emily to walk away from. As one naturally expects, things spiral out of control for Emily, as the tantalizing siren song of even bigger payouts calls to her, which, of course, yield higher risks. Emily gets romantically entangled with Youcef, which ultimately leads to an intense moral decision that Emily is compelled to make. It’s the moral ambiguity of this situation that really gives Emily her layers. The film ends in a rather surprising manner, with a not-so-typical lesson dispensed — yet, it’s one that makes sense in the financially constrictive world that so many of us live in.

Some family trauma runs deep. That’s the obvious, major theme of writer-director Laurence Vannicelli’s thoroughly disturbing, pristinely acted film Mother, May I? It’s an extremely trippy, disturbing look at how tortuous moments of one’s past can inform just about everything happening in the present…and none of its good. It’s a polarizing film, with audiences starkly divided (some think it’s brilliant, others contend that it’s confusing psycho-gobbledygook), but critics pretty unanimously agreed that this is an intellectually rich, complex film (83% Tomatometer on RT).

Emmett (Kyle Gallner, starring in yet another mind-bender) has just inherited the house he grew up in — after the abrupt passing of his mother, Tracy (Robin Winn Moore). Emmett travels to the cabin with his gf, Anya (Holland Roden), with the sole goal of quickly selling the place and never thinking about it again. The issue is that no matter how hard Emmett may want to shelve the past for good, it always has a way of creeping back into his consciousness. Anya even loses her own personality and appears to become possessed by mom’s narcissistic, vengeful spirit. Roden’s take on this segment of her character is astoundingly, terrifyingly real. Alas, some films are more about a specific tone and feeling than the actual plot. Mother, May I? does have a cool (if somewhat befuddling) story, but it’s the dreadful setting and fearless performances that give it its full weight. It’s perhaps best to view this fine work with an open mind, to let the richness of the characters and the ambiguity of their struggles and oblique motivations fully blossom onscreen.

Subverting expectations. That’s what all good thrillers accomplish, especially the psychologically tinged ones. That’s the name of the game in writer-director JT Mollner’s stirring nail-biter Strange Darling (85% Popcornmeter, 96% Tomatometer on RT). The film begins with a seemingly familiar movie trope — that of the predatory male — and then things flip around in such a deliciously satisfying manner that it’s a real dark beauty to behold. Showcased with supersaturation and extra vivid colors, the whole movie is a subversion of the typical “famed killer” myth, the type of spooky story told around campfires, with dubious attachments to actual facts.

Another tale based in Oregon, the story begins with a woman, The Lady (Willa Fitzgerald), and a man, The Demon (Kyle Gallner, who really knows how to pick stellar projects), coming together for the first time at a seedy motel for a no-strings-attached romantic encounter. In The Demon’s car, the pair painstakingly goes over the “rules” of their hook-up, while also casually chit-chatting about whether either of them is a serial killer. It initially appears that The Demon just may be that kind of special sociopath… Then the action shifts, and splits into several different timelines (it sounds confusing, but it’s smartly presented and fun to follow along with), as a cat-and-mouse chase sequence propels the narrative forward (and laterally). The Lady runs into an older couple, Frederick (Ed Begley Jr.) and Genevieve (Barbara Hershey) who come off as friendly, but then are shown to be slightly mental believers in an impending “doomsday.” Without spoiling the zesty surprise too much, it’s eventually revealed that the person with truly nefarious designs is not who the audience initially was led to believe it was — and the very idea of a trustworthy narrator is often hard to come by.

Films that criticize religion often seem blasphemous to staunch believers and logical and necessary to the agnostic. Writer-directors Scott Beck and Bryan Woods’ wild ride of a faith-infused nail-biter, Heretic, doesn’t point a judgmental finger at only one religion, but rather at the very concept of faith in general. With changes in perspective throughout, the ultimate goal of this loopy, brilliantly constructed movie is not to show that all religions are based on fraudulent foundations, but rather that if one holds on to their faith (whatever that may be), they are actually more open and wiser than their wily, yet close-minded, foes.

The story starts out with two Mormon missionaries, Sister Barnes (Sophie Thatcher), who’s been around the block a bit in terms of real-life experiences, and Sister Paxton (Chloe East), who displays the wondrous naivety of a child. During a storm, they elect to hit up one more house in their mission of conversion. The home belongs to an ostensibly friendly older man, Mr. Reed (Hugh Grant, absolutely terrifying with his measured, crocodile smile). While the two girls are prohibited from entering a domicile unless a woman is present, Mr. Reed charms them into believing that his wife is just in the kitchen, baking a delicious-smelling blueberry pie. As the three of them engage in a polite, yet somewhat rousing conversation about the Mormon faith, it’s eventually divulged that Mr. Reed has alternate intentions than just a little faith-testing tête-à-tête. As the situation escalates into a tension-packed mind game where Mr. Reed attempts to deconstruct all religions and get the girls to see that all faiths were born of the same basic lie (while keeping them hostage in his maze-like house of horrors), the girls have to use their own wits to escape. What starts off as a rather cynical take on religion ultimately concludes with a beautifully illustrated message about staying true to what you believe to be morally just.

Four franchises, four completely different ways of getting underneath your skin. This is the one built out of your particular fear, why it works so well on you, and what it would leave behind.

Your nightmare does not wait outside the door. It waits for you to close your eyes, which you are going to have to do eventually, because nobody has ever won that particular argument.

Your nightmare is not really the doll. It is the face of the person you tell, and the way they decide, very kindly, that you have imagined all of it.

Your nightmare has your number, knows your name, and has almost certainly been inside your house before. The worst part is that you already know them.

Your nightmare is not really about you. It is about the people asleep down the hall, and the feeling that whatever is here is not interested in leaving.

As with everything that comes from the delightfully demented mind of writer (and here, director) Charlie Kaufman, I’m Thinking of Ending Things is no exception in terms of boundlessly creative weirdness. Yet, unlike his other works, this film begins in what appears to be one genre, that of the quirky comedy, morphs over the course of the movie (psychological thriller and even horror), and then concludes in the magical realism sphere. As the viewer experiences all the highs and lows of this meta-metaphorical roller-coaster, the final takeaway is a heartbreaking, highly thought-provoking one.

This odd film begins with a fun road trip set-up, where a Young Woman (Jessie Buckley, charmingly dynamic as usual) — who is sometimes referred to as Lucy, other times Louisa — narrates for the audience, and mostly muses about how she wants to “end things” with her beau, Jake (Jesse Plemons, borderline creepy, sad, and hilarious, as usual). The not-so-happy couple arrive at Jake’s parents’ farm house, where things quickly veer off into a surreal realm. Jake’s mom, Mother (Toni Collette), and papa, Father (David Thewlis), are quaint and hospitable at first, but then suddenly begin to age right before our very eyes — and take on a grotesque, almost horror movie chimera-like nature. The Young Woman and Jake eventually leave the bizarre house and hit the snowy road again. Then, things shift dramatically once more, and it’s eventually revealed that this whole scenario was just an elaborate fantasy that has been running through the mind of a depressed high school janitor (an elderly, even sadder manifestation of Jake). When the action morphs to that of a highly choreographed dance sequence, you know you’re in prime Kaufman territory; fiercely cerebral, ironic, and eerily lovely.

Mixing sci-fi and thriller genres can result in sensational films (Blade Runner, Alien, Minority Report), but this mash-up can also lead to some really outlandish stinkers. Thankfully, writer-director Drew Hancock did his homework and created one of the best films of the decade with Companion (93% Tomatometer). A Gen-Z homage to the robot sub-genre, this darkly hilarious film is a highly entertaining homage to Ex Machina, with a twist of Knives Out mystery stylishly stirred in.

While the charming and likable Jack Quaid plays the male lead, Josh, this is really Sophie Thatcher’s film. Her crown jewel of a performance as Iris, the lovely, obedient cyborg, is one of the highlights of recent film history. Thatcher rose to fame with her feisty character Natalie in Yellowjackets, (and, of course, in Heretic) but she really gets to showcase her full, wonderfully wide range here in Companion. The twist-filled story begins with Josh taking his new “girlfriend,” Iris, to meet his friends at a very remote, luxury cabin that’s owned by the Russian boyfriend, Sergey (Rupert Friend), of Josh’s “friend,” Kat (Megan Suri). While there are several clever hints weaved in, it’s not until the second act that Iris is revealed to be a highly sophisticated bot. The thing is, she has been programmed to “love” Josh, and will do seemingly anything for him. Thatcher is a master at making this machine feel simultaneously fabricated and emotionally grounded (Iris is genuinely curious about the world). Anyway, the next major twist reveals that Josh doesn’t have the purest of intentions, and he and his co-conspirators have engineered an entire plot to run away with Sergey’s copious cash stash. The fiery turns don’t stop there, but suffice it to say that we are all rooting for Iris by the end of this seriously smart, fundamentally fun flick.
