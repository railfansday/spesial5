Teens who eat more junk food may have more mental health and behavior problems

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

A review of 17 Australian studies linked diets high in fried foods, sweets and sugary drinks to poorer teen mental health and lower scores on some school tests.

Australian teenagers who more often ate fried foods and sweets and drank sugary drinks tended to have more mental health or behavioral problems.

A review of 17 Australian studies also linked similar eating patterns to lower scores on some school tests. Findings on body weight were mixed.

Yet sugary drinks and other high-calorie, low-nutrient foods are known to contribute to excess weight in children.

The studies were gathered in a new review. Its first author is Zegeye Abebe Abitew, a research associate at Flinders University in Adelaide.

The senior author is Yohannes Melaku, an associate professor at the Flinders Health and Medical Research Institute.

Food that packs many calories into a small serving is described by researchers as energy-dense.

“In these vital formative years of children’s growth and development, there are clear benefits of following a healthy diet high in fruit, veg, whole grains and fish compared to eating too many ‘energy dense’ fatty, sweet and highly processed grains, red meat and other foods,” Melaku said.

According to the authors, the findings add to the case for programs that promote healthy eating from early in life. The links were also described by them as suggestive rather than causal.

Most studies measured diet and health at a single point in time. Eight drew on the same long-running cohort in Western Australia.

Six research databases were searched by the team, and more than 1,200 articles were screened.

Seventeen studies met the criteria. These studies were published between 2008 and 2019. The children in them ranged from age 1 to age 19.

Studies that scored children’s diets against a set standard of healthy eating were left out. Abebe Abitew’s team kept studies that let the data show how children actually ate.

In each study, diets were recorded with food questionnaires, 24-hour recalls or food diaries. Statistics were then used to find the foods that tended to be eaten together.

Together, the 17 studies reported 39 eating patterns under many different names. These patterns were sorted into two groups by the reviewers.

Vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes and fish formed the core of the healthy patterns. Processed meats, refined grains, fried potatoes, sugary drinks, sweets and takeaway food were typical of the unhealthy ones.

Twenty of the 39 patterns fell in the unhealthy group. Each study then compared children who followed a pattern closely with children who followed it less.

Four of the studies dealt with mental health, and all four involved teenagers between 13 and 19.

The first came from Western Australia and involved teenagers aged 13 to 15. Withdrawn, anxious or depressed behavior was scored higher among those who ate more of the unhealthy pattern. Their scores for aggression and delinquency were higher too.

Teenagers from the same state were tracked for three years in a second analysis. An unhealthy pattern at age 14 was followed by more aggressive and delinquent behavior at age 17. This link was reported for girls.

Victoria supplied a survey of about 3,300 teenagers. Depression symptoms were more likely among the boys who ate more of an unhealthy pattern.

The fourth study drew on about 1,800 14-year-olds. A high score on the unhealthy pattern went with about twice the odds of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, or ADHD. For the healthy pattern, no such link with ADHD appeared.

Just one of these four studies tracked teenagers over time. Diet and mental health were recorded together in the remaining three. For that reason, those three can’t show which came first.

Three further studies dealt with thinking skills and school results.

About 600 teenagers in Western Australia took part in the first. Their diets were recorded at age 14, and their thinking skills were tested at age 17.

Reactions were slower among those who had eaten more of the unhealthy pattern. The same teenagers also made more errors when a maze learned earlier had to be recalled.

Fewer errors were made by those who had eaten more of the healthy pattern.

A South Australian study involved 315 children between the ages of 9 and 11. Children who ate more sweets, fried food and soft drinks scored lower in reading and numeracy.

Their spelling, grammar and punctuation scores were lower as well, and writing was the single exception.

The third study reported lower mathematics and reading scores in Western Australian teenagers. Again, the lower scores belonged to those who ate more of the unhealthy pattern.

A single combined estimate had been planned by the team. That plan was dropped because every study relied on its own tests and its own way of scoring diet. The review therefore offers no single figure for the size of the gaps.

Body weight, body fat and related conditions such as fatty liver disease were covered by nine of the 17 studies.

For most eating patterns, no connection with body fat turned up at all. One national study of children aged 2 to 15 is an example. Neither the healthy pattern nor the unhealthy one was linked with body fat there.

A national survey of 12- to 16-year-olds produced a result in the other direction. Overweight was somewhat less likely among boys who ate more of a high-fat, low-fiber pattern. Among girls, there was no link.

South Australian children aged 1 to 4 were the subject of another study. Obesity was tied to just one pattern there, and it was built on grains, fruit, vegetables and red meat.

The results had been different in the team’s earlier review of Australian adults. Eating patterns were consistently linked with heart and metabolic disease in that work.

Differences in method may help explain the mixed results in children, the authors wrote.

Studies that found no link with body fat were concentrated among those relying on short records. Only one or two days of eating were captured by such records.

A child’s diet changes from one day to the next, so a real link can be weakened by short records. A different tool was used in most of the Western Australian studies.

That questionnaire listed 212 foods and asked about the previous 12 months.

Eight of the 17 studies drew on the Raine Study. It began in Western Australia between 1989 and 1991 with 2,900 pregnant women. Their 2,868 children have been followed since before birth.

One group of children is shared by many of the studies. The evidence is therefore narrower than the count of studies suggests.

The authors wrote that the findings may not apply to all Australian children. First Nations children and those from culturally and linguistically diverse families were among the groups named.

A whole way of eating is described by a pattern. So the review can’t say which foods within it account for the links.

Some outcomes rest on a single study. One study of tooth decay in toddlers found no link with eating patterns. The same was true of one study of brain tumors in children.

Eleven of the 17 studies were rated as high quality by the team, and six as medium. Anxiety disorders, autism, sleep problems and diabetes were not examined in any of the 17.

Only 4.3% of Australian children aged 2 to 17 eat the recommended daily servings of fruit and vegetables. That figure is cited by the authors, and the servings are set by the Australian Dietary Guidelines.

One in three Australian children aged 8 to 13 has a mental health problem. That was found in a poll by the Royal Children’s Hospital in Melbourne.

The authors pointed to places to act. One is school meal programs, which have been tied to better learning and behavior in earlier research.

Another is the supermarket. About two-thirds of shelf space there goes to foods the guidelines say to limit.

More families have been left short of food by the recent rise in the cost of living, the authors wrote.

“While cost-of-living pressures can make healthy choices challenging, combining nutrition education with structural changes to improve affordability and access to healthy foods is essential for supporting children’s health and development,” Abebe Abitew said.

In none of the 17 studies was a child’s diet changed and the result then measured. So the review can’t say whether improving a child’s diet would improve mental health or grades.

According to the authors, future research should follow children for years. Diets should be recorded repeatedly with the same tools, and changes in diet should be tested directly.

The full study was published in the journal Current Nutrition Reports.
