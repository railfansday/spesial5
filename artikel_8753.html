Your First Gravel Ride Should Feel Like an Adventure—These 3 Tips Will Help You Enjoy It

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Gravel takes different prep than road riding—these basics help you ride off road with confidence.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

The more riders I talk to, the more I see people switching over to gravel bikes. I was leading a group ride for a shop in New Jersey and the group primarily consisted of road cyclists on their first gravel ride. The line of the day was, “this is so fun—it’s like being a kid on a bike.” Gravel is challenging, but it’s also a different type of fun once you get off paved roads.

Over the last few years, I’ve focused more on gravel riding than any other discipline of cycling. My lunch rides typically end up being on the road and my strictly fun rides end up being on the mountain bike. But I’ve been gravitating more and more to the gravel bike because it can do a little bit of everything.

I’ve taken gravel bikes on everything from 100-mile road rides with 10,000 feet of climbing to rides on singletrack trails where we should have been on a hardtail mountain bike at a minimum. On bikepacking routes, gravel day rides, and family rides towing my kids in their bike trailer, the gravel bike has been my go-to rig.

If you’re new to the sport, or even just getting into the category of gravel riding, it's easy to feel like there’s a mountain of knowledge needed as a barrier to entry. Here we’ll break down the top gravel tips you need to know to find success in those first few rides and beyond.

When I ran a shop this was the first place I would start with folks who were gravel curious. The touchpoints on a bike are both where you contact the bike and where the bike touches the ground. The pieces of gear to focus on here are: chamois, gloves, saddle, bar tape, and tires.

The chamois and gloves are the first two because they are the parts on your body that touch the bike. I don’t like to use gloves that are overly padded, but I do like gloves that have a good level of tackiness to help with staying gripped to the handlebars. This year I’ve been using the Most Days Full Finger Gloves from Handup for everything from century gravel group rides to hard gravel rides with plenty of singletrack. The chamois can be a little more subjective, but I almost always suggest going with chamois bibs over chamois shorts.

Bibs stay in place better than shorts and this is especially important from the movement you’re doing on a gravel ride. I’ve had plenty of people ask if they really need padded shorts for riding and the short answer is no. You can go riding with regular basketball shorts, but you’ll have a much better time riding with padded shorts or bibs. I like to compare it to riding a road bike on gravel. You can absolutely do that, and for a long time that’s what people did, but there’s a better way. My favorite pair are the Q36.5 Dottore Pro Bibs because they stay in place incredibly well and are comfortable from the first hour in the saddle to the 11th hour. The price point is a realistic hurdle for these Q36.5 bibs, but our Deputy Test Editor at Bicycling, Tara Seplavy, calls out this pair available on Amazon for just $40 in her roundup of cycling shorts.

I personally like a tape that has some tackiness while also offering a bit of cushion for some dampening after miles of chattering gravel. My favorite tape is the Lizard Skin DSP Ultra Bar Tape. It comes in a range of colors and thicknesses, but I usually go with the 2.7mm or 3.5mm for people riding gravel. If you want a little extra comfort over the miles size up to the 3.5mm.

When it comes to saddles this is much more subjective because everyone’s sit bones are different and your preferred saddle can change depending on your position on the bike. Personally I have different saddles on each of my bikes based on how I use that specific bike. The biggest note on saddles is to avoid going with something super wide and overly padded. This can be a desirable thing to reach for after you’ve developed a bit of soreness from your initial rides, but try to avoid the beach cruiser type of saddles. These can cause extra friction and that’s the last thing you want to add to your gravel ride.

Finally, we get to where the bike touches the ground—the tires. When someone is looking to add comfort and versatility to a gravel bike I usually recommend bumping up the tire size. Most bikes don’t come stock with the widest tire you can fit on the bike, so check the technical specifications for your bike to see how wide you can go. Some of my favorite gravel tires are the Pirelli Cinturato H tires and the Continental Dubnital tires for their versatility on a range of terrains.

This is a broad tip, but it is the most all-encompassing piece of advice I can give for gravel. Gravel riding burns more energy mile for mile and climb for climb compared to a road ride. Keep this in mind when you’re putting together your nutrition and hydration plan as well as when you’re building the route. It’s important to bring more calories than you think you need because you’ll likely be burning more calories compared to a road ride; it will take longer to get to the next refill stop compared to what you would expect, and those refuel spots are fewer and farther between on gravel roads and dirt trails compared to a road ride.

When it comes to setting up my route I always suggest riders have a way to navigate. When I got into gravel riding I used my phone for navigation with a handlebar mount on the bars. This worked initially, but because of the mud on some gravel routes, the possibility of falling, and how fast navigating on my phone killed the battery I quickly switched to a dedicated head unit. Head units that are capable of navigating reasonably well can range from a few hundred dollars to just shy of a thousand. Currently I’m testing the COROS Dura 2 Solar and I love the fact that battery life can last for days of solid run time. A dedicated bike computer head unit is something that’s nice to have as you start to adventure, but if you currently have a smartwatch you can likely use that to navigate for now. It’s more convenient having the head unit in front of you for navigation compared to a smart watch. Lately I’ve been trying a few different watches from Garmin and COROS as a substitute for a bike computer and once I set up turn-by-turn notifications I’ve gotten used to navigating by the watch. Plus I can use the watch for other activities like runs, family hikes, and the occasional paddle.

Flat tires will eventually happen on a gravel ride. Step one is to bring a good flat kit with a reliable set of tire levers, a multitool (with a chain breaker), at least one tube, and a pump for inflation. I also typically bring a dollar bill as a backup for a tire boot in the event that I really blow out my tire. Ever since I had to use fallen leaves as a tire boot I always carry something to cover a large hole in the tire.

Step two is knowing how to use the tools you brought. Bringing a chain breaker tool or tire levers are great, but they won’t be much help if you aren’t able to figure out how to use them. When someone was buying a new bike from me I used to encourage them to practice taking the wheels off the bike, tires off the wheels, tubes out of the tires, then putting it all back together. Getting some laps on this process from the comfort of your home will make the process feel much smoother when you’re trying to finish the flat fix on the side of the trail.
