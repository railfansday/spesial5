Why Couldn’t I Be Happy for Someone I Love?

With the holidays upon us, many of us will gather with loved ones, sharing platefuls of food, laughter, and important updates about our lives. If you’re recently engaged, expecting, or have received an impressive accolade at work, what better time to announce the news than when you’re all in the same room?

But hearing someone’s good news can be unexpectedly fraught, especially if you’re struggling. You might want to rejoice for them but find you can’t. By understanding why good news is uniquely powerful in making or breaking a relationship, you can navigate the holidays with self-compassion and avoid a sting from swelling into something bigger.

When we receive a friend’s bad news, we learn where we stand in their life. Finding out about our friend’s surgery through the grapevine, for example, reveals something about the closeness of the bond: the earlier we’re told, the closer we feel to that friend.

If you find out you’re not in their inner circle of confidantes, it can make you question how close your friendship even was: “Fluffy died, and you didn’t tell me? Am I even your best friend?”

Good news reveals closeness too. Researchers call it a norm of close friendship to share good news, so when we don’t, our friends may question how close we are. When we hide good news from others, research finds, they report feeling more insulted and less close to us.

Hearing that a dear friend is engaged, expecting, or has scored a promotion reshapes how we think about our own experience. While we may have felt at peace with our job title, hearing that a friend got promoted may make us suddenly feel inadequate. We compare our friend’s newly achieved status to our own, and make inferences regarding our value. Good news, then, forces us to confront our status.

Of course, this doesn’t happen all the time. If someone else’s positive news is in an area you don’t identify with, you can revel in their success. Your friend can win an award for being the best baker in the tri-state area. If you don’t care about being an elite baker, cheering them on doesn’t require you to measure yourself against them. However, if you also dream of being an accomplished baker, it will be much harder to be happy for them because of what their good news means for you.

Abraham Tesser’s Self-Evaluation Maintenance Model (1988) explains this well. His model predicts that when someone close to you outperforms you in an area central to who you are and what you care about, the comparison uniquely stings. That’s why we might take someone’s good news personally.

Sometimes the answer is simpler: I wanted it for myself. That’s envy, and it’s normal.

As you hear happy updates from others this holiday season, be gentle with yourself, as you might’ve just learned something new about your relationship with the other person. It’s normal to have complicated feelings in the moment.

If you felt excluded because you weren’t told when you expected to be, start by celebrating their news, if you can. Later, you can let them know you’d love to hear updates sooner: “I love hearing your news. I hope you know you can always come to me with things like this.” Opening the conversation this way will hopefully give you more insight into their thinking. It also helps to reflect on who heard first and why. Do those people play a bigger role in your friend’s life right now? Are there sensible reasons your friend told other people first? Perhaps this friend shares work updates with you, but keeps personal updates to another circle. Now you know how they regard your relationship, and you can recalibrate your expectations.

If you felt inadequate after hearing the news, you’ll probably need some time and space to process the jolt. For example, if your friend is having a baby and you’ve recently had a miscarriage, you can tell your friend, “I’m so happy for you, and I need a little time to take it in. I’ll call you this weekend.” If you simply wanted the thing for yourself, own it: I wanted that job. Envy points to something you want, and you can still show up warmly while the envious feeling settles.

By understanding why you’re struggling to be happy for people you love, you give yourself the best chance of keeping the relationship once the feelings pass.
