Reframing How to Manage Gen Z—Could the Problem Be Us?

In my new book, The Future Begins With Z—I challenge business leaders who report that young employees are the toughest age group to manage. I suggest we reframe the issue: Instead of complaining about their attitudes, unprofessional conduct, and disrespect, we should look for any kernels of truth and foresight into the future. It’s easy for older generations to assume the world is getting worse.

No doubt, there’s a tension at play—but I wonder if we’re solving the wrong problem.

Is Generation Z genuinely difficult to manage, or are they simply less willing to quietly accept workplace norms that previous generations were taught never to question?

It is easy to describe a generation as demanding or entitled when it asks for more feedback, greater flexibility, meaningful work, attention to mental health, and for leaders to listen. But a more revealing question is why we interpret these expectations as unreasonable rather than as signals that the traditional relationship between employee and employer is shifting.

Caleb would agree. After he graduated college, he went to work for a Fortune 500 company in New York, serving on the business development team. Caleb was a talented 22-year-old who quickly saw ways the company could improve their use of AI, as well as its HR and operational systems. His supervisor, however, wasn’t interested in his ideas and told Caleb to “pay his dues” before he offered any advice. In less than a year, Caleb quit his job and has since launched three businesses of his own. He’s now leading one that leverages AI to get work done more efficiently. I believe his first boss missed out on an incredible talent.

Nicki would agree. She was 21 years old when her supervisor exposed how little he knew about his young team member. He accused her of having a poor work ethic, since she never worked a minute past 5:00 pm. She had to explain that she left at 5:00 to rush over to a side hustle, since her salary wasn’t enough to pay the bills. (She worked another side hustle on weekends as well.) Like Caleb, Nicki left the company. This turnover will continue to happen until older managers stop stereotyping and get acquainted with their young employees.

Ashton would agree. He started working as a sixth grader but saw jobs differently than his much older boss. This 11-year-old left behind a structured lawn company to start his own business after noticing his neighbors struggling with dirty trash bins. He wondered: Would someone pay to have them cleaned? He discovered they would. Within months, Ashton’s new company was earning him a high income to put in savings, and his work went viral on TikTok, showing how a young leader solving a local problem can inspire everyone.

Reframing this issue has helped so many organizations I have served. Rather than treating Gen Z as a workplace problem to solve, I ask leaders to consider what this generation might be revealing about the future of work.

Perhaps it’s time we seek growth over comfort and familiarity. If we do, however, it will require intentionality. I’m in Orlando today, spending time with my new grandson, Max. He’s 9 weeks old. When he’s awake, he constantly fidgets and groans, making sweet little noises and movements. Doctors say babies often do this in search of comfort. Each move is a search for comfort for their bodies or food if they’re hungry. This is a natural urge humans have all our lives—to find comfort and satisfaction. Call it "the comfort zone."

Leaders, however, must resist this natural quest for comfort and seek growth and progress. In our day, I believe this means learning from our young instead of just lecturing them. Let’s reframe how we perceive our youngest teammates. Maybe, just maybe, they’ll lead us into a better future.
