Later Daters Still Love Sex

Among many destructive, ageist stereotypes prevalent in America, one of the most pervasive is that older adults aren’t interested in, capable of, or engaged in having sex. On the contrary, research shows that about three-quarters of those aged 57-64, over half of those aged 65-74, and a quarter of those aged 75-85 had sexual contact with someone in the past year. And this doesn’t even count those who would have chosen to have sex but had no opportunity.

While the stereotypes are alive and well regarding the nability of older adults to have sex, many, if not most, either do have, or are interested in having, sexual activity with a partner. It’s true older adults may struggle with aspects of sex they didn’t confront when younger—men with the ability to maintain erections or ejaculate; women with lubrication or discomfort. But there are excellent treatments for these conditions, and humans are creative. Older adults engage in satisfying romantic relationships without sexual activity, satisfying sexual relationships without romance, and everything in between. And in terms of sex, older couples not only enjoy intercourse, but also non-penetrative sexual activities like hugging, cuddling, sleeping naked; manual, masturbatory, and oral play activities, and more.

A recent study by Harris and Melanson (2026) interviewed 100 single older adults aged 60-83 (50 women and 50 men) recruited from dating sites for gender, racial, and educational diversity. More older adults are single today than ever before (due to longer lives and the acceptance of divorce), and this group is understudied compared to older adults with partners. The Harris and Melanson study was limited by heterosexual participants, but we can extrapolate many of its findings to all relationships:

Sex is a healthy part of life throughout adulthood, whether one is single, partnered, or in between. How it looks and when it happens may change, and some adults may do well without a partner, but the majority of older adults, like younger adults, find partnered sex an important ingredient of healthy social lives, life satisfaction, and wellbeing.

Harris and Melanson surveyed an often-ignored group of older adults—those who are single. Because they found them through dating sites, we know participants in the sample were at least somewhat interested in pursuing romantic and/or sexual partnerships. It’s up to future researchers to explore whether single older adults not interested in dating are equally interested in sex (with another person), or simply feel content alone; as well as how these results may be different in LGBTQ+ and other marginalized populations.

Harris, L., & Melanson, C. (2026). “The Shop Is Not Closed”: Sex and Sexuality Among Older Adult Daters. The Journal of Sex Research, 1–13. https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2026.2614315
