How House and Senate vacancies are filled, and what changes in each chamber

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Free daily briefing on global business news.

When a member of Congress dies or resigns, the Constitution, state law and chamber rules decide who fills the seat, who runs the office and how votes change

The Constitution treats an empty seat in the House of Representatives very differently from an empty seat in the Senate. A House district stays without a voting representative until its voters choose a replacement in a special election. In many states, a Senate seat can be filled by a governor's appointment before any ballot is cast.

That split comes from two parts of the Constitution. Article I, Section 2 sets the rules for House vacancies. The 17th Amendment, ratified in 1913, moved Senate elections to a popular vote and set the rules for Senate vacancies.

Understanding what happens when a member of Congress dies or resigns means following several tracks at once. The seat itself is only one of them. A departing lawmaker also leaves behind a staff, a constituent office, committee assignments and a floor vote that no one can cast.

Each of those pieces runs on its own set of rules. State law sets the timing of special elections, and the governor issues the writ that calls one. In the House, the Clerk takes over administration of a vacant office and its staff. The Secretary of the Senate does the same job for an empty Senate office. Constituents can still get help with casework while they wait for a new member.

Committee seats open up, and party and chamber processes decide who fills them. Seniority, which shapes committee standing in both chambers, does not pass to whoever wins the seat next. A successor does not inherit the predecessor's place in line.

Vote math shifts as well. In the House, a vacancy lowers the whole number of the chamber, the figure used to calculate a quorum. Most House votes are decided by a majority of members present and voting. The Senate keeps working with an empty seat, and the vice president votes only when senators are equally divided. In a closely divided chamber, a single vacancy can narrow a party's working margin.

Resignation follows a set procedure too. A member usually sends a letter to the state's governor and to the chamber's presiding officer, and can choose the date it takes effect. Final pay, retirement and survivor benefits follow separate federal rules.

A vacant seat in the U.S. House of Representatives can be filled in only one way: a special election. The Constitution gives no one the power to appoint a temporary replacement. That includes a governor, a political party and the House itself.

The rule comes from Article I, Section 2, Clause 4. It says that when a vacancy happens in a state's House delegation, the state's executive authority shall issue writs of election to fill it. In practice, that executive authority is the governor. A writ of election is the formal order that sets an election in motion.

The wording matters. The clause tells the governor to call an election, not to choose a person. The decision about who represents the district stays with its voters, just as it did for the member who left.

This design matches the House's original role. Article I, Section 2 says representatives are chosen by the people of the several states. From the start, the House was the one part of the federal government whose members were elected directly. An appointed House member would break that link between voters and their representative.

The process begins once the seat is formally empty. A member who dies, resigns or is expelled leaves the seat vacant. The Clerk of the House then notifies the executive of the member's state, which opens the path to a writ and a new election.

Until voters choose a successor, the district has no voting member in the House. No stand-in can cast floor votes or introduce bills on the district's behalf. Legislative representation for those residents waits for the special election.

The winner does not start a fresh two-year term. A special election fills only the rest of the departed member's term. The seat then appears on the ballot again at the next regular election.

Changing this arrangement would take more than a new law. The special election requirement is written into the Constitution itself. Allowing anyone to appoint a temporary House member would therefore require a constitutional amendment.

That makes the House different from the Senate, where a separate provision governs vacancies. Every House member who fills a vacancy arrives through the ballot box.

The 17th Amendment, ratified in 1913, sets the basic rule for an empty Senate seat: the state's governor must issue writs of election to fill it. It also lets each state legislature authorize the governor to appoint a temporary senator until voters choose a replacement.

That two-part structure explains why Senate vacancies are handled differently from one state to the next. The Constitution supplies the framework. Each state legislature decides how much of it to use.

The amendment grew out of a larger change. Under the original text of Article I, Section 3, state legislatures chose U.S. senators. The 17th Amendment moved that choice to the voters, who now elect senators directly. The vacancy clause follows the same logic. Voters pick senators in regular elections, and they also get the final say when a seat opens early.

Before the amendment, the Constitution handled vacancies another way. If a seat opened while a state legislature was in recess, the governor could make a temporary appointment. That appointee served until the legislature met again and filled the seat. The 17th Amendment replaced that arrangement with the writ-of-election requirement.

The amendment's text makes any appointment temporary. An appointed senator holds the seat only until "the people fill the vacancies by election as the legislature may direct." The appointment bridges the gap until an election and does not replace one. The legislature also directs how that election takes place.

States are not required to allow appointments at all. The power belongs to the legislature to grant or withhold. Some states fill Senate vacancies only by special election, leaving the seat empty until voters act. Others authorize the governor to appoint someone and then hold an election later.

Because the amendment leaves these choices to each state, the details vary. The timing of the special election, any conditions on the governor's pick and the length of an appointee's service all depend on state law. The constant is that an election eventually decides who holds the seat.

The winner of that special election generally serves the remainder of the departing senator's six-year term. Senate terms are staggered under Article I, and a vacancy does not reset the cycle for that seat. A senator who wins a special election and wants to stay must run again when the original term ends.

The amendment also included a transition provision. Its final paragraph said it would not affect the election or term of any senator chosen before it became part of the Constitution. Senators already picked by state legislatures finished their terms under the old system.

In most states, a governor can name a temporary senator to fill a vacant seat. That appointee serves only until voters choose a successor in a special election. Each state legislature decides whether its governor has that power and what limits come with it.

The authority comes from a single clause of the 17th Amendment. It lets a state legislature empower the governor to make temporary appointments "until the people fill the vacancies by election as the legislature may direct." That wording leaves the details to each state.

Some states let the governor pick anyone who meets the constitutional requirements. Others attach conditions. A number of states require the appointee to belong to the same political party as the senator who left. Some go further and require the governor to choose from a short list of names submitted by that party.

Any appointee must meet the same qualifications as an elected senator. Article I, Section 3 of the Constitution requires a senator to be at least 30 years old. The appointee must also have been a U.S. citizen for at least nine years and an inhabitant of the state when chosen.

The appointment becomes official through paperwork. The governor issues a certificate of appointment, which is presented to the Senate as the new member's credentials. The Senate holds the final say on seating. Article I, Section 5 makes each chamber the judge of the elections, returns and qualifications of its own members.

An appointee takes the same oath of office as an elected senator. Once sworn in, an appointed senator holds the same vote as any other member. The appointee can introduce bills and vote on legislation and nominations.

The appointment is temporary by design. State law sets when the special election takes place, and the winner serves out the remainder of the unexpired term. Some appointees run in that election to keep the seat. Others serve as placeholders and leave once the elected successor takes office.

Each state decides when and how its own House special elections run. The governor issues the writ that starts the process, but state election law shapes the calendar, the nomination method and the ballot. As a result, the wait for a new representative varies from state to state.

Federal law leaves the scheduling to the states. Under 2 U.S. Code Section 8, state law sets the time for holding an election to fill a House vacancy. Some states give the governor a fixed window in which to call the election. Others leave the governor wider discretion over the date.

Nomination rules differ just as much. In many states, voters choose party nominees in a special primary before the special general election. In others, party committees or conventions pick the nominees, and a single election follows. Some states use an all-candidate primary, in which every contender appears on one ballot regardless of party.

Those choices shape the calendar. A primary followed by a general election takes longer than a single election with party-selected nominees. Candidate filing deadlines, ballot printing and certification periods add further time. Ballots must also reach military and overseas voters early enough to be returned.

Some all-candidate systems add a runoff. If no candidate wins a majority in the first round, the top finishers meet in a second election. That step pushes the swearing-in date back further.

A governor may also place the special election on the same day as an already scheduled primary or general election. Combining the two spares local election offices the expense of running a separate vote. The trade-off is a longer stretch without a voting representative for the district.

The winner does not start a fresh two-year term. A special election fills only the remainder of the departing member's term, in the same district that member represented. Once the result reaches the House, the winner takes the oath of office and can begin voting. The seat then comes up again at the next regular election.

When a House seat becomes vacant, the Clerk of the House takes charge of the departed member's office and supervises its staff until a successor is sworn in. In the Senate, the Secretary of the Senate handles the administration of a vacant senator's office.

This arrangement keeps a district or state from losing access to help with the federal government while the seat sits empty. Phones stay answered, and constituent requests can still move forward in Washington and in local offices back home.

In the House, the authority comes from the chamber's own rules. They direct the Clerk to oversee the staff of a member who has died, resigned or been expelled. The Clerk does not represent the district or speak for its voters. The role is administrative, covering payroll, the office budget and the daily work of the remaining employees.

Casework is the main function that continues. A constituent who needs help with a Social Security claim, a veterans' benefit, a delayed passport or an immigration case can still contact the office. Staff can make inquiries with federal agencies for that person, much as they did before the vacancy.

The staff cannot act as a lawmaker. With no member in the seat, no one in the office casts votes, introduces bills or signs on as a cosponsor. Nobody in the office speaks for the district on pending legislation.

The staff jobs are temporary. Employees remain on the chamber's payroll during the vacancy, but a newly elected member picks their own team. The successor may keep some previous employees, hire new ones or combine the two.

The Senate follows a parallel path through the Secretary of the Senate, the chamber's chief administrative officer. In states where a governor can appoint a temporary senator, the appointee takes over the office and its staffing decisions once sworn in.

The member's own papers follow a separate rule. Congress treats a member's office papers as the member's personal property. Committee records, by contrast, belong to the chamber. After a death in office, the family or estate often decides where those personal files end up.

Timing also shapes the handoff. A member who plans a resignation can prepare staff and wrap up open cases in advance. A sudden death in office gives the staff no such warning.

A departing member's committee seats become vacant, and that member's seniority ends with the vacancy. Each party, working through its own internal rules and a vote of the full chamber, chooses who takes the open slots. A successor starts with no inherited rank and builds seniority from his or her own first day of service.

Committee membership belongs to the individual lawmaker, not to the district or the state. When a House member or senator resigns or dies, every assignment that person held opens up. That includes seats on full committees and on subcommittees. Congressional practice does not reserve those seats for whoever wins the special election or receives a governor's appointment.

In the House, each party fills its own committee slots. Republicans route assignments through their Steering Committee. Democrats use their Steering and Policy Committee. The full party conference or caucus then approves the selections, and the House adopts a resolution formally electing members to committees.

The Senate follows a similar path. Each party conference makes its selections, and the chamber ratifies them by resolution.

A party has options once a seat opens. It may give the slot to the incoming member, move a sitting colleague into it or leave it unfilled for a time. The parties negotiate the split of majority and minority seats on each committee, usually at the start of a new Congress. That ratio determines which party controls the vacant slot.

The departure of a committee chair carries more weight than the loss of a rank-and-file member. The majority party chooses a new chair through its own internal rules, followed by a House or Senate resolution. House rules also require each standing committee chair to designate a vice chair from the majority party. The vice chair presides over meetings and hearings when the chair is absent.

Seniority cannot be handed to anyone else. A successor's standing on a committee and in the chamber begins with his or her own swearing-in. That standing affects where a member sits on the committee dais and where the member falls in line for a future chairmanship or ranking-member post. In the Senate, seniority also sets the order in which senators choose office suites.

An appointed senator is treated the same way. Service counts from the day the appointee takes the oath, not from the day the former senator first took office.

A vacancy shrinks the whole number of the House, the base for calculating a quorum, but it leaves the rule for passing most bills unchanged. Those votes turn on a majority of members present and voting, not a majority of the full membership.

Article I, Section 5 of the Constitution makes a majority of each chamber a quorum to do business. Under House precedent, that majority is measured against members who are chosen, sworn and living. A seat left empty by death, resignation or expulsion drops out of the count.

The Speaker announces each vacancy and the adjusted whole number from the floor. With all 435 seats filled, a quorum is 218 members. With one vacancy, the whole number falls to 434, and a quorum stays at 218. With two vacancies, the whole number is 433, and a quorum drops to 217.

Delegates and the resident commissioner of Puerto Rico sit outside this calculation. They do not count toward a quorum of the full House. They also cannot vote on final passage of legislation.

Passing a bill follows a separate rule. A measure succeeds when the yeas outnumber the nays among members who vote. A tie defeats it. Members who are absent or who answer "present" do not affect the margin. An empty seat has the same effect on the tally as a member who skips the vote.

The practical effect shows up in party margins. When a vacancy falls in the majority party's ranks, that party has one fewer vote to spare on a measure that splits along party lines. A vacancy in the minority party gives the majority more room on the same kind of vote.

Some decisions require more than a simple majority. Passing a bill under suspension of the rules needs two-thirds of those present and voting. Overriding a presidential veto requires two-thirds of members voting in each chamber, with a quorum present. In both cases, vacant seats are left out of the math.

The election of a Speaker has its own arithmetic. The winning candidate needs a majority of the votes cast for a person by name. Vacancies, absences and "present" votes all lower the number of votes needed to win the gavel.

The House presumes a quorum is present unless a member raises a point of order that one is not. A quorum call or recorded vote then measures attendance against the adjusted whole number.

A Senate vacancy does not stop the chamber from working. The Senate keeps debating and voting with fewer than 100 members, and the vice president still casts a vote only when senators are equally divided.

The tiebreaking rule comes from Article I, Section 3 of the Constitution. It names the vice president as president of the Senate but gives that officer no vote unless the chamber is evenly split. A vacancy leaves that limit unchanged.

A vacancy does change the arithmetic around a tie. With 99 senators in office, a vote in which every senator takes part cannot split evenly. One side will always hold at least one more vote than the other. Ties remain possible when senators are absent or decline to vote, so the vice president's role does not disappear.

The Senate's quorum also adjusts. Senate Rule VI defines a quorum as a majority of the senators duly chosen and sworn. An empty seat lowers that base. With 99 senators serving, 50 make a quorum, compared with 51 when all 100 seats are filled.

Final passage of most bills requires a simple majority of senators voting. Under that standard, a vacancy matters most through its effect on party balance. Take a Senate split 50-50, where the vice president breaks party-line ties. If one side loses a member, the other side holds 50 of the 99 filled seats. With full attendance, that side can win a party-line vote without the vice president.

Ending a filibuster follows a different rule. Senate Rule XXII sets the cloture threshold for most measures at three-fifths of the senators duly chosen and sworn. With 99 senators serving, three-fifths still rounds up to 60 votes. A single vacancy leaves that usual bar in place.

The affected state keeps one voice in the chamber. Every state holds two Senate seats regardless of population. A single vacancy cuts a state's Senate delegation in half until the seat is filled. Its remaining senator continues to vote on the floor. The empty seat casts no vote until a successor, whether appointed or elected, takes the oath of office.

A member of Congress resigns by putting the decision in writing. The letter usually goes to two recipients: the governor of the member's home state and the presiding officer of the chamber where the member serves.

Each recipient has a separate role. The governor is the state's executive authority. That is the official who issues the writ of election that starts the process of filling the seat. The presiding officer informs the chamber that one of its members is leaving.

In the House, the presiding officer is the Speaker. In the Senate, it is the vice president, who holds the title of president of the Senate under the Constitution.

The departing member picks the effective date. A resignation can take effect immediately, on the day the letter is delivered. It can also take effect on a specific future date named in the letter.

That choice controls when the seat legally becomes empty. The vacancy begins on the effective date. It does not begin on the day the member announces plans to leave or on the day the letter is written.

Until the effective date arrives, the lawmaker remains a sitting member. A member who sets a future date keeps the powers of the office in the meantime, including the right to vote on the floor and in committee.

The chamber does not hold a vote to approve a resignation. Colleagues and party leaders have no power to accept or reject it. The written notice is the act that matters.

The letter itself is usually short. It typically states the decision to resign and the date it takes effect. A member is not required to give a reason.

In the House, a resignation letter is typically laid before the chamber and printed in the Congressional Record. That creates a public, official record of the departure and its timing.

Resignation differs from expulsion. Under Article I, Section 5 of the Constitution, either chamber can expel a member only with a two-thirds vote.

A member's congressional salary stops when service ends, whether that happens through resignation or death. Retirement benefits a member has already qualified for are not erased by leaving early. A deceased member's family may qualify for survivor payments under federal retirement and Social Security rules.

Pay follows the same basic logic as other federal salaries. It is earned for time in office. A member who resigns is paid through the effective date of the resignation and no further. A successor is paid for that person's own service, not for the stretch when the seat sat empty.

Retirement coverage depends on when a member first entered federal service. Members who first arrived in 1984 or later are generally covered by the Federal Employees Retirement System, known as FERS. That system combines three parts: Social Security, a basic annuity and the Thrift Savings Plan. The Thrift Savings Plan is a 401(k)-style investment account. Members who entered earlier may be covered by the older Civil Service Retirement System.

Leaving office does not cancel a pension a member has already earned. A member needs at least five years of service to be eligible for a congressional annuity. A House member who serves a single two-year term therefore does not earn one. Eligible former members draw benefits once they meet their system's age and service requirements. Money a member contributed to a Thrift Savings Plan account stays with that person after departure.

A death in office brings a different set of provisions. Surviving spouses and eligible children can receive survivor benefits under the retirement system the member belonged to. The amount and eligibility depend on factors such as the member's length of service. Members also pay into Social Security. Their families may qualify for Social Security survivor benefits under the same rules that apply to other workers.

Congress has a long-standing custom of approving a separate death gratuity for a deceased member's survivors. The payment is not automatic. Lawmakers have traditionally provided it through legislation, often as a provision in an appropriations measure.

Members can also enroll in the Federal Employees' Group Life Insurance program, which pays out to the beneficiaries they name. None of these benefits pass to the member's successor. The new member starts building service credit and retirement eligibility from the first day in office.
