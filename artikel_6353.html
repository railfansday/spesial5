Scientists built a wearable device meant to let a friend in another city smell your coffee

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

An electronic nose names a food from its fumes, a message crosses the internet, and a wearable puffs out the matching scent, so far only in a lab.

Sending a friend a picture of your coffee takes a second. The smell stays in the kitchen, however, because phones carry pictures and sound but nothing for the nose. A new wearable device now gets around that.

A sensor sniffs the coffee and software recognizes it. Then a short message tells a device on your friend’s wrist to release a coffee smell that it already holds.

What crosses the internet is a label rather than the smell itself, and that is both the trick and the limit. The system is a laboratory prototype.

It knows five smells so far: beer, cake, coffee, orange, and red wine. It reproduces each one from a scented liquid loaded into the device ahead of time.

Engineers at Ohio State University built the system and named it Scentronic. Chunyu Yang, a doctoral student in the University’s Department of Materials Science and Engineering, is the first author.

Jinghua Li’s group ran the work with colleagues at Zhejiang Laboratory in China.

In an interview with Earth.com, Yang described what the device is for. “Scentronic has the potential to allow people to share smells with others over long distances, much like we currently share images, sounds, and videos,” he said.

“For example, someone could share the aroma of their coffee with a friend in another city.”

The sniffing end is a ring of six gas sensors around a spot where the food sits. Each sensor is a heated chip of tin dioxide.

Vapors from food, such as the alcohol in beer and wine, change how easily electricity flows through the sensor. Engineers call a sensor array like this an electronic nose.

No single sensor can tell coffee from cake. The six together produce a pattern, so the team trained a pattern-matching program on it.

They recorded each of the five foods 50 times, for 200 seconds at a stretch. That made 250 recordings, one brand of each: the cake was a bag of King’s Hawaiian sweet rolls.

On recordings the program had not seen before, the best version named all five foods correctly.

Coffee gave the faintest signal, and the authors wrote that many of the compounds behind its aroma may not be the kind that these sensors detect well.

Once the program decides on coffee, that word is all that travels. It goes to a program on a cloud server and on to the wearable, a trip that took about 65 thousandths of a second in the team’s tests.

Recognizing the food takes longer, from several seconds to a few tens of seconds.

The wearable is built around a thin disk that vibrates about 110,000 times a second. A cotton pad feeds it scented liquid from a small chamber, and the vibration pushes that liquid through tiny holes as a fine mist.

Switching the vibration on and off sets how much mist comes out.

A wristband version of the device holds two smells. A necklace holds five, lined up, as the authors wrote, “like pearls on a string.” Run flat out, a cartridge empties in under five minutes, and at the lowest setting it lasts more than 35 minutes.

After an hour of continuous use, the warmest spot on the device measured 96.4°F (35.8°C), below body temperature.

Sixteen volunteers from the University kept their noses 8 inches (20 centimeters) from a device while a smell of orange rose through ten strength levels, and said when they noticed it.

The middle level was where the most volunteers first noticed it, and the spread formed a bell shape, which told the team which settings people can actually smell.

Fourteen volunteers then took tests built like a standard smell exam. After learning the five food scents from labeled tubes, they identified single scents from the device about 73 percent of the time.

They named both parts of a two-scent mixture about 71 percent of the time. The hardest test was ranking five strengths of an alcohol scent from weakest to strongest, and they got about 59 percent of those right.

The volunteers were smelling scents released in the same room. Earth.com asked Yang what surprised him when volunteers first received a smell sent from a distance.

“These were separate experimental evaluations, and we did not specifically investigate volunteers’ reactions to receiving odors transmitted remotely,” he said.

Medicine is the use the authors focus on. Head injuries, infections, and diseases of the brain can dull, distort, or erase the sense of smell, and smell problems became a common aftereffect of COVID-19.

Today’s clinic tests use scratch-and-sniff cards or scented pens at a few fixed strengths.

An adjustable device, the authors wrote, could let a clinician raise an odor step by step, over the internet, until a patient notices it. That would find the threshold without an office visit.

A patient who needs a strong puff to notice anything has a weakened sense of smell, the kind of loss seen after COVID-19.

One who notices a faint puff that others miss may be oversensitive, which happens during migraines and pregnancy. The National Institutes of Health describes these smell disorders.

The team also built a cup with five scent cartridges in its lid and five small pumps in its wall, each pump holding a flavoring liquid.

When the electronic nose smelled beer, coffee, or the two together, the cup released the matching aroma and dripped the matching flavor into the water inside.

In a second demonstration, a person wearing a Meta Quest 3 headset reached for a virtual beer or coffee on a virtual shelf. The real cup then dispensed that drink’s flavor and smell.

The authors wrote that today’s virtual reality headsets focus on sight and sound, while smell and taste shape how real an experience feels.

The authors also wrote that making food seem to taste better could matter for patients with nutritional problems, for astronauts facing unappealing food supplements, and for soldiers eating field rations.

Five smells is a small vocabulary, and the authors wrote as much. The device reproduces a category, coffee rather than this particular coffee, from a liquid chosen in advance, and it doesn’t match the chemistry of the original.

Yang told Earth.com that the number of odors was limited mainly by the time it took to collect training recordings. Adding more, he said, depends on how sensitive and selective the sensors are and on how complex the mixtures get.

The human tests were simple, with few control conditions, and the authors wrote that this could introduce bias. Each food came from a single brand, so whether the program would recognize a different coffee is untested.

Next steps the team listed include working with smell researchers under controlled conditions and recording brain signals while the device delivers a scent.

“Scentronic is currently a laboratory prototype,” Yang said, and “we expect it will take several years, potentially longer, before such devices become commercially available.”

Until then, every smell the device has sent was sent and received inside a lab.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
