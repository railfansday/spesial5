Tiny sparrows hold ancient clues to human heart disease

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

A detailed map of sparrow heart cells reveals similarities with human hearts and clues to how birds adapt to life at high altitude.

A tiny sparrow has more in common with us than you’d think – at least when it comes to the heart.

Scientists have built the first single-cell atlas of a wild bird’s heart, analyzing more than 200,000 cells and nuclei from newborn and adult Eurasian tree sparrows.

What they found is a remarkably conserved cardiac blueprint.

Similar core cell types, gene activity patterns, and disease-linked pathways – shared across species separated by 300 million years of evolution.

The study was led by researchers at the Institute of Zoology at the Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Scientists compared heart cells from sparrows living at sea level in Cangzhou (10 meters altitude) and high on the Tibetan Plateau at Gahai (3,400 meters).

The team, led by Yan Hao, Xiang Zhou, and Qingshuo Zhao, sequenced 168,634 neonatal cells and 33,694 adult nuclei.

Eleven major cardiac cell types showed up in sparrow hearts. These include cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and immune cells.

That’s similar to what we find in mice and humans. But the proportions shift dramatically between life stages.

Cardiomyocytes, the heart’s muscle cells, accounted for 22% of the neonatal sample and 11% of the adult sample.

Endothelial cells, which line blood vessels, accounted for 13% of the neonatal sample and 31% of the adult sample.

The baby heart is built for rapid growth. The adult heart is built for endurance – a dense network to keep blood and oxygen flowing under sustained demand.

Using a computational tool called CAME, the researchers mapped sparrow heart cells against those of mice and humans. Matching rates ranged from 86.7% to 99.5%.

Cardiomyocytes were the most conserved cell type of all, with cross-species gene expression correlations between 0.57 and 0.62. That’s a tight overlap for animals that last shared an ancestor before dinosaurs went extinct.

Fibroblasts and endothelial cells were more species-specific, with human-sparrow correlations dropping to 0.23–0.24.

That makes biological sense. Heart muscle cells must meet strict demands for electrical signaling and contraction.

Supporting cells like fibroblasts have more freedom to adapt to each species’ particular mechanical environment.

The team didn’t stop at cataloging cell types. They asked whether the genes conserved across species also happen to be the ones that go wrong in human heart disease.

The answer was a strong yes. In adult hearts, genes linked to known human cardiac pathologies showed tight expression correlations between tree sparrows and both mice and humans.

The sparrow-human correlations (0.53 to 0.66) were statistically comparable to mouse-human correlations (0.52 to 0.72).

That’s a remarkable finding. The similarities suggest that sparrows could help researchers investigate biological pathways involved in human heart disease.

Sparrows living at 3,400 meters face a persistent shortage of oxygen, known as chronic hypoxia.

Their hearts are larger, with more blood vessels, a typical high-altitude adaptation.

At the cellular level, those adaptations diverge sharply by age. Adult high-altitude cardiomyocytes showed 191 differentially expressed genes, mostly tied to cardiac muscle development.

Neonatal ones showed only 22, focused instead on energy metabolism. Just two genes overlapped.

While baby hearts at altitude appear to prioritize energy metabolism under limited oxygen, adult hearts emphasize structural changes. So they respond to the same stressor differently depending on life stage.

Among the altitude-adaptation genes, one stood out: IGF1R. It encodes a receptor in the insulin-like growth factor pathway and sits at a hub connecting MAPK signaling, cytoskeletal organization, and cardiac muscle contraction.

It’s already known to drive heart enlargement in mice and humans. The researchers found it upregulated in high-elevation sparrow cardiomyocytes too.

To test whether it actually causes hypertrophy in birds, they overexpressed IGF1R in chicken cardiomyocyte cultures.

The cells grew significantly larger, with elevated levels of hypertrophy markers NPPA and ACTA1.

Gene activity changes were consistent with involvement of the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway.

Growth-promoting genes like BDNF, BCL2, and MYC went up.

The study used different sequencing technologies for neonates and adults, which could introduce technical artifacts when comparing life stages.

The IGF1R experiments were done in cell culture, not living birds. So the gene is a plausible contributor to high-altitude heart growth, but not yet a confirmed driver in vivo.

And while the sparrow-human gene correlations are impressive, they were somewhat weaker in neonatal hearts. This suggests developmental trajectories diverge more than adult endpoints do.

The bigger picture is about what hearts share after 300 million years of going their separate evolutionary ways.

Birds and mammals independently evolved four-chambered hearts as well as endothermy. And now it turns out they built them with strikingly similar molecular machinery.

That shared regulatory logic means sparrows could become a useful model for human cardiovascular disease.

The study is published in the journal The Innovation Life.
