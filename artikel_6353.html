Some of the most effective treatments for back pain are also the most affordable

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

A review involving nearly 15,000 people suggests that several drug-free treatments for lower back pain can improve health outcomes and reduce overall costs.

Treatments such as yoga, acupuncture, and cognitive therapy might seem like added expenses for people with lower back pain.

However, a new review suggests that these approaches can improve health outcomes while reducing overall costs.

Researchers reached that conclusion after reviewing 20 papers covering 24 studies and 14,884 people.

Eight treatments that use no drugs and no surgery were both more effective and less costly than usual care in at least one way of counting the costs.

The authors wrote that these treatments are still far from routine in most countries.

Manuela Ferreira, a professor at the George Institute for Global Health in Sydney, was the senior author. Her team included researchers at the University of Sydney and the University of Warwick in England.

“Low back pain is the world’s leading cause of disability, but many effective treatments can also save healthcare dollars,” Ferreira said.

“Our review found that approaches such as exercise, yoga, acupuncture and psychological support can help people recover while providing good value for money.”

Pain in the lower back is the leading cause of disability worldwide, and treating it costs a lot. In Australia, back problems accounted for about 2.2% of all health spending in the 2020 to 2021 financial year.

The team searched ten research databases for studies that compared what a treatment cost with what it achieved. They kept only treatments backed by at least one of three major guidelines.

Those come from the World Health Organization, the United Kingdom’s National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, known as NICE, and the North American Spine Society.

Benefit was measured in a unit that combines how long a person lives with how well they live during that time. Health economists call it a quality-adjusted life year.

The team converted every cost to 2024 British pounds and used the same cutoff NICE uses. A treatment counted as worth paying for if each extra year of healthy life cost less than £20,000.

Twenty papers published between 2005 and 2026 met those criteria.

They covered 24 studies, 55 comparisons with usual care, and 14,884 people in nine high-income countries, most often the United Kingdom.

Usual care in these trials meant ordinary first-contact care: a doctor’s advice, a booklet, some medication, and sometimes a referral to a physical therapist.

Six single treatments came out both more effective and less costly than that.

They were yoga, acupuncture, cognitive therapy, support for people managing their own pain, education aimed at reducing fear of the pain, and mindfulness-based stress reduction.

Two combinations did the same: workplace advice paired with a gradual return to activity, and a program of diet and physical activity.

In an interview with Earth.com, Ferreira was asked what someone with back pain should ask a doctor first. She suggested two questions.

“Is there anything more serious causing my back pain? If not, which is the case for most people, can you teach me or refer me to someone who can teach me how to manage my own symptoms?” she said.

In health economics, a treatment that is both more effective and less costly is described as dominant.

In the whole review, only one comparison went the other way, and that was a British massage program.

Massage cost about £324 more per person than usual care in 2024 money, and it gained no extra healthy time at all.

Whether a treatment saves money depends on whose bills get counted.

The review sorted every result three ways: the health system’s costs, an insurer’s or employer’s costs, and society’s costs, which add in lost wages and missed work.

A British yoga trial shows why that matters. In it, 313 people with chronic or recurring back pain were offered 12 weekly group classes.

Counting only health care, the classes added about £715 per person, and each extra year of healthy life cost about £19,300, just under the £20,000 bar.

Counting lost workdays too, the yoga group cost society about £293 less per person than the usual-care group. And the yoga group still gained more healthy time.

The biggest saving in the review came from a Dutch program for workers on sick leave with chronic back pain.

It paired workplace advice with a gradual return to activity, and it cut society’s costs by about £7,315 per person compared with usual care. An Australian cognitive therapy program cut them by about £2,671.

Savings like those count only when lost work is part of the sum.

A treatment did not have to save money to be considered cost-effective.

Counting the health system’s costs, 14 of 18 comparisons passed, including exercise alone, spinal manipulation, and exercise paired with cognitive therapy.

Counting society’s costs, 15 of 19 passed, and counting an insurer’s or employer’s, 11 of 14 did. Passing was the rule, but confidence in each pass varied.

Where a study reported how likely its treatment was to be good value, the figure ran from 50% to 99.6%.

One of the clearest savings came from an enhanced course of acupuncture in the United States, with extra sessions added to a standard 12-week course. It cut health care costs by about £396 per person.

Acupuncture and massage were tested in many of the studies, and their results were inconsistent.

The authors wrote that this points to context, who is paying, and how the treatment is delivered. A Belgian modeling study found osteopathy was dominant from a payer’s perspective.

The three modeling studies in the review met far fewer items on their quality checklist than the trials met on theirs, though. The weakest of them met two of 57 items.

A review in 2011 of 26 studies reached a similar verdict on some of these treatments, including exercise, acupuncture, and cognitive behavioral therapy.

Nine new economic evaluations have come out since the most recent review of the topic, in 2019.

The authors wrote that theirs is the first to cover every treatment the current guidelines recommend, measured against usual care.

Ferreira’s team wrote that yoga, acupuncture, cognitive behavioral therapy, and treatments aimed at reducing fear and stress still aren’t routinely offered or paid for in most countries.

Working out why, the team wrote, should be the next focus.

Asked what surprised her most, Ferreira said it was that these treatments are not always available to patients.

“I was pleased to see so many options can benefit both patients and society,” Ferreira noted.

“The good news for people with back pain is that some of the most effective treatments are also among the most cost-effective.”

“Investing in evidence-based care such as exercise, physical therapies and psychological support could help more people get better while reducing pressure on health systems.”

Every one of the 24 studies came from a high-income country, so the authors wrote that their findings are unlikely to carry over to lower-income settings.

Ferreira said testing them in poorer countries would take new trials and studies with local patients, clinicians, and policymakers.

Those would check that the treatments are safe, suited to local conditions, and acceptable.

Costs are tied to the health system that recorded them, and the studies measured them too differently to pool into one estimate.

Some people were left out by design. Studies of sciatica, where the pain runs down the leg, weren’t included, and neither were people who had back surgery in the past year.

Out-of-pocket costs, community services, and lost work were reported less often than the treatment’s price.

The larger gap is in what the review had nothing to compare. Not one of the studies that met its criteria tested a drug, an injection, or an operation for chronic pain in the back. Those treatments are paid for all over the world.

“We have not found studies on guideline endorsed pharmacological or surgical interventions for low back pain and therefore highlight the urgent need for those as well,” Ferreira told Earth.com.

The authors wrote that future studies should count lost work and community costs too.

The full study was published in the journal PLOS Medicine.
