Compared to window or portable air conditioners, ductless systems are more permanent, more powerful, and typically quieter. They also give you more control over specific rooms, so you can cool the spaces you actually use instead of the whole house at once. There are some drawbacks, though.

Ductless ACs run pricey (even setting aside the cost of installation). They’re also more visible than central air vents, and you need to size and place them carefully to cool a room evenly. DIY installs or wrong-sized units can cause issues in the long run.

Maintenance can also be difficult, as not all HVAC professionals are familiar with these products. However, for an older home with radiator heat (like mine is), it’s the best way to add efficient air conditioning without blocking most of our windows.

First, decide how many indoor units you need and where you’ll place them. Unlike window units, mini-split systems are permanent fixtures, so carefully consider where they’ll be most efficient within your home.

Generally, a single ductless air conditioner can handle cooling a targeted area or a single room. If you’re cooling down multiple rooms, consider a multi-zone system so that you get even temperatures throughout your home.

It’s also essential to select a unit that’s powerful enough for the room it’s in—too small and it may not cool your space. The cooling capacity of air conditioners is typically measured in British Thermal Units, or BTUs, per hour.

As a general rule of thumb, choose a unit that delivers 20 BTUs per square foot of living space. So, a 200-square-foot room needs at least a 4,000-BTU air conditioner. Those sensitive to noise should consider units with a quiet mode feature.

It’s also important to consider the electrical system in the area you plan to install the mini-split. Many larger mini-splits require 208- to 230-volt power, which works with both commercial 208V electrical service and the 240V circuits typically used for major appliances in homes, while smaller systems may run on standard 120V outlets without any needed upgrades.

Quieter units are more desirable for bedrooms, living rooms, and nurseries, where a peaceful environment is essential. Look for ductless air conditioners with noise levels between 19 and 30 decibels, since this range can be compared to the sound of leaves rustling or a soft whisper.

Units with levels between 30 to 40 decibels are still suitable for other areas of the home, since this range is comparable to background noise or casual conversation.

While shopping for your ductless air conditioner, you may see “SEER” with a number next to it. SEER stands for Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio, which measures how much energy your air conditioner uses during the annual cooling season. The higher the SEER rating, the less energy your unit uses to cool your home.

You may also see “SEER2,” a newer efficiency standard introduced in 2023 that uses updated testing conditions meant to better reflect real-world performance. SEER2 numbers are typically slightly lower than SEER ratings for the same unit, but they represent the same idea: higher numbers mean more efficient energy usage.

A product with an Energy Star certification has been rigorously tested by the United States Environmental Protection Agency for its energy efficiency. EPA-recognized laboratories test products to Energy Star specifications to ensure manufacturers produce energy-saving products.

Although testing guidelines change per product category, all items must prove they consume less energy than standard models without compromising performance.

Mini-splits are more permanent and expensive than window or portable air conditioners, so they make the most sense when you plan to cool the same space for several seasons. Most systems also require professional installation, and larger units may need electrical upgrades before you can install them.

They’re also not invisible. The indoor air handler usually stays mounted high on the wall year-round, which may bother homeowners who prefer the low-profile look of central air vents. Proper sizing and placement matter, too, since an oversized, undersized, or poorly positioned unit can lead to uneven cooling, wasted energy, or poor humidity control.

Ductless air conditioners cool your home without using the network of ducts that central HVAC systems rely on, says Lincoln. “The way I explain ductless cooling to homeowners is simple: the system conditions the room directly instead of trying to send cooled air through ductwork first,” she explains.

Instead, ductless air conditioners connect a small outdoor condenser to one or more indoor air handlers using refrigerant lines. Because there’s no ductwork involved, these types of units are often more efficient and easier to install in older homes, additions, or finished spaces where running ducts would be difficult. They also allow for “zoned” cooling, meaning you can control the temperature in individual rooms instead of conditioning the entire house at once.

These systems are also more customizable than a whole-house system. “Where mini-splits really shine is zoning,” explains Lincoln. “One outdoor unit can support multiple indoor heads, so different parts of the house can be conditioned based on how those spaces are actually used.”

Both describe systems that pair an outdoor condenser with one or more indoor air handlers connected by refrigerant lines instead of ductwork. The “split” part refers to the system being divided between indoor and outdoor components.

Some mini-splits provide cooling only, but many modern models are actually heat pumps, meaning they can both cool and heat your home using the same system. Adding air conditioning to an older home that previously utilized radiator or baseboard heat can be tricky—squeezing ductwork into closets and unused corners feels a bit like remodelling Jenga. These units solve that issue without much fuss.

“Installation is where good equipment gets made—or wasted,” advises Lincoln. “Mini-splits look deceptively simple once they’re on the wall, but installation is not casual work. Companies size mini-splits systems based on BTUs, which measure how much heating or cooling power the unit can deliver.

“When people ask how to calculate the right BTU for a room, the honest answer is that manufacturer charts are a starting point, not a finish line,” says Lincoln. “For a window unit, those charts can get you close. For a mini-split, a proper load calculation is the better answer.” This is another reason it’s great to work with an expert installer before ordering—they can guide your purchasing based on your needs.

Lincoln explains that it’s not a straightforward calculation, in most cases. “Room size is only the starting point. Sun exposure, ceiling height, insulation, window size, window direction, occupancy, and heat from electronics or appliances all change the cooling load,” she adds.

Smaller systems around 9,000 BTU often work for bedrooms or small offices, while 12,000–18,000-BTU systems handle larger living areas. As a rough rule, most homeowners look at units sized for the square footage of the space they want to heat or cool, but must consider all of these other factors, too.
