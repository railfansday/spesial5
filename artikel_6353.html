Breast cancer leaves signs in blood that may hint at its return

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

A new blood test showed promise for spotting breast cancer and predicting relapse, but larger studies remain essential.

Mammograms can sometimes miss breast cancer in women with dense breasts. A new blood test called EPINUC showed promise in detecting breast cancer regardless of breast density.

The test also found signs linked to cancer returning within five years.

Larger studies are needed to confirm whether it can help detect cancer early and predict its return.

The test was developed in the lab of Efrat Shema at the Weizmann Institute of Science.

For the new study, her team worked with Guy Ron, a physicist at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. Ron’s group belongs to the university’s Racah Institute of Physics.

The cancer status of every woman was already known when her blood sample was analyzed. The test has not yet been applied to women undergoing routine screening.

Inside a cell, DNA is wound around protein spools. Each spool, with its loop of DNA, is called a nucleosome.

Small chemical tags are added to the spool proteins. These tags help set which genes the cell uses, and biologists call them epigenetic tags.

Dying cells, whether healthy or cancerous, release nucleosomes into the bloodstream. The tags stay on.

Tumor DNA is the target of several blood tests for cancer. In Shema’s method, the tagged spools are instead examined individually under a microscope.

Each type of tag is marked with a fluorescent label of a different color. Tags that occur together on a single spool can therefore be identified.

The test needs less than 1 milliliter of plasma, the liquid part of blood – about one-fifth of a teaspoon.

Colorectal cancer was detected by an earlier version of the test.

Two further tags and three proteins related to breast tissue were added for the present study. A total of 29 measurements is now obtained from each sample.

The relevant tags were first sought in tumor tissue. Twelve women who had not yet received any treatment provided the samples.

The operations were performed at Kaplan Medical Center in Israel. Noa Furth, a researcher in Shema’s lab, is one of the study’s lead authors.

“Once a tumor is removed, there’s only a short window of opportunity before its epigenetic tags begin to deteriorate,” said Furth.

“So as each surgery began, the doctors would alert me. I would rush the tissue sample from the hospital to our lab on ice, and we’d start analyzing it that very day.”

The team measured the tags in more than 1.1 million individual cells.

Eleven of the 12 tumors were luminal. Hormone receptors are present on the cells of luminal tumors, which generally respond well to hormone-blocking drugs.

Each luminal tumor also contained a minor population of unusual cells. These cells resembled a different form of breast cancer, known as basal-like. A worse outlook is associated with that form.

Hormone signaling was reduced in the basal-like cells.

The same pattern of tags was observed in these cells across patients. In laboratory cultures of breast cancer cells, greater movement was recorded for cells of this kind.

The researchers then investigated whether genetic differences could explain the aggressive cells.

New colonies were established in the laboratory, each from a single cell.

“The differences appear to be mainly epigenetic,” Shema said.

“When we grew breast cancer models in the lab starting with nonaggressive cells, an aggressive population always emerged eventually.”

The test was then applied to stored blood from 77 women with luminal breast cancer. All had been treated at Mayo Clinic in Florida. Blood from 30 healthy women was also tested.

A computer model was trained to distinguish cancer samples from healthy samples. Its performance score was 0.95, where 0.5 indicates chance-level performance and 1 indicates perfect separation.

An identical score was obtained for the 42 patients with tumors in the smallest size class. Such tumors measure about 0.8 inches (2 centimeters) or less.

No change in performance was seen across density scores, which are assigned by radiologists from mammograms. This was also true of women with extremely dense breasts.

Samples from two Israeli hospitals and a second Mayo batch were subsequently included. The combined set comprised 137 patients and 51 healthy women.

Three-quarters of these samples were used to train a new model, and the remainder served as a test.

In the test group, 92% of the 13 healthy women were correctly identified as cancer-free.

Cancer was detected in 85% of the 34 patients and missed in 15%.

The test did about as well in 22 women with triple-negative breast cancer, which lacks hormone receptors.

Luminal breast cancer usually responds to treatment, but in many women it comes back. Differences among the cells within a tumor are suspected to be one reason tumors return.

All 77 Mayo patients gave blood before surgery. The women were then followed for at least five years. Cancer returned in 33 of them within five years, while 44 remained disease-free.

Two tags characteristic of the basal-like cells were elevated in the blood of women who later relapsed.

A model built on the blood measurements separated the two groups. It scored 0.81 on the same scale. Models based on age, tumor stage, grade, and other standard measures scored lower.

A score of 0.92 was recorded in a second, smaller set of 30 patients.

These differences in blood markers were also present in women with early-stage or low-grade tumors. Such tumors are rated as lower risk by standard measures.

The team also looked at Oncotype DX scores. Oncotype DX is a gene test performed on tumor tissue to estimate the risk of relapse.

Its scores did not differ between 40 women who stayed disease-free and nine who relapsed.

All samples had been collected earlier and were analyzed once the outcomes were known. The group to which each sample belonged was not concealed from the analysts.

The groups were small as well. Cancer returned in only 10 of the 30 patients in the second set.

Blood from healthy women was collected at the Weizmann Institute. Blood from patients was supplied by hospitals in the United States and Israel.

Nearly all of the tumors examined were luminal. Triple-negative tumors are usually treated with drugs before surgery, and few untreated ones were available.

The authors wrote that the tumor findings may not extend to other types of breast cancer.

Signals from tumor cells, damaged healthy tissue, and the immune system are combined in blood. A tag found in blood therefore cannot be attributed to the basal-like cells alone.

Both tumor tissue and blood were available from ten women. For most tags, higher levels in the tumor were accompanied by higher levels in the blood.

The blood-test method is covered by a patent application on which Shema is a named inventor. The exclusive license is held by a company called Epivision Genetics. Four of the study’s authors are affiliated with that company.

Larger studies of the blood test are still required, the authors wrote. In such studies, blood would be collected first and each woman’s outcome learned afterward.

A separate question concerns the basal-like cells. It remains to be tested whether tumor cells can be kept from switching states by epigenetic drugs.

“We’re now looking for treatments that could deprive cancer cells of their epigenetic flexibility – and perhaps even coax cells that have already turned aggressive into adopting less aggressive traits,” Shema said.

No such treatment was tested in this study.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.
