XSW: An Equally-Weighted Basket Of Mainly Mid-Cap Software Stocks

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

The State Street SPDR S&P Software & Services ETF offers diversified, mid-cap-heavy exposure to the high-growth, high-margin U.S. software sector.

XSW's equal-weighted approach minimizes individual stock concentration but results in high turnover, while volatility and tracking inefficiency are on the higher side.

The ETF's erratic and unreliable distributions reflect its focus on growth stocks that prioritize reinvestment over dividends.

Other alternatives, such as the iShares Expanded Tech-Software Sector ETF and the WisdomTree Cloud Computing Fund ETF are also discussed.

The State Street SPDR S&P Software & Services ETF (XSW) was launched in September 2011 and has accumulated total AUM [assets under management] of over $0.5B over the last +15 years. XSW, which is issued by a subsidiary of State Street CorporationâSSGA Funds Managementâis priced at an expense ratio of 0.35%.

SSGA Funds Management doesnât actively pick stocks that make up this portfolio; instead, it looks to mimic the performance of an index called the S&P Software & Services Select Industry Index [SSSSII], which is built and maintained by a third party called S&P Dow Jones.

S&P Dow Jones Indices [SDJI] crafts SSSSII by fishing for suitable software & services candidates from a broader market index called the S&P Total Market Index [STMI]. Note that the latter doesn't just cover large, mid, and small caps, but micro-caps as well. After clearing certain liquidity and market-cap barriers, stocks from STMI need to be involved in one or all of these business activities: application software, systems software, interactive home entertainment, or IT consulting & related services. All chosen stocks also need to meet the requirement of being US-based.

Unlike most competing ETFs, this product doesn't weigh its holdings on the basis of their market caps but assigns equal weights to all, with those weights being re-adjusted four times per year.

At any given time, SDJI seeks to ensure that this portfolio will have minimum coverage to at least 22 stocks, but as of November 2026, XSW covers 129 stocks in total.

In aggregate, software stocks dominate this portfolio (almost 90%), with application software stocks in particular, exercising considerable heft (over two-thirds of the portfolio). For those not familiar with these two pockets, system software makers build software that ensures that different computer hardware, operating systems, cloud infrastructure, etc., work smoothly. Systems software also typically serves as the foundational infrastructure on which application software is then harnessed. The latter, which sits on top of systems software, comes across as more thematic, with different domains, industry workflows, markets, etc., requiring their own unique breed of application software.

From a market-cap perspective, XSW doesnât go down the more popular route of weighting stocks on the basis of their market caps, and because of that, we have a portfolio that is more oriented towards mid-caps (43% of the total holdings). Large-caps play second fiddle, while small-caps also have a decent aggregate weight of over one-fourth of the portfolio.

Some readers may be perplexed to note that micro-cap exposure is so minimal, given that in the previous section of this article, Iâd highlighted how XSWâs base universe (the S&P Total Market Index) didnât just include large, mid, and small caps but micro-caps as well. Well, the makeup of STMI still holds true, but SSSSII (XSWâs tracking index) also ensures that all stocks with a market cap of less than $0.5B are not considered (which pretty much rules out micro-caps). Besides during the quarterly rebalancing date, existing constituents that have seen their market caps dip to less than $0.3B (this is what accounts for the current sub-2% exposure) are also cast aside post rebalancing.

To better understand this, investors need to note that XSW mainly focuses on growth stocks that prefer to redeploy their free cash flows (if any) back into the business, indulge in M&A [mergers & acquisitions], or engage in share buybacks (rather than distribute dividends). Also, since this is an equally weighted setup, rather than a market-cap weighted setup, XSW isnât able to maximize the largesse from some of the larger and consistent dividend payers such as Microsoft Corporation (MSFT), International Business Machines (IBM), Oracle Corporation (ORCL), Roper Technologies (ROP), Accenture plc (ACN), etc.

Investors who are not comfortable witnessing wild swings in the returns of their portfolio could find it difficult to embrace a product like XSW, whose annualized volatility of over 31% is more than double that of the volatility that a median ETF experiences.

Given that XSW ensures equal weights of its holdings four times in a year, this ETF is likely to see plenty of churn. Besides, someone who gravitates to the philosophy of holding onto oneâs winners will find it hard to reconcile with a product like XSW, where high-performing stocks see their wings get clipped every quarter, even as laggards keep receiving a fillip.

Those who want a true-blue passive ETF that lives up to its mantle of replicating a certain index wonât find comfort in a product like XSW that has been prone to experiencing pretty wide tracking errors of 4-8% across different time periods (for context, most ETFs only witness tracking errors of 1-2%). These wide tracking errors are likely driven by XSWâs policy of only picking a sample of stocks from SSSSII (in aggregate, this sample is supposed to have the same overall characteristics as SSSSII), rather than seeking to replicate the latter to a tee (which could be deemed as the more cost-efficient tracking policy).

A decent chunk of this portfolio consists of enterprise software vendors whose economics have traditionally been centered around a "per-seat" subscription pricing model; this portfolio could be vulnerable to the AI-induced winds of change. Basically, the growing prevalence of highly productive AI agents could reduce the incentive for enterprise clients to rely on slow-on-the-uptake software vendors, who bill by the seat, and gravitate to the AI-native offerings of modern AI labs, who typically bill on the basis of outcome (rather than seats).

Growth-chasing investors will appreciate a portfolio like XSW that focuses on businesses that belong to a market that is poised to expand at a double-digit CAGR of 11%, which translates to over 3x expected global GDP growth (3-3.5%) through 2030.

XSW is designed for investors who dislike seeing concentration of their portfolios in just a few holdings. Besides, XSWâs top 10 stocks in aggregate account for only around 11-12% of the entire portfolio, whereas for most other ETFs it is around four times higher (the top 10 ratio for the ETF median is 46%).

Most of the popular technology sector-focused ETFs tend to over-index their portfolios to giants and large caps (mainly because most of them follow market-cap weighted indices). XSW, with its tilt towards mid-caps, bucks this trend, and comes across as more suitable for those who want to diminish exposure to the largest stocks (70% of the portfolio consists of stocks besides large-caps). Mid-cap and small-cap heavy businesses are still far from hitting a saturation state in their overall life cycles and are primed to see a stronger growth runway (be it on the sales, earnings, or cash flow front), as their financial base is much lower.

Besides XSW, two other similar passively managed ETF alternatives come in the shape of the iShares Expanded Tech-Software Sector ETF (IGV) and the WisdomTree Cloud Computing Fund ETF (WCLD).

ETF investors who want exposure to the high growth and high-margin software sector, with an emphasis on mid-caps, and minimal individual stock concentration, will likely appreciate XSW. The ETF is prone to significant churn, tends to see ample volatility, and has an erratic distribution schedule.
