Types of Investments – Know Whether You’re Buying Ownership or a Loan

Nearly every investment makes you an owner or a lender. Owning stocks or property can grow your money but can fall hard, while lending through bonds and bank CDs pays interest and swings less. Before you buy anything, name which one it is and what would make it lose value.

A product’s name won’t always tell you which one you’re buying. A brokerage menu or a sales pitch can list dozens of choices, and sorting each into owning or lending shows what it can do for you and how it can lose your money.

As an owner, your money rises and falls with what you own, so there’s no cap on your gain, and you can lose everything you paid. As a lender, you’re owed interest and your money back, so your return is mostly that interest. You lose if the borrower can’t pay, and your money buys less over time if prices climb faster than your interest.

Funds don’t add a third group. A fund bundles many stocks, many loans, or both, so it does the job of what it holds and carries the same risks.

These make you an owner, and their main job is growth.

These make you a lender. Their job is income and safety, and their value usually swings less than ownership’s.

These are funds, which bundle many of the investments above, and holding many investments at once limits the damage when one fails.

A mutual fund and an exchange-traded fund (ETF) differ in how you buy and sell them, not in what they can hold.

Options and other derivatives, contracts tied to another investment’s price, fit neither group. An option is a contract that lets you buy or sell an investment at a set price by a set date. It’s a bet on where the price goes. A buyer loses everything paid if the option expires worthless, and a seller’s losses can be unlimited.

Naming the group and what makes it lose value matters most with a product you can’t place. Say you’re offered a note paying a “guaranteed” 9%. A note is a loan, a company’s written promise to pay you back, so it sits with the lenders. But if it’s a loan to one small company with no credit rating, you could lose all of it if that company fails, a risk more like owning a stock than lending to a bank. The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) warns that notes like this, sold widely to individual investors, are often scams.

A loan’s interest rate is the quickest clue to its risk. When it pays far more than Treasuries or insured CDs of the same length, the extra interest is the borrower paying you to accept a bigger chance of losing your money.

Then match the group to the job and the timeline. Large-company stocks as a group have lost money in about one of every three years, on average, the SEC’s guide to asset allocation says, so ownership suits money you won’t touch for years. Money you’ll need within a few years belongs on the lending side, where savings, CDs, and Treasury bills rarely lose money.
