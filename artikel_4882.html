How Long Should We Wait for Sex, Cohabitation, and Marriage?

"Wise men say, 'Only fools rush in,' but I can't help falling in love with you." — Elvis Presley

How long should you wait before having sex? Moving in together? Getting married? These are among the most common questions people ask about romantic relationships. Yet there is no magical timeline that guarantees success. The value of waiting depends on the partners, their circumstances, and the nature of their relationship. Still, one principle often proves valuable: patience.

"Patience is not simply the ability to wait. It's how we behave while we're waiting." — Joyce Meyer

There's a crucial distinction between waiting and patience. Waiting is passive: Time passes while little changes. Patience, by contrast, is active. It involves investing in the relationship while recognizing that some developments should not be rushed. Patience creates the conditions for intimacy, trust, and commitment to deepen naturally. Unlike procrastination, which unnecessarily delays action, patience reflects wise timing. The important question is not how long we wait, but whether we practice patience rather than merely let time pass (Ben‐Ze'ev, 2019; and here).

"I don't have a set time frame for sex, but it's always before it gets super serious. I've got to test-drive the car before buying it." — A woman

"My boyfriend and I had sex on the first date, and we're still together a year later and talking about getting engaged soon. The right person isn't going to think less of you because of the speedy sex." — A woman

Sexual attraction is a defining feature of romantic love, making the timing of sexual intimacy especially significant. Research often finds that couples who delay sex report greater relationship quality and lower breakup rates. Waiting may allow emotional intimacy and communication to develop before physical intimacy takes center stage. Some studies suggest that couples who wait several months before becoming sexually involved report greater satisfaction and relationship stability (Vancour & Fallon, 2017).

Yet these findings do not mean that sex on the first date is a mistake. What matters most is not the calendar but the context. Partners differ in their values, attachment styles, and goals. For some well-matched couples, early sexual intimacy may strengthen their initial bond. The important question is therefore not, "How many dates should we wait?" but "Are we both comfortable with the pace at which intimacy is developing?"

"With love and patience, nothing is impossible." — Daisaku Ikeda

Premarital cohabitation has become the norm for most American couples. Yet living together can serve very different purposes.

Research suggests that couples who move in together mainly to "test" the relationship are more likely to break up than those who cohabit because they genuinely want to spend more time together (Chapman et al., 2026). One reason is that living together creates practical and financial interdependence, making it harder to evaluate the relationship objectively. Couples who move in together after discussing long-term plans, or after becoming engaged, generally report better marital outcomes than those who simply drift into cohabitation because it feels convenient.

Researchers therefore distinguish between sliding into cohabitation and deciding to cohabit. Sliding involves little deliberate discussion or commitment; deciding reflects conscious consideration of the relationship's future. Indeed, more than half of cohabiting couples report that they had not explicitly discussed moving in together before doing so. The central issue is not whether couples should live together before marriage, but whether cohabitation expresses mutual commitment and thoughtful deliberation rather than uncertainty and inertia.

"The ideal amount of time to date before getting married is at least a whole year. It's crazy to marry someone before you've seen what they're like in winter." — A woman

No dating duration can guarantee marital success. Nevertheless, many relationship researchers and therapists suggest that two to three years often provides enough time to know a partner across different circumstances while preserving romantic momentum.

Dating typically differs from hooking up because it is part of a broader process of building an enduring relationship. Yet the distinction is far from absolute. Many successful relationships begin as casual encounters. One-night stands sometimes become lifelong partnerships, and dating apps such as Tinder, often criticized for promoting casual sex, have also become important pathways to marriage. The route matters less than what develops along the way.

"Lovers are patient and know that the moon needs time to become full." — Rumi

Love has no universally correct timetable. There are no strict rules governing when to have sex, how long to live together, or how long to date before marriage. What matters is not simply how long partners wait before taking the next step, but whether they use that time wisely. Passive waiting merely allows the clock to advance; active patience allows intimacy, trust, and commitment to deepen. Romantic flourishing depends less on the passage of time than on what partners do with it.
