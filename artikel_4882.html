The Mustache Was Never the Point of Movember

The first time I showed up to a GORUCK training day was not long after I finished my first Ironman. I didn’t go looking for it. A friend from work dared me.

GORUCK is an endurance event where you pack a backpack full of bricks and take on challenges. Some involve getting cold, wet, and sandy. Others involve carrying odd-shaped objects around. The real events start at 10 p.m. and run until the sun comes up.

The training day was in daylight at Baker Beach in San Francisco, and it was all dudes. We had the same curiosity and the same burning desire to see what we were made of. We also wanted to find other knuckleheads who think spending time in the ocean at 2 a.m. is perfectly normal. Dare I say, fun.

What I didn’t know at the time was that some of the men I met on that beach would still be good friends of mine 14 years later. I wrote about those friendships in “The Power of Crucible Events to Solidify Male Friendships.”

That got me thinking about the origins of Movember.

In 2003, two friends in Melbourne, Travis Garone and Luke Slattery, were having a beer at the Gypsy Bar in Fitzroy. The conversation drifted to fashion trends that keep coming back. They started wondering where the mustache had gone.

So they decided to bring it back. Travis emailed their friends with a subject line that doubled as a dare: “Are you man enough to be my man?”

There was no cause that first year. No fundraising page. No awareness campaign. Just 30 men growing mustaches for a month.

The cause came the next year, when the group decided to grow for men’s health. Today, millions of men around the world take part.

Co-founder Adam Garone put it plainly: “Movember itself started with friends getting together, growing Mo’s and having fun.”

That’s the part that gets lost. Movember didn’t start as a health campaign that happened to involve friends. It started as a group of friends who happened to build a health campaign.

There’s a popular idea that men’s friendships are broken because men won’t open up. If guys would just talk about their feelings, the theory goes, the loneliness would lift.

Tyler Austin Harper isn’t buying it. Writing in The Atlantic, he argued that the real crisis of male friendship comes from something else. Men haven’t lost the ability to connect. They’ve lost the shared activities they connect through.

That fits what I’ve seen. Men don’t usually become friends sitting across a table and sharing. They become friends standing next to each other, doing something side by side.

It also takes time. A lot of it. University of Kansas researcher Jeffrey Hall estimates it takes 40 to 60 hours to form a casual friendship, and more than 200 hours to become good friends. And not all hours count the same. Hanging out and joking around builds friendship. Working together counts for much less.

That’s the hidden genius of a dumb challenge. It gives you hours together that feel like play. Nobody on that training day was on the clock. Nobody was networking. We were a bunch of guys carrying bricks around a beach because we wanted to.

A month of growing a mustache works the same way. It’s time with your friends you’d never think to schedule. It also shows your care for your fellow men.

This year’s campaign is built around a simple message: Connection doesn’t have to be a big heart-to-heart.

As Movember’s marketing director, Helena Jennison, put it, “Connection doesn’t always have to start with a difficult conversation.” Sometimes it’s a laugh. Sometimes it’s a shared interest. Sometimes it’s growing a mustache.

It works. According to Movember’s own research, two in five people who took part made a new social connection through it.

The charity funds the same idea year-round. In Newcastle, England, a program called Men’s Pie Club starts from a simple premise. Men like eating pies. So they learn to make them together. The conversations come later, as the men get to know each other.

Look at what’s going on in each of these. Nobody signs up for “emotional connection.” They sign up for a mustache, a pie, a beach full of bricks. The activity is the excuse. The friendship is what they actually came for, even if they’d never say so.

Men need a reason to be in the same place at the same time. Give them one, and they’ll usually take it from there.

You don’t need a cause to start. You need a dare.
