A ranching couple took photos of rock spires carved from ancient ash and helped create Chiricahua National Park

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

A 27-million-year-old eruption left a caldera 12 miles wide in Arizona. Its rock spires, protected since 1924, are now Chiricahua National Park.

Around 1900, a teenager named Ed Riggs climbed Sugarloaf Mountain, in what is now Chiricahua National Park in southeastern Arizona. He was hiking with Ben Erickson, aged nine, the brother of the woman he would later marry. From the top they looked down on a canyon full of rock pinnacles.

Ed only got to explore it after World War I, when he came home from the Army. The pinnacles are what is left of ash from a volcano that erupted about 27 million years ago.

On October 20, 2026, President Donald J. Trump signed the Chiricahua National Park Act. It turned the monument that Ed and his wife Lillian pushed for into a national park.

NASA published a Landsat 9 image of it on October 28, but the change is to the name and status, not to the trails or the scenic drive.

Ed came from a ranching family in the Sulphur Springs Valley. He served in the war as an aerial photographer. The National Park Service page on Ed Riggs quotes him calling the canyon “some of the most wonderful country.”

Lillian was the daughter of Swedish homesteaders Neil and Emma Erickson. She taught school until about 1918. That year she and her sister Hildegard named the family place Faraway Ranch, the NPS profile of Lillian Erickson Riggs says.

In the summer of 1921 Lillian was in Los Angeles, trying to start a writing career. Ed helped out at the ranch and wrote to her about a big house in the upper garden “to take care of the tourist trade.” They married on February 26, 1923.

The couple cut trails into the rocks and took photographs. Some of the pictures went to the county fair in Douglas, where a Dr. J.J.P. Armstrong saw them and came out to look for himself.

He and Ed explored the side canyons together. The park service says they were probably among the first non-Indigenous people to see Natural Bridge and Echo Canyon.

In August 1923, Arizona Governor George W.P. Hunt rode out on horseback in pouring rain from Faraway Ranch into the Chiricahua National Forest. Ed wrote afterward that the party came back “a wet bunch I can tell you but well pleased with what they had seen.”

Hunt backed a monument on the Forest Service land. President Calvin Coolidge established Chiricahua National Monument on April 18, 1924. Ed and Lillian then advertised Faraway Ranch as the closest place to stay.

In the Depression, Ed took a job with the Civilian Conservation Corps as a local trail foreman, at $1,800 a year. Lillian kept running the cattle and the guests.

Ed built the Sugarloaf Trail, with a tunnel blasted through rock. Then he asked Frank Pinkley, superintendent of Southwestern National Monuments, to let him route a trail down to Echo Park.

Pinkley agreed, but told him that if he failed he should “just hand in your resignation.” The Echo Canyon Trail needed eleven switchbacks in one quarter-mile.

In 1934 the monument passed to the National Park Service. More than 6,000 people in about 1,000 cars came to the dedication at Massai Point, according to the NPS history of Massai Point.

A 1997 U.S. Geological Survey guide dates the eruption to 26.9 million years ago. Its authors were John S. Pallister and Edward A. du Bray of the USGS and Douglas B. Hall of the University of Arizona.

According to the guide, magma pooled a few miles down, just south of today’s park. The rock on top broke and gas escaped. The foaming magma swelled up to 50 times its volume in a series of explosions.

More than 100 cubic miles (over 400 cubic kilometers) of magma came out. Hot ash and pumice covered at least 1,200 square miles (3,100 square kilometers).

Mount St. Helens put out about a tenth of a cubic mile in 1980. The empty chamber’s roof caved in and left the Turkey Creek caldera, 12 miles (19 kilometers) wide and at least 5,000 feet (1,500 meters) deep.

Ash of a similar age elsewhere in the West kept other records. At John Day Fossil Beds in Oregon, 29-million-year-old ash holds paw tracks left by a cat with retractable claws.

Calderas can matter for other reasons too, and the McDermitt caldera on the Nevada-Oregon border may hold a large lithium deposit.

In the park area the ash filled an old valley at least 1,600 feet (490 meters) deep. It was hot enough to weld into solid rock. The layer is known as the Rhyolite Canyon Tuff, named for the canyon where it is best exposed.

The tuff cracked along vertical joints as it cooled. Water got in, froze and thawed, and pried the blocks apart, the NPS Geodiversity Atlas says.

What stayed standing became pinnacles, columns and balanced rocks. Mind you, the USGS guide thinks the columns we see today are less than three million years old, a fraction of the tuff’s age.

NASA’s satellite singles out Big Balanced Rock, where a large boulder sits perched on top. Civilian Conservation Corps crews named many of the formations in the 1930s, the park service says. The Chiricahua Apache, whose homeland this is, already called the area the Land of the Standing-Up Rocks.

The Operational Land Imager on Landsat 9 took the NASA picture on July 2, 2026. Lauren Dauphin of the NASA Earth Observatory processed it. The worn tuff looks gray, and Rhyolite Canyon and Bonita Canyon show up as green lines of trees.

The Chiricahua Mountains are one of the sky islands of southern Arizona, ranges that stand alone above the desert. Sky islands in other places have their own residents, like the four new chameleons described from Mozambique’s sky islands.

NASA’s write-up, by Adam Voiland, says four regions meet in the park, from the Sonoran Desert to the Sierra Madre. About 1,000 plant species grow there, from agaves and prickly pear to pine, fir and oak.

The Interior Department release describes a park of about 12,000 acres. Nowhere does it give figures for staff, money or expected visitors.

Jessica Bowron, who is exercising the authority of the National Park Service director, said that while the name is changing, the agency’s commitment stays the same.

From orbit, single spires are too small to pick out, NASA notes, so the gray patch in the image is the tuff as a whole.

Lillian Riggs went blind in 1942 and closed the guest ranch in 1970. She sold most of the Stafford land and cabin to the park service in 1968 and the rest of the homestead in 1974. She died in 1977, after moving to a nursing home in Willcox.

Ed died in 1950. His gravestone says he engineered the Echo Trail, and it adds, “He wished this to be his monument.” The trail still winds through the spires he first saw from Sugarloaf as a boy.
