AI searches 50 million chemicals for sweeteners without the bitter aftertaste

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

An AI search of more than 50 million chemicals identified 92 potential sugar substitutes that could deliver sweetness without a bitter aftertaste.

A sip of diet soda can leave a bitter or metallic taste in your mouth. Finding a sugar substitute that delivers sweetness without that lingering taste has proved difficult.

Shayma El-Atawneh and Amiram Goldblum at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem used artificial intelligence to look for better options.

Their search covered more than 50 million chemicals and produced a shortlist of 92 possible sweeteners.

The researchers have not tested how those candidates taste. But they have narrowed an enormous search to a list that a laboratory could realistically work through.

The tongue detects sweetness through one type of receptor, a tiny sensor made from two proteins working together. It has 25 receptor types for bitterness.

Those bitter sensors recognize many different chemicals, including toxic substances. They help warn animals about things they should avoid swallowing.

Some sweet molecules also activate bitter receptors, so the tongue picks up both tastes. That overlap helps explain why a sweetener can leave a bitter aftertaste.

Saccharin, cyclamate and aspartame all came from accidental discoveries. Researchers still struggle to reproduce the clean taste of table sugar.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration has approved six high-intensity sweeteners as food additives: saccharin, aspartame, acesulfame potassium, sucralose, neotame and advantame.

Stevia leaf extracts and monk fruit extracts also sweeten foods and drinks.

Some earlier studies have linked artificially sweetened drinks with higher risks of stroke and dementia, though those associations do not prove cause and effect.

Other research has connected some sweeteners with changes in gut bacteria and difficulty processing glucose.

The new study focused on predicting taste. It did not establish whether any candidate would be safe to eat.

Goldblum’s group originally developed its algorithm, called Iterative Stochastic Elimination, or ISE, to find potential drugs.

Here, the researchers used it to search for patterns in chemicals with known tastes.

The program tries ranges of molecular properties at random and checks how well they separate different groups. It discards weak combinations and keeps useful filters, each built from five property ranges.

It draws on 205 calculated features, including how electrical charge spreads across a molecule and how the molecule behaves in oil and water.

Another feature counts chiral centers, points in a molecule that allow different mirror-image arrangements.

The training data included 7,709 sweet compounds from one database and 316 well-studied sweeteners from another. A third supplied several hundred bitter compounds and 208 tasteless ones.

The models found different clues for each taste. Sweetness predictions relied on charge distribution and chiral centers, while bitterness predictions relied on acidity and positive charge.

The researchers then screened three supplier catalogs containing 2.2 million, 30.7 million and 17.9 million molecules.

Candidates moved forward only if the sweetness models gave them high scores and the bitterness models gave them negative scores.

That narrowed the search, but it left another question to answer: could the selected molecules fit into the tongue’s sweet receptor?

In 2025, three teams published the first detailed structures of the human sweet receptor. One structure, listed as 9NOV in the Protein Data Bank, shows sucralose sitting inside a binding pocket.

Scientists produced that view by freezing the protein and imaging it with electrons.

El-Atawneh and Goldblum used it to simulate how their candidates might fit into a receptor section that closes around sweet molecules like a clamshell.

Existing sweeteners provided a comparison: advantame scored -7.8, sucralose -7.2 and saccharin -4.8, with more negative scores suggesting stronger binding.

The researchers set a cutoff of -6, roughly the average for the approved sweeteners.

Candidates also needed hydrogen bonds at three of five selected receptor sites and contact with five of eight others.

After removing molecules with chemical groups known to produce misleading laboratory results, the team had 556 candidates.

The researchers selected 92 for further work. Of these, 43 had no more than one chiral center, making them easier to obtain and test; the other 49 had the strongest sweetness and lowest bitterness predictions.

They also compared all 556 candidates with known compounds that interact with the sweet receptor and with one another.

Most had distinct structures, suggesting that the search could lead to unfamiliar families of sweeteners.

Fourteen of the final 92 already appeared in patents as sweet-receptor modulators, compounds that alter the receptor’s activity.

Finding them again offered a useful check on the method, though they were not new discoveries.

Among approved drugs, about 10% scored as bitter and roughly 2% as sweet, a pattern consistent with the tongue’s wider range of bitter receptors.

Sucralose exposed a clear weakness. The sweetness models gave it negative scores, while the bitterness models gave it slightly positive ones.

The researchers attributed this mistake to a structural feature that their training data did not adequately cover.

The program struggled with chemistry outside the examples it had learned from.

They also deliberately favored fewer false alarms, accepting that the models would miss some real sweeteners. A shortlist filled with wrong predictions would waste laboratory time.

The docking scores had limits, too: they did not reliably indicate how much sweetener was needed to activate the receptor.

These calculations use a fixed protein structure and leave out water, movement and changes in the protein’s shape.

The study did not include laboratory or taste tests of the 92 candidates. Its models predict whether something might taste sweet, but not how sweet it would be.

According to the researchers, only one taste-prediction effort over the past decade had led to a sweetener confirmed in laboratory tests.

In 2020, another team tested three natural compounds and found that one, arctiin, activated the human sweet receptor, although only at high concentrations.

Goldblum disclosed a pending patent in the paper’s competing-interests statement, giving him a potential financial interest in the work.

The candidates still need tests to establish their taste, safety and suitability for food.

The next step is to obtain the molecules and test their effects on the receptor, using the results to improve future predictions.

The full study was published in the journal Food Chemistry.
