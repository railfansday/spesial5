From Under Armour to Telegram: 10 familiar names in 2026 breach data

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Data breaches in 2026 have touched 10 brands most people know, from Under Armour and AT&T to Telegram, and show how attackers reach customer records

A phone call, not malicious code, opened the door in two of the 2026 breaches on this list. The hacking group ShinyHunters used a phishing call to get into Charter Communications' Salesforce $CRM environment in April 2026. It used the same approach against Madison Square $SQ Garden Sports Corp. There, a call to a low-level employee led into the company's Microsoft $MSFT Entra identity system.

The second recurring pattern is pressure. Groups on this list posted victims on leak sites, set deadlines and published stolen files when no payment arrived. The Russian ransomware group Everest listed Under Armour $UAA on its leak site in November 2025 and gave the company seven days. Under Armour did not respond publicly. After the ransom went unpaid, Everest released the customer dataset in January 2026. ShinyHunters published MSG Sports data the day after the company missed its ransom deadline.

The third pattern is that stolen data keeps coming back. A dataset of 176 million records, built from AT&T $T's 2019 and 2024 breaches, resurfaced in February 2026. Social Security numbers in it that were previously encrypted now appear decrypted. That is why this list describes what happened in 2026 — a posting, a release or a resurfacing — rather than claiming every company was first hacked this year.

The 10 slides that follow draw on SpyCloud's log of data breaches in 2026 and focus on names most readers already know. They are a selection, not a ranking. The list covers Canvas maker Instructure, Under Armour, AT&T, Charter Communications and its Spectrum brand, MSG Sports, Exact Sciences, DentaQuest, WhatsApp and Telegram. It also covers a breach at Klue that reached LastPass, Huntress, Recorded Future, Tanium, Jamf and 8x8.

Breach sizes are reported in different units, and the numbers can disagree. In the MSG Sports case, ShinyHunters claimed about 26 million records. The published data held nearly 10 million unique email addresses. SpyCloud's own log shows 55.4 million. Where an attacker's claim, a confirmed count and SpyCloud's figure differ, the slides label each one separately. That way readers can see who is counting what. For anyone with an account at one of these companies, the exposed data ranges from email addresses to Social Security numbers.

Instructure, the Salt Lake City company that makes the Canvas learning management system, appears in the 2026 data breach list compiled by SpyCloud. SpyCloud is a security firm that tracks stolen credentials and leaked datasets.

Canvas is software that schools, colleges and universities use to run their courses online. Instructors post syllabi, readings and assignments on the platform. Students use it to submit work, take quizzes and check grades.

That daily use is why the name is familiar to many households. Students, parents and teachers sign in to Canvas throughout the school year.

Under Armour $UAA customer data went public in January 2026, when the Everest ransomware group released a dataset holding 72 million email addresses. The release came after Under Armour left a ransom demand unpaid.

Everest, a Russian ransomware group, first named the sportswear company on its leak site in November 2025. That timing shapes how the incident should be described. The listing belongs to late 2025, while the publication of the customer data is the 2026 event.

A leak site is a public page where an extortion group names the companies it says it has hit. A listing there tells the victim, its customers and other criminals that the group claims to hold stolen files and intends to publish them.

Everest set a seven-day deadline on the Under Armour listing. A short countdown is a pressure tactic. It gives a company a fixed window to pay before the data goes out, and it makes the threat visible to anyone watching the site.

Under Armour made no public response while the clock ran. No payment followed, and Everest went ahead and released the dataset.

The scale of that release does not depend on Everest's own claim. The 72 million figure is a confirmed count, and SpyCloud's findings back it up. That sets this case apart from incidents where the only available number comes from the attackers.

The unit matters as well. The figure counts email addresses, not customers, accounts or total records. One person can have more than one address, and an address can appear in a dataset without a matching active account.

An email address is not a password or a payment card. It is still the login name for many shopping, fitness and banking accounts. A leaked address gives criminals a ready target list for phishing emails that pose as a trusted brand. It can also be paired with passwords exposed in other breaches and tried against other services, a technique known as credential stuffing.

A dataset of 176 million AT&T $T records resurfaced in February 2026, according to SpyCloud. Social Security numbers in the set that had once been encrypted now appear in decrypted form.

AT&T is the Dallas-based telecommunications company behind one of the largest wireless and home internet businesses in the U.S. The records in this dataset were not taken in a new 2026 intrusion. They were assembled from earlier AT&T breaches in 2019 and 2024.

What changed in 2026 was the condition of the data. The dataset brought the material from both earlier incidents together in one place.

Encryption scrambles a piece of data so it cannot be read without the matching key. An encrypted Social Security number in a stolen file is far harder to use for fraud than the same number in readable form. Once the encryption is stripped away, the number can be read and copied directly.

A Social Security number is a nine-digit identifier issued by the Social Security Administration. Banks, lenders, employers and insurers use it to confirm who a person is. That is why it is a central target for identity thieves.

Unlike a password, a Social Security number does not expire. The Social Security Administration assigns a new number only in limited cases, such as when a person can show ongoing harm from misuse of the old one. For most people whose numbers leak, the original number stays in use.

One protection available in the U.S. is a credit freeze. A freeze blocks new lenders from pulling a person's credit file at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. Federal law has made placing and lifting a freeze free since 2018.

For AT&T customers whose details appeared in the 2019 or 2024 breaches, the February 2026 resurfacing puts that old information back in circulation in a more usable form.

Charter Communications, the cable and internet provider that sells service under the Spectrum brand, was breached in April 2026. The ShinyHunters group got into Charter's Salesforce $CRM environment through a phishing phone call. Confirmed counts start at 4.9 million, and a claimed count reaches 42 million, according to SpyCloud.

A phishing call, often called vishing, works by persuading an employee to hand over login details or approve access. In a typical attack, the caller poses as someone the employee has reason to trust, such as a colleague or a member of an IT help desk. The request is framed as routine.

The weak point in this kind of attack is a person rather than a software flaw. When an employee hands over access willingly, there is no malicious file or code for security tools to flag.

Salesforce is a customer relationship management platform. Companies use it to store customer accounts, contact details and records of service interactions. A system like this is designed to gather customer information in one place. Access to a company's Salesforce environment is therefore a route to its customer data.

The two figures tied to this incident measure different things. The 4.9 million figure is the low end of the confirmed counts. The 42 million figure is a claimed count, not a verified total. The confirmed number marks the lower bound of what has been checked. The claimed number marks the upper bound of what has been alleged.

Madison Square $SQ Garden Sports Corp. was breached after the ShinyHunters group placed a phishing call to a low-level employee. The group used that call to get into the company's Microsoft $MSFT Entra identity system. ShinyHunters posted the breach on its leak site in June 2026.

MSG Sports owns two of New York's major-league franchises: the Knicks of the NBA and the Rangers of the NHL. Both teams play their home games at Madison Square Garden in Manhattan. MSG Sports is a separate public company from the business that operates the arena itself. Its shares trade on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker MSGS.

Microsoft Entra is Microsoft's identity and access platform. Until a 2023 rebrand, Microsoft called it Azure Active Directory. Organizations use it to decide which employees can sign in to which cloud apps and services. Administrators can also use it to require multifactor authentication and to set conditional access rules that limit sign-ins by device or location. A working login there can reach whatever systems that account is permitted to use.

The intrusion started with a phone call. That method is often called vishing, short for voice phishing. The target was a low-level employee, not a senior executive.

After the leak site posting, ShinyHunters gave MSG Sports a deadline to pay a ransom. MSG Sports did not meet that deadline. The group published the data the following day.

Three different figures describe the size of the breach. ShinyHunters said it held about 26 million records. The data it published contained nearly 10 million unique email addresses. SpyCloud's own log shows 55.4 million for the incident. SpyCloud's number is more than twice the size of the attacker's claim.

The figures come from different counters and use different units. A record is typically one row in a database. The same person can appear in many rows. A count of unique email addresses removes those duplicates.

Exact Sciences, the Madison, Wisconsin, cancer diagnostics company best known for the Cologuard at-home colorectal cancer screening test, appears in SpyCloud's reporting on 2026 data breaches.

DentaQuest, a U.S. dental benefits company, appears on SpyCloud's list of data breaches reported in 2026.

WhatsApp, the messaging app owned by Meta $META Platforms, is one of the well-known brands that appear in SpyCloud's roundup of 2026 data breaches.

The Klue breach appears in SpyCloud's 2026 data breach tracking as a single incident that touched six other technology companies: LastPass, Huntress, Recorded Future, Tanium, Jamf and 8x8.

The incident carries Klue's name. The six companies linked to it are the more familiar brands, and SpyCloud groups all of them under the Klue entry.

The listing shows that a single breach record can reach several companies beyond the one named in its title.

Two separate scraped Telegram datasets appear in SpyCloud's tracking of 2026 data breaches. One, labeled Part 2, contains 328.9 million records. The other contains 54.2 million records.

Both datasets show up as rows in SpyCloud's breach table. Each row lists a dataset name and a record count. The listing gives no date for either file. It does not describe how the data was gathered, and it includes no response from Telegram.

The absence of a date limits what the entries reveal. An entry in 2026 tracking places a dataset in this year's log. It does not by itself show when the information inside was collected. Without a date, the listing cannot indicate whether these files were assembled this year or circulated earlier.

The word "scraped" sets these entries apart from most of this list. Scraping generally refers to automated collection of information that a service displays or returns, such as public profile details. That is a different act from breaking into a company's internal systems. SpyCloud's table does not say which method produced these two files or which fields the records hold.

The two figures count records, not people. One person can appear more than once in a single dataset. Separate datasets can also share entries. SpyCloud does not report how many unique Telegram accounts the files cover or whether the two files overlap. The listing also does not explain what the Part 2 label refers to.

Each file also comes with a single count. The listing names no attacker group, so there is no claimed figure to set beside SpyCloud's number. No confirmed count from Telegram appears either. That leaves SpyCloud's table as the only source for both totals.

For Telegram users, the entries confirm that two large data collections tied to the messaging app appear in 2026 breach-tracking data. They do not establish what information about any individual user is included.
