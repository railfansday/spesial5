15 things to know about how congressional committees work

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Congressional committees handle most of Congress's detailed work, from markups to subpoenas, and here is how they operate and which panels shape taxes, spending and defense

The floor votes that make headlines are usually the last step in a long process. Much of what Congress decides about a bill — its wording, its scope, whether it advances at all — gets settled earlier, in committee rooms on Capitol Hill.

Committees exist for a practical reason. The House has 435 voting members and the Senate has 100. Neither chamber can study tax law, military budgets, judicial nominees and telecommunications policy as a single body. So each chamber splits its members into panels with defined subject areas. Those panels review legislation, call witnesses, question officials and keep watch over the federal agencies that carry out the laws Congress writes.

For anyone following policy, markets or regulation, congressional committees are where the details take shape. A tax change, a defense budget or a new rule for broadband providers typically passes through at least one committee before a full chamber votes on it. Many bills never make it out. The members who lead these panels can decide which proposals get hearings, which get amended and which quietly stall.

This guide covers 15 subjects in two parts. The first part explains the mechanics. It covers what committees do, how standing committees differ from select, joint and conference committees, how subcommittees split up the work and how jurisdiction is assigned. It also explains the roles of chairs and ranking members, the path a bill takes from referral through markup, and the hearing and subpoena powers that support congressional oversight.

The second part profiles several committees widely seen as influential. They are the House Rules Committee, which sets the terms of floor debate, and the House Ways and Means Committee, which handles taxes. The House and Senate Appropriations committees write annual spending bills. The Armed Services committees in both chambers handle defense policy. The Senate Finance, Judiciary and Foreign Relations committees each shape a major area of federal policy. The House Energy and Commerce Committee rounds out the selection with one of the broadest jurisdictions in the chamber.

This is a selection, not a ranking. Influence on Capitol Hill shifts with party control, leadership priorities and the issues in front of Congress at any given moment. Committee sizes, chairs and membership also change from one Congress to the next. The structure itself changes far more slowly.

Congressional committees are the smaller working groups where most of Congress's detailed legislative work takes place. They review bills, hold hearings and monitor federal agencies, turning two large chambers into a set of specialized panels.

The reason comes down to scale and time. Neither chamber could study every proposal on taxes, defense, farming, health care and foreign policy as a single body. Committees let smaller groups of lawmakers focus on a defined set of issues and then report back to the full chamber.

That division of labor builds expertise. A member who serves on the same panel for several terms learns its agencies, programs and policy debates in depth. A generalist sitting in on every issue could not match that knowledge. Committee staff add to it. Many staffers are lawyers, economists or policy specialists who draft legislative text and prepare questions for hearings.

The Constitution does not create committees or describe them. Article I, Section 5 gives each chamber the power to determine the rules of its proceedings. The House and Senate have used that authority to build their committee systems. Those systems have changed shape many times since the first Congress met in 1789.

Woodrow Wilson described the system's weight in his 1885 book "Congressional Government." He contrasted Congress in session, which he saw as Congress on public exhibition, with Congress in its committee rooms, which he called Congress at work. Wilson wrote the book as a graduate student at Johns Hopkins University. He became the 28th U.S. president nearly three decades later.

Committees also act as filters. Lawmakers introduce far more bills than either chamber could ever vote on. Committees decide which proposals receive attention, which get rewritten and which are set aside. That screening role gives committee members significant influence over what reaches the floor.

The work extends beyond writing laws. Committees question cabinet officials and review how federal money is spent. They also examine whether agencies are carrying out the laws Congress passed. In the Senate, committees take up presidential nominations before the full chamber votes on them.

Standing committees are the permanent panels of the House and Senate, and they write most of the legislation Congress passes. They carry over from one two-year Congress to the next, and each has a fixed subject area set out in chamber rules.

That permanence is written into the rulebooks. In the House, Rule X $TWTR lists each standing committee and the topics it controls. In the Senate, Rule XXV does the same job. Because jurisdiction is defined in advance, a new bill on farm subsidies or federal land has a predictable home before anyone introduces it.

The names describe the work. The House has standing committees on Agriculture, Natural Resources and Veterans' Affairs. The Senate has the Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee and the Health, Education, Labor and Pensions Committee. Each has authority to consider legislation and report it to the full chamber.

The committees endure, but their membership does not. At the start of each new Congress, party leaders and party steering panels hand out seats. The full chamber then approves those assignments by resolution. A lawmaker who stays on one committee for several terms builds seniority and subject knowledge, and both carry weight inside the panel.

Much of a standing committee's legislative work is authorization. An authorizing bill creates or renews a federal program and sets its rules. It can also cap how much may be spent on that program. The money itself is provided through a separate process.

Each standing committee also employs its own staff. These aides draft bill text, prepare hearings and track the agencies under the panel's jurisdiction. That staff gives the committee institutional memory that outlasts any single chair.

The roster of standing committees can change. The Legislative Reorganization Act of 1946 consolidated dozens of panels into a much smaller set in both chambers. In 1995, at the start of the 104th Congress, the new House Republican majority abolished three panels. They were the Merchant Marine and Fisheries Committee, the Post Office and Civil Service Committee and the District of Columbia Committee. Their duties moved to other panels.

New permanent committees can also grow out of temporary ones. After the Sept. 11 attacks, the House set up a select committee on homeland security. In 2005, it became a standing panel, the House Committee on Homeland Security.

Select, joint and conference committees are the panels Congress creates outside its permanent committee structure. Select committees take on a defined task, joint committees bring together members of both chambers, and conference committees have historically settled differences between House and Senate versions of a bill.

A select committee is usually formed by a resolution of one chamber to examine a specific issue or event. Many of these panels investigate and issue reports rather than write legislation. Some expire once their work is done.

The House Select Committee on the January 6th Attack followed that pattern. It operated from 2021 to 2022, held public hearings and released a final report in December 2022 before it dissolved. Decades earlier, the Senate formed a select committee in 1973 to investigate the Watergate scandal.

Not every select panel is temporary. The Senate Select Committee on Intelligence, created in 1976, and the House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence, created in 1977, still oversee U.S. intelligence agencies. The Senate also uses the label "special committee," as with the Special Committee on Aging. That panel studies issues affecting older Americans but cannot report bills.

Joint committees draw members from both the House and Senate. They tend to focus on research, study or administrative matters rather than drafting bills for floor votes. The Joint Economic Committee, established by the Employment Act of 1946, examines economic conditions and policy. The Joint Committee on Taxation employs a nonpartisan staff that estimates the revenue effects of tax legislation for lawmakers in both chambers.

Conference committees are temporary panels assembled for a single bill. The House and Senate must pass identical text before a measure can go to the president. When their versions differed, each chamber appointed negotiators, known as conferees, to work out a compromise.

The result is a conference report. Each chamber must then approve it, and the rules generally bar amending it on the floor. That gave the small group of members chosen to negotiate considerable leverage over the final text.

Formal conferences have become less common in recent practice. The chambers now often resolve differences by passing amended versions back and forth until one side accepts the other's text. Leaders can also negotiate a final version informally before either chamber votes.

Congressional committees split their workload in two ways. Jurisdiction, written into each chamber's rules, decides which committee receives a bill, and subcommittees divide that committee's territory into narrower subjects. Together they determine which small group of lawmakers examines a proposal first.

In the House, committee jurisdiction is spelled out in Rule X $TWTR of the chamber's rules. The Senate lists its committees' subject areas in Rule XXV of the Standing Rules of the Senate. These rules describe each panel's territory by subject area, such as agriculture, the armed forces or revenue.

When a bill is introduced, it has to be sent somewhere. In the House, the Speaker refers bills to committee. In the Senate, the presiding officer does so. In both chambers, the parliamentarian advises on which committee has a valid claim, and that advice is generally followed. Each referral can become a precedent that shapes where similar bills go in the future.

Many bills touch more than one subject. House rules allow a single bill to be referred to several committees, with the Speaker designating one as the primary committee. The Senate generally sends a bill to the committee whose jurisdiction covers the bill's predominant subject matter.

Overlapping claims can turn into turf disputes. Health policy shows how this happens. In the House, parts of Medicare fall under Ways and Means, while other parts fall under Energy and Commerce. A major health bill can therefore require work from both panels.

Committees sometimes settle these overlaps through an exchange of letters between chairs. In that arrangement, one chair agrees to forgo action on a particular bill. The letter also records that the committee is not giving up its claim to the subject in future legislation.

Inside most full committees, subcommittees handle narrower slices of the panel's territory. A subcommittee typically holds hearings on its topics. In many committees, it also conducts a first markup before the full committee takes up the bill. That specialization lets a few lawmakers and staff build deep knowledge of a single program, agency or industry.

Subcommittees follow the same partisan structure as their parent panels. The majority party holds each subcommittee chair, and the minority party names a ranking member. The full committee keeps final authority over the legislation. A subcommittee can shape a bill's text, but only the full committee can report it to the full chamber.

The majority party in each chamber holds the chair of every committee, and the chair largely decides what that committee does. The minority party's top member on the panel serves as ranking member, the chair's main counterpart and the minority's lead voice.

A chair's power rests mostly on agenda control. The chair decides which bills the committee takes up, when it meets and which witnesses appear. A bill the chair declines to schedule usually goes nowhere, whatever support it has elsewhere in the chamber.

The majority party also holds most of the seats on each committee. Party leaders settle these ratios at the start of each Congress. The ratios generally track each party's share of the full chamber. That edge lets the majority win most committee votes on party lines.

Staff are another source of influence. Committee staff are typically divided between majority and minority, with the chair directing the majority side. Those aides draft legislation, prepare hearings and follow the agencies under the committee's jurisdiction.

The ranking member cannot set the agenda, but the role still carries weight. Ranking members lead the minority's questioning and coordinate its amendments and dissents. They also direct the minority staff. When a chair wants bipartisan backing for a bill, the ranking member is the first point of contact.

Seniority once decided chairs almost automatically in both chambers. That changed in the House in 1975, when the House Democratic Caucus voted to remove three sitting committee chairs. Party conferences and caucuses now choose committee leaders, and seniority is one factor among several.

Term limits also shape who holds the gavel. In 1995, House Republicans adopted a rule limiting committee chairs to three consecutive two-year Congresses, or six years. Senate Republicans set their own conference limits on how long a member can lead a committee.

When party control of a chamber flips, every chair changes hands at once. Ranking members from the old minority typically become the new chairs. Former chairs usually step into the ranking member role.

The shift can also happen in the middle of a Congress. In 2001, Sen. Jim Jeffords of Vermont left the Republican Party and began caucusing with Democrats as an independent. His switch gave Democrats control of the Senate. Democrats then took over the committee gavels.

Most bills must clear a committee before the full House or Senate considers them. The path runs from introduction and referral, through optional hearings and a markup session where members amend the text, to a committee vote on reporting the bill.

The process starts when a member introduces a bill. In the House, a member places it in the hopper, a box at the front of the chamber. In the Senate, a senator introduces it on the floor or submits it to the clerk.

Referral comes next. In the House, the Speaker refers each bill to the committee or committees with jurisdiction over its subject. The parliamentarian advises on that decision. In the Senate, the presiding officer refers bills, also on the parliamentarian's advice. A broad House bill can go to several committees, with one panel usually named as primary.

Referral does not guarantee action. A committee can simply decline to take up a bill. Any bill not enacted by the end of the two-year Congress expires. Sponsors must reintroduce it in the next Congress to try again.

For bills that do move, a committee may first hold a legislative hearing to take testimony on the proposal. Hearings are not required, and some bills go straight to markup.

Markup is where the text changes. Members meet to debate the bill section by section and vote on amendments. Committees often work from an amendment in the nature of a substitute. That device replaces the entire text of the introduced bill with a new version. Members can then offer amendments to the substitute itself.

The markup ends with a vote on whether to report the bill to the full chamber. A committee can report a bill favorably, with amendments or without a recommendation. It usually files a written report explaining the bill's purpose and the changes made during markup.

Reported bills also carry a price tag. The Congressional Budget Office generally prepares a cost estimate for bills that committees approve. Lawmakers use that estimate when the bill reaches the floor.

House members have a tool for getting around a stalled committee. A discharge petition signed by a majority of the House — 218 members when every seat is filled — can force a bill out of committee and onto the floor.

Congressional hearings are the main way committees gather facts, question officials and scrutinize the executive branch. When witnesses refuse to cooperate, many committees can issue subpoenas that legally compel documents or testimony. That authority comes from each committee's own rules and the rules of its chamber.

A hearing usually follows a set pattern. Witnesses submit written statements in advance, deliver short opening remarks and then answer questions from members. Questioning typically alternates between majority and minority members. Witnesses can include cabinet officials, agency heads, corporate executives, academics and private citizens.

Hearings serve different purposes. Legislative hearings examine a specific bill or policy problem before members write or revise legislation. Oversight hearings look at how existing laws are carried out and how federal money is spent. Investigative hearings examine alleged wrongdoing, whether inside the government or in the private sector.

Oversight is a core committee function, yet the Constitution never mentions it. The power is treated as implied by Congress's authority to legislate and to fund the government. The Supreme Court endorsed that view in McGrain v. Daugherty in 1927, a case arising from a Senate investigation of the Justice Department. The court held that the power of inquiry is an essential part of the legislative function.

That power has limits. In Watkins v. United States in 1957, the court ruled that congressional investigations must serve a valid legislative purpose. Congress cannot expose private affairs simply for the sake of exposure. In Trump v. Mazars USA in 2020, the court set out factors to weigh when Congress subpoenas a sitting president's personal records.

A subpoena carries legal force, but enforcing one can be slow. If a witness defies it, the House or Senate can vote to hold that person in contempt of Congress. The matter can then be referred to federal prosecutors for possible criminal charges. A chamber can also turn to federal court and seek a civil order compelling compliance.

Witnesses who lie to a committee face separate legal risk. Knowingly false statements to Congress can be prosecuted under federal law, whether or not the witness was under oath.

Committees also draw on outside investigators. The Government Accountability Office, a nonpartisan agency that works for Congress, audits federal programs and publishes reports that often become the basis for oversight hearings. Agency inspectors general supply similar findings from inside the executive branch and report their results to Congress.

The House Rules Committee decides how most major bills reach the House floor. It writes a resolution, called a rule, that sets the terms of debate on a bill, including which amendments, if any, members may offer.

That gatekeeping role makes the panel an arm of the House majority leadership. The majority party holds a lopsided share of its seats, well beyond its margin in the chamber as a whole. Under each party's internal rules, the Speaker chooses the majority's members on the panel. The minority leader chooses the minority's members.

A rule can take several forms. An open rule lets any member offer any germane amendment. A closed rule bars floor amendments entirely. A structured rule allows only the specific amendments the committee lists. Open rules for major legislation have become rare in recent Congresses.

A rule also sets the length of general debate and divides that time between the two parties. It can waive procedural objections, known as points of order, that members could otherwise raise against a bill. That lets leaders bring forward bills that might break a chamber rule, such as a budget limit.

Every rule is itself a House resolution. The full House must adopt it by majority vote before debate on the underlying bill can begin. The minority party usually votes against rules as a bloc. A failed rule vote therefore tends to signal a split inside the majority. The bill then stalls until leaders find a new path.

Some bills bypass the Rules Committee altogether. Under a procedure called suspension of the rules, the House can pass a bill with a two-thirds vote. Debate is limited to 40 minutes, and no amendments are allowed. Leaders use it mostly for less contested measures.

The committee's power has shifted with the speakership. Speaker Joseph Cannon chaired the panel until 1910. That year, a coalition led by Nebraska Rep. George Norris removed the Speaker from the committee. Decades later, chair Howard W. Smith of Virginia used the panel to delay civil rights bills. Speaker Sam Rayburn won a narrow 1961 vote to enlarge the committee and weaken that blockade.

The Senate has no direct equivalent. Its Rules and Administration Committee handles chamber rules, federal elections and Senate administration. Floor debate in the Senate runs largely on unanimous consent agreements. Ending a filibuster on most legislation takes 60 votes for cloture.

The House Ways and Means Committee writes federal tax law. Its jurisdiction also covers tariffs, trade, Social Security and Medicare. That puts some of the government's largest revenue sources and benefit programs under one House panel.

The committee's tax role starts with the Constitution. The Origination Clause, in Article I, Section 7, requires that bills for raising revenue begin in the House of Representatives. The Senate may propose or accept amendments to those bills, but it cannot introduce them first. As a result, major tax legislation usually begins at Ways and Means. That includes bills that change rates or create new credits and deductions.

Ways and Means is one of the oldest panels in the House. Its origins date to 1789, the first year Congress met under the Constitution. In its early decades, it handled federal spending as well as revenue. In 1865, the House created a separate Appropriations Committee and moved spending bills there. Ways and Means kept its focus on how the government raises money.

Trade is the committee's second major area. The Constitution gives Congress the power to lay duties, and Ways and Means handles tariff legislation on the House side. Trade agreements that require changes to U.S. law reach the committee as implementing legislation.

Social Security and Medicare form the third area. The committee writes the laws that govern Social Security retirement and disability benefits. It also oversees the payroll taxes that fund those benefits. It shares jurisdiction over parts of Medicare with the House Energy and Commerce Committee. Its work also extends to unemployment compensation.

The committee also has a specific legal tool for tax data. Section 6103(f) of the Internal Revenue Code allows the Ways and Means chair to request individual tax returns from the Treasury Department. The same provision covers the chair of the Senate Finance Committee and the Joint Committee on Taxation. Ways and Means used that authority to seek Donald Trump's tax returns, first requesting them in 2019. It received them in 2022 after a long court fight.

The Joint Committee on Taxation relies on a nonpartisan staff to estimate how much revenue tax proposals would raise or cost. Lawmakers on Ways and Means use those estimates as they weigh a bill's effect on the federal budget.

The House and Senate Appropriations committees write the annual spending bills that fund federal agencies. Agencies cannot legally spend discretionary funds until Congress enacts those bills. That makes the two panels the main gatekeepers of discretionary federal spending.

That authority traces to Article I, Section 9 of the Constitution. The clause states that no money shall be drawn from the Treasury except through appropriations made by law. It gives Congress, not the president, the final say over how federal dollars are spent. The arrangement is commonly called the power of the purse.

In the 118th and 119th Congresses, each chamber's committee splits its work among 12 subcommittees. Each subcommittee drafts one of the regular appropriations bills. Examples include the subcommittees on Defense, Homeland Security and the Legislative Branch. Appropriations subcommittee chairs, especially in the House, are often nicknamed "cardinals" on Capitol Hill. The term reflects their control over specific parts of the budget.

Appropriations are different from authorizations. An authorizing committee creates or continues a program and may recommend a funding level for it. An appropriations bill provides the actual money. A program can be authorized and still receive nothing if appropriators decline to fund it.

The committees mainly control discretionary spending. A large share of the federal budget is mandatory spending, which includes Social Security and Medicare. Those programs operate under permanent law. They do not need a yearly appropriation to pay benefits.

The federal fiscal year begins on Oct. 1. When Congress has not passed all of its regular spending bills by then, it often approves a continuing resolution. That stopgap measure keeps agencies running, generally at existing funding levels, for a set period. Lawmakers sometimes bundle several bills into one large package, known as an omnibus, instead of passing each separately.

If neither the regular bills nor a stopgap is enacted, funding lapses. The Antideficiency Act bars agencies from spending money that has not been appropriated. The result is a government shutdown, in which agencies halt work that the law does not exempt.

Control of funding also gives the committees leverage beyond dollar amounts. Spending bills can attach conditions and limits to how agencies use their money. Those provisions let appropriators shape policy. That reach adds to why seats on these panels are widely seen as influential.

The Senate Finance Committee writes the Senate's tax legislation and oversees the largest federal benefit programs, including Medicare, Medicaid and Social Security. It also handles trade law and screens nominees for several top economic posts, including Treasury secretary and U.S. Trade Representative.

Finance is one of the Senate's oldest panels. It was among the original standing committees the Senate created in 1816. Its jurisdiction today covers revenue, tariffs and the federal debt. It also covers a wide range of health and income-support programs.

On taxes, the committee is the Senate counterpart to the House Ways and Means Committee. When a revenue measure arrives from the House, Finance drafts the Senate's own version of the tax provisions. The two chambers must then settle their differences before a bill can reach the president. Major changes to the Internal Revenue Code pass through this panel on the Senate side.

Health care accounts for a large share of the committee's work. Medicare and Medicaid were both created by the Social Security Amendments of 1965. Finance has jurisdiction over both programs in the Senate. It also covers the Children's Health Insurance Program. Medicare payment rules for hospitals and physicians are written into laws this committee shapes.

Social Security falls under Finance as well. The program dates to the Social Security Act of 1935. Changes to benefit formulas, retirement ages or payroll tax financing would go through this committee before reaching the Senate floor.

Trade is the third major area of its work. Finance handles legislation on tariffs, customs and trade agreements. After a president negotiates a trade deal, Congress typically passes an implementing bill to put it into U.S. law. Finance has jurisdiction over that bill in the Senate.

The committee also writes trade promotion authority legislation. That legislation sets the expedited procedures Congress uses to consider trade agreements, including limits on amendments and debate.

Nominations give the panel another source of leverage. Nominees for Treasury secretary and U.S. Trade Representative come before Finance for hearings and a committee vote. So do nominees to lead the Department of Health and Human Services and the Internal Revenue Service. The same goes for nominees to run the Social Security Administration and the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services.

The House and Senate Armed Services committees write the National Defense Authorization Act, the annual law that sets Defense Department policy and authorizes its spending levels. The Senate panel also vets senior military officers and Pentagon nominees before the full Senate votes on them.

The NDAA is one of the few major bills Congress passes every year. Lawmakers have enacted it annually for decades. That record makes it a frequent vehicle for amendments on subjects only loosely tied to the military. Each chamber's committee drafts and marks up its own version. The House and Senate then settle their differences before a final bill goes to the president.

The bill's reach is wide. It sets troop levels, military pay raises, benefits and personnel rules for service members. It authorizes weapons programs such as ships, aircraft and missiles, and it shapes how the Pentagon buys them. It also extends to national security programs at the Energy Department, including the nuclear weapons work of the National Nuclear Security Administration.

Authorization is not the same as funding. The NDAA sets policy and spending ceilings, but it does not provide the money itself. That step belongs to the defense spending bill written by the Appropriations committees. The two processes run in parallel each year, and their final numbers do not always match.

The Senate Armed Services Committee carries an added duty under the Senate's advice and consent role. It holds hearings on civilian nominees such as the secretary of defense and the service secretaries. It also reviews promotions and assignments for senior military officers, including the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff and combatant commanders.

Most officer promotions move through the committee and the Senate floor in large batches without controversy. That routine can break down when a single senator objects. In 2023, Sen. Tommy Tuberville of Alabama held up hundreds of senior military promotions for months. He did so to protest a Pentagon policy covering travel for abortion care.

The House committee has no confirmation role. The Constitution gives that power to the Senate alone. The House panel's influence runs through the NDAA text itself and through oversight hearings with military leaders on readiness, budgets and overseas operations.

The Senate Judiciary Committee screens nominees for federal judgeships, from district courts up to the Supreme Court. It holds their confirmation hearings and votes on whether each nomination should advance to the full Senate.

That role comes from the Appointments Clause in Article II of the Constitution. The president nominates federal judges, and the Senate gives its advice and consent. In practice, the Judiciary Committee does most of the vetting before the full chamber votes.

Federal judges appointed under Article III hold their posts "during good behaviour," which in effect means life tenure. A single confirmation can influence court rulings for decades after a president leaves office.

The process usually starts with paperwork. Nominees fill out a detailed committee questionnaire covering their careers, writings, finances and past cases. Committee staff and senators review that record before any public session.

Next comes the confirmation hearing. The nominee answers questions from senators of both parties, and outside witnesses may also testify. Supreme Court hearings draw national television coverage and can run for several days. Hearings for lower-court nominees tend to be shorter and often include several candidates at once.

After the hearing, the committee votes. It can report a nomination favorably, unfavorably or without recommendation. A nomination that never gets a committee vote generally stalls.

The committee has long followed the "blue slip" custom for nominees who would serve in a senator's home state. Under it, the chair asks those senators to signal whether they support the nominee. How strictly chairs honor blue slips has varied over time and by type of court.

The House has its own Judiciary Committee. It plays no part in confirming judges. The Constitution gives that power to the Senate alone.

Judgeships are only part of the Senate panel's portfolio. Its jurisdiction also covers federal criminal law, immigration and antitrust. Bills that affect the federal courts themselves, such as proposals to create new judgeships, also go through the committee.

The panel considers senior Justice Department nominees as well, including the attorney general. Proposed constitutional amendments fall within its reach too.

The Senate Foreign Relations Committee is the panel that reviews treaties before the full Senate votes on them. Under the Constitution, the president can ratify a treaty only after two-thirds of senators present give their consent.

That requirement comes from Article II, Section 2. It gives the president power to make treaties "by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate." The executive branch negotiates and signs an agreement. The president then sends it to the Senate, where it is referred to Foreign Relations.

The committee can hold hearings on a treaty and question administration officials about its terms. If members back it, they report a resolution of ratification to the floor. That resolution can carry conditions, such as reservations, understandings or declarations. These conditions shape how the U.S. interprets its obligations under the agreement. The committee can also hold a treaty without acting on it.

Treaties are handled differently from ordinary bills. A bill that has not passed dies when a Congress ends. A treaty stays pending until the Senate approves or rejects it, or until the president withdraws it. As a result, some agreements have sat in committee for years.

The two-thirds threshold has blocked major agreements before. The Senate refused to approve the Treaty of Versailles after World War I. That decision kept the U.S. out of the League of Nations. Henry Cabot Lodge, who led the opposition, chaired Foreign Relations at the time.

Treaty work is only part of the job. The committee considers the president's nominee for secretary of state. It also reviews nominees for ambassadorships and other senior State Department posts. Its hearings give senators a public forum to question these candidates before a committee vote sends a nomination to the floor.

The panel also writes legislation on foreign assistance and the operations of the State Department. That work gives it a say in how the U.S. delivers economic and security aid abroad.

The Senate created Foreign Relations in 1816, when it set up its first group of standing committees. The House has its own counterpart, the Foreign Affairs Committee. That panel handles foreign policy legislation but has no formal role in treaty approval.

The House Energy and Commerce Committee holds one of the widest portfolios in Congress. Its reach spans health care, energy, telecommunications, consumer protection and interstate commerce.

Much of that breadth rests on the Constitution's Commerce Clause. Article I gives Congress the power to regulate commerce among the states and with foreign nations. The committee's work draws on that grant, from rules for products sold across state lines to the structure of national energy markets.

Health care is one of its largest areas. In the House, the committee handles Medicaid. It shares jurisdiction over parts of Medicare with the Ways and Means Committee. It also oversees major federal health agencies, including the Food and Drug Administration, the National Institutes of Health and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Drug approval rules, medical device regulation and public health programs all come under its review.

Energy is another core area. The committee writes legislation affecting the Department of Energy, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission. Its environmental work covers air quality and drinking water through laws such as the Clean Air Act and the Safe Drinking Water Act.

Telecommunications gives the panel authority over the Federal Communications Commission. Broadband, spectrum, broadcasting and much of the policy debate over the internet fall within its lane. Consumer protection adds the Federal Trade Commission and the Consumer Product Safety Commission to its oversight list.

That mix means very different industries answer to regulators within the committee's reach. Drugmakers, electric utilities, wireless carriers and makers of household products all operate under agencies the panel oversees. A single bill from this committee can change the rules for an entire sector.

The committee is among the oldest standing committees in the House. Its name and scope have changed over time as Congress added, removed and reassigned subject areas. To manage a portfolio this large, it organizes its work through several subcommittees. Each one focuses on a narrower slice, such as health, energy or communications and technology.
