How a 20-Year-Old Texas Climber Lost His Life on the Matterhorn

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Kieran Liner, a 20-year-old climber based in Austin, TX, died in an accident on the Matterhorn in October. (Photo: Courtesy Precenia Newman)

A 20-year-old climber named Kieran Liner from Austin, Texas, died on October 27 after falling from the Matterhorn’s Hörnli Ridge. He was climbing with his friend Eli Hawkins.

Valais Cantonal Police said the two climbers were ascending shortly after 6 a.m. when rocks fell near them at about 11,500 feet. Hawkins lost sight of Liner and immediately called for help. An Air Zermatt helicopter responded. Rescuers found Liner below the route and pronounced him dead at the scene.

Prosecutors opened an investigation to determine the precise circumstances of the fall. The initial police statement reported rockfall nearby, but didn’t specify exactly how Liner fell or died.

Hawkins, who says he met Liner in Austin around five years ago, called him his “best bud” and his climbing partner. “It is what it is,” Hawkins told Climbing. “He died doing what he loved.”

Originally from Pensacola, Florida, Liner had lived in Austin since 2020. According to Hawkins, Liner liked Modest Mouse, IPAs, and American Spirit Blues. But what he loved most, Hawkins wrote, was climbing in the mountains.

In an obituary shared last week, Liner’s sister Precenia Newman remembered her younger brother as a skateboarder, golfer, and alpinist—someone who brought the same enthusiasm to everything he pursued. “He approached everything in life with 150% of himself,” Newman wrote.

Newman described a close circle of friends who had become family through shared adventures and everyday life. Among them was Hawkins, whose friendship and life as Liner’s roommate she singled out as especially meaningful. She also remembered the love surrounding Liner from his parents, Justine Hamel and Nick Liner, as well as his siblings, grandparents, aunts, uncles, nieces, and nephews. Newman has organized a fundraiser to bring him home so the family can celebrate his life in Pensacola and Austin.

While Hawkins grew up climbing as a kid with his father and uncle, Liner got into the sport later in life, but it quickly became his passion. Hawkins said the two of them had strong mentors, including Hawkins’s dad Stephen, Ben Darce, and his uncle Bryan Hawkins who owns a climbing gym in Everett, Washington. “He was fearless,” Hawkins said of Liner. “I was fearless—still am. I’m going to keep climbing for him. We just climbed hard and partied hard.”

This past July, Liner had started an Instagram account called @mountkieran dedicated primarily to climbing and mountaineering content. His first reel captured climbing Mt. Rainier. Most of his content leaned humorous, with jokes about “Conrad Anger” and poking fun at himself as an “Austin influencer” while board climbing.

But occasionally, Liner shared more serious content, like footage of ascending a steep snow pitch with a quote from Reinhold Messner: “To go on an adventure, you need difficulties, you need danger. If death were not a possibility, coming out would be nothing.”

Generally considered the most popular and easiest route by which to ascend the Matterhorn, the Hörnli Ridge rises roughly 4,000 vertical feet from the Hörnli Hut to the 14,692-foot summit. Rated AD (“fairly difficult”) on the alpine scale, the route combines sustained exposed scrambling with technical rock climbing rated around 5.4. In general, parties take four to five hours for the ascent, plus another five to six hours for the descent, which involves downclimbing and rappelling much of the same terrain.

Fixed ropes assist climbers on certain sections, but parties also bring their own ropes. Snow and ice can be encountered near the summit, requiring crampons and an ice axe. The technical grade captures only part of the challenge of navigating and descending that exposed terrain in mountaineering boots.

To learn more about the Hörnli Ridge where Liner’s accident took place, I reached out to my friend Steph Abegg, who claimed the fastest known time by a woman on the route this past summer.

In 1985, Abegg’s dad soloed the Matterhorn in a T-shirt. Twenty years later, when Abegg and her sister were in Zermatt and decided to climb it, that was the image they held in their minds. Their dad was an experienced climber, and he’d enjoyed ideal conditions on his ascent.

Accordingly, the Abegg sisters expected a casual adventure. Instead, they encountered verglas—a thin glaze of ice on the rock—and a whiteout. They turned around at the Solvay Hut, an emergency shelter at 13,133 feet. At this point, they were roughly 2,440 vertical feet above the Hörnli Hut, where the climb begins, and still about 1,560 feet below the summit.

“It could be said I underestimated the Matterhorn myself in 2005,” Abegg told me. “Had we continued, it’s entirely possible we could have gotten into trouble.”

This past summer, Abegg returned, climbing the Hörnli Ridge with a partner before completing a solo, unsupported round trip from Zermatt in 10 hours, 8 minutes, and 19 seconds—the fastest women’s time then recorded on the FKT website, supported or unsupported. A month later, Kalie McCrystal lowered the record to 6 hours, 47 minutes, and 33 seconds.

The route’s moderate technical grade, fixed ropes, and popularity can make the Hörnli Ridge seem approachable. But finding the line and staying on solid rock demands attention. Stray from the standard route and the rock can become looser. Altitude, changing weather, and fall consequences add to the difficulty.

During Abegg’s partnered ascent this past summer, a washing-machine-sized boulder was accidentally dislodged. It tumbled down a gully, triggering what she described as a significant rockslide, mostly off route. Higher up, loose rock littered the climbing line. Abegg described rocks ranging in size from marbles to baseballs, or even volleyballs, that climbers could knock onto people below. The terrain, she said, had historically remained under snow year-round.

“Had to move very carefully,” she wrote. Guides stopped taking clients up the route during her trip.

Aside from the omnipresent risk of rockfall in any alpine environment, a 2025 study found that climate change has caused an increase in rockfall on the Matterhorn as permafrost thaws. Just a couple months ago, German online magazine Lacrux published an article about the increasing hazards of rockfall on the Matterhorn, as well as on Mont Blanc.

“Having the innate mountain sense to know when to call it is a valuable quality on an objective like this,” Abegg said.

Andy Hansen, an IFMGA-certified (International Federation of Mountain Guides) guide based in Longmont, Colorado, has guided clients on the Matterhorn twice. Speaking from his own experience, he strongly recommends hiring a guide for a first attempt.

He said recreational climbers in Western Europe are generally more accustomed to hiring guides than their counterparts in the United States. One advantage of hiring a guide on the Hörnli Ridge is how closely they follow variable conditions on a route, according to Hansen.

“The local guides have an intimacy with the mountain that gives them the advantage of having the route finding dialed, understanding the conditions of the mountain from day to day, and knowing where and when to move quickly to manage the rockfall hazard,” Hansen told me.

Hansen observed an unusual amount of rockfall in the Alps this past spring and summer, calling the Hörnli Ridge noticeably more unstable. He pointed to local guides’ familiarity with the ridge and their day-to-day knowledge of its conditions. A weather forecast can tell any climber how warm it’s likely to get, but knowing the ridge helps a guide judge which sections are most prone to rockfall, where to move quickly to limit exposure, and whether to climb that day at all.

However, whether a guide would have changed the outcome for Liner is an entirely different question. “It’s virtually impossible to say with any degree of certainty that had this party been with a mountain guide, the outcome would have been any different,” Hansen said. In his view, it was the kind of misfortune that could befall a guided party, too.

Hawkins told Climbing that they were aware of the risk of rockfall and had accepted it as a possible and uncontrollable hazard that you can’t “really do anything about.” He added that he never considered hiring a guide because he has sufficient experience and wants to take on alpine objectives independently. “If you do hire a guide, you didn’t climb the mountain,” he said of his philosophy.

While a guide is not required to climb the Matterhorn, it does materially improve your chances of reaching the summit. According to Global Summit Guide, an independent, research-based mountaineering resource based in Utah, the summit rate varies considerably depending on whether climbers are traveling with a guide.

A Global Summit Guide report updated this past July states that while the overall Matterhorn success rate is 55%, guided climbers see a 72% success rate, while unguided climbers hover around a 38% success rate. Meanwhile, first-timers like Liner and Hawkins only have an 18% shot at reaching the top. Furthermore, during the popular climbing season of May through October, October has the lowest success rate of 22%.

Global Summit Guide also reinforces the Matterhorn’s reputation as Europe’s most deadly mountain, with an average of eight to 12 fatalities annually. They note that about 1 in 140 climbers perish in their attempt to summit. More than 500 people (and counting) have died on the Matterhorn to date.

Dialogue and debate over the broader trends indirectly related to Liner’s death have already  emerged in the climbing community.

After Liner died, a Reddit thread in r/Mountaineering sparked debate about the climbing-related content he and Hawkins had posted on their Instagram accounts. “Obviously, there are videos of me and him partying in Europe, but when we’re actually climbing, I take precautions and I’m not an idiot when I climb,” Hawkins told Climbing. “I take safety as a number one precaution.” He added that Liner would “probably be laughing” at what Hawkins sees as misinformed comments online.

Then, in an Instagram reel posted on November 2, Washington-based gear manufacturer High Mountain Gear turned the discussion toward a different topic: a video Norwegian climber Magnus Midtbø made about his 2025 Matterhorn solo. High Mountain Gear took particular issue with Midtbø looking up how to use crampons shortly before his climb. In the comments of High Mountain Gear’s reel, some agreed that a climber with Midtbø’s reach ought to think about the example he sets. Others asked why he was being brought into a conversation about someone else’s accident.

Hawkins explained that he and Liner had set the Matterhorn as a big goal independent of any online content. “It’s just a climb we wanted to do for a long time,” Hawkins said of the Matterhorn. “It had nothing to do with any Instagram videos.” He noted that he wasn’t very familiar with Midtbø’s Matterhorn video.

Beyond this recent incident, when Midtbø’s video came out, Swiss public broadcaster SRF reported that Midtbø talked openly about his limited preparation. He struggled with the altitude and got off route. Other climbers had to call him back to the right line. Once he got down, he admitted he had underestimated the risk and urged viewers against following his example. Air Zermatt’s rescue chief Anjan Truffer told SRF that he worried viewers might still interpret Midtbø’s climb as a success, without appreciating the gap between his climbing ability and their own.

In Midtbø’s defense, however, he could have cut the moments when he was tired, lost, or scared. But he chose to leave them in. Some viewers in the YouTube comments took the climb as a cautionary tale. Others wanted a follow-up with a guide explaining where things nearly went wrong. Ultimately, given the irrelevance of Midtbø’s video to Hawkins’s and Liner’s Matterhorn objective, as well as the full disclosures that Midtbø provided in his video, the notion that YouTube content is to blame for this or any other fatality seems besides the point.

As a guide, Hansen sees responsibility on both sides of the screen. He said creators like Midtbø have some accountability for what they put out, even when they include a disclaimer. But viewers must assess whether their own skills match the demands of any climb described in online content.

Abegg’s father came home with a story about soloing the Matterhorn in a T-shirt. Abegg and her sister came home with a story about turning around at the Solvay Hut. Midtbø came home, too. But in his video, you can see how close he came to a different outcome when he lost the route on exposed terrain.

Kieran Liner didn’t come home. He was 20 years old. The mountain doesn’t care what drew you there, whose story you heard, or how long you spent preparing. Even the most experienced climber can be in the wrong place at the wrong time, especially when it comes to escalating hazards like rockfall.

Liner’s family is planning to celebrate his life in Pensacola and Austin. In her obituary, Newman invited everyone who knew him, shared his adventures, or found inspiration in his life to join them. “Whether you knew him for a lifetime or crossed paths with him only briefly,” she wrote, “you are part of the story he leaves behind.”

Climbing Magazine reached out to Kyle Willis of High Mountain Gear and Magnus Midtbø for comment, but have not received a response.
