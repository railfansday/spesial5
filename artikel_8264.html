Editor’s Letter: Why Do Hard Things Outside? Our Outsiders of the Year Answer

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Anyone who does hard things outside has heard it. Why climb a mountain that’s already been climbed? Why run 253 miles across a desert? To test the limits of human potential is the tidy answer. The real one, as our Outsiders of the Year show, shifts like snow on a big face: it’s different for everyone, and never quite the same twice.

This year Alex Honnold free soloed a 1,667-foot skyscraper live on Netflix, after being explained away as a brain-scan curiosity for two decades. Now 40, with two preschoolers, he fits climbing in where he can. Some days that means getting up at 2 a.m. to climb a thousand-foot granite wall to still make it home for dinner. Why bother? “In some ways, climbing is my only opportunity to feel like I’m good at something,” he tells Features Editor Frederick Dreier. Any parent who has squeezed in a run between school pickups knows the feeling.

Jim Morrison and his partner Hilaree Nelson shared a dream no one had pulled off: to climb Everest’s North Face and ski it back down. In 2022, Nelson died on Manaslu, and Morrison swore off the mountains. Then his dog started pulling him back up the trail, a little higher every day. In 2025, with Jimmy Chin filming beside him for Everest: The Other Side, Morrison skied 9,000 feet off the top of the world. At the bottom, he screamed, “That one’s for you, Hil.”

Not everyone on Everest gets to ask why. Sherpas still take the biggest risks so others can reach the top. This spring, Hillary Dawa Sherpa, a 57-year-old cook, was pushed toward the summit when his outfitter came up a guide short. Descending with eight empty oxygen bottles (lose one and he’d be docked $250), he ran out of air and was assumed dead. Six days later, he dragged himself out of the Khumbu Icefall on a broken leg. He’d only gone because someone else’s summit needed him.

For Christina Koch, the answer was bigger than herself. In April, she flew around the moon on Artemis II under a patch her crew designed so “A II” read as “all.” When she heard the whole planet was watching, she cried, and in zero gravity, the tears had nowhere to fall.

Rachel Entrekin’s answer is the hardest-won of all. As a teenager, she nearly didn’t survive. In treatment for an eating disorder, she learned a rule she still runs by: do the very thing you least want to do. This year she ran 253 miles across Arizona and became the first woman to win the Cocodona outright. “Running helps me practice who I want to be when things get tough,” she says.

Adventure has never had a bigger audience. Millions watched Honnold’s climb in real time, and this fall, Everest is all over movie screens. Almost none of those viewers will ski Everest or solo a skyscraper. But every one of those moments sends someone out the door to find their own answer. Ask anyone at a trailhead on a Sunday morning why they came: to do something hard, to carry someone with them, to feel small in front of something vast, to become who they want to be.

The Northflag gets that the best travel gear does its job without asking for attention. The name nods to the 1950s British North Greenland Expedition, where the explorers wore Tudors, but its smartest feature is modern: a true GMT, so you can set local time the moment you land and still know the time back home. It’ll keep running for almost three days after you take it off, and it looks as right at a trailhead as at the dinner table.

I’ve logged about 50 miles of Italy’s Via Francigena in it, both hiking and running, and ran all over London and Paris. Best part: it doesn’t look ridiculous with civilian clothes. One shoe, three jobs, and more room in my carry-on.
