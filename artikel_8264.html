💪 Into the High Sierra: ❄️ Snow, Ice & Forrester Pass 🏔️

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

I packed up from my stealth spot before daybreak and used the park bathroom to change and brush my teeth.

Sitting at a picnic table in the shade, I pulled up my blog. That’s when I realized I had forgotten to hit publish on the post I had scheduled for the previous week. I rushed to get it published, feeling frustrated with myself and the growing pressure to keep up with my writing. Once my blog was published online, I spent the remainder of the morning working on my next entry.

I picked at my leftover Chinese food for breakfast as I sorted through my resupply and packed my backpack for the trail.

Walking through town, I stopped at Carl’s Jr. for a Double Quarter Pounder with Cheese and a strawberry milkshake. I packed the burger away to eat later on the trail.

Before hitching out, I stopped at Elevation Gear, where I finished my ice cream and spent a few minutes visiting with Marble, Electrolyte, and Pickles while telling them my plans.

Walking along the road toward the Horseshoe Meadow turnoff, I dropped my pack to hitch. I stood with my thumb out, hoping for a ride. A few vehicles stopped to offer rides, but they were heading either to Whitney Portal or the Alabama Hills. A couple of other drivers pulled over only to tell me they didn’t have room.

The gestures were kind, but each time a vehicle stopped, my hopes went up. Having them dashed by another apology made the wait even more discouraging.

After 30 minutes with no luck, I grabbed my backpack and walked farther out of town.

A farmer working in a field across the street told me he had been watching me try to hitch a ride down the road. He wished me luck.

The driver, Taquito, told me he was a Triple Crown thru-hiker from Omaha. He was on vacation, hiking and providing trail magic to hikers. We talked about hiking, his Triple Crown experiences, and the trail magic he was sharing with hikers. He took me all the way to the Horseshoe Meadow trailhead.

At the trailhead, Taquito offered me even more trail magic in the form of Gatorade, an apple, and Gummy Clusters. Taking advantage of the nearby trash cans, I drank the Gatorade, ate the apple, and demolished my double burger before heading out on the trail. I tucked the Gummy Clusters away for later.

Leaving the trailhead at 4:30 p.m., I began my hike into the mountains. Lone Pine had been hot during the day, now, in elevation the evening air was getting cold. The loose, sandy trail made walking difficult, especially with my heavy backpack. I moved slowly, tired and a little stressed about finding a place to camp before the elevation started.

I spent too much time looking off-trail for flat spots. Two miles in, the trees began to grow thicker, I suddenly came across a sandy clearing with a rabbit. Seeing it hopping around so carefree made the area feel safe and inviting. I liked the idea of sharing this little clearing with the rabbit, so I decided this was where I would camp.

I used my newly acquired ice axe to scrape and level the sandy ground, making a flat spot for my tent.

For dinner, I made a Frito taco bowl with dehydrated pea-protein “beef” and dried refried beans. It hit the spot after a long day. With a full belly, a newly leveled tent spot, and a rabbit for company, I crawled into my tent. Too tired to write, I fell asleep around 8 p.m. with a bit of daylight still lingering. I had a long way to go, but at least I was back in the mountains.

I woke up at 3 a.m. to a dark, chilly morning. I was camped on the Cottonwood Pass Trail, 2.6 miles from its junction with the PCT at Mile 751.4. Nearly 1,200 feet lower in elevation. I opened one of my tent vestibules to help ventilate the condensation before burrowing back into my quilt for a little more rest.

At 4:15 a.m., I woke to the howling of coyotes. It made me smile. I love being in nature. Still tucked into my quilt, I reached for my headlamp in the top pocket of my backpack, where I always keep it. Although I normally sleep with it around my neck, I knew exactly where to find it.

While getting breakfast ready, I also pulled out my snacks for the day. I try to eat my heavier, bulkier foods first to lighten my pack. As I was packing up, I spotted Spicy Bite (Southern California) hiking toward town. He graciously offered to carry out my trash. Everything was inside a Ziploc bag, including half a family-size bag of Fritos that I had hiked in. I slipped the bag into his backpack without making him take it off. One less thing to carry!

A little after 7 a.m., I was hiking across a green, grassy meadow covered in frost. The sun hadn’t yet risen over the mountains, and the air grew colder as I approached a stream. After crossing it, I looked up at the steep mountainside ahead. I checked my map, trying to figure out where the trail passed through the snow-covered pass above. I began preparing myself for the climb.

The trail entered a pine forest and began to switchback.

The sun began to warm the earth. Chipmunks scurried over the white boulders and fallen logs along the trail. At the last switchback before the top, I stopped for a break. I was above 11,000 feet and beginning to notice a slight headache coming on. After five minutes, I pushed on to the top and finally rejoined the PCT.

Just minutes later, I stopped at the first trickle of flowing water. My headache was slowly developing, and my stomach wasn’t feeling right. I needed to hydrate and give myself time to adjust before continuing farther into the Sierras. I spent the next hour and a half sitting beside the trail at 11,132 feet, drinking water and trying to stay warm. My headache improved slightly, but I still wasn’t feeling quite right.

Pushing to higher elevations can be dangerous. In 2022, I had been just a few days behind a young hiker who lost her life on Forester Pass due to altitude-related issues. That experience had stayed with me and reminded me not to take the mountains lightly.

As I packed up, I noticed another hiker continuing past the Lone Pine exit. It was Diva (Tennessee), who had already built up his trail legs after hiking the Appalachian Trail and then the Florida Trail over the past year. He was planning to continue north to Kearsarge Pass.

We hiked together to the frozen Chicken Spring Lake, where we met a group of local high school students and their teachers. The students were about to graduate and were on a 20-day backpacking trip.

Having company lifted my spirits, even though I still wasn’t feeling 100%. Before leaving the lake, I noticed my feet were feeling swollen and my shoes were getting tight. With the altitude already on my mind, it was another thing I was paying attention to. I took an ibuprofen, collected a little more water, and said goodbye as Diva continued north.

As I climbed over the next pass and entered Kings Canyon National Park, the landscape opened up to beautiful views. A meadow stretched out to my left, while the trail wrapped around the mountainside. To my right, the majestic pink and white rock walls of Cirque Peak towered above me at 12,900 feet.

I stopped for lunch and noticed dark clouds gathering in the distance. I moved more snacks into my hip-belt pocket. With most of my elevation gain behind me for the day, I wasn’t too concerned about being caught in a storm while exposed on a climb, though I did pick up my pace.

As thunder rumbled in the dark clouds, I hurried to the next water source. I found a few flat spots nearby and collected water, letting my gravity filter do its work while I set up my tent. With the storm moving in, I wanted to get settled before it arrived.

Happy with my setup, I was about to put my gear inside when I looked up. Above my tent, I saw one dead tree leaning against another, with yet another dead tree on the opposite side. With thunder sounding closer, I quickly decided to move my tent. I relocated to a completely different spot a short distance away.

Another hiker, Scott (New Zealand), came down the trail. He was planning to continue a few more miles before setting up for the night. I finally threw my gear into my tent just as it started to hail.

Small hailstones continued falling steadily. I occasionally bumped the sides of my tent to knock off the accumulating ice. It was only 5 p.m., but I was happy to be inside and dry, with enough water for the night.

I boiled water and mixed half a Mountain House pepperoni pizza meal with half a chicken alfredo meal.

After the hail stopped, the golden sun came streaming through the pine trees. Tiny white pieces of hail were scattered across the ground, contrasting with the beautiful green landscape.

I brushed my teeth and placed my bear can behind a large fallen tree as the sun dipped below the mountains.

Crawling into my tent, I thought about how far I had come. I was proud of myself for making it this far on the trail and stepping into the high-altitude Sierras. It had taken bravery and strength to venture this far into the wilderness on my own. Even with the cold, I was genuinely happy to be surrounded by such a beautiful place. I felt grateful to be there, with a deep respect for the mountains and the wilderness I am fortunate enough to experience.

At 3:45 a.m., my alarm pulled me out of sleep. I wanted to eat up any food that required cooking today so that tomorrow, when I pushed over Forrester Pass, I could just grab bars and snacks and keep moving.

After boiling water, I made oatmeal and a mix of hot cocoa and coffee.

Part of the reason I struggle to hike with others is that I’m a slower hiker and take longer to get out of camp. I get frustrated when I’m the last one to leave, and that frustration can often affect my attitude throughout the day.

This morning, I realized that taking my time getting out of camp was a form of self-care. When I give myself time to get everything done without rushing, I’m happier and more relaxed. I also know I’ve taken care of everything at camp and haven’t forgotten any chores.

At 7 a.m., I continued my hike. The trail descended toward Rock Creek, where it opened into a beautiful grassy valley surrounded by snow-covered mountains. Water was flowing everywhere. It was a completely different landscape from the desert I’d been hiking through the past two months.

As I approached Rock Creek, I passed established campsites, a few with hikers still packing up. The creek was rushing, and the morning air was cold.

The stepping stones used later in the year were covered in inches of rushing water. Looking downstream, I spotted an old fallen tree with no bark. It would likely be slippery with the frost that morning. A little farther down, I saw a newer-looking fallen tree across the creek. Thinking this might be an option, I started climbing onto it, but some of the bark slipped off, revealing a smooth, slippery surface. Looking farther along the log, I noticed more sections where the bark was missing.

I hiked back upstream, past the trail, but didn’t find any better options for a dry crossing. I felt most comfortable getting my shoes wet, hoping that if I crossed on the rocks, I could at least keep the rest of my clothing dry.

I tried stepping across the rocks first. As soon as both feet were on the slippery rocks, I decided it would be better to just cross the creek. Plunging my legs into the freezing current, the water reached my knees. Even part of my rolled-up pants got wet as I climbed out on the other side.

I met a NOBO JMT hiker named Will (California), who had taken off his shoes to cross the creek and was putting them back on.

At the top of the next hill, I took a break on a boulder with a view, dreaming of French toast with syrup, maybe even peanut butter.

Scott from the day before walked up while I was taking a break. This was when we officially met.

Hiking through a rocky boulder field, I heard black-capped Chickadees chirping something that sounded like the word “cheeseburger.” Every time I heard it, I couldn’t help but laugh and found myself getting hungry!

Reaching Crabtree Meadow, I crossed Whitney Creek using a log. I felt a little uneasy about my balance with my backpack and how high the log was above the creek, but I made it across. I was proud of myself!

After crossing Whitney Creek, I passed the first trail junction leading to Mount Whitney

The PCT climbed through exposed switchbacks with some great views. I stopped halfway up to apply sunscreen.

Passing the second junction to Whitney, I was slightly tempted to take it, but continued hiking the PCT. I didn’t have enough food to add the extra day and a half, and the last forecast I’d read was predicting snow in a few days.

I picked my way through some patchy snow before arriving at Wallace Creek. After hiking up and down the creek looking for a dry crossing, I couldn’t find one. This time I took off my shoes, socks, calf compression sleeves, and gaiters before crossing.

The creek was cold, the rocks were slippery, and the water was high enough to get the bottom of my shorts a little wet.

I dried my feet with a bandanna and let them air out for a bit before putting everything back on.

Clouds started rolling in as the afternoon continued. I needed to push to get as close to Forrester Pass as I could.

As I approached the climb up Big Horn Plateau, the clouds grew closer, and I could hear thunder rumbling. Seeing a cluster of trees about 300 feet off the trail, I crossed a small creek to take a break, eat a snack, and assess the storm.

With a snack in my stomach, most of the dark clouds had moved off to the south, though I could still hear thunder rumbling in the distance. I ventured back to the trail, still cautious as I headed toward a treeless area at 11,056 feet, with a metal bear canister in my backpack.

As I climbed onto Big Horn Plateau, it began to snow. The clouds obscured some of the panoramic views of the Sierra mountains, though I could still see much of the surrounding landscape. I was exhausted and cold, and I wasn’t particularly happy about being out there in the weather. But stopping wasn’t even a consideration.

My last obstacle for the day was Tyndall Creek. One of the trail notes mentioned a more manageable crossing about a half-mile upstream. As I started heading upstream, a hiker on the other side spotted me and came down to the creek.

I didn’t see him right away and couldn’t hear him over the rushing water. He pointed toward the spot where the trail crossed and told me to just cross there.

Again, I took off my shoes, socks, gaiters, and calf compression sleeves before stepping into the frigid water.

There were a few small groups of hikers scattered among the campsites. I found a semi-flat, semi-secluded spot and set up camp. I made dinner and got ready for bed. Tomorrow was going to be an early start. I was a little nervous about Forrester Pass. I had 5 miles to the top and didn’t want to be plunge-stepping down the north side in the afternoon. I was glad I wouldn’t have to cross Tyndall Creek in the morning.

At 3:00 a.m., I woke to bright stars in the cold morning sky. I mixed coffee into cold water and packed snacks into my pockets. After packing up camp, I took off my puffy and hit the trail at 4:40 a.m.

Hiking in the early morning light, I didn’t need my headlamp. The colors in the sky were stunning.

The trail crossed small snowmelt creeks, weaving in and out of stream crossings. At one point, the melting snow had created a stream right across the trail.

My feet crunched over the frozen ground, and I slipped a bit on a patch of ice. I realized I needed to be more careful crossing snow bridges, especially with rushing water underneath.

The only person I saw that morning was Scott, who passed me. I was still hoping to find a hiking buddy for the pass, and I was glad everything was so frozen.

As I continued climbing, I kick-stepped up increasingly steep icy fields, trying to avoid the boulders that were beginning to melt and weaken the snow in the morning sun.

As I looked up in awe at 13,897-foot Junction Peak next to it, Forrester Pass, at 13,120 feet, loomed ahead like a boss. I could see two people crossing over its scary icy chute above.

Just then, I heard someone hiking on the ice behind me. I was surprised to see Scott again. He had taken a break off trail, and I must have passed him at some point.

I told Scott we should hike together up and over the pass, and he agreed. I’m sure he did it just so I would feel more comfortable hiking it. Once we were out of the valley bowl, the switchbacks were mostly clear. They received afternoon sun each day.

As I made my way toward Forrester Pass, I felt a mix of intimidation and excitement. The climb ahead was daunting, and the icy terrain made me nervous, especially the chute. I was more worried about crossing the chute than the north side, although it would turn out that I probably should have been more concerned about the other side. Still, I felt confident in my ability to tackle the challenge. Reaching Forrester Pass would be a huge milestone, both physically and personally. It was the highest point on the PCT, and I was excited to see what it would take to get there.

When we finally reached the ice chute, I watched Scott scoot across the iced-over, foot-shaped holes in the ice. I questioned whether I should take the time to put on my microspikes. I hadn’t worn them yet on the trail.

My heart started to race. I secured my microspikes to the bottom of each shoe and exchanged one of my trekking poles for my ice axe. I crossed the ice chute one foot at a time, never looking down and maintaining three points of contact. Just like that, I was on the rocky trail on the other side.

Thrilled to be over the ice chute, I hollered down into the valley below. Just a few more switchbacks to the highest point on the PCT!

As I attempted to take a drink of my coffee, I discovered that the liquid around the lid had frozen, and there were chunks of ice inside the bottle. Thirsty as I was, I laughed. How often do you get a frozen coffee with a view like this?

As I crested the pinnacle of the PCT, Forrester Pass, I hollered at the top of my lungs in excitement. We stopped to take in the view and take photos on the blisteringly cold pass. Afterward, we decided it was too icy to glissade, so we would hike down instead. There was more snow on the north side because it received less sunlight during the early spring months. I was glad I had my microspikes. Scott didn’t have any.

Unable to see the trail, we followed footprints as we switchbacked down the ridge, eventually ending up farther out than we should have. Unaware that we were that far off trail, I told Scott I would probably be fine from there, and he picked up his pace.

Following the footprints, we ended up at the edge of the ridge. Scott climbed down over the snow and rocks and continued on. Dark, foggy clouds began rolling in. I hesitated, wondering whether I should backtrack alone to look for the trail or attempt to follow him. I was still reluctant to use my ice axe.

Just then, Scott slipped and slid, landing in a pile of rocks. He yelled out that he was hurt.

Thankfully, Yak and Gezelle were already below us and rushed over to help. I tossed them my first-aid kit.

I decided it was unsafe to try to climb down with my heavy, off-balance backpack, so I took it off and lowered it down using my bear line.

By the time I safely reached the bottom, Scott had already continued hiking on his own. The girls were packing up the first-aid kit and shouldering their packs as two young hikers who had just glissaded down the pass hiked toward us.

They had been hiking with the girls and were fast hikers. We were really concerned about Scott, even though he had an SOS button. The guys hiked ahead to try to catch up with him.

The three of us women continued hiking downhill, passing stunning teal-blue alpine lakes and crossing streams alongside the roaring Bubbs Creek. We were surrounded by some of the most epic views of beautiful white granite peaks.

At Mile 784, at an elevation of 10,961 feet, I took off my microspikes. Eventually, most of the snow was gone, replaced by puddles along the rocky trail and the occasional large snowdrift that I had to plunge-step through.

Below 10,000 feet and back in the trees, the girls stopped for a break while I continued hiking. When I reached Bubbs Creek, I noticed a downed tree upstream. I decided to take my shoes off and wade across instead. Waterfalls cascaded everywhere, and the creek was rushing with so much water. Along the trail, I also spotted a young buck.

two guys from earlier, who were taking a break on the side of the trail. They told me Scott was still alive and hadn’t bled out. This was where they had caught up to him, and he was still hiking at a good clip. I was happy to hear the news and relieved that he was doing okay.

With my early start, my quilt had some condensation on it when I packed up. I considered stopping to dry it out, but noticed a cooler breeze and clouds starting to roll in from the west. I wondered if we might get some precipitation.

I continued down the switchbacks toward Vidette Meadow. I was exhausted by the time I reached the trail junction, where there was a bear box. I found a spot a little farther off the trail and set up my tent around 12:30 p.m.

I collected water and filtered 1.75 liters. After making my Chicken and Vegetable Rice Mountain House meal and having cocoa coffee, I settled in for a nap from 1:00–2:30 p.m. While I napped, my body heat helped dry the condensation from my quilt.

After waking, I decided to stay at the lower elevation rather than climb higher because the weather was uncertain. I spent the afternoon blogging and relaxing at camp. I was almost out of food, and after eating a big lunch, I settled for some candy and made tea. Around 7 p.m., I fell asleep again.

At 11:30 p.m., I woke to the sound of precipitation hitting my tent. It was barely sleeting. I checked the tension on my tent, then curled back into my quilt.

In the morning, my tent was covered in a very thin layer of sleet that had slightly melted on contact and stuck to the tent. One guyline had slipped out of place, and a corner of the tent was sagging under the weight. The ground was dusted with tiny sleet pellets and snow.

Before putting on my shoes, I slipped a twist-tie snack bag over each foot. If my feet got wet in these temperatures, I might have trouble keeping them warm. I wanted to do everything I could to keep them dry.

Around 7:00 a.m., I left camp, making fresh tracks on the trail.

Not long after starting, I arrived at a wide stream feeding into the roaring Bubbs Creek. I picked my way across its multiple channels, stepping on fallen logs and large rocks while using my trekking poles for balance.

I arrived at a trail junction, turned right, and began climbing out of the canyon. As I gained elevation, the trees began to thin, and the terrain gave way to solid rock. As I switchbacked across the cliffs, I had a beautiful view of Kings Canyon and could see the valley I had hiked down the day before. Forrester Pass itself was hidden behind a mountain.

The Kearsarge Pinnacles, along with East and West Vidette Mountains, towered over the valley, dusted with snow. The scenery was so beautiful that it hardly looked real, with every detail highlighted by the white snow.

Crossing flowing streams and seasonal snowmelt, I was glad I had put the bags over my feet that morning. My shoes got damp, but my feet stayed dry and warm.

There was a brief relief from the climbing as I passed the first trail junction toward Bullfrog Lake and Kearsarge Pass. The plateau was flat and sandy, with a few scattered pine trees. I could see unfamiliar mountains towering in the distance and hear what sounded like military jets booming overhead, though I couldn’t see a single one.

I took the second trail junction, adding 7.6 bonus miles up and over Kearsarge Pass to the trailhead. As I climbed, the trees became increasingly sparse. Near the cutoff leading back down to Bullfrog Lake, I noticed a ranger’s note posted on a rock. It was titled “Bear Info” and warned hikers that bears in the area had gotten food from people and would continue trying to get more. It reminded hikers never to leave food unattended and to keep all food and scented items secured.

The note also included some rather unusual advice about what to do if you encountered a bear!

At 10:00 a.m., I reached Kearsarge Pass at 11,760 feet after hiking five miles and gaining 2,200 feet in elevation.

Inside the Sierras, the skies were partly cloudy with patches of blue. On the Highway 395 side, the sky was cloudy and gray, and I could see a storm blowing in from the north. I was relieved that I had beaten the storm to the pass.

I met a hiker named Crush who was hiking back over the pass. Just then, it started to lightly snow, and it continued throughout the rest of my hike. I was cold as I began the long switchbacks down toward Onion Valley. Big Pothole Lake was covered in ice, its surface a deep blue. On the other side of the lake, three people were practicing self-arresting with their ice axes on the snow.

Passing Gilbert Lake, I noticed patches of green grass and scattered trees around the partially frozen lake. The three men I’d seen practicing with their ice axes passed me on the trail. I was too shy to ask for a ride, thinking they would be long gone by the time I reached the trailhead.

As the trail continued downhill, I had views of waterfalls cascading down the steep valley walls. In the final mile, the parking lot came into view. I could still see the three men, so I picked up my pace, hoping they were still at the trailhead. If they were, I was going to ask for a ride.

When I reached the parking lot, I hurried over to where the three men were sitting in their vehicles with the windows down. Thankfully, they had been waiting for me!

Frank and his sons had permits for Mount Whitney on Friday, weather permitting, and had been practicing with their ice axes at elevation. They were heading to Lone Pine in their two vehicles and offered me a ride.

I stopped at the post office in Lone Pine to pick up my new base layer. Frank and his sons then dropped me a few blocks away at the gear store, where I repacked my backpack.

I was hoping to get a bunk at The Hostel California in Bishop. The hostel was first come, first served, and with cellphone service issues in the area since the windstorm back at Kennedy Meadows South, I wasn’t able to secure a spot ahead of time.

I hiked to the north edge of town and stuck out my thumb, hoping to catch a ride toward Bishop.

Someone pulled over, but they were only going as far as Independence. I thanked them but decided to hold out for a ride all the way to Bishop.

Mark, from Tehachapi, picked me up. He was heading past Bishop to retrieve his family’s RV at June Lake. His family had been camping there with their trailer when they decided to drive into Yosemite the previous day. It ended up snowing, and Tioga Road closed, leaving them unable to get back to their trailer. They had to drive home to Tehachapi, and Mark was now making the trip back to retrieve the RV.As we approached Bishop, I called The Hostel California to check for a bunk. Unfortunately, they were full. Not sure where to go that wouldn’t cost me over three bills, I asked Mark to drop me off at Smart & Final.

I picked up a bagged salad, a pint of ice cream, a gallon of milk, and a box of cereal for dinner and breakfast. Then I started walking down the snowy streets of Bishop.

Hotel prices were high because the Mule Days festival had just taken place. Heartbroken that the hostel was full and unsure whether I knew any other hikers in town, I started walking down the snowy main street, going from motel to motel asking for a hiker discount.

By my third stop, I was struggling to hold back tears. If only I knew someone in town I could split a room with. The snowy weather had left the streets nearly empty.

After leaving my fourth motel, I was considering stealth camping when an angel named Erine pulled over. She had seen me at the store earlier and noticed me wandering down the street. She was a local teacher.

She asked where I was headed. Not really sure myself, I explained that I was looking for a motel that might offer a discount for hikers.

When we arrived at the motel, Erine, the angel of a woman, paid for my room. She also made sure I got my backpack and groceries inside quickly because of the precipitation.

Before leaving, she gave me her phone number in case I needed anything while I was in Bishop and gave me a hug. Then she was gone.

After taking a shower and doing some laundry, I spiderwebbed my bear line around the room. There weren’t many good places to tie it off, but I managed to create a makeshift clothesline.

I spent some time blogging before eating half the salad for dinner. I also brushed the caps of my two Smartwater bottles, which I had been carrying since Mexico. It’s a chore I try to do in every town.

I fell asleep in a soft, warm bed with the TV on.

The skies had cleared up in town. You could still see fresh snow covering the mountains, with clouds beginning to move back over them. I spent my morning eating cereal, drinking coffee, and blogging. It felt good to have time to catch up without feeling so rushed and to have a little less stress on my shoulders.

I received a group message and found out that Google was in town staying at the Motel 6. He offered to split his bed with me for the night.

When checkout time came, I walked down the road with my backpack to the Motel 6. Google and two other hikers had been there for days, and their gear was scattered around the room. I threw my backpack in a corner away from everyone else’s gear.

There was a group wanting to go to Keough’s Hot Springs outside of town. I was unsure if I wanted to go, still having chores to do before leaving town. With snow still predicted in the mountains that day, I had no excuse not to go.

With no rideshares available, we started walking down the road and eventually found a hitch who even offered to take us the few miles off the main road to the hot springs.

Once there, we entered the bathhouse. There was a large, warm pool for swimming and a smaller, hot, shallow pool for soaking. The warm pool had no roof, offering a glimpse of the surrounding scenery. Dark storm clouds hung over the Sierra peaks, making being in the warm water feel even better.

After spending a few hours at the pool, most of it soaking in the hot water and enjoying a pretzel Google shared with me, the group walked the mile back to Highway 395 to hitch a ride to Bishop.

The storm had given the desert valley a beautiful, moody feel, with clouds draped over the White Mountains in front of us.

After returning to the motel with my resupply box, I set it aside to deal with tomorrow. I still hadn’t decided how many days of food I would need for the next stretch. With several challenging mountain passes ahead, I wanted to take some time to make sure I was prepared for what was coming. I spent the rest of the evening relaxing in the room while the others headed out for pub trivia and bowling. It was nice to have a quiet evening before heading back into the mountains.
