Fellow Physicians, Let AI Fill in the Blanks

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

— This new technology can make it easier to get it all done

A few weeks ago, I saw a patient for a regularly scheduled follow-up for a problem they'd been having, and they told me they were scheduled to see a specialist to address this problem the following week.

When I looked through my schedule at the patient's chart that day, I noticed there was already a note for that date in the future that had been created a few days earlier. Curious, I opened the note up to see what it contained.

Had the patient been mistaken or had I misunderstood? Had they already seen this specialist?

The note was apparently authored by a member of that specialist's office staff, and seemed to be a nearly completely fleshed-out office visit record addressing the problem, except for a few placeholder stars (***) that apparently were what was going to be added on the day of the actual visit.

"This patient with a past medical history of X, Y, and Z presents with a complaint of *** that has been going on for *** days, at its worst *** out of 10 in intensity, radiating to their ***, made worse by ***, alleviated by ***."

Then there was a lot of other templated material pulled from the patient's chart, including their chart-noted past medical history, past surgical history, social history, medications, allergies, and the results of recent labs and several relevant imaging studies.

At the end there was an assessment and plan: "The patient presents with *** and the following options were offered to the patient, ***, ***, ***. The risks, benefits, and alternatives of *** were discussed with the patient, including the following potential outcomes and the patient wishes to proceed."

You get the picture -- fill in the blanks.

With the incredible amount of documentation we're required to do, shortcuts and ways to make things easier are definitely a good thing. In most cases, no one really reads the charts that carefully, but it is useful to have it all there so that critical information can be ascertained and so we all know what is going on.

In our electronic medical record, there are some amazing tools (and seemingly more every week), including ambient listening that crunches our conversations with patients and turns them into medical text, artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted responses to patient portal messages, and form fillers that can churn out a printed copy of standard medical forms.

As we keep building on these systems, and finding more sophisticated ways to make the lives of our patients and our own lives better -- especially freeing us up from administrative tasks that don't need clinical brainpower -- I'm hopeful the rest of this rote stuff will fall away, and that the documentation and everything else that needs to be done can happen smoothly and quickly.

I envision a day when really smart predictive and generative AI systems will be listening along, working by our sides, making sure we don't miss anything, and helping us accomplish all the administrative and bureaucratic tasks heaped upon us. It may even help us with clinical decision-making, ordering tests as necessary, interpreting follow-up results, generating patient instructions, refilling prescriptions.

While I'm sitting there and discussing the patient's medical condition and our plan, it's nice that right now, ambient listening turns that into the patient's History of Present Illness for my office note. But it would also be great if it generated something that could be popped into the After Visit Summary, instructions for the patients to take home.

When I mention the lab testing I want to get, rechecking their cholesterol to see response to a statin, getting electrolytes after newly starting an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, rechecking their hemoglobin A1c for ongoing monitoring of their diabetes, the orders should be entered in the system at the same time as I say these words, associated with the correct medical condition and thus the correct diagnosis code, and queued up for me to review and sign.

I never want any of this to be autonomous, running on its own, practicing medicine. But there are so many opportunities for this to safely take a leap, to truly make our healthcare system more equitable and more efficient and, believe it or not, more caring.

Patients shouldn't have to struggle to get a form completed, to get a refill of their chronic medications, to get a follow-up referral to a specialist they have been seeing for an ongoing medical condition, to order their mammogram or colonoscopy or flu shot or shingles vaccine.

Never take us out of the loop but use this new technology to make it easier for us to take care of them, to get it all done.
