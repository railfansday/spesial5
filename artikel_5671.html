After difficult two years, medical school group’s leadership isn’t letting its guard down post midterms

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

West Coast Biotech & Life Sciences Reporter

ANAHEIM, Calif. — After spending a tumultuous two years entangled in many of the most consequential health and science policy debates of President Trump’s second term, the Association of American Medical Colleges isn’t ready to let its guard down after Democrats won control of the House and Senate.

That was one of the core messages delivered to the 4,100 medical educators, research heads, clinicians, and trainees who convened earlier this month for its annual meeting here in Southern California, just a short walk from Disneyland and California Adventure. For a few surreal days, walking along palm tree-lined streets and beneath a cloudless sky, you were just as likely to pass a medical school dean as a family of four in matching Mickey Mouse ears.

Inside, standing before a packed conference hall, AAMC Chief Public Policy Officer Danielle Turnipseed ticked through the nonprofit’s many policy battles since January 2025, from fighting reductions to National Institutes of Health funding to concerns around Medicaid cuts to proposed visa fines and restrictions that would affect international physicians and researchers. She stressed that a Democrat-led Congress won’t mean that these or other issues will go away.

“The work still remains,” Turnipseed said. “By no means do the issues that we talked about fade away: How physicians are paid, how our hospitals are supported, how student financial aid issues are resolved. And there are so many new people to meet on both sides of the aisle.”

To better understand how AAMC’s leadership views this moment, STAT sat down with CEO David Skorton, a cardiologist and former president of Cornell University and the University of Iowa. Skorton, who is retiring next June, said he was hopeful lawmakers would continue to reject attempts by the Trump administration to place grant decisions under political control, and that they’d seriously consider a proposal to modernize how the federal government pays for research overhead, also called indirect costs. He also expressed optimism that bipartisan support for biomedical research will continue despite Sen. Susan Collins (R-Maine), a prominent Republican advocate, losing her reelection bid.

But he pointed to rising medical mistrust as a lasting underlying issue, citing AAMC’s own surveys on the topic. The findings underscored that, in many cases, mistrust is fueled by memories of times when organizations have not been trustworthy.

“I do not blame the public at all for that,” he said. “I believe that trust needs to be earned.”

The following conversation has been lightly edited for length and clarity.

How has the current political climate shaped the tone and content of this year’s meeting?

There are uncertainties in every aspect of our world, whether it’s research funding, medical education, student loans, immigration issues. A quarter of the practicing physicians in the country are international medical grads. In some disciplines, it’s much higher than that. And certain things that are happening in the administration and in Congress in the last year or so have put financial strain or the threat of financial strain forward. For all those reasons, I think people want to be together, want to discuss things in an open way, and we’ve tried to plan the sessions to cover those issues.

There’s a whole session track on “biomedical research and discovery in changing times.” Speaking of change, how does the Democratic takeover of Congress affect your outlook on policy areas that are important to AAMC?

I’m hopeful that we can get on with doing the research and spend less time arguing about the ground rules. But I do think that some of the stuff falls on us. For example, in the area of facilities and administrative costs, indirect costs, we need to be more transparent and clear about how those are audited. We’re part of this Joint Associations Group that has developed this thing called the FAIR model [which would require institutions to directly account for certain overhead expenses in project budgets]. I think that will be at least potentially more transparent, not only to the general public, to voters, but to the people in Congress and in the administration. Time will tell.

Congress has blocked the White House Office of Management and Budget from putting grant reviews more directly under political control, but that restriction ends Dec. 11. Do the midterm results give you confidence those changes won’t take effect?

Even though the overall Uniform Guidance change is halted by the continuing resolution for another month, I am concerned that there’s sufficient evidence that they are trying to make some of these changes agency by agency, which would be a more indirect way of doing the same thing. I think this is a very big mistake, and that’s why we came out so strongly against the change. We’re a nonpartisan organization, and it’s my job to work with the administration [and] both sides of the aisle. Nothing about this election changes the principles on which we operate, or what we’re aiming for.

Are you concerned that Collins losing her Senate seat will make it harder to find Republican support for research funding?

She was a very strong — is a strong champion for us. But in general, it’s been bipartisan support. I’m not cocky or overconfident or anything like that. But this time I’ll use the word optimistic that this will continue to be bipartisan support because it helps everybody. … I think people don’t disagree about the need for it because there’s too many unsolved problems.

The political winds ebb and flow, and I’ve been around a long time in many different leadership positions. I’m most concerned that the election in ’24 and public polling suggest that we have lost a lot of the trust of the public, both as researchers [and] as clinicians. … We have an organization called the Center for Health Justice. They do national polling in communities. We asked the community members when we first set up the organization six years ago, “What can we do to educate the public so they’ll trust us?” And their [first] answer, which is on our website, is, “We’re already educated. That’s why we don’t trust you.”

I believe that’s what we need to work on, what we can do to put our house in order to earn people’s trust. That will reflect itself in politics, in policies.
