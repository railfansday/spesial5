Lincoln’s Thanksgiving Lesson For Us Now: Don’t Claim Victory Yet

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

One hundred sixty-three years ago, Americans gathered for what began an unbroken annual tradition: Thanksgiving. It came one week after Abraham Lincoln stood in a field at Gettysburg to dedicate a cemetery. In three days of fighting there that July, 50,000 Americans had been killed, wounded, captured, or gone missing. That summer, after Gettysburg and Vicksburg, Lincoln had called for a day of thanksgiving for the Union’s victories. By fall, he asked for something more. He did not ask a grieving country to set its losses aside. He asked it to give thanks despite the losses, to remember the “widows, orphans, mourners, or sufferers,” and to work to “heal the wounds of the nation.”

Some Americans will come to the table this year carrying wounds of their own. And amid a common narrative that Americans are hopelessly divided, many may feel a bit lighter because the nation came together to hold free and fair elections this month. Still, many will sit across the table from people who view the results differently.

Some observers have called this month’s midterms a “Gettysburg moment.” The comparison is apt, but not for the reason most people think. Gettysburg was not a turning point on July 3, 1863, when the guns went quiet. It became one in the hard months that followed, because of what ordinary people did next.

When Lincoln delivered his address at Gettysburg that November, he didn’t take credit. He didn’t use the moment as an opportunity to win a factional political debate. Instead, he reminded the country of its core values and focused on the unfinished work ahead. Lincoln gave us a lesson for our times about what to do with a moment of reckoning. He came to dedicate a cemetery, and instead of taking a victory lap, he used his brief address to look ahead:

“It is for us the living . . . to be dedicated here to the unfinished work which they who fought here have thus far so nobly advanced.”

With those words, Lincoln passed the work to the people.

The hardest fighting was still ahead. It became one because of what those still living did next. In the months after Gettysburg, the South didn’t simply give up just because their defeat seemed inevitable. Instead, it dug in, fighting harder to make victory for the Union as costly as possible. But, Union soldiers kept fighting in a series of battles — some of the bloodiest of the war — slowly but steadfastly liberating parts of the South from enslavement. By 1865, the grim work paid off and the Union had prevailed.

In 2026, we are standing now where the crowd stood when Lincoln came to Gettysburg: after the battle, before anyone knows how the war ends and with grueling work ahead.

In 2021, after January 6, the United States was first added to a global list of “backsliding” democracies. The slide has since sharply accelerated. Trump has pardoned the people who stormed the Capitol, sent troops into American cities, sought to suppress the right to vote, and went to war with Iran without Congress.

But as a Republican-controlled Congress abandoned its constitutional duty to check the president, and powerful law firms, universities, and media companies capitulated, ordinary people stepped forward. Residents filled the streets to protect neighbors from armed federal agents. Millions joined “No Kings” marches in all 50 states. People and state attorneys general filed more than 900 lawsuits and won orders protecting people’s rights.

Earlier this month, despite threats from the White House and its allies, Americans voted. Roughly 49 percent of eligible voters cast ballots, the second-highest midterm turnout since 1914. Power shifted in Texas. Mississippi elected its first Black senator since Reconstruction. Kansas sent a Democrat to the Senate for the first time in 94 years. Since the election, the political conversation has fixated on what new Democratic majorities should do with their power, who might run in 2028, and whether the Republican Party will reform itself. These are important questions, but some of this discourse falls back into a habit the media and political classes must break: It treats Americans as spectators. This election was won by people who would not sit on the sidelines: voters who showed up despite threats, neighbors who organized, Americans who refused to accept that the outcome was already decided. The most important question now is not what political parties will do, it is what we, the people, do next.

Democracy is not decided on Election Day. It is built between elections — by people, not parties — in communities, courtrooms, statehouses, and school boards. And the gains voters made at the polls can be taken apart there, too, quietly, while no one is watching.

We are already seeing this. In Texas, outgoing officeholders have met behind closed doors to discuss stripping power from the lieutenant governor voters just chose. U.S. Senate Republicans are racing to confirm lifetime judges before they lose their majority in January. And the president named Stephen Miller White House chief of staff days after the election, while his administration threatens members of Congress with criminal prosecution.

No ballot will stop any of that. What can is what produced this month’s results: people who refused to look away — the plaintiff who signed a lawsuit, the neighbor on the sidewalk, the friend calling their lawmakers, the volunteer knocking doors.

We must pay attention after the cameras leave. Lame-duck sessions, rule changes, and quiet appointments are where hard-won results get undone. The threats from the executive branch will continue through 2028, and the lawsuits and movements that held the line will be needed more, not less.

We must stay focused. Americans want a government that delivers — costs they can afford, healthcare, safe air travel — and we should insist on it. We should also reject a false choice between accountability and a government that delivers. Values-based accountability is not retribution. It is how a democracy shows that no one is above the law, and it is part of delivering for people. We also should stay focused on longer-term changes that the nation and the people need to avoid a backslide in the future. Even if bolder changes like the U.S Supreme Court or passing comprehensive voter reform won’t get past this president’s veto, the next two years are the time to build the case for them. The people should not let political gridlock curtail their demands for a changed future. We must build community. This fall, voters rejected culture wars and runaway oligarchs. Elon Musk alone spent an estimated $474 million to elect Republicans. In Texas, Republicans poured at least $78 million into anti-trans ads against Senator-elect James Talarico, nearly half of the GOP’s ad spending in the race. Texas flipped anyway. Voters showed they could see past division, and that they weren’t for sale. We should expect attempts to divide us to accelerate. The ties we build across differences are the infrastructure of self-government, and they hold only if we keep using and building them.

Gratitude and grief are two sides of the same coin. Lincoln understood that, and he asked a wounded nation to hold both. This Thanksgiving, they call us to the same thing: to find the grit and grace to keep going, and to make the fighting chance the midterms gave the people our nation’s watershed moment. Some 15,000 were in the crowd at Gettysburg. Lincoln wasn’t speaking only to his generals, his Cabinet or Congress. He was speaking to the people. The work was theirs. Now it is ours.
