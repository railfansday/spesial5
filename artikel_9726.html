Whose Money Is It When Someone Else Holds It?

At the end of October, roughly $6.3 billion left Binance wallets across ten blockchains, most of it within about 20 minutes. It is the largest crypto theft on record, about 4X times the Bybit hack of 2025, and the FBI links it to North Korea. Binance has pledged to cover all user losses.

For most readers that is where the story ends. For anyone whose assets are held by a third party, it is where the real question begins, Binance could absorb a loss of this size, could the institution that holds yours?

In less than 48 hours, about $3 billion of the stolen funds was frozen, roughly 47% of the total. More than $2.3 billion of it was frozen by Tether and Circle, the companies behind USDT and USDC. Unlike Bitcoin, these tokens are controlled by their issuers who can blacklist an address and make every token it holds impossible to spend.

Part of the loot was held in these stablecoins so the issuers were able to stop it. Binance has not said what became of the remaining $3.3 billion, and investigators warn that professional laundering networks convert crypto into cash faster than recovery teams can follow.

Recovery is a race measured in hours, and it is won by whoever reaches the parties able to stop the funds first, issuers, exchanges and courts.

Binance has two layers of protection, The first is SAFU, an emergency fund created in 2018 and held in Bitcoin, which stands at about $1 billion. The second is the company itself, Binance says it has set aside more than $6.3 billion of its own funds to cover this incident, and analysts do not see a meaningful risk to its solvency. Users are protected by the second layer, not the first.

A guarantee is only as strong as the balance sheet behind it. Very few institutions could absorb a loss six times the size of their dedicated reserve and keep operating normally, and the smaller the institution holding your assets, the less its promise is worth.

Binance chose to cover every loss, and it had the balance sheet to do it. Without such a promise you may discover only after the loss that you were never the owner of what you thought you held.

Either your assets are held separately in your name and you can take them back, or you hold a claim, and you join a line of creditors waiting for whatever is left. FTX customers spent years in that line.

On the surface you can’t tell which one you are, until trouble starts. The difference is in the fine print, and almost nobody reads it until it is too late. Capital protection starts here, asmart financial architecture decides which side of that line you stand on, and it can only be built before the loss.

The first hours decide the outcome so the preparation has to happen earlier. Ask yourself these 3 questions while everything is calm:

1. How much of my wealth is allowed to sit with any single institution?

2. Can I prove within an hour what I own and how it is held?

People who recover assets quickly are usually the ones who had already answered these questions.

Crypto vanishes in minutes. Lose a property, a company stake or an account abroad and the fight to get it back can take years.

The deciding factor is identical, what was already in place before the loss, An unbroken chain of title, an ownership structure that holds up in court and a jurisdiction chosen on purpose decide whether you recover the asset or only the right to sue for it.

Which of those 3 questions could you answer with confidence today?
