We're fully, you know, believing the conversations that we're having with AI. They could be coming from another human. So, the tendency for us to anthropomorphize the conversations, the interactions that we're having with AI is growing and growing.

And you see people who are not just having a sort of delusional psychosis sometimes of aggrandizement of their own intellect when talking to AI and convinced because of some of the sick fancy that's built into AI that they're super intelligent or their ideas are incredibly unique and creative, or that they're actually the ones to save humanity. You know, I think that the problems that we see with people who are potentially susceptible to that are just made much worse by the current evolution and this current state of AI that we have today. It's just so real. It's so convincing. And we then tend to make it more human.

Jaya Baloo: Yeah, so really what I'm talking about is that, you know, we already have a whole bunch of threats right now when we're dealing with AI. And that's really about AI's capability to both uncover vulnerabilities as well as having vulnerabilities in and of itself. So, AI as a potential for insider threat. As we already have these problems, both in our legacy tech debt and in the new technology that we're building with AI, we have more problems.

Now, I have no idea who thought this was a great idea to take these things that are entrenched with these problems, and put it into something that can actually cause physical harm and teach a robot, which by the way, I should be clear, this was the Unitree humanoid robot. And the issue with this that I have is that this can cause actual physical harm. What is it actually bringing humans in terms of value? And honestly, I would be much happier with a humanoid robot that can do the wash, fold the laundry, and sort it out in the kid's hamper. That would be helpful. It wouldn't be something where I'd be terrified to have this thing anywhere near us. And I think that this is something that I feel is genuinely problematic. Just because you can doesn't mean you should when it comes to some of these implementations. And if you cannot get the security right, don't exacerbate and increase the potential for doing damage and harm to humans.

I feel like we forgot that as we're building. There are multiple security issues with its humanoid robots. And I think knowing that you can remotely take over these robots, and again, like they're capable of physical harm, I think that we really need to rethink and really considerately position these types of devices in light of things like the Cyber Resilience Act.

You know, should we allow hardware and software manufacturers to bring out this kind of technology with the potential risk that it poses? And not just from a cyber perspective, but from a physical one as well.

DR's Becky Bracken: What were some of your other top examples of what's another one that you'd like to highlight here of people being kind of weird about AI?

Jaya Baloo: Well, so I think a slide that you may have initially seen is, and I kind of didn't want to reference it right away in my first answer, was about the fact that we're getting strained in terms of the religiousness around AI. That we both have this tendency to first say, actually, you know, maybe they're sentient, which Anthropic has been talking about in the Vatican with different religious leaders trying to talk about the potential consciousness of AI.

And although I love science fiction, I love it, I just feel, are we really there yet? Are we there, or is this a marketing ploy? What is this? And I'm genuinely trying to understand because it polemicizes what should be a very rational debate into two camps. Those who believe, like already now, we're at the point of talking about consciousness and sentience of these AI systems.

And then the other camp, which actually rebukes AI in the name of Jesus. And I think that was the slide that people also laughed at the conference about. But this is real. And I find it bizarre that we have these phenomena happening and that we don't fully understand the societal consequences either of introducing these systems, which again, let's talk about what they are. They're a bunch of graphics cards in a data center running software.

I am a full Kool-Aid drinker of the notion of complexity and emergence and understanding that even though with a relative set of simple ingredients you have potentially complex outcomes. Drank that Kool-Aid long ago. I believe in this stuff, but I just don't think we're there yet.

So, I feel this has a sort of reductionism effect for the debate that we should be having over how do we do responsible AI by pulling it into these two polemic camps.

DR's Becky Bracken: You also tied it to a lot of end-of-times cult-like thinking, but it does raise the question, which you sort of alluded to a moment ago, how much of this is being driven by marketing of these frontier models, of the [Sam] Altmans of the world, of the Peter Thiels of the world who are out talking. How much of a receptive audience are they finding in the cybersecurity sector, do you think? I mean, is that the primary dynamic at play?

Jaya Baloo: Well, look, I think that we need to separate what is actually happening, which is we do have a bunch of vulnerability discoveries that are happening because of AI. We do have automation for real. It is genuinely happening. We do have automation of these hacking frameworks from Strix, RTec-Ai, Cairn. We are seeing hacking frameworks that are actually in RTec specifically, that are actually being used in real-world hacks. So I'm not trying to discount the facts.

However, what I'm urging to do is the call that, for example, Ciaran Martin, the former head of GCHQ (Government Communications Headquarters), always makes, which is, "Calm the FUD down."

And the fear, uncertainty, doubt that we're introducing here is not helpful. And the reason that I find it really unhelpful is because we know to a large extent how we should perform some sort of containment in terms of egress filtering and sandboxing of these systems.

They may not be perfect, but we've always worked with this notion of defense in depth: assume that one measure fails and that you have additional countermeasures to prevent an actual collapse of the security of the system. I think that that is something that we are actually forgetting right now. And I think it's really important to say, OK, what are the things we genuinely need that would be considered helpful innovation for this? And I think that what we're missing is a lot of the observability here around these AI systems.

So, for example, if we're trying to understand how these systems have potentially been poisoned or Trojanized inherently, then the ability to actually understand how that training data has been populated, even when you only have open-weight models, that would be helpful.

Another thing is additional understanding around this notion of J-space (Jacobian space). So, agent intent. If you know that you have a human operator that brings their own intent to the mix, you know and understand that an AI system might have capabilities and intent that diverge from that human operator, you need to understand when and where that first point of evidence for collecting that divergence could be. We're not there yet either.

So, I would like us to focus on those things rather than the FUD, like Ciaran Martin and a bunch of other renowned cybersecurity people have been saying for a while, like we really need to focus on the things that are breaking.

And really need to focus on the things that we need to be building rather than everyone up in arms about this "Vulnpocalypse," "Breachpocalypse," etc. Apocalypses that are predicted.

DR's Becky Bracken: What is the best way to push back against this sort of rampant anthropomorphization of these agents? You know, I mean, even when researchers are using words like they will "cheat," which assumes that there is a moral compass there to begin with. But at the same time, the goal is for them to be as human as possible? How do we thread that needle?

Jaya Baloo: "They will cheat" is the reward hacking notion that this is a utility that is trying to find the shortest path to success. That means that things that a human would consider part of that moral compass, not to cheat, not to lie, not to evade, etc., that we are projecting this somehow on these AI systems and assuming that the guardrails that we've put in place will cover it.

But I don't think that you can make that assumption clearly, because I don't think we're always in the position to inspect all of the guardrails and know when they've been appropriately applied. And so, I think that there's also this issue of finiteness. Can you have a finite set of guardrails that are actually encompassing the kinds of things that you want to do? And I think the answer to that is probably not.

You know, you’re probably still going to have a few things that escape your potential for, like having this perfectly constructed set of guardrails. Those are like mathematical limitations.

What I would love to encourage us to do is genuinely try to understand better the types of technology that we put into our networks, give access to our data, give a single API token and say "YOLO" to our enterprises, because I don't think we have this under control. And I think that's the real worry that I have.

If we don't understand fully and we're not aware of all of the potential risks, for example, cheating or lying, misrepresentation, etc., if we don't understand that, then we certainly can't have all of these elevated privileges and permissions that we're doing. I think this was the point I was trying to make about when you have capability, you need to kind of always have this subset of this accountability and authorization that you're giving something. And right now, we forgot that lesson. You know, we said, here's all the stuff that you've got access to. You go. And I really feel like we need to rewind a little bit just this part. And that requires better understanding of what they're capable of in terms of the risks.

DR's Becky Bracken: So, what is the next step forward? If you had your team and you were seeing some of this sort of extreme thinking, you know, seeping into your defense planning, what would you say to them? What is the correct reset function here?

Jaya Baloo: I'm really worried about the fact that we are genuinely at risk of losing some of the critical thinking when we're applying AI systems. And you see that already with our coding practices; we are allowing more and more of that initial thinking. Every time we tell people about "you should do this because it's secure," if it's not an easy or fun thing to do, you know people won't do it, right? The utility surpasses the convenience. All of the brain elements that would constitute your frontal lobe warning you for this risk. Like, we just lose it. We become teenagers again. Our risk level and tolerance are high. We are happy to take these risks if it makes our life a little bit easier. And I think we shouldn't forget that.

So, AI as an assistant is an incredibly powerful, incredibly useful tool. And especially when we're trying to drive efficiencies with all this technology and all this cost.
