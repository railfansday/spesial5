Dear Robot: How Travel Is (and Isn’t) Talking to AI

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Enter your email for a complimentary article + exclusive offers.

Travel brands invest deeply in AI visibility, but according to Skift's audit of 50 major travel websites' crawler policies, few tell bots where to go — and the directives don't always match who actually gets in. That looks like a shaky starting point, as the rules of AI engagement change.

Travel brands say they want AI to find them. Their websites often tell AI something else, or nothing at all.

That’s the finding of Skift’s review of robots.txt files for 50 major travel sites. These files tell automated programs, or crawlers, whether they’re welcome, and only 10 of the 50 explicitly addressed AI crawlers. The rest lumped them in with every other bot or left them to default settings.

The gap matters more as AI becomes a way travelers shop. In July, Adobe reported that AI visits to travel sites surged 119% year over year. But there’s a “major bottleneck,” according to Adobe analyst Vivek Pandya. “Significant portions of U.S. travel and retail sites remain unreadable to machines, restricting their presence across AI search results,” he wrote in a blog post.

“When an LLM cannot easily read brand content (such as amenities, pricing, or availability),” Pandya added, “potential revenue is being left on the table.”

Skift reviewed robots.txt files — the file that tells automated crawlers whether they're welcome — for 50 major travel sites and found that only 10 explicitly addressed AI crawlers. The other 40 left AI access to catch-all rules or default settings written for other purposes. That gap gained weight on Oct. 30, when Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI backed a proposal that would let a site opt out of AI training by all four with a single robots.txt entry — but only if the site adds it explicitly, since catch-all rules won't carry it. Among the sites that did set AI rules, the pattern favored visibility over training: Expedia and Vrbo give AI search crawlers broad access, Tripadvisor blocks training crawlers while allowing some search access, and Best Western and ITC Hotels explicitly welcome OpenAI's training crawler. Airbnb made the only major change in the sample, moving from allowing GPTBot and ClaudeBot in August to disallowing access across the board by October. Posted policies also diverge from enforcement: eight sites, mainly hotels, returned 403 Forbidden errors to Skift's automated requests, and CraftedStays found Marriott, Hilton, IHG, Hyatt, and Four Seasons name no AI crawler on their banned lists while their bot protection refused fetches outright.
