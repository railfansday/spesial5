The Best Places to Go in Asia in 2027

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

It’s that time of year when we drop our expert-curated list of the best places to go in Asia in 2027. But before we do, we have to acknowledge that 2026 was a challenging year across the continent. Amid a shifting geopolitical environment and climate change events, there is now a renewed sense of purpose as communities across Asia look at art, food, and nature for a brighter 2027.

Our list of the best places to go in Asia in 2027 showcases the sheer diversity of experiences on offer for every kind of traveler. For the outdoorsy, new long distance hiking trails in the Caucasus, via ancient wine caves and Silk Road-era caravansaries, take you deep into Armenia’s history. Tourists will always flock to Japan’s glittering megacities, but on their outskirts—like in Niigata Prefecture just two hours from Tokyo—regenerative tourism models like the Echigo-Tsumari Art Triennale draw visitors into the country’s stunning rural landscapes.

Penang is a foodie favorite, and 2027 brings a clutch of revamped stays and inventive new restaurants spotlighting heritage ingredients. Further afield in the Philippines, the emerging slow-food culture in Negros Occidental is worth planning a trip around.

For that vibrant city energy, 2027 is a big year, as Jaipur celebrates its 300th anniversary with a year-round calendar of commemorative events. Bangkok has a whole roster of buzzy new hotel openings lined up, while Shanghai is set to see a culture-filled year with the biennale and new museums along the waterfront. From skyscraper-dotted metropolises to remote landscapes sustained by community initiatives, Asia gives everyone a reason to visit in 2027. —Malavika Bhattacharya

Go for: nature, history, and slow travel on foot

Amernia's Transcaucasian Trail is dotted with monasteries, ancient caves, and roadside inns—and in 2027, it will feel even easier to tackle thanks to new maps, updated trail sections, and trekking services.

Built among the peaks and valleys of the Caucasus, Armenia’s ancient roots and mountainous landscapes reward slow travel. Along the country’s two long-distance trails, the Armenian National Trail (ANT) and the Transcaucasian Trail (TCT), medieval monasteries rise above dramatic gorges, Silk Road-era caravansaries dot historic mountain routes, and ancient caves recall thousands of years of winemaking history. In Yerevan, one of the world's oldest continuously inhabited cities, historic fortresses and pale pink stone buildings sit side by side with a colorful café and restaurant scene.

In 2027, Armenia's growing outdoor scene makes it especially exciting for adventurous travelers. The TCT, which will eventually span nearly 1,900 miles across Armenia, Georgia, and Azerbaijan, is becoming easier to tackle with new downloadable maps, updated trail sections and signage, and trekking services offering itinerary planning and luggage transfers. The Armenian section of the TCT extends from Arevik National Park in the south, past Syunik’s cave dwellings and the Tatev Monastery, rising against deep river gorges; through the volcanic massifs of the Gegham Mountains, the forests of Dilijan National Park, and ending at Lake Arpi National Park near Georgia.

Meanwhile, the ANT, a 620-mile network that will eventually connect more than 100 villages, opened its first sections in June 2026 with more to open next year. Trails currently extend through Tavush and Lori, and soon Vayots Dzor, where visitors can birdwatch in alpine steppes, stay with families, and cook herb-filled meals with local hosts, while sampling lavash (Armenia's UNESCO-listed flatbread) baked in a deep, clay oven, and regional bounties like forest berries, mushrooms, cornelian cherry, and rosehip soup. Collectively, the trails offer a new way to experience Armenia's landscapes and communities on foot. —Alicia Erickson

Go for: a luxury and lifestyle hotel boom reshaping the city

Golden hour in Bangkok Yai's historic canal district, beneath the towering Wat Paknam Big Buddha, showcases the bustling city's eternal allure.

Already one of the world’s most-visited cities, Bangkok is giving travelers new reasons to return in 2027. A remarkable wave of hotel openings is part of a broader luxury boom sweeping the Thai capital, which ranked fourth globally for new luxury store openings in 2025. There’s been a recent influx of designer flagships, including Gucci's renovated and reopened boutique, Ralph Lauren’s concept store at Central Embassy, and Acne Studios at Siam Paragon.

Bangkok is also expanding its world-class dining, contemporary art, and urban green spaces, while preserving the street stalls, markets, and neighborhood restaurants that made it famous. It now has nine restaurants on Asia’s 50 Best Restaurants list, more than any other city in the region, with acclaimed newcomers, such as the reopening of Sra Bua by Num Weerawat at Siam Kempinski Hotel Bangkok, and a growing natural wine scene.

The city’s hotel boom spans luxury, lifestyle, and design. Canopy by Hilton Bangkok Sukhumvit opened in August 2026, while Fairmont Bangkok Sukhumvit; The Langham, Custom House, Bangkok; and YOTEL Bangkok Sukhumvit are accepting reservations for later in the year. Other 2027 openings include Conrad Bangkok Sukhumvit Queen's Park, the renowned Siam Motors Building, which is transforming into the Siam Pan Pacific Bangkok, Pan Pacific Serviced Suites Bangkok, and Hotel Indigo by IHG. There have also been rumblings that Belmond’s luxury Eastern and Oriental Express will return to Bangkok in 2027.

The Green Bangkok 2030 Project—an urban plan to increase Bangkok's green spaces to 110 square feet per person—helped drive the 2026 reopening of the mile-long Green Bridge, a car-free greenway connecting Lumpini Park and Benjakitti Park. Song Wat Road has emerged as a modern cultural hub—with famed street food, an array of art spaces, artisan coffee shops, and vintage clothing—amid expanded plans to transform Chinatown.

The December 2025 opening of Dib Bangkok, Thailand’s first museum devoted exclusively to international contemporary art, was another significant milestone. Australia’s Museum of Old and New Art has also announced plans for a Bangkok outpost, further strengthening the city’s reputation as a serious art destination. —Kaila Yu

Go for: the Pink City’s 300th anniversary

Old becomes new in Jaipur, which celebrates its 300th year as a city in 2027. The opulent Sarvato Jaipur (pictured), for example, was once a terrace pavilion used for an audience with the maharaja.

In 1727, Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II established Jaipur as the capital of his kingdom, envisioning it as a financial and artistic hub. As the city marks its 300th year in 2027, the City Palace—the current residence of the erstwhile royals—readies for the occasion with new exhibits across its galleries, as well as in private spaces not usually accessible to the public. Traces of Us, an exhibit by the Jaipur Centre for Art, marks the anniversary with contemporary works by artists like M.F. Husain and Bharti Kher displayed across the palace. A terrace pavilion once used for an audience with the maharaja is now the stylish, open-air restaurant the Sarvato, where Sawai Padmanabh Singh partners with hotelier Abhishek Honawar to showcase a modern, six-course menu championing both the everyday and the celebratory food of Rajasthan, served with a spectacular view of Jaipur.

Honawar, best known for his boutique Michelin Key property the Johri, a restored 19th-century haveli in the Old City, added three more suites this year to the hotel’s original five keys. Set to open later in 2026 is Hawa Haveli, an eight-key stay with a restaurant and cocktail bar, housed in a 19th-century merchant mansion—another intimate boutique property from the same team.

For three days in December 2026, the Jaigarh Heritage Festival brings folk artists and craftspeople to the 11th-century Jaigarh Fort to celebrate the city’s 300th anniversary, while the Jaipur Literature Festival in January brings the stalwarts of the literary world to the Pink City.

A themed walk, Jaipur 300: A City of Craft and Courtyards, led by Neeraj Chauhan of Jaipurnama, traces three centuries of living heritage. And to carry a piece of Jaipur back with you, the Jaipur Watch Company has released two special timepieces inspired by the City Palace’s beautiful Leheriya Gate and Peacock Gate. —Shalbha Sarda

Go for: slow food culture, heritage ingredients, and eco-friendly experiences

The 18-villa Manami Resort in Sipalay offers a conservation-first stay that connects visitors to the surrounding landscape.

Cooking at fish farm 7 Hectares, which uses hyperlocal ingredients, is a way to tap into Negros Occidental's slow-food movement.

Formerly home to wealthy sugar barons and sprawling plantations, the 19th-century beginnings of industrialized sugar production, today Negros Occidental is at the heart of a thriving slow-food scene. In 2027, this Negros Island province, 330 miles south of the Philippines capital Manila, will play host to the prestigious triennial Organic World Congress, as well as the returning Terra Madre Asia and Pacific slow-food festival.

In the biennale’s first iteration, Michelin-starred chefs appeared alongside local producers like Francisco “Kiko” Torno, owner of 7 Hectares fish farm, whose pop-up kitchen serves slow-food dishes cooked with hyperlocal ingredients. And he’s not the only one: The island’s farmers, fishermen, cooks, and chefs are all determined to champion the homegrown, and many welcome visitors on local food tours.

A short boat ride off the coast takes nature lovers to the award-winning Suyac Island Mangrove Eco-Park, which is expected to be nominated for the prestigious ASEAN Sustainable Community-Based Tourism Award in 2027. Islanders guide visitors through an old mangrove forest with giant fruit bats, navigated by wooden walkways, telling stories about their community. Stay at conservation-first Manami Resort in Sipalay, which joined the Michelin hotels list in May 2026, and has 18 villas, each with its own “nature butler,” and a marine biologist to help guests to tune into their environment. —Claire Turrell

Go for: landscapes reimagined through art

The Hoshitoge terraced rice fields, fed by Niigata’s winter snowmelt, still yield an abundant harvest each autumn. It is here, in these zones between human settlements and mountain landscapes—known as satoyama in Japanese—that Niigata's beauty shines.

Travel in Japan is no longer just about the big cities. For travelers interested in sustainable tourism, the focus is shifting to rural areas, such as Niigata Prefecture on Japan’s west coast, just two hours from Tokyo. In Japan, “satoyama” describes the transition zones between human settlements (sato) and mountain landscapes (yama), where communities have traditionally managed forests and farmland in harmony with nature. As rural populations decline, these cultural landscapes are disappearing, but in Niigata, the Echigo-Tsumari Art Triennale has emerged as a model for regenerative tourism. Returning for its 10th edition in July 2027, the contemporary art festival uses creativity to revive the region.

Spanning Tokamachi and Tsunan in southern Niigata, Echigo-Tsumari is a vast landscape of rice terraces, forests, and villages. The triennale brings large-scale works by Japanese and international artists into abandoned buildings and open spaces—from MAD Architects’ immersive Tunnel of Light in Kiyotsu Gorge to Christian Boltanski’s meditative The Last Class inside a shuttered school—while bringing revenue to depopulated villages. Locals are involved as guides and hosts, ensuring income stays within the community.

At a time when overtourism is challenging Japan’s hotspots, the festival demonstrates how cultural tourism can support rural communities off the beaten track. This ethos extends to Niigata’s hospitality scene. In June 2026, NIPPONIA Niigata Furumachi Hanamachi transformed three historic buildings in Niigata City’s geisha district into a dispersed hotel. Guests are encouraged to treat the entire neighborhood as the hotel and take part in cultural experiences such as geisha performances. Yadoya Dōmichi, a similar dispersed-hotel concept in the mountains outside Itoigawa that accepts just one group per night, opened in January 2026 and offers guests the chance to join in with everyday village activities such as planting rice in spring and building snow huts in winter. —Karen Gardiner

Go for: new ways to explore the island’s cultural and culinary heritage

Root House by Gēn is one of several Penang restaurants embracing the city's culinary roots anew.

Soori Penang, a shophouse-turned-hotel, is a star example of how the city's historic buildings have been given a second life.

Penang’s kopitiams, hawker centers, and beloved nasi kandar joints culminate in a layered culinary landscape which, like its styles of architecture and sites of worship, reflects over two centuries worth of maritime trade, British colonialism, and waves of settlers from China, India, and beyond. Now, a clutch of chefs are spinning modern takes on heritage produce. At newcomer Root House by Gēn, chef-founder Johnson Wong interprets the movement of people from China to Malaysia into a menu that presents traditional ingredients like century egg and fermented bean curd in a fine-dining format.

Within the mural-lined streets of George Town, historic shophouses-turned-hotels offer firsthand immersion into the UNESCO World Heritage Site. Newcomer Soori Penang is noteworthy as it was driven by a deeply personal restoration mission: Architect Soo K. Chan once called the now-converted shophouses home. Part of the Leading Hotels of the World, it resides alongside the 175-year-old Khoo Kongsi clan house and will soon unveil the three-bedroom Clan House Suite as well as a cigar and whiskey lounge. The Qing Suites, meanwhile, is a forgotten 1904 annex within the Cheong Fatt Tze or The Blue Mansion of Crazy Rich Asians fame. Once housing the servants’ quarters, it now stands as a 13-suite boutique property on the historic Lebuh Leith.

A brainchild of Hong Kong actress Amy Yip, Penang’s affinity for durian will translate to the world’s first durian-themed luxury hotel, complete with private tasting lounge, expected to debut in late 2027.

In early 2027, the popular Gurney Drive Hawker Center will relocate to Gurney Bay, an expansive promenade and public park facing the Andaman Sea. Further afield, a new cable car at Penang Hill, a UNESCO-designated biosphere reserve, will both serve as an additional tourist attraction and alleviate the strain on the century-old funicular that ascends to its peak, 2,700 feet above sea level. Operations are set to begin in late 2027. —Samia Qaiyum

Go for: a tropical island transforming ahead of the global spotlight

The wellness-focused Regent Phú Quốc shows how this destination has become one of Southeast Asia's fastest growing resort hubs—and 2027 is the year to reap the benefits for yourself.

Phú Quốc has transformed over the past decade from a sleepy fishing island into one of Southeast Asia’s fastest-growing resort hubs. White-sand beaches and fishing villages still edge an interior blanketed by protected rainforest parks, but increasingly share space with resorts and entertainment complexes. Now, another transformation is underway as the island prepares to host the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in November 2027.

The scale alone is dramatic: SOM is designing a waterfront district with an 800,000-square-foot convention center and performance arena. Phú Quốc International Airport is expanding toward a capacity of 24 million passengers annually, while homegrown Sun PhuQuoc Airways builds connections across Asia. Thousands of hotel rooms are coming, too, with Rixos, the Luxury Collection, JW Marriott Marquis, Moxy, and Conrad among some of the many openings slated over the next year.

For all the development, there are still quieter corners—whether kayaking mangrove-lined waterways in the north or checking into the pleasantly understated, wellness-centric Regent Phu Quoc. But this is also a place to surrender to spectacle: soar over turquoise waters on an 6-mile cable car or catch the nightly extravaganza of lasers, acrobatics, and pyrotechnics at the Kiss of the Sea show. —Dan Q. Dao

Go for: a revitalized waterfront best explored on foot

Snøhetta's Shanghai Grand Opera House venue and landmark, which opened in October 2026, illustrates the cultural revolution stirring within the city.

China’s trading powerhouse, Shanghai, sits on the Huangpu River, a commercial lifeline dividing the city, and home to its most famous waterside promenade, the Bund. South along the river, the 16th Shanghai Biennale will open in November 2027 at the Power Station of Art, a contemporary art museum crowned with a 540-foot chimney.

In the West Bund, a former industrial waterfront turned arts district, where more museums are joining its riverside row, the annual West Bund Art and Design fair will draw galleries from across Asia and beyond in the same weeks.

On the river’s opposite shore, in Houtan, Snøhetta's Shanghai Grand Opera House opened in October 2026. It’s a world-class venue for the performing arts and a civic landmark in its own right: a spiraling ramp climbs the fan-shaped roof to a terrace open to all, with Expo Culture Park spread out below.

Shanghai’s hotel landscape is also shifting in 2027. Fairmont Peace Hotel, Victor Sassoon’s art deco creation, and Bund landmark dating to 1929, which once welcomed film star Charlie Chaplin and playwright Noël Coward, is due to reopen as Raffles Peace Hotel Shanghai, with its “historic treasures” preserved. And on the North Bund, a disused office block facing the Bund across the water becomes Lux's first Shanghai hotel, Lux On the Bund, with 100 river-view rooms. —Yolaine Feng

Go for: a regional food and wine scene, with ancient landscapes and new hotels

To appreciate Yunnan in 2027, pair the buzzy city energy of Kunming, which has turned into a destination in its own right, with serene countrysides accessible via towns like Lijiang, where the Amandayan (pictured) is based.

China, as it appears on our TikTok and Instagram feeds, is all gleaming skyscrapers, driverless cars, and neon-lit megacities. Yunnan, in China’s southwest, offers another view: a part of the country where those same advances exist alongside snowcapped mountains, ancient tea forests, highland lakes, and villages tucked into valleys. Start in Kunming, the provincial capital. Long regarded as a gateway to the rest of Yunnan, the city has become a destination in its own right. Here in Spring City a few experiences are essential, like digging into crossing-the-bridge noodles (guòqiáo mǐxiàn) and wandering the Dounan flower market, one of the largest in Asia. There are also new restaurants and bars giving the city fresh energy—and many of which have Yunnan wine now appearing on their menus (Shangri-La’s high-altitude wines, in particular, are starting to make a name for themselves in China). For a taste of the new, book a table at Yijin Sting or Cui House, both of which pair contemporary Yunnanese cooking with local pours. Between meals, the new Cuihu Urban Greenway, opened in 2025, makes it easier to explore the city center on foot; it connects Cuihu Lake with Kunming Old Street, which boasts museums, cafés, and historic façades. The hotel scene is kicking up too: Shangri-La Kunming opened in 2026 with an immersive local tea experience.

Kunming is just the starting point. From there, head west to the city of Dali, where the Cangshan mountains rise behind the old city and Erhai Lake stretches out beyond it. The Three Pagodas have stood at the foot of the mountains for more than a thousand years. Mostly, though, Dali is a place to slow down—and the Dali Edition hotel, opened in June 2026, caters to travelers who wish to do so.

Further flung are destinations like Lijiang, with a charming Old Town and easy access to the scenic Blue Moon Valley. Between Lijiang and Shangri-La lies Tiger Leaping Gorge, where the Jinsha River squeezes between the over-three-mile-high mountains, where one- and two-day treks are well worth the effort. Shangri-La, at the northern end of the trip, is largely Tibetan in culture: Time your visit to Songzanlin Monastery for the morning chants, then spend a late afternoon in Dukezong Ancient Town before walking up the hill to the giant prayer wheel, where you can join locals in spinning it. Yunnan promises much to explore—and rewards those willing to dig deep into its reaches. —Ali Farooqui
