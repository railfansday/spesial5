What I learned sitting on both sides of the REO assignment

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

A former servicer turned Texas REO broker outlines the habits that improve outcomes, including first 72-hour execution, cash for keys follow-through, defensible BPOs with a plan and proactive communication. The focus is speed, evidence and consistency across long timelines.   AI Summary

I have read an REO assignment from both chairs. For years I sat on the servicer side — short sales at Ocwen, then REO asset management at ShellPoint — deciding which brokers got files, reading their BPOs. Today I run a Texas brokerage that sells bank-owned homes.

I only crossed over in 2021, so the memory is fresh: I run my shop the way I once wished my brokers would run theirs. Good REO outcomes are a team sport; winning teams share a few habits.

The first deliverables come due in 24 to 72 hours, depending on client — no warm-up period. From the asset manager’s chair, the hardest files were rarely the damaged ones. They were the quiet ones: two weeks gone, no occupancy call made — uncertain means occupied, never undecided — the property unsecured, the pre-marketing clock blown: 21 days to listed if vacant, 45 to out if occupied.

The brokers I trusted did the unglamorous work immediately: occupancy confirmed, property secured, condition documented with photos and a first call that said, “Here’s what we’re dealing with.” Ours opens with a pre-marketing review naming every preservation item needed — we don’t wait to be told.

An occupied property is the first fork in the road, and cash for keys is the fastest, cheapest, most humane way through. We average ten to 15 agreements a month, and none of them close themselves. They take follow-up — second and third conversations, not one knock — and thinking outside the box: timing a family’s move, help with logistics, solutions no form anticipates.

Sometimes the right answer sits outside the servicer’s delegation of authority, and it happens when a broker brings a solution and a servicer trusts them enough to flex. That trust, earned file by file, pays for itself in avoided evictions, shorter timelines and properties in better condition.

Every broker has felt the temptation to offer the number that keeps the file; every asset manager felt the hangover when that number meets the market. Price from evidence — photographs, repair scope, listing history — so the asset manager can defend it up the chain, to investors and auditors who never saw the house.

Valuation extends to the details most people walk past: the cleanup, the repairs, the small things that decide the showing — a property put in its best light, with passion for the work.

When an issue surfaces, don’t take it at face value. Investigate it, confirm it’s legitimate, find the solution and solve it fast. And don’t just turn in the BPO: the valuations that stood out came with a marketing plan attached — how the broker would position the property, who the buyer was, what gets cleaned, repaired and photographed before launch.

A number says what a house is worth. A plan says you intend to go get it. That effort goes a long way.

The REO of 20 years ago is not the REO of today. Buyers shop online first, and a bank-owned home competes on the same screen as every listing in its zip code. The standard is the best marketing photos you can produce, virtual staging where warranted and a floorplan. Treating a client’s asset as a second-class listing is a choice — and clients notice who makes it.

In asset management, I didn’t expect perfection; Texas REO will humble anyone. What I couldn’t use was silence. A problem in week one is a task; that problem in week six is a failure.

So we run communication as a system: Once a property is under contract, the asset manager gets a weekly update on it, on schedule, news or not. The brokers I never had to chase were the ones whose updates arrived before I thought to ask.

Two rules cover the rest: If you say you’ll do something, do it — and if you make a mistake, own it plainly and fix it. Never make excuses. Honesty, delivered early and on schedule, is how a client learns they can count on you.

This business is a full-contact sport. Some assignments close in 60 days; some take two years. If you aren’t willing to work a file faithfully for as long as it takes, REO probably isn’t for you. And sometimes helping the client means doing the right thing on a file where there’s nothing in it for you. Do it anyway. That willingness — proven over years, not promised in a pitch — is what turns a vendor into a partner.

Behind every broker an asset manager trusts stands a team: occupancy, preservation, valuations, closings and 100 logistics that never make a report. Managing assets well is a true partnership — broker and team on one side, asset manager and servicer on the other, both pointed at the same goal: the best value for the client.

That means all ten members of my team and our 12 agents, and all the vendors who stand with us in the field — and behind me, my wife and my family. What we’ve achieved in five years rests on their support and on our clients’ trust — the ones who took a chance on me. None of this could be accomplished without everyone believing in me.

I have been the person assigning the file and the person standing in the vacant kitchen at 7 a.m. Both jobs are harder than they look from the other chair — and get easier when the person across the table treats you like a partner. This business doesn’t need disrupting. It needs people who sat in both chairs and remember the view from the other one.
