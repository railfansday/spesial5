Exclusive: The All’s Fair Cast Held Court in Couture at the Season 2 Premiere and After-Party

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

“This season is just more of everything,” Kim Kardashian told Vogue, as Sarah Paulson shrieked “Yes!” in agreement, adding, “More drama, more sex, more fun, more fashion, more shenanigans.” They were talking, of course, about All’s Fair, Ryan Murphy’s panache-packed Hulu series following high-powered divorce attorneys with silver tongues, scores to settle, and exceptionally good wardrobes to do it in. On Monday, the cast gathered on Manhattan’s West Side for the New York premiere of season two, the third installment of their whistle-stop publicity tour.

Much like in the series itself, there was no shortage of drama at the Highline Studios: white velvet ropes, marble, and an infinite amount of gold. Add to that the parade of couture emerging from black cars, and suddenly the first Monday in November seemed as glamorous as a certain Monday in May. “This is All’s Fair! You either go big or go home,” quipped hairstylist Sharif Poston while giving Niecy Nash-Betts’s hair a final once-over with a shiny metallic comb.

The show’s costuming certainly sets the precedent for such excess. Fred Leighton brooches, Balenciaga gowns, and no shortage of Birkins. “The show’s wardrobe has the biggest budget I’ve ever seen,” revealed actress Denise Richards. “I was actually quite impressed by it.” (And that’s coming from a former Bond girl.)

For this red-carpet outing, some went the lawyerly route, while others dialed up the haute factor. Teyana Taylor managed to tick both boxes in an architectural Nicholas Oakwell Couture pantsuit, festooned with a giant bow below the waist. Her summation of the evening was the same as hers of the second season: “It’s drama and suits!” Similarly, actor Nyle DiMarco nodded to the legal theme in a Paul Smith suit, “straight off the runway.” And Nash-Betts’s Louis Vuitton look had multiple acts. “This dress turns into three or four different things—so keep your eyes on me.” Sure enough, the next time I saw her, the gilded halo that initially framed her was nowhere to be seen.

“She looks so good,” director and executive producer Anthony Hemingway said as Kardashian arrived in a lace-up Ashi Studio gown with a sweetheart neckline and a snatched bodice, accentuated with lace, croc embossing, and ribbons. Everyone seemed to stand a little straighter as Kardashian walked in—but sure enough, as soon as she started chatting, the room relaxed.

“I’m excited for people to see how funny we are,” Kardashian said. “There’s a lot more comedy this season,” Paulson added, hooking a thumb toward Kardashian, who leaned right into it. “I’m the funniest,” she teased. Paulson had her own theory: “Kim did a movie with all these comedy geniuses in between seasons one and two,” she said, explaining that her co-star came back to set brimming with notes. When Paulson said she’s still waiting for Saturday Night Live to call, Kardashian chimed in with an idea of her own: “Should we do it together? Please!”

Though there were photos to take and interviews to give, the cast still stopped mid-carpet for a catch-up. Glenn Close, in Matières Fécales, had missed the first two stops on the press tour. “I’m excited to see my friends,” she said, holding hands with Kardashian through an entire interview. Taylor, who’d been busy wrapping up another project as director, felt much the same. “You have to understand the FOMO I’ve been having.”

“We had long TV days that lasted up to sixteen hours sometimes,” Naomi Watts added. “It’s a grind, but we somehow find a way to laugh in between takes.” She credited Murphy with putting together a group that clicked. “There’s not an outsized ego amongst us.” For Taylor, that sisterhood mattered especially on the days she turned up feeling less than her best. “Everybody’s got their silent battles,” she said. “Come to work with them, and with this cast, you just know that you’re going to be surrounded by love.”

For reality TV regular Dorinda Medley, who plays Loni and worked mostly with Glenn Close, taking on a scripted role was a chance to try something new. “All these little steps in your life move you a little closer to who you are,” she said. “For so many years I was Dorinda someone.” She ticked off the surnames she’d gone by and the roles she’d played offscreen. “Mother, wife, widow. Now, for the first time, I’m just Dorinda. There’s incredible freedom in that.” Medley, in an embellished Gucci dress that she jokingly called “very casual,” already had a pitch for her next collaboration with Murphy. How about setting American Horror Story at Blue Stone Manor, her home in the Berkshires?

“We rarely get a chance to rock together. We’re always working,” Hemingway, the director, said. He was looking forward to a chance to “hang out, have a cocktail, even do a little two-step, and just laugh.” And at Crane Club nearby, there was ample opportunity for all four. Around the red velvet-draped dining room, servers made the rounds with truffle arancini and caesar salads, a giant ice sculpture of the show’s initials presided over the raw bar, and the DJ was good enough to make you forget it was a school night.

If the carving station said wedding reception, the cocktail menu said divorce. Bartenders whipped up drinks named Contested Assets, with habanero-spiked mezcal number, and Alimony, which came with gin, vodka, vermouth, sake, and a little cucumber. Taylor had a special order in mind. “I need a real big margarita,” she told Vogue. Her favorite? “Passionfruit. Sugar rim. There—you’ve got sugar, spice, and everything nice,” she said, dancing as she delivered the recipe.

For a cast that had reserved tables ringing the room, they sure spent little time in their seats. And who could blame them? Ash Hodgkinson was doing magic tricks for a knot of guests, while Murphy, in ochre-tinted aviators, made duck faces for the camera with Paulson. When the DJ put on “Plain Jane,” there was Nash-Betts, dancing with her girls. “I love those moments,” Hemingway had said. “That’s living.”

As for any flak the show had received, Anna Delvey, who knows a thing or two about mixed press and courthouse fashion, had a thought. “It says a lot about people, how they take it,” she explained—stating that some people “are too serious about it.” In her view, anyone busy cataloging the show’s excesses was liable to miss the joke. “They obviously know what they’re doing,” she said of the silliness.
