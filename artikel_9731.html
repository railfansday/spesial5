From Hookups to CRMs: How the Web Summit Generation Grew Up

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

In the Old West a rumor would run about a spot with gold, somewhere in the wilderness.

Hundreds, sometimes thousands of people would hit the road and join the place.

The prospectors would go and dig the dirt, and buy their dinner with the gold they found during the day. Or have no dinner if they didn't find any.

Most of these cities disappeared once the gold ran out. Some of them became permanent settlements and entered the legend.

My first Web Summit was in 2024. It was wild. My founder booked us tickets, took an Airbnb, put a bundle of banknotes in an empty candy box for the local expenses, and we found ourselves with tens of thousands of similar men and women in a big conference room with badges, all chasing hype, money and adventure.

Web Summit looks on the surface like a normal tech conference. Yet it's the first one that institutionalized the “Night Summit”, an informal workation play zone with musicians and foosball tables rocking the scene from 6 to 10pm. After the “Night Summit” you had the parties that would go until the morning.

It's also a place that helped propel startups like Stripe, Uber, Revolut, Datadog, Twilio and ElevenLabs, helping them expand their market share, raise capital or recruit new talent. The 2026 Lisbon edition welcomed over 70 000 attendees from over 160 countries, over 3000 exhibiting companies and nearly 2000 investors. Web Summit single-handedly placed Lisbon on the global map of tech, making it a sought-after hub for expats, entrepreneurs and investors overnight.

As editions passed, a lot of companies went bankrupt or were integrated into bigger groups. My startup survived and multiplied its income and headcount year after year.

I've had all kinds of crazy stories. Once in Qatar, my CEO called me and told me to not reach the hotel room where we were bunking together at the West Bay Pullman because he was playing with someone inside. In another edition, our host forgot to send us the room code, so we spent the first night partying across Lisbon. I would have drinks directly on the booths with clients during negotiations, go to parties, eat on company money in luxury restaurants and come back from the club at 3 a.m. raking in thousands in commissions from my sales a few weeks later, when the contracts got signed.

I find myself in a bittersweet mix of nostalgia and psychological (if not physical) hangover. After a few trips, my CEO imposed some quotas, and late-night prosecco cups were traded for Excel Sheets and map planning for checking on a predefined list of prospects on the venue.

I turned from a charismatic young hustler to a boring, lanyard-wearing corporate executive chasing leads and closing deals on a thin margin.

Suddenly our industry had a lot more competitors and we weren't the only smart guys on the block making a quick buck on outstaffing programming and IT stacks to developing countries. Our accounts grew bigger too, and the tricks I used to bill higher rates after the fifth beer looked far less effective to pitch a multi-million euro deal to a board of a GCC state-owned conglomerate.

My CEO started taking less cash with him and we had to keep the receipts to get reimbursed for our groceries of the week. Participants and booths became more sober too, and the freebies we had a few years ago went thinner as the interest rates went up.

The “Night Summit” started feeling like late night labor with new “meetups” looking much less romantic, now that jumping on a trampoline was replaced by “speed dating” 20 strangers in 30 minutes to pitch one's product in one breath.

After 7 years of business, my “startup” became a legitimate firm, and since the novelty wore off, what used to feel like an adventure now feels like a crowded boomtown where the “fun” and the spontaneity turned into a working Monday with some perks, like free coffee and a Turkish carpet on the wall.

Web Summit's transformation illustrates the mutation every new industry eventually goes through as it matures. It's painful, sometimes a little annoying, but it's a necessary step to transition from a bunch of broke dreamers into what we are and have always been: accountable operators building real, durable businesses.
