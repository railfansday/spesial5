Free as in freedom or free as in beer: which free does open source promise?

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Posted on Nov 13 • Originally published at courtneyr.dev

Originally published at courtneyr.dev as part of the Merge Conflicts series.

Free as in freedom means you hold permissions over a program, whatever you paid for it. Free as in beer means it cost nothing. English uses one word for both, so software licenses had to pick one. They picked freedom. Here's the difference, where WordPress lands, and why open source got a second name.

Back to the hypothetical food pantry from the four freedoms post. At a volunteer fair, two booths offer it software, and both signs say FREE:

Which booth gave away free software? The Free Software Foundation says booth two, and here's why.

"Thus, 'free software' is a matter of liberty, not price. To understand the concept, you should think of 'free' as in 'free speech,' not as in 'free beer.'"

Free Software Definition, Free Software Foundation, page dated 2026-06-09. The same page holds claim C-XF-029.1

In other words, free beer describes the price tag. Free speech describes what you're allowed to do, and nobody hands out speech by the pint. Booth one is free as in beer. Booth two is free as in freedom, even with a setup fee.

Both signs say FREE. Only one booth hands over the freedoms.

The mix up didn't start with app stores. It sits in the founding text itself. The GNU Manifesto went out under Richard Stallman's name. It says GNU is a system "which I am writing so that I can give it away free to everyone who can use it."2 Then a footnote on that sentence corrects it.

"The wording here was careless. The intention was that nobody would have to pay for permission to use the GNU system."

The GNU Manifesto, footnote 1, GNU Project, page updated 2021-11-02.2

So the best known document in free software needed a footnote to say free as in freedom. If its own author needed that note, you can forgive yourself for needing a whole post.

Yes. The license WordPress ships under says so in its preamble: "When we speak of free software, we are referring to freedom, not price."3 The same paragraph protects your freedom to distribute copies "and charge for this service if you wish."3

A price, though, can't buy or sell the freedom itself. The FSF puts it this way: "a low price doesn't make the software free, or even closer to free."4 Its essay adds that "users don't have to pay the distribution fee in order to use the software."4 That's because anyone with a copy may share it, which is freedom 2.

That leaves booth one. The FSF says "freeware" "has no clear accepted definition."6 It adds that the term "is commonly used for packages which permit redistribution but not modification (and their source code is not available)."6 In short, that's free beer with no freedom in the glass. A gratis app can be the right tool for a pantry, as long as nobody mistakes it for free as in freedom.

What counts as a derivative is less settled, and WordPress.org says so. "There is some legal grey area regarding what is considered a derivative work," its license page reads.7 It continues: "but we feel strongly that plugins and themes are derivative work and thus inherit the GPL license."7

A 2009 WordPress News post quoted a legal opinion from the Software Freedom Law Center. By that opinion, "the PHP files are subject to the requirements of the GPL while the images and CSS are not."11 The post then said WordPress.org would "only promote and host things on WordPress.org that are 100% GPL or compatible."11 This post reads the opinion as quoted there, not the opinion itself.

So a paid theme can be free as in freedom in its PHP code, while its images and stylesheets carry other terms. Therefore, read the license file, not the price tag.

Both camps agree the list of programs barely differs. The OSI says both terms mean licenses that "guarantee a certain specific set of freedoms."13 So both describe software that's free as in freedom. Instead, they disagree about why that matters, and each says so in its own words.

Free software and open source, each described by the organization that keeps its definition

The "open source" label dates to a strategy session on February 3, 1998, in Palo Alto.14 Before that, "free software" carried both meanings and the confusion that came with them.
