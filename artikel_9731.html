Custom payloads for the DJI Matrice 400: what can be integrated and how

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Posted on Nov 10 • Originally published at yuanzhirobot.com • AI-assisted

Short answer: The DJI Matrice 400 is listed at about 9.74 kg with roughly 6 kg of payload and up to 59 minutes of flight time, which is enough to carry a Zenmuse gimbal and a second, custom payload at the same time: a 3D LiDAR, a gas-sensing pod, a loudspeaker, an onboard AI computer or a long-range mesh radio. What decides success is not the headline payload but four things: a balanced and vibration-isolated mount, a power and data path the drone accepts, time-synchronised data, and software that turns raw sensor output into a result your team can use.

Before adding anything, list what is built in. The Matrice 400 has its own RTK positioning and obstacle-sensing radar, and it carries DJI Zenmuse gimbals natively. If a Zenmuse H30T, L2 or P1 already answers your question, buy that and stop: DJI payloads are calibrated, supported in DJI Pilot and FlightHub, and need no custom work. Custom integration is worth it when you need a sensor DJI does not make, an onboard computer, your own data path, or several sensors flying together.

These are the payload classes we are asked about most often, with real weights and power from our component catalog. Weights are for the sensor itself and exclude bracket, cables and enclosure.

As a worked example, a Zenmuse H30T (920 g) plus a Livox Mid-360 (265 g) is about 1.19 kg of sensors, roughly 20% of the rated payload. That leaves real room for a compute box, bracket and cabling, which on a drone often weigh as much as the sensors themselves.

A drone payload is a mechanical design problem first. The centre of gravity of the whole payload should sit close to the aircraft's own, or the flight controller spends thrust correcting a constant tilt and flight time drops. LiDARs and IMU-dependent sensors need vibration isolation tuned to the propeller frequencies, but not so soft that the sensor wobbles relative to the RTK antenna, because every millimetre of unmodelled motion becomes error in the point cloud. We design form-fitting brackets around the aircraft's mounting interfaces and keep the sensor's field of view clear of the landing gear and propellers.

There are two routes. The first is DJI's Payload SDK (PSDK) path: the payload talks to the drone through DJI's payload interface, shows up in DJI Pilot, can receive aircraft position and time, and can send small amounts of data back to the pilot. This is the cleanest option for anything the pilot needs to see or control. The second route is an independent power supply and radio for high-bandwidth or power-hungry parts, for example a Jetson AGX Orin or a full-rate LiDAR stream, which would exceed what a payload port is meant to provide. Many real payloads combine both: PSDK for control and position, an isolated rail and a mesh or 5G link for the heavy data.

A custom sensor is only as useful as its timestamps. LiDAR scans, thermal frames and gas readings must share a clock with the aircraft's RTK position and attitude, through PPS and time messages or a hardware trigger. Without that, the map smears and a gas peak cannot be tied to a location. If you need heading from a separate GNSS receiver, a dual-antenna module such as the Unicore UM982 weighs under 2 g on its board; the antennas and their separation are the real design constraint.

Most of the engineering hours go here. Typical work includes a ROS 2 driver set for the added sensors, onboard detection on the gimbal video, LiDAR-inertial mapping, and a ground-station view that overlays results on a map and exports reports.
