“Sexy, Weirdo Bond”: Why Women Love Robert Pattinson’s Casting as 007

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

It feels like we might finally get a more vulnerable, less sexist James Bond.

Whether you were waiting with bated breath or not, the years-long wait to find out who the new James Bond is over, with the announcement that everyone’s fave Gen Z Robert Pattinson is set to take the reins.

In many ways, his casting is totally unsurprising—he is, seemingly, Hollywood’s only leading man right now, appearing in The Drama, The Odyssey, and Primetime this year alone (with Dune: Part Three still to come), on top of being literal Batman. And yet, at the same time, Rob as Bond feels like a break with tradition. I mean, he’s still a handsome straight white guy — but he’s not your typical alpha male.

There’s a silliness, darkness, and freakiness (complimentary) to Pattinson and the characters he’s played. With projects famously traversing both indie flicks and major blockbusters, he tends to appear as more fuckboy, weirdo, or Bond villain than the emotionally distant, hunky womanizer typically synonymous with 007. And women in particular seem to be into it.

I’m not just talking about women who grew up crushing on Cedric Diggory and Edward Cullen, either. Not to assign too much weight to the James Bond franchise, but Pattinson’s casting comes at a time when we’re grappling with what masculinity means today. In an era when misogynistic manosphere figureheads are as prevalent on our screens as Hollywood stars, there’s less appetite for clichéd portrayals of the archetypal alpha Bond guy, who’s super jacked, can’t express his emotions, and treats women like disposable objects.

For heterosexual women, this extends to our romantic lives, too. A new survey by female-led dating app Chyrpe found that over half of women wouldn’t actively look for an “alpha male” in a partner, with 70 percent saying they’d rather date a supportive, emotionally available man who’s happy to let them take the lead (Bond as a sub?!).

“The alpha male is traditionally understood as a man who is dominant, assertive, and accustomed to being in control,” says Gigi Engle, relationship expert for Chyrpe. “There’s an assumption that he’s emotionally self-sufficient, sexually dominant, and rarely, if ever, vulnerable. Psychologically, this represents a pretty narrow and restrictive understanding of masculinity.”

Despite what the manosphere would have you believe, this performance of masculinity doesn’t work for anyone involved. “When men feel they have to constantly be this ‘dominant’ character, there’s often very little room for emotional openness or genuine partnership,” continues Engle. “Women may simply be getting tired of having to accommodate someone else’s need to feel powerful. It’s boring, frankly.” In fact, the same Chyrpe survey found that 33 percent of men feel pressured by social media to embody the alpha male stereotype, while 30 percent say male friends ridicule them if they don’t take on an alpha role in their relationship.

There’s an opportunity, then, for macho franchises like James Bond to change things. “There’s a growing thirst for a masculinity that feels more human and less traditional,” says Engle. “For decades, popular culture has sold us the idea that an attractive man is someone who is powerful, emotionally restrained, physically strong, and always in control. But those expectations are starting to feel dated and lame. We’re becoming more interested in men who can hold complexity. We want guys who are comfortable with women who don’t need them to take charge.”

It feels like in Pattinson’s hands, we might see 007’s softer, nerdier, more emotional side—which could be a good thing for both men and women.

At least, that’s what a bunch of women I spoke to are hoping for. “He’s a James Bond you can see shooting the bad guys and also snuggling up in front of the telly watching Bridget Jones—and he’s kinda awkward,” says Clare.

Another Claire tells me: “I just cannot imagine him enthusiastically doing some of the cringier, misogynistic lines or scenes with female costars. I think he’ll be able to bring charisma, equally he doesn’t try to overpower his female costars. He holds his own in a scene without it ever needing to take anything away from the people he’s sharing that scene with.”

Nicole says a “different portrayal of masculinity would be nice,” but if we don’t get that, “he should be bisexual at least.”

Others, including Engle, are excited by the darkness Pattinson might bring to Bond. “He has an unconventional screen presence, to say the least,” she says. “There’s an introspective, slightly unpredictable quality to him that feels different from the traditionally hyper-masculine Bond we’ve become accustomed to. I’d love to see a Bond who retains the confidence, danger, and sexual charisma of the character but is allowed to have real emotions.”

Another woman I spoke to, Kitty, feels similarly: “First of all, [Pattinson] is my all-time crush, but I’m excited because I wonder if they’ll take the opportunity to make him darker and explore the theme of him being an alcoholic and reclusive. Similar to how he made Batman emo, will they make Bond more moody? Because so many people have explored how fucked up [Bond] actually is as a person, it would be cool to bring it to the front.”

Or, as Elise sums it up: “Let’s fucking go for sexy, weirdo Bond.”

Until the first film drops in 2028, we’ll have no idea how Pattinson will play Bond—but there’s no harm in projecting our emotionally intelligent fantasies onto him in the meantime.
