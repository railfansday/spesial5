An ancient camp in Alaska holds amazing clues about how the first people traveled all the way to North America

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Ice Age people who crossed the Bering Land Bridge built a lean-to, an earth oven, and a sewing kit at an Alaska camp 13,800 years ago.

The Bering Land Bridge is usually described as a temporary highway that Ice Age hunters walked across to get to the Americas.

On a bluff above the Tanana River in interior Alaska, though, some of those people stopped and stayed a while. They put up a shelter, dug a cooking oven, and sewed with bone needles. Furthermore, they came back to the same spot for up to two centuries.

The camp is 13,800 years old, and its shelter is the earliest potential case of a built dwelling anywhere in North America.

The people who used it weren’t a small hunting party passing through. They were a group of related families, and they knew the land well enough to fetch stone from 230 miles away.

Kelly Graf, a professor in the Department of Anthropology at the University of Kansas, leads the dig. The site is called Shég’ Xdaltth’í’, which means “they were there” in Alaska Native Athabascan.

In an interview with Earth.com, Graf said the finds suggest the site was “a place where the folks here lived and considered it their home.”

The camp is about 34 miles (55 kilometers) south-southeast of Fairbanks, on a bluff roughly 115 feet (35 meters) above the Tanana River. A bedrock knob behind it blocks the northwest wind.

Since 2016, Graf’s team has worked a single excavation block of 269 square feet (25 square meters). This is the same area as the floor of a one-car garage.

Radiocarbon dates put its finds in three Ice Age layers. The oldest, from about 13,800 years ago, is the one this study is about. That layer alone has produced more than 80,000 pieces of stone, bone, and charcoal.

In that oldest layer, the team found a large oval of charcoal-stained ground at least 10 feet (3 meters) across, packed with thousands of small artifacts and bones.

The team read it as most likely a lean-to that opened to the south. It had a hearth near its center and a shallow pit full of refuse beside it.

A stone slab about 8 inches (20 centimeters) on a side lay inside the shelter, and Graf counts it among her favorite finds.

“It was sitting within a dwelling feature, and all around it were really bashed-up bison bones. And there were a lot of quartz and quartzite-rock hammerstones – which were the hammers they used to break the bones,” Graf said.

“So we think they were using these stones to fully process the bones to extract marrow and likely to render grease. This was all happening near a large earth oven,” she added.

The oven was a bed of charcoal more than 4 feet (1.2 meters) across, holding 230 fire-cracked rocks. It had a cap of earth covering it, possibly making it suitable for slow cooking.

The oldest layer produced nearly 7,000 bones, most broken into fragments. Researchers at the Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology, in Germany, used protein signatures to identify the bone fragments.

They found 373 pieces that came mostly from steppe bison, with mammoth, horse, caribou, hares, foxes, a lynx also present. Some were bones from different types of birds, including grouse and swans.

Graf told Earth.com the camp would have held a group of related, extended family members making up a few households. They refurbished stone tools and broke down carcasses around open-air fires.

The bone tools say the same thing. The team found eight fragments of sewing needles, four still carrying part of the eye, along with an awl tip and an antler pendant with a drilled hole.

Waterfowl bones hint at summer visits, and fox and lynx bones at winter trapping. The camp may have been used in more than one season.

The camp has no storage pits and none of the dug-in houses seen at permanent Ice Age sites in Siberia, though. So Graf’s team calls the people here semi-settled rather than settled near the Bering Land Bridge.

The stone came from a wide area. All 25 pieces of obsidian trace to one source, Batza Téna, about 230 miles (370 kilometers) to the northwest.

One piece of rhyolite, tested at Texas A&M University, came from Triple Lakes in what is now Denali National Park.

Six finished tools stand out. They’re flaked on both faces into a diamond shape, and most have a stem with edges that were ground smooth. These could have been bound to form a handle or shaft.

The team hasn’t yet examined them for wear, so it won’t yet call them spear points or knives. For now they are hafted bifaces.

“It’s the only site in Alaska that features this clear diamond shape,” Graf said.

“And the only other places at this time that have such points in a northern context are on the Russian side of the land bridge. It suggests people traveled across the Bering Land Bridge to and possibly from this location.”

The Russian sites she means, Nikita Lake and Berelekh, also lack the tiny stone blades called microblades that mark Swan Point, the oldest site in the same river basin.

Graf’s team proposes that the two toolkits belonged to two separate migrations out of northeast Asia across the Bering Land Bridge.

At least 15 of the bones belonged to wolves. One lower jaw was measured against dire wolves from the La Brea tar pits, Ice Age wolves from Alaska, and modern gray wolves, and its shape fit none of them.

The team thinks it belonged to a population unlike any wolf known from Alaska, ancient or modern.

Asked by Earth.com what surprised her most, Graf named the sheer amount and variety of tools, food, and features. A habitation site like this one is rare in the late Ice Age, she said.

She also named “the wolf remains that suggest familiarity with humans and resources they can gain from a relationship with humans.”

Genetic studies suggest Siberian wolves gave rise to the first dogs in North America, so the people of Shég’ Xdaltth’í’ may have kept dogs.

Wolves and the earliest dogs leave bones that are hard to tell apart, though, so that stays a suggestion for now.

Several of the features extend into the walls of the excavation block, so the lean-to and the oven have each been only partly uncovered.

The bigger question is who these people were. Geneticists expect that several distinct populations lived in Beringia, the region around the land bridge, at the end of the Ice Age.

Settling which of them lived here will take ancient genomes, Graf’s team said. Until those are recovered, the link between the toolkit and a particular people stays a hypothesis.

“The next step is completely excavating the features we have only partially uncovered. We need to test our hypotheses about site use,” Graf said. “It would be excellent to find another dwelling feature!”

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
