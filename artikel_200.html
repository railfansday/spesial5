Emails reveal how Trump allies pushed pardons for election deniers

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Former Trump lawyer urged justice department officials to seek pardons for those falsely claiming he won the 2020 presidential election in their states

In the fall of 2025, Cleta Mitchell, a key lawyer in Donald Trump’s efforts to overturn the 2020 presidential election, contacted a justice department official with an urgent request.

Criminal cases against people who signed certificates falsely claiming that Trump won the presidential election in their state – known widely as fake electors – were pending in Georgia, Michigan, Arizona, Nevada and Wisconsin. She knew the official, pardon attorney Ed Martin, was working on a memo outlining why those involved in the fake elector scheme should receive a presidential pardon.

Mitchell, a key figure in the movement to sow doubt about US elections, wanted the justice department to move forward with the pardons immediately before any of the cases went to trial. “Time is of the essence,” she wrote.

“If trials (and in the Michigan case, an appeal of the dismissal) are allowed to occur, the additional financial and emotional costs to these loyal Trump supporters will be enormous,” she added in the message, which was obtained by the watchdog group American Oversight via a Freedom of Information Act request, and provided to the Guardian. “It is essential that the pardons move forward as quickly as possible before trials are scheduled and before these defendants are subjected to further expense and mental anguish. Please let me know what else I can do to help get this done.”

Martin’s response to Mitchell’s email was brief. “Thank you for this,” he wrote. “Very helpful.”

While the president eventually did issue a blanket pardon to the fake electors weeks later, the pardon was largely symbolic and did not affect the state-level charges they faced.

The email exchange offers insight into the machinations that took place behind the scenes as key players in the election denial movement shaped the administration’s efforts to relitigate the 2020 election won by Joe Biden and to absolve loyalists of wrongdoing.

“While the pardons were federal, not for the ongoing state cases, the documents shed light on the extent to which Mitchell and other Trump allies were using their access to get President Trump to act. Protecting our democratic system requires ensuring that no one, regardless of political influence or proximity to power, is placed above the law,” said Chioma Chukwu, executive director of American Oversight.

A person close to the pardon effort said Martin had been pressing and working on the issue for months. Mitchell did not respond to a request for comment.

In Georgia, at the time, the criminal case against Trump and fake electors were pending before the prosecuting attorneys council after Fulton county district attorney Fani Willis was disqualified. Mitchell suggested that a federal pardon could get the case dismissed.

Other states, including Georgia, a slate of Republican electors had met privately and signed a certificate claiming Trump was the winner of the presidential election. Republicans sought to have those certificates recognized during the counting of the vote on 6 January 2021 – an effort that was unsuccessful.

“If the pardons are issued promptly, before a new prosecutor is appointed, the Council can move for dismissal of the only remaining charges in the case, being the charges against President Trump,” she wrote to Martin. The prosecutor who eventually took over the case dismissed the charges, though it was for reasons unrelated to the pardons Trump issued.

Mitchell’s interest in the Georgia case was notable. She was on the telephone call when Trump put pressure on the Georgia secretary of state, Brad Raffensperger, to find enough votes to overturn the election. A special purpose grand jury in Georgia recommended criminally charging Mitchell for her involvement in the effort to overturn the election in the state, though she ultimately was never charged.

Mitchell is a key figure in the election denial movement that has spread since the 2020 election. She has helped organize a network of local activists who have spread false and misleading information about elections. She is also linked to organizations that have provided funding for groups that have spread misleading information about elections.

In Martin, Mitchell had a powerful ally. Before joining the justice department, he participated in the effort to overturn the 2020 election, speaking at a rally in Washington on 5 January 2021. “What they’re stealing is not just an election. It’s our future, and it’s our republic,” he said during the speech. On 6 January, he posted on X: “I’m at the Capitol right now. Abd [sic] I was at the POTUS speech earlier. Rowdy crowd but nothing out of hand. Ignore the #FakeNews.”

Martin represented some of those charged for January 6, and served on the board of an organization that provided financial assistance to their families. Before being appointed pardon attorney, he served as the interim US attorney for the District of Columbia, a position from which he fired and demoted prosecutors who worked on January 6 cases.

Writing about the Arizona case, Mitchell urged a pardon before a grand jury could file charges, saying it would “be optimum to stop the persecution of these defendants, which include two of Charlie Kirk’s top lieutenants at TPUSA [Turning Point USA]”. Kirk had been killed in Utah only weeks earlier.

Summarizing the Michigan and Wisconsin cases, Mitchell wrote that a presidential pardon could save defendants further financial burden. “Some of them have already spent their retirement funds, mortgaged their homes to pay legal fees, and suffered significant emotional hardship over the past four years. Allowing a trial to proceed is simply unacceptable.”

The US constitution gives the president broad power to pardon people convicted of federal crimes. But not state ones. Nonetheless, Mitchell has publicly advanced a novel legal argument that a federal pardon for the fake elector scheme could wipe out the state charges.

“The U.S. Constitution created the presidential electors and the federal Electoral Count Act controls the proceedings by which the electors conduct their federal responsibilities,” she wrote in an op-ed in the Federalist in November 2025. “We can reach only one conclusion: These are not true state charges, these are not state actors, and the entire scheme to concoct some odd collection of purported violations of state law are a nullity.” Mitchell also publicly advocated for the pardons on X.

The Oversight Project, a rightwing group, published a memo in November 2025 making similar arguments.

Trump eventually did issue a blanket pardon for the fake electors on 10 November 2025, though those pardons didn’t legally affect the state-level cases. In Wisconsin, the criminal case against three architects of the fake elector scheme is still pending. The cases against fake electors in Arizona, Michigan and Nevada have stalled for reasons unrelated to the pardons.

The day Trump issued the blanket pardons, Martin received an email congratulating him on the pardons.

“It was a long project,” Martin wrote. “But I am glad we stuck the landing.”
