Hannah Lillith Assadi wins National book award for ‘visionary’ novel Paradiso 17

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Assadi’s third novel was inspired by the story of her late father, a Palestinian who escaped the Nakba as a child in 1948

Palestinian-Jewish author Hannah Lillith Assadi has won the National book award for fiction, one of the US’s most prestigious literary prizes, for her novel Paradiso 17.

Assadi’s third novel is a study of the psychic toll of displacement on migrants, through the tale of a Palestinian man who escaped the Nakba in 1948, when more than 700,000 Palestinians fled or were expelled from their homes by Israel. The story was inspired by her father, Sami Abdul-Fattah Al-Assadi, who fled the Nakba as a child with his family, before studying in Italy and moving to New York.

In a tearful speech, Assadi said, “What a beautiful story this has been, from its origins at my father’s deathbed until now, ending here and accepting this award. And so full of pain.

“In the course of its writing, I have experienced deep personal grief and unimaginable collective grief. I have watched cancer ravage my father’s body and genocide ravage his people and their children. And perhaps this is the Palestinian story on this earth. So beautiful. So painful.”

The judges praised Assadi’s “elegant lyricism” and “ambitious bending of time and space” and called the novel “a visionary, finely wrought portrait of rupture and endurance”.

Assadi won the $10,000 prize against Edwidge Danticat’s Dèy, Julián Delgado Lopera’s Pretend You’re Dead and I Carry You, Valeria Luiselli’s Beginning Middle End and Morgan Thomas’ Mad Eden.

Egyptian author Abdelrahman ElGendy won the $10,000 nonfiction prize for his debut Huna: A Memoir of Revolution, Prison, and Becoming, about his six years as a political prisoner in Egypt. ElGendy was unable to attend the ceremony after being denied a visa to the US, so his prize was collected by his editor Helen Thomaides, who read out his speech.

ElGendy said he had dreamed of turning his experience into a memoir as it gave him distance from the physical abuse he endured, and revealed he had practised award acceptance speeches under his duvet in prison.

“Today that dream should have come full circle but, like so many of the circles ruptured in my life, it will remain unfinished, broken by an oppressive regime with a fascist ideology whose concentration camps stand ready to swallow the bodies it deems undesirable,” ElGendy wrote.

The New York Times speculated earlier this month that ElGendy was not even eligible for the award as he did not meet its citizenship or residency requirements after fleeing the US due to Ice deportations, but the National Book Foundation, which administers the National book Awards, disputed this and said it did not “have any concerns regarding the eligibility of his title”.

Samar Yazbek of Syria, a finalist for the books in translation category, was also denied a visa, with the National Book Foundation describing both decisions as “unjust”.

The state department declined to comment on any specific cases, but issued a statement to Associated Press saying that “the Trump Administration continues to put America and its interests first by fully enforcing US immigration laws and ensuring lawful travel through our visa process. As Secretary [of State Marco] Rubio said, ‘a visa is a privilege, not a right’.”

Publishers submitted 1,912 books to the National book awards this year, with the winners judged by panels of authors, editors, booksellers and others in the literary community.

Brenda Hillman won the $10,000 poetry category for Still House in the Desert: An eco-contemplation, praised as “a rigorously unsentimental portrait of the stillness … of the poet’s childhood home after her mother’s death”.

David Leviathan and Gabriel Duckels won the $10,000 young people’s literature category for The Fight of Our Lives: AIDS in America.

Indian-British author Salman Rushdie was presented with a medal for “distinguished contribution to American letters”, while paediatrician and author Perri Klass was honoured for “outstanding service to the American literary community”.

Rushdie recalled being welcomed into the US literary scene after spending years under police protection in London after the fatwa over The Satanic Verses, and used his speech to thank the late Ursula K Le Guin for liking his first novel. “She was just about the only one who did! And it meant everything to me,” he said, to laughs.

“All I want to say is that it has been quite a ride, from over there to over here,” he added. “And to ride into this town and end up in this saloon, with these companions, is a hell of a thing.”

Actor and author Kyle MacLachlan hosted the ceremony, held at Cipriani Wall Street, while singer Michelle Zauner, author of the bestselling memoir Crying in H Mart, performed onstage.
