Observation of tunable superradiant frequency combs

Nature (2026) Cite this article

Cavity quantum electrodynamics1 with quantum emitters coupled to resonators provides a powerful platform for engineering light–matter interactions2,3 and exploring many-body phenomena4,5. In particular, superradiance6—arising from collective quantum interference among emitters—has been explored as a route to ultrastable continuous radiation7. However, engineering superradiance in the time domain to realize periodic pulsed sources or frequency combs remains largely unexplored. Here we investigate the non-equilibrium many-body dynamics of a driven spin ensemble coupled with an on-chip superconducting resonator and uncover a dynamical phase transition from continuous-wave to periodic pulsed superradiant emission. To quantitatively capture the observed dynamical phases, we introduce a driven-dissipative cavity-quantum electrodynamics model that elucidates how the periodic pulsed superradiant phase emerges from collective, periodically repeating spin dynamics stabilized by the interplay of coherence growth, disorder and dissipation. We also find that rare-earth ion spin systems with both optical and microwave transitions enable phase-synchronized, dual-rail superradiant frequency combs in both the microwave and the optical domains. Our results not only open new opportunities for atom-based frequency-comb sources and programmable superradiant generation, but establish a fundamental connection between periodic pulsed superradiance and the emergence of a continuous time crystal8,9,10 as a new non-equilibrium phase in driven open systems.

The invention of the laser stands among the most transformative technological advances of the twentieth century11. Laser operation relies on stimulated emission, whereby coherent radiation emerges once the gain exceeds loss in a resonant cavity12. By contrast, superradiance, first proposed by Dicke in 195413, can also lead to continuous coherent radiation arising from a collective many-body dissipation process in which emission results from quantum interference among an ensemble of emitters6. Remarkably, compared with conventional lasers, superradiant emission can have exceptional frequency stability due to the long coherence of atomic transitions, along with output power that scales nonlinearly with ensemble size7. Superradiance has been observed across a wide range of platforms in both free space and cavity-coupled systems, including cold atoms14,15,16, atomic gases17,18, artificial atoms19,20,21 and solid-state defects22,23,24.

Specifically, from a conceptual perspective, conventional lasers and superradiant light sources represent two complementary limits of light–matter interaction in a cavity-coupled system. Conventional lasers rely on stimulated emission, where the coherence is governed primarily by the cavity field. Superradiant systems, however, operate in the opposite regime, where coherence resides predominantly in the atomic ensemble, and radiation emerges from collectively synchronized dissipation driven by atom–atom correlations mediated by a lossy (bad) cavity (Fig. 1a–c). In this regime, the dynamics are naturally described within the framework of cavity quantum electrodynamics (QED), where light–matter interactions are treated at the fundamental quantum-mechanical level and different photon emission mechanisms can be individually identified25. This perspective has motivated extensive studies on superradiant dynamics in cavity QED systems1,2,26,27, including superradiant bursts28,29, triggered superradiance30 and ultra-narrow-linewidth superradiant lasers7,31,32.

Historically, advances in laser physics have demonstrated that introducing nonlinear elements can drive systems into unstable dynamical regimes33, enabling the development of mode-locked lasers that generate periodic, ultrafast optical pulses34. This periodic emission in the time domain, corresponding to a frequency comb in the spectral domain, dramatically expands the impact of lasers across science and industry35,36. This naturally raises curiosity into whether superradiant systems can support frequency-comb-like emission that produces time-periodic, phase-coherent pulse trains, and if so, what mechanism underlies this behaviour, and whether the resulting time-domain pulsing can be engineered.

Although recent theoretical37 and experimental38,39 efforts have begun to explore periodic pulsed superradiant dynamics in driven-dissipative systems, a clear understanding of their physical origin and underlying mechanisms, as well as comprehensive experimental demonstrations, remains elusive. Here we develop a unified theoretical and experimental framework that elucidates how superradiant frequency combs emerge in a driven cavity QED system of quantum emitters through the interplay of atomic coherence, dissipation and disorder, where the nonlinearity is provided by superradiant emission. Building on this framework, we experimentally investigate non-equilibrium superradiant dynamics in a disordered spin ensemble coupled to a superconducting microwave cavity, and show that robust, phase-coherent superradiant pulse trains can be both probed and engineered, with their pulse repetition rate set by the ensemble cooperativity. Unexpectedly, we also observe simultaneous and correlated periodic microwave and optical superradiant emissions that are both time-synchronized and phase-coherent. We refer to this phenomenon as dual-rail superradiant frequency combs, which opens new opportunities for ensemble-based quantum technologies. Furthermore, our results reveal a fundamental connection to a continuous time crystal, that is, a dynamical phase of matter in driven, non-equilibrium cavity QED systems characterized by spontaneously broken continuous time-translation symmetry and the emergence of robust temporal order.

To develop an intuitive understanding of the physical origin and mechanisms underlying superradiant frequency-comb formation, we consider an ensemble of frequency-inhomogeneous two-level quantum emitters (\(| \downarrow \rangle ,| \uparrow \rangle \)) coupled to a common lossy cavity mode and driven by an external pump. The microscopic dynamics of such cavity–ensemble interactions are imprinted on the cavity output, which serves as the primary experimentally accessible observable of the system’s cavity QED behaviour. In contrast to conventional laser systems, where emission is dominated by stimulated emission (second term in equation (1)), superradiant systems are governed by collective spin correlations (third term), with the photon emission rate scaling as \(\langle {\widehat{S}}_{\uparrow \downarrow }{\widehat{S}}_{\downarrow \uparrow }\rangle \propto {N}^{2}\). This collective behaviour acts as an effective nonlinearity, providing the foundation for dynamical phase transitions in superradiance.

Although three different mechanisms contribute to the photon emission rate, the presence of superradiant dynamics can first be identified by monitoring the emission following a transient excitation pump pulse (Fig. 1di). In this case, superradiant ensembles have a delayed burst of emission arising from stochastic quantum fluctuations that initiate synchronized dissipation of individual emitters, providing a clear signature of superradiance14,28.

Transitioning from a transient pump to a continuous-wave pump, the superradiant ensemble can support continuous emission by reaching a steady-state condition under the continuous-wave pump (Fig. 1dii). Analogous to a conventional laser, this condition is achieved when the cavity-mediated gain, \(\frac{4N{g}^{2}}{\kappa }\), is greater than the atomic dephasing rate, Γ (here, Γ is the linewidth of the emitter ensemble). Equivalently, defining the ensemble cooperativity as \(C=\frac{4N{g}^{2}}{\kappa \varGamma }\), continuous-wave superradiant emission occurs when C ≥ 1 (Fig. 1e, solid black line).

Crucially, when the coherent cavity–ensemble coupling strength, \(\sqrt{N}g\), exceeds the cavity-induced decoherence rate, κ/2, the steady-state dynamics can break down, turning continuous-wave into periodic pulsed emission (Fig. 1diii and Fig. 1e, dashed black line). In this regime, quantum interference between the strongly dressed cavity–ensemble states drives oscillatory behaviour in the photon emission. Surprisingly, however, we find that the presence of moderate disorder (defined as an extra inhomogeneous contribution to spectral line broadening beyond the homogeneous linewidth) substantially lowers the critical coupling threshold, thereby facilitating the phase transition into the pulsed phase (Fig. 1e, solid red line). Refer to the Supplementary Information for details on the theoretical phase boundary, which is estimated on the basis of a Hopf bifurcation model.

Using our driven-dissipative cavity QED model, we elucidate how such periodic pulsed emission directly originates from an emergent, time-periodic trajectory of the spin ensemble, in which collective coherence builds up, collapses through synchronized dissipation, and is then rebuilt under continuous pumping (inset of Fig. 1diii). We find from both our experiment and the theoretical model that this persistent pulsed emission repeats robustly without decay, with a periodicity, T ∝ 1/C, set by the ensemble cooperativity, C. This periodic superradiance phase, arising intrinsically and spontaneously from time-independent cavity QED dynamics, stands in stark contrast to conventional mode-locked lasers, where the periodicity is externally imposed by the cavity round-trip time. Such spontaneously broken time-translation symmetry, leading to the emergence of temporal order, defines a continuous time crystal—a class of non-equilibrium phases of matter that has been explored in a variety of driven-dissipative systems8,9,10,40.

We use rare-earth-ion-doped crystals to experimentally explore superradiant dynamics; such crystals offer highly coherent transitions in both the microwave and optical domains at cryogenic temperatures41,42, and are readily compatible with on-chip cavity QED architectures43,44,45,46. In particular, we use an ensemble of ytterbium-171 ions doped in a yttrium orthovanadate crystal47, whose excited-state spin manifold forms a two-level system, \(\{|\uparrow \rangle ,|\downarrow \rangle \}\), with a microwave transition frequency of ω = 2π × 3.4 GHz and an inhomogeneously broadened linewidth of Γ = 2π × 160 kHz (Fig. 2a). A superconducting microwave resonator with a cavity linewidth of κ = 2π × 3.6 MHz is fabricated on the crystal to couple to the excited-state microwave spin transition. We measured a maximum collective coupling strength of \(\sqrt{N}g\approx 2{\rm{\pi }}\times 1.1\) MHz, corresponding to a normalized coupling strength of \({g}_{{\rm{norm}}}=2\sqrt{N}g/\kappa \approx 0.6\), which lies below the critical threshold necessary for inducing the periodic superradiant phase in the absence of disorder (Extended Data Fig. 1).

Importantly, although the total available number of ytterbium ions in the cavity is on the order of Ntot ≈ 4 × 1010, we can control the effective emitter number, N ≤ Ntot, which participates in superradiant dynamics by varying the optical pump power that transfers population to the \(|\uparrow \rangle \) state in the excited-state manifold (Fig. 2a). We experimentally verify that superradiance dominates the dynamics of our dense cavity-coupled spin ensemble by observing that the delay time (τd) of a superradiant burst under a transient pump scales inversely with the effective ensemble size, N, that is, τd ∝ 1/N (Extended Data Fig. 2). Notably, by simply varying the continuous-wave pump power, we can continuously tune the ensemble cooperativity, thereby enabling systematic exploration of superradiant dynamics and phase transitions across three distinct regimes: no superradiance (regime I), continuous-wave superradiance (regime II) and periodic superradiance (regime III) (Fig. 2b; Fig. 1e, yellow line).

We first probe non-equilibrium dynamics under a weak continuous-wave pump at low collective cavity coupling strength. In this regime, the \(|\uparrow \rangle \)-state population is insufficient to establish collective behaviour, and no superradiant emission occurs (Fig. 2c,d, regime I).

However, as the pump power increases and crosses the critical threshold at which the ensemble cooperativity exceeds unity, a steady-state coherence emerges in this non-equilibrium setting, giving rise to continuous-wave superradiance (regime II). Intuitively, in the Bloch-sphere picture, this can be understood as a balance of competing contributions acting on each spin, where disorder, dephasing and coherence growth terms compensate one another, resulting in a collectively enhanced steady-state coherence (Fig. 2b, regime II). In the Fourier spectrum, we find that the emission linewidth is around 30 Hz—a value that is orders of magnitude narrower than the inhomogeneous ensemble linewidth (Fig. 2d, regime II; Extended Data Fig. 3).

Upon further increasing the pump power, and thus the collective coupling strength, we observe that the continuous-wave superradiant emission becomes unstable, leading to a breakdown of the balance, which in turn gives rise to time-periodic pulsed emission dynamics (regime III). Essentially, a stronger pump provides a larger nonlinear perturbation in the collective ensemble coherence, which triggers synchronized dissipation across the entire spin ensemble. The decayed emitters are then repumped into the excited state, forming a closed, repeating cycle that generates a persistent superradiant pulse train (Fig. 2c, regime III). This behaviour can also be understood as a Hopf bifurcation that signals the breakdown of steady-state dynamics and leads to the emergence of a stable limit cycle8. In the spectral domain we observe a frequency-comb structure with a narrow comb linewidth of about 50 Hz, indicating stable phase coherence of the pulses (Fig. 2d, regime III).

We analyse the emission spectrum in detail to systematically determine the phase boundaries across the three distinct superradiant phases (Fig. 2e). Specifically, we define the total emitted spectral power as \({A}_{{\rm{tot}}}={\int }_{-\infty }^{+\infty }S(\omega )d\omega \), and the total sideband power as \({A}_{{\rm{sb}}}={\int }_{-\infty }^{{\omega }_{p}-\Delta \omega /2}S(\omega )d\omega +{\int }_{{\omega }_{p}+\Delta \omega /2}^{+\infty }S(\omega )d\omega \), where S(ω) is the power spectral density of the time-domain emission signal, ωp is the frequency corresponding to the peak spectral density and Δω is a narrow integration window that isolates the central spectral peak. These quantities serve as effective experimental order parameters that distinguish the three phases in our experiment. Concretely, the onset of continuous-wave superradiance (regimes I to II) is marked by a sharp increase in Atot, whereas the transition from the continuous-wave to the pulsed superradiant phase (regimes II to III) is signalled by a sharp increase in Asb. To more clearly characterize the second phase transition, we introduce the normalized sideband fraction, which is defined as the ratio Asb/Atot. This quantity, which has also been used to characterize the crystalline fraction in time-crystal phenomena8,48, provides a clear signature of the transition into the periodic superradiant phase (Fig. 2e, inset).

Crucially, this robust temporal ordering in the superradiant dynamics emerges spontaneously under strictly time-independent continuous-wave pumping, without any externally imposed modulation or timing reference. By repeating the experiment under identical conditions, we find that the onset time of the superradiant pulse train is spontaneously random from experiment to experiment (Extended Data Fig. 4), providing direct evidence of the spontaneous breaking of continuous time-translation symmetry8. Our experimental measurements and numerical simulations consistently indicate that the system satisfies the defining criteria of a time-crystalline phase (Extended Data Table 1).

A natural follow-up investigation would be to determine what sets the periodicity of the persistent, periodic superradiant phase. According to our theoretical framework, the periodicity of the superradiant bursts is determined by the ensemble cooperativity, which depends on the number of emitters participating in the superradiant dynamics. To probe this experimentally, we selectively excite different spectral subsets of ytterbium ions by intentionally detuning the pump carrier frequency across an optical inhomogeneous linewidth of about 2π × 92 MHz. This allows us to systematically vary the number of emitters excited to the \(|\uparrow \rangle \) state within the microwave superradiant manifold while keeping the pump Rabi frequency unchanged (Fig. 3a).

As shown in Fig. 3b, the threshold for the onset of superradiant emission increases as the optical pump is detuned away from the centre of the inhomogeneous linewidth, indicating that pump detuning serves as an effective control knob for tuning the collective cavity–ensemble coupling strength. In regime III we observe that the superradiant burst periodicity, T, also depends sensitively on the pump detuning (Fig. 3c): T increases as the detuning moves away from zero. Numerical simulations show strong quantitative agreement with our experimental observations, further corroborating the effective tuning of the collective coupling strength via pump detuning in our system (Fig. 3d and Extended Data Fig. 5).

As our theoretical framework reveals that T scales inversely with the ensemble size N, while the intensity scales quadratically with N, we rescale both the time and intensity axes of all measured superradiant burst profiles, acquired at different detuning values, by their corresponding effective ensemble size. Remarkably, we find that all data collapse onto a single universal curve (Fig. 3e,f). This universal collapse demonstrates that the periodic superradiant phase is governed by collective scaling laws set by the effective size of the driven spin ensemble, independent of the microscopic details of individual emitters.

Collectively, our experimental findings, in agreement with our theoretical model, indicate that the periodic superradiant phase is a robust collective phenomenon representing a non-equilibrium phase of matter in driven open quantum systems. To further substantiate this, we perform extensive numerical simulations addressing the following three key aspects (Extended Data Fig. 6). First, we confirm that, in our experimental regime, the nonlinear superradiant term associated with collective ensemble correlations dominates the conventional stimulated emission term by more than an order of magnitude. Second, we verify that the observed pulsed phase originates from a large atomic ensemble, as it cannot be explained by a single emitter with an artificially enhanced coupling strength. Third, we demonstrate that disorder plays an essential role, as the periodic superradiant phase does not emerge in the absence of disorder for the experimentally measured coupling strength, gnorm ≈ 0.6. This finding implies that inhomogeneous broadening is not merely a source of decoherence, but can instead be harnessed to engineer collective quantum interference, thereby reshaping photon-emission dynamics in the cavity QED system.

Although our experimental results so far have focused on superradiance in the microwave domain, our ytterbium-ion-based system intrinsically comprises a three-level structure that involves both microwave and optical transitions. Direct optical superradiance is not observed due to the low optical cooperativity49 (of about 0.14). However, we find that the periodic superradiant dynamics in the microwave transition is transferred to the optical transition via a coherent Raman scattering process50 (\(|g\rangle \langle \uparrow |\cdot | \uparrow \rangle \langle \downarrow |\Rightarrow |g\rangle \langle \downarrow |\)). Consequently, the transferred optical field coherently inherits the temporal dynamics of the microwave field, giving rise to correlated, dual-rail frequency-comb generation in both the microwave and optical domains (Extended Data Figs. 7 and 8). As shown in Fig. 4a, the optical and microwave signals have temporally synchronized burst sequences, demonstrating that both emissions originate from the same underlying collective dynamics. To quantify the coherence between the two emission channels, we analyse their relative phase difference and observe a zero-phase offset, indicating in-phase locking across the microwave and optical domains (Fig. 4b). We further characterize the spectra of the dual-rail frequency combs and confirm that the narrow comb structures are well matched and have the same repetition rate (Fig. 4c).

Our work positions disordered solid-state cavity QED systems as a versatile platform at the intersection of cavity QED, non-equilibrium physics and quantum metrology. A central result is a unified understanding of dynamical phase transitions in a driven cavity QED system, where the periodic pulsed superradiant phase can be identified as a continuous time-crystal phase. We also demonstrate the unexpected observation and tunable engineering of robust dual-rail superradiant frequency combs in both the microwave and optical domains. We believe these results pave the way for stable atom-based frequency comb sources, superradiance-based waveform synthesis and coherent microwave-optical quantum interconnects.

Our platform opens several promising avenues for future exploration. The repetition rate is currently limited by the optical pumping rate required to rebuild population inversion. Incorporating a high-cooperativity optical cavity would Purcell-enhance the repumping process, which would increase the transferred optical emission strength and enable higher repetition rates and faster control over ensemble participation, while also allowing direct generation of a tunable optical superradiant frequency comb. In parallel, recent advances in superconducting microwave technology offer new opportunities to dynamically engineer the cavity-mediated interactions that govern the collective dynamics. Combined control over ensemble coupling, dissipation and detuning will unlock a powerful route to programmable quantum light sources, enabling on-demand synthesis of tailored emission waveforms and new paradigms in coherent quantum control.

Our theoretical framework predicts how the burst periodicity (T), peak power (Ppeak) and average emission power (\({\overline{P}}_{{\rm{MW}}}\)) scale with the number of participating emitters, N (Extended Data Fig. 5a). First, the burst periodicity is governed by the ensemble cooperativity, leading to the scaling of T ∝ 1/N (Extended Data Fig. 5b). Second, each burst originates from the collective emission of an inverted ensemble of N atoms, resulting in the emission of N photons. As the superradiant burst duration, τw, scales inversely with the ensemble size, that is, τw ∝ 1/N, the peak power—proportional to the burst energy divided by its duration—scales quadratically with N, that is, Ppeak ∝ N2 (Extended Data Fig. 5c). Third, the average emission power follows from the burst energy multiplied by the repetition rate, leading to the same quadratic scaling with N, that is, \({\overline{P}}_{{\rm{MW}}}\propto {N}^{2}\) (Extended Data Fig. 5d).

As shown in Extended Data Fig. 5, all of these scaling predictions are in excellent agreement with the experimental data. This agreement enables a universal rescaling of the experimentally observed superradiant pulse trains in terms of the effective ensemble size N, or equivalently, the experimentally tunable cooperativity.

From numerical simulations, the spontaneous emission factor is estimated to be nsp = 2.6 quanta, leaving nth = 3.2 quanta for the thermal occupancy of the microwave resonator, corresponding to an effective resonator temperature of around 0.6 K.

We also observe a correlation between the microwave and optical transition frequencies, consistent with previous observations56. The laser linewidth is carefully calibrated using the Pound–Drever–Hall error signal, yielding a locking linewidth of 22 kHz. This corresponds to a 27 Hz limit on the microwave emission frequency, as shown in Extended Data Fig. 3b.

On the optical side, the transferred superradiant emission inherits the linewidth of the optical pump laser due to energy conservation. The data shown in Fig. 4c of the main text correspond to heterodyne detection between the optical emission and the pump laser, which in principle should yield the same linewidth as the microwave comb (about 50 Hz). However, the measured optical comb linewidth is about 1 kHz, probably due to frequency jitter arising from the time delay between the optical path through the dilution refrigerator and the local oscillator path.

We evaluate whether the observed periodic pulsed superradiance can be classified as a continuous time-crystal phase by applying a set of five criteria commonly associated with time-crystalline order8,9,10,40,48.

First, the underlying Hamiltonian should be time-independent, ensuring that any emergent periodic behaviour is not trivially imposed by external driving. Second, the observed dynamics should arise from genuine many-body dynamics, rather than from single-particle effects or externally enforced synchronization. Third, the system should have persistent and well-defined periodic temporal behaviour in its steady state. Fourth, this periodicity should correspond to the spontaneous breaking of continuous time-translation symmetry, meaning that the system evolves with a characteristic frequency that is not explicitly set by the Hamiltonian. Fifth, the observed behaviour should be robust against perturbations such as noise, dissipation and experimental imperfections, indicating the presence of a stable dynamical phase rather than fine-tuned transient dynamics.

We systematically evaluate these criteria across different experimental platforms and summarize the results in Extended Data Table 1. This comparison provides a unified framework for assessing whether periodic superradiant dynamics can be understood within the broader context of continuous time crystals.

To further corroborate that our system spontaneously breaks continuous time-translational symmetry, we apply a transient optical pump for 1 ms and monitor the emergence of the periodic pulsed-emission phase. The temporal phase, θk, of the continuous time crystal is defined from the onset time of the periodic burst oscillations as θk = 2π × τk/T (mod 2π), where k indexes individual experimental realizations and τk is the burst onset time in the kth run. As shown in Extended Data Fig. 4, the system first undergoes superradiant burst dynamics and then relaxes into a long-lived periodic pulsed phase that persists without decay over the observation window. Crucially, the onset time, and hence the phase θk, varies randomly over 2π across repeated experiments, indicating that the phase is not determined by the transient pump. This self-evolved randomness provides evidence that temporal order emerges spontaneously, consistent with the spontaneous breaking of continuous time-translational symmetry.

Furthermore, as shown in Extended Data Fig. 7, the observed periodic superradiance represents a robust dynamical phase in which both microwave superradiance and the transferred optical superradiance exhibit essentially non-decaying oscillations under continuous pumping.
