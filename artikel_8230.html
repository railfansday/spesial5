Andrew Skurka on Pro Tips for Older Hikers

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Hi, I’m Andrew Skurka. In my 20s, I was a dirtbag thru-hiker. I was the first person to do the C2C Route, the Great Western Loop, and the Alaska-Yukon Expedition.

For the last 17 years of my life, I’ve been running a guided backpacking service. Every year, I get to go to places like southern Utah, Alaska, Yellowstone, and the High Sierra to teach other people backpacking skills.

If you’re watching this video before Saturday, December 5th, 2026, and if you live in the Denver metro area, I invite you to join us for a live Backpacker Radio episode at the Skylark Lounge in Denver.

And if you’re looking to have an exceptional backpacking experience in 2027, please consider joining me on one of my guided backpacking trips. We are accepting applications starting Monday, December 7th, 2026. For more information, you can learn more here.

Today’s video is geared toward backpackers who are getting up there in age. I’m going to help set your expectations about what’s to come and also give you ideas about how to keep backpacking fun late into life.

Now, importantly, I should mention I’m not old at 45, but I do work with a lot of older backpackers. And I think what’s fair to say, too, is that at 45, I’ve been backpacking for over half my life, since 21.

And backpacking for me has changed as I’ve gotten older, and I expect that it will continue to change as I get into my 50s and 60s and 70s.

In some respects, backpacking gets easier as you get older. So specifically, you tend to have more money, you have more time, and you’re probably more motivated because you know that the window of opportunity is closing.

But mostly, for backpackers who are getting older, the key takeaway should be that backpacking is going to get harder physically.

Putting in those miles, hiking all that vertical, trying to recover overnight, sleeping on the ground—none of that gets easier as you get into your 50s, 60s, and 70s. It was much easier when you were in your sprightly 20s and 30s.

So the key thing that I would say to an older backpacker is, what are you going to do? Are you going to stop backpacking? Or are you going to make some adaptations and some tweaks to your approach to make sure that you can keep backpacking late into life?

Let me put this conversation into context by giving you some statistics from our guided trip program.

So every year, we have about 400 to 500 clients on our trips. And I’ll give you some specific data from 2026, although these patterns haven’t changed a lot in many years.

In 2026, 24% of our clients were in their 50s, 23% of our clients were in their 60s, and only 4% of our clients were in their 70s.

I’m not in my 70s, but I do have 70-year-old parents and 70-year-old in-laws. I don’t want to speculate about what might be happening in the 70s that’s responsible for that big drop-off.

But to anyone who’s approaching this phase of life, this empty-nester, retiree stage of life, just realize that the window will close.

So if you have goals, if you have backpacking goals, just make sure not to wait too long on them because as you get older and older, backpacking definitely seems to get more difficult and participation rates go down.

There are certainly exceptions to these demographic trends that I just gave you.

I would call out, for example, Greybeard, who at 91 just became the oldest person to thru-hike the Appalachian Trail. But he’s a freak. He’s a total freak. He’s such an outlier.

For the average backpacker, for the average person, I would expect that your backpacking interests will fade during your 60s or into your early 70s.

Let’s talk about the physical challenges of backpacking and how you can mitigate or adapt to them.

As we get older, the first reckoning is that putting in the same amount of miles and the same amount of vertical that we did when we were younger just gets harder.

You can push into it, but you’re not going to recover as quickly, and the time it takes you to do those miles and that vertical is going to be longer, which cuts into camp time.

So you might not like the solution, but I just think it’s realistic: You need to start planning less ambitious itineraries.

Relatedly, but it’s a little bit more specific, one thing that we really observe with our older clients is a loss of power and strength.

We see this mostly when climbing up hills. An older client who keeps up with the group no problem on flat terrain or downhill will just move toward the back of the pack on those long, extended climbs.

They just no longer have that power and that strength, that horsepower to push uphill and carry their bodies and their packs against gravity.

So the solution here, part of it, again, is similar to the first solution. Maybe just be a little less ambitious in your planning.

But you also can mitigate that by doing more uphill-specific training. You can also add weight training into your mix of exercises.

And then one little technique change is that instead of taking those big, long, powerful strides that you may have taken in your youth, shorten your stride and increase your cadence.

I started doing this years ago, even, and I just found that it put a lot less pressure on my joints and on my muscles. I didn’t feel nearly as worked at the end of the day by those big, long extensions that require a lot of power and strength in my muscles.

Among our older clients, we also see a loss of agility and finesse. And this becomes apparent on any kind of uneven walking surface: talus, scree, scrambles, slickrock.

One is just to take those sections a little bit more slowly and with more caution.

Another way to manage it, if you’re not already, is that you should definitely be adding trekking poles to your kit. These will give you just a little bit of extra stability. It’s like now you have four legs instead of just two. So some extra stability.

And then one last thing you can do, and you can integrate this into your training, is to emphasize balance.

So do exercises that require balance, for example, one-legged squats or exercises on a BOSU ball. Anything to kind of increase that sensitivity about your position in the air and things that strengthen all those connective ligaments and muscles.

A fourth observation of our older clients, and when we’re talking about old, most people are thinking 50s, 60s, but I will say just as a 45-year-old who has been running since high school, one of the big differences that I see as I get on in age is just the lack of recovery.

In my 20s, I’ll just, here’s a remarkable number, even today.

So when I did the Great Western Loop, it was 208 days, and I averaged 33 miles a day. I was 27 years old.

I cannot at this age fathom hiking that many miles per day, day after day after day. I definitely feel like that sustained effort, that wear and tear, would break me at this point.

So longer recovery, or less efficient recovery, should just be the expectation among older backpackers.

Here are a couple of things that you can do.

First is that you can build recovery days into your itinerary.

So maybe days one, two, three are normal. Maybe day four is a little bit easier. And that way you can be strong again on days five and six.

Or maybe you’re just a little bit more patient with your trips.

So if you’re going on, say, a seven-day trip, days one, two, three, you finish the day still feeling like there’s plenty more left in the tank. And that way you can be strong on days four, five, and six.

In comparison, a younger person would charge out of the gate and would be able to recover well enough to remain strong throughout the trip. That just doesn’t happen as you age.

So building in some recovery would be one option.

The second thing you can do is create itineraries that have some flexibility in them.

So this is a little bit more difficult to do with point-to-point itineraries. For example, if you’re going to do a section of, say, the Colorado Trail, it’s easier to do if you plan a loop.

And if you plan that loop, just identify some places where you can go where you can add some distance and some vertical, but also places where you can shorten your route, too.

And that way, if you’re not recovering, if you feel like you need an easy day but you need to stay on an itinerary, you have that kind of built in.

The third thing you can do to help with recovery is that you should be carrying plenty of ibuprofen.

Use this with some care. It can create some kidney issues, so just be aware of the limits on how much ibuprofen you can take in. But ibuprofen is an anti-inflammatory. It will help with some recovery.

And then one more thing you can do to help your recovery is just emphasize self-care when you’re out on the trail.

I’m not necessarily, like, I have seen clients carry foam rollers to give themselves a little bit of a massage at night.

You could also be taking ice baths if you can find cold water. You might do some light stretching in the morning before you get going.

But just anything to kind of take it easier on your body and let your body endure that stress and strain better than if you just kind of raw-dogged it.

Everyone’s gonna love that I just said raw dog.

The fifth experience you might have as an older backpacker is that you’re getting less restful sleep.

And I’ll say this as someone whose wife woke them up multiple times last night because I was apparently snoring so loud.

So here are a couple of solutions for you.

First, if you are not already using a plush, thick, warm sleeping pad, with the Therm-a-Rest NeoAir XLite being, I would say, like a minimum, you should go ahead and do that.

So upgrade from your Therm-a-Rest RidgeRest or your Therm-a-Rest Z Lite. That’s for some other generation now. Get something comfortable to sleep on.

The second thing you can do is bring a pee bottle with you, for the folks who can’t hold it as well as they used to.

Have a dedicated pee bottle. You can bring this into your shelter at night, and that way you don’t have to go outside in the middle of the night when you have to go pee.

You can just pee in your bottle, put it off to the side, and go back to bed.

In addition to the sleeping pad, you also want to make sure that you’ve got a nice pillow.

In my 20s, on all my big, long thru-hikes, I slept on lots of things that no normal person would ever want to sleep on.

I would use my nasty shoes. I would sleep on bags of trash, like wrapper trash from candy bars and energy bar wrappers. I would sleep on my water bottles. Anything to give my head a little bit of a lift.

So I have a real air pillow. It’s about that wide, that tall, it’s like two and a half, three inches thick.

And then the last thing, if you have sleep apnea, there are lightweight CPAP machines out there.

So you can do some research, and if that makes the difference, I mean, they still weigh a couple of pounds, but if that makes the difference between you sleeping well at night and being able to backpack for an extended period of time, or you trying to go backpacking and you never get a good night’s rest and the maximum trip duration is like three days before you’re just utterly exhausted and have a bad attitude because you’re not sleeping well, then carry the four-pound machine and move on with your life.

You should also be thinking about what you can do to make your camping experience more comfortable.

So one easy solution would be to at least bring a sit pad.

Even better, maybe investing one pound-ish in a nice camp chair that allows you to sit 12 inches or 15 inches off the ground as opposed to having to sit on the ground.

Good little trick if you haven’t already, though: If you’re carrying a bear canister, which would be required equipment in places like the High Sierra or Rocky Mountain, you can also use your bear canister as a camp chair.

Also think about the type of shelter that you’re using.

As an older person, you don’t really want to be, or like to be, crawling on the ground, and you don’t have as much flexibility.

So shelters that have a side entry are way better than shelters that have an entry at the head.

And also, give yourself a shelter with a little bit of additional interior room.

Instead of maybe doing the Hyperlite Mountain Gear Mid 1, which is a wonderful shelter, I use it most of the summer, maybe instead get a Zpacks Duplex, which is for two people.

It’ll give you a little bit of extra room to move around, and you’ll feel a little bit less cramped.

So that way you can sit in your shelter and spend time in your shelter more comfortably.

The last thing that I hear from our older clients is that their feet start to change shape.

So specifically, they get a little bit wider, which might have something to do with the fact that their arches start collapsing and their feet start to get flatter.

And the last thing that happens is that the fat pads on the underside of their feet become thinner.

So a lot of our older clients tend to gravitate toward a more cushioned shoe like this one.

If you notice with this shoe, just look at this big, fat foam midsole that wraps all the way around the shoe.

This ends up being a more cushioned, comfortable thing to wear. Maybe the analogy, it’s a little bit like driving a Cadillac.

There’s a lot more that goes into selecting a shoe than just the midsole cushioning, but all things being equal, if you have two shoes that fit well and that are well-suited for your application, but one has a thicker midsole than the other, maybe gravitate toward this one.

As we age, we tend to lose our strength or power, and we also tend to lose our balance.

So as an older backpacker, one really easy way to mitigate that is you could just lean hard into the ultralight gear thing.

Twenty-three years ago, I actually was an intern for GoLite in the summer of 2002, and I remember at that time GoLite wasn’t getting a lot of traction in the backpacking community, except for among older hikers.

Because for them, they saw that GoLite was creating gear, and it was an approach that allowed them to backpack later into their life.

Because at the time, think about backpacking, everyone backpacked like NOLS or Outward Bound, with these huge packs that weighed 50, 60 pounds.

So GoLite was sort of pitching this alternative, and a lot of the older backpackers saw the possibilities.

I have older backpackers, older clients, who have gone like crazy with the lightweight gear.

They’re the ones who usually show up with the lightest packs because they usually have the most discretionary income.

I have a couple of clients who, it’s like, it’s DCF shelters, it’s DCF stuff sacks, it’s DCF bear hangs.

I think that if they could find it, they probably would buy DCF underwear.

I mean, they have just gone full in on ultralight gear.

That’s not to say that you need or can buy a bunch of DCF gear. If your financial means are a little bit more limited, there are actually a lot of free ways to lighten up your pack.

Here are a couple of things I would suggest.

One is that before you buy anything, you do a lot of homework.

You really understand the likely conditions that you will encounter, and you really have a heart-to-heart with yourself about what your backpacking style is going to be.

See another video that we’ve done about that.

And then you just buy the right stuff the first time instead of having to cycle through gear that isn’t quite what you thought it was going to be.

So that’d be number one: Just buy right the first time.

Part of lightening up is just taking only things that you need, and that results in you not having to spend money on things that are extraneous and discretionary and that actually don’t help your backpacking experience at all.

And my last tip for saving money on gear would be to buy equipment that is versatile.

So buy equipment that will be appropriate for a wide range of conditions and locations, and that way you can just have one kit that’s kind of a jack of all trades, that tends to work pretty well in all of the locations where you might want to backpack.

I’ve talked about the physical challenges that older backpackers face and how to mitigate or address some of them.

I’ve also talked about the importance of lightweight equipment.

The last thing that I’d like to talk about to close is the importance of shifting your mindset toward backpacking as you age.

Let me just tell you, from a personal perspective.

In my 20s and 30s, I was a hard-charging backpacker.

It was dawn to dusk every day, as many miles as I could do, as much vertical as I could do.

I just leaned into that, the physical challenge of backpacking.

I wanted to see as much as I possibly could with a limited amount of time, and I wore it like a badge of honor if I could hike 35 or 40 miles a day.

As I’ve gotten older and my body is less cooperative with that approach to backpacking, I have just accepted that backpacking can look different.

I have leaned into other aspects of the backpacking experience.

I have become a much more capable companion while backpacking instead of just being sort of a thorny soloist.

I have leaned into all the interpretive opportunities of backpacking.

I’ve learned about birds. I’ve learned about geology. I’ve learned about history. I’ve learned about archaeology.

And then I also have just started to love even more the peace and quiet that backpacking gives me.

So my spring usually starts with a backpacking trip in southern Utah, and the thing that I always respond to when I go out there is just how quiet and peaceful it is out there.

And that’s just such a welcome break from my ordinary life.

And even though in the spring in Utah I’m no longer doing those 35- or 40-mile days, I love backpacking just as much.

Maybe in short, the thing to think about as an older backpacker is that just getting out is a win.
