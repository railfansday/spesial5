I have double-checked and yes, original 'GTA killer' Driv3r really was that rubbish

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

At this point the tragedy of Driv3r, or Driver 3, or my personal favourite Drive-thrur, is well thumbed. A familiar litany of unrealised ambition, heinous bugs, dull open-world design, and an expensive yet audibly bored voice cast, the accidental comedic highlight of which is Michelle Rodriguez flatly reeling off car theft orders from what sounds like the inside of a Michelle Rodriguez-sized tupperware container.

A more widely forgotten detail is that, for a while, there was a non-zero chance - a belief, even - that Drive Three Er was going to be the opposite. The 2003-2004 hype cycle had sowed the notion that this would in fact be the open-world crime thriller to knock Grand Theft Auto off its perch, and even 22 years before GTA 6's idolising reviews, that was no small promise; Rockstar were still enjoying the balmy success of Vice City, and we could see San Andreas cresting the hill on a stolen, shining BMX. "Ah, but did they have Michael Madsen doing doughnuts in car parks?," publishers Atari asked the world, either unaware or in denial of the spluttering, clapped-out rattletrap they were about to wheel into the showroom.

You likely know the rest, though one of the dangers of familiarity is the subsequent breeding of complacency, which in this case can manifest as a thawing acceptance. I succumbed to this myself, having recently installed a legally unlinkable copy of Drivethreeuh, mind opened to the possibility that maybe it wasn't that bad. Six hours later, I am here to tell you: it was.

Almost nothing here works, figuratively and literally. Within three minutes of the opening mission, before Madsen's undercover cop Tanner goes undercover, you're ordered to join some marked police cars to head for a bust – but because the rozzers' primary function is to give chase to you, later on, driving too close crosses their wires, provoking them into blue-on-blue ramming maneuvres. Later, in a motorbike sprint down an inexplicably exploding beach, cartel goons are positioned across the sand, presumably to take drama-enhancing pot shots. They're so slow, however, both to notice your presence and to physically raise their guns, that they're barely out of their idle animations by the time you whizz past. And your own guy isn't much better: an assassination mission, which ends with your target cornered from an elevated position, requires that you take a few steps backwards first, as Tanner can't drop his shootin' arms low enough to land a hit from the balcony he's on.

Unlike Vice City's Tommy Vercetti, he can at least swim, which by 2004 standards gives the seaside Miami and Nice maps a fair verisimilitude, particularly when a shot-up boat engine forces the abandoning of ship. No amount of product-of-its-time concessions, though, can account for how woodenly unpalatable the on-foot action is. Controls are stiff and shooting feels meaningless, with zero tangible sense of bullets being sent nor received; killing a man carries all the weight of just shouting at him to lie down, and if it weren't for sound effects and the dwindling health bar, I could park Tanner in front of a firing squad of identically-modelled thugs with almost zero impression that his lungs were filling with Uzi rounds.

Driver, of course, began as a pure wheelman sim, later installments only letting you hoof it after someone at developers Reflections began smelling that Rockstar money. It was therefore always unlikely, even for the purported GTA killer, that this would achieve action-adventure sophistication in its bipedal bits. Even so, god, it's woeful. Stingy checkpointing also means that failure is often punished by having to replay those tedious, flavourless shootouts in full.

The driving is better, which is a bit like saying there's decent cheese in this cheese and smallpox toastie. It's reasonably slidey, when throwing the back end through a curving French alleyway, and in contrast to the gunplay, there is a somewhat realised tactility to ploughing around in two tonnes of rolling metal. It has problems of its own, mind. Cars feel okay when they're moving, but contact and crashes are leaden, sluggish, visually underwhelming occasions that I would call non-events, were they not so slow to recover from that a single nasty impact can scupper a timed mission. Granted, I quickly re-learned how vital it is to keep both eyes on the road, as other vehicles, pedestrians, lamposts, and other detritus tend to pop into existence some mere fifty feet in front of my speeding bumper.

The result, of both this graphical shortcoming and the overly punishing crashes, is a getaway car sim that too often encourages you to drive like cataract-ridden grandparent. Worse, whoever put Letty Ortiz in a lunchbox must have also been in charge of the engine noises, producing an array of miserable high-rev dins – especially for the motorbikes, which screech like amateurishly autotuned vacuum cleaners. There are also shades of that deranged cop AI in ordinary commuters, who I've repeatedly seen swerving and slaloming around empty lanes, seemingly for no reason beyond violent confusion.

Strangely enough, my 2026 return has largely dodged the game's infamous glitchiness, the worst instance of physics defiance being a garage door that appeared to lift Tanner into the air before propelling him, incorporeally, through the car shed's roof. I recall much worse, from the days when I'd have my dad rent it for me from Blockbuster. Mission-critical NPCs getting stuck on walls; flipped pursuit cars spinning endlessly; getting flung into space when walking on an overturned vehicle (a common occurrence, given the actual 'crawling out' animation sometimes failed to play, the game instead teleporting you onto the spring-loaded underside instead).

I've thus far escaped these, but as far as I know, they're still lurking dormant, and none of what I've witnessed with adult eyes has been funny or stupid enough to imbue Driv3r 3 The Third with a Wiseauean so-bad-it's-good quality. It's just bad. It was always bad. Why did I even entertain the possibility that it wasn't bad?

Reflections, to their credit, accepted their folly. Narratively unrelated sequel Driver: Parallel Lines tucked away a bin full of discarded copies, at least until Ubisoft bought up the series and patched it out, and for all the fantastical coma silliness of direct followup Driver: San Francisco, that was sensible enough to keep Tanner where he best belongs: hands on the wheel, foot on the gas, gun holstered. And was a far, far better game for it, too.

As 2011's San Francisco launch also remains the most recent Driver release, I regretfully can't end with the assurance that DrIIIver's legacy is a mere blip on a redeemed franchise. That stain stank, and did for ages, very possibly to the extent that GTA's one-time biggest rival never recovered.

Still, even if Reflections couldn't fully turn around their fortunes, the consistent improvement from this to Parallel Lines to San Francisco showed that they did learn something. Maybe so too did anyone else who went on to make an open-world action game. Which wouldn't be the worst outcome; after all, those who do not learn from gnarled, stinking wrecks like this are doomed to repeat them.
