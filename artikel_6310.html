Northern ice sheets may have cooled the deep ocean and pulled carbon from the air, causing the last Ice Age

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

A simplified ocean model shows cooling only the far north drew nearly as much ice age carbon into the sea as cooling the whole planet.

The ice ages are known for the ice sheets that spread across the far north. But much of an ice age’s cold came from carbon dioxide leaving the air. That carbon went into the ocean largely at the other end of the planet, around Antarctica.

A new modeling study suggests the two ends were connected. Cooling only the ocean’s far north pulled nearly three-quarters as much CO2 out of the air as cooling the entire planet.

Madison Shankle of the University of St Andrews led the work with colleagues at the National Oceanography Centre and the University of Chicago.

The test ran in a simplified computer model of the ocean with no real ice sheet in it. So the result is a mechanism, not a measurement of the past.

Shankle said the team had “produced evidence of a plausible mechanism” linking the northern ice sheets to the changes around Antarctica that pulled CO2 from the air.

“Our study takes the step of actually demonstrating them working in model simulations, which is a big step forward for this field,” Shankle added.

Ice ages are paced by slow wobbles in Earth’s orbit, which change how much summer sunlight reaches the far north. Less summer sun lets ice sheets grow there.

However, the wobbles alone can’t make the planet as cold as it got. And the deep ocean is the only store big enough to hold the missing carbon.

Much of the deep ocean’s water last touched the air in the Southern Ocean around Antarctica. This is where scientists have looked for the carbon.

Antarctic ice cores show that sea ice there grew and shrank in step with the northern ice sheets,. It did not change with the sunlight over Antarctica itself.

In 2001, two researchers proposed an answer. Cold water sinks in the North Atlantic and flows south through the deep ocean.

If a northern ice sheet chilled that water, the chill would eventually arrive around Antarctica. This would cause sea ice to grow there, and set off the changes that store carbon.

Shankle’s team tested the idea in the MITgcm ocean model. The model ocean is one basin, 2.5 miles (4 kilometers) deep. It has a flat floor and runs from 70 degrees south to 70 degrees north.

Air temperatures over it are fixed, so it can’t grow an ice sheet,. The team cooled the air to stand in for one.

The team ran four versions. One had warm, preindustrial-style air everywhere, and one was ice-age cold everywhere, 3.6°F (2°C) cooler at the equator and 10.8°F (6°C) cooler at the poles.

The other two cooled only the air north of 40.5 degrees north, either halfway to ice-age temperatures or all the way.

The cooled band is where northern water sinks to the deep, and it is small. It covers just 13 percent of the model’s surface. Each run lasted 15,000 model years, long enough for the deep ocean to settle.

Cooling the north cooled the whole model ocean. The biggest temperature drop was at mid-depths of 3,300 to 6,600 feet (1,000 to 2,000 meters). In the fully cooled northern run, that layer ended up colder than under global cooling.

The far north turned out to have outsized reach. In the full northern run, the whole ocean cooled by 4.5°F (2.5°C) on average. An earlier run that applied the same cooling to the far south instead cooled the whole ocean by only 1.1°F (0.6°C).

The cold water grew sea ice around the model’s Antarctica, and ice thickness south of 60 degrees south roughly doubled in the half-cooled run and tripled in the full one.

Thicker ice leaves more salt behind when it freezes, so the bottom water got saltier and the deep ocean separated into more distinct layers. Records of the real ice ages show both features.

Atmospheric CO2 in the model fell by 14 parts per million with the half-cooled north, and 32 ppm with the fully cooled one. Cooling the whole planet brought it down 43 ppm. So the north alone produced 74 percent of the drawdown.

Most of that came from a simple fact: cold water holds more carbon dioxide.

Because the northern cooling reached the whole deep ocean, the extra carbon that the colder water absorbed matched or exceeded the global-cooling run.

The thicker Antarctic sea ice added more by capping water before it could give its carbon back to the air, at about half the strength of the global run.

Marine life worked against the storage: the cooler, more layered ocean left more nutrients unused at the surface, which released some carbon rather than storing it.

The real ice ages had iron-rich dust and shifting winds that fed ocean life, and the model has neither.

Air trapped in ice cores shows CO2 fell from about 280 ppm to about 180 ppm, and the model wasn’t built to match that.

A 2022 study cooled the North Atlantic in a more realistic two-basin model and saw CO2 rise by 6 ppm instead. Its Atlantic current strengthened and brought deep carbon back toward the surface.

In that model, the deep water started out warmer, about 43°F to 46°F (6°C to 8°C) against 36°F to 39°F (2°C to 4°C) in the new one. It may not have cooled enough to grow southern sea ice, Shankle’s team wrote.

The new model has limits of its own. It has a flat floor, no real ice sheet, no changing winds, and no seasons, and its CO2 doesn’t warm or cool the air.

The air over its Antarctica also stayed at warm, preindustrial temperatures, which probably held back the sea ice and understated the effect, the authors wrote.

Whether a real ice sheet, with its winds and seasons, could set off the same chain in the real Southern Ocean is the open question. The team’s next step is a model with a growing ice sheet built in, and they want the idea tested in fuller models of the Earth.

The full study was published in the Journal of Climate.
