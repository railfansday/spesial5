An overlooked brain region offers new clues to how memory works

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

In mice, a thin brain sheet called the claustrum kept a smell or a touch in working memory for 30 seconds, and quieting it cut performance.

Anyone following a conversation holds the last sentence in mind while the next one arrives. The sound is gone within a moment, yet the brain keeps using it for seconds, and where it keeps it is only partly understood.

Part of that job, at least in mice, belongs to a sliver of nerve cells wedged between the cortex and the structures beneath it.

The sliver is called the claustrum. In mice, it kept a trace of a smell or a touch for up to 30 seconds. Quieting it cut the animals’ scores on a memory task by roughly 40% to 60%.

Alan Carleton and Ivan Rodriguez of the University of Geneva led the work, with Anindya Bhattacharjee as first author.

Carleton’s lab in the Department of Basic Neurosciences studies how the brain handles the senses, and the claustrum caught the team’s attention because of its wiring.

“Its neurons are connected to almost all regions of the cortex, which puts it in a good position to receive and influence information from very different networks,” Carleton said.

The short-term hold the study tested is called working memory. Reading and deciding both depend on it, and it is impaired in disorders such as schizophrenia and ADHD.

It runs on brain circuits spread across many regions, and how those circuits hold information over time isn’t clear.

The team trained head-fixed mice on a game of same or different.

A tone marked the start of each trial. Then came a one-second cue. It was one of two odors, or a puff of air on the left or right whiskers.

After a delay of 5 to 60 seconds, a second cue arrived. If it differed from the first, the mouse earned a drop of water by licking a tube. If it matched, licking earned nothing.

Thirty mice learned the odor version, and within about 400 trials they were scoring above 70%. Twenty-three of the 25 mice given the whisker version learned it too.

Scores fell as the delay grew. The memory faded at the same rate for both senses, with a half-life of about 20 seconds in the typical mouse.

In seven mice, the team watched claustrum cells through a tiny lens while the animals played. Each cell carried a sensor that glows when the cell is active.

About a quarter of the cells fired at the cue, and about 60% fired during the wait. Roughly a quarter responded to one cue but not the other.

The cells’ activity shifted as the wait went on. Even so, a computer program reading the whole population could still tell which cue the mouse had received, right up to the second cue, at every delay tested.

On trials the mouse got wrong, the program did far worse.

“Far from being a static memory, neuronal activity changed over time,” Rodriguez said. “Nevertheless, the identity of the stimulus remained sufficiently present to be decoded up to 30 seconds later.”

Only a small share of the cells tracked licking or movements of the nose, mouth or forepaw, about 8% at most for any one of them.

The findings suggest that the memory trace was not simply a reflection of the animal’s movements.

To test whether the claustrum was needed, the team dialed its cells down in two ways. A drug that acts only on cells carrying an engineered receptor cut scores by about 40%.

Optogenetics, in which cells carry a light-sensitive protein and a fiber delivers light for about one second, cut them by 40% to 60%.

The light trials were mixed in with ordinary trials in the same session, and performance suffered regardless of when the light was delivered.

Dimming the claustrum during the first cue, early in the wait, late in the wait or during the second cue hurt in every case.

“The result was similar regardless of the type of stimulus, olfactory or tactile, and regardless of when the disruption occurred during the waiting period between the two stimuli,” Carleton said.

Mice carrying a harmless protein instead of the light-sensitive one played as well with the light on as off.

The same mice then played a version with no delay, telling two odors or two puffs apart on the spot. The light had no effect on that.

The mice’s pupils, body movements and licking looked the same with and without it, and they didn’t lick early during long waits. What fell was the rate of correct licks on trials where the cues differed.

“Disrupting the claustrum specifically impairs the ability to use temporarily held information to guide behavior, without affecting perception itself,” Carleton said.

The sliver is thin and poorly bounded, so the team could only partly quiet it, and the mice still scored well above chance. Other regions carry short-term memories too, and this study doesn’t say how the work is shared.

A second team reported in June on a set of cells in and around the claustrum that connect to the prefrontal cortex. Mice needed those cells while holding a location in mind in a maze.

Silencing them during the decision itself improved performance, the opposite of what the Geneva team saw.

The two groups used different tasks and targeted different cells, and neither result settles which cells do what.

How the claustrum keeps a memory steady while its own activity drifts is the open question.

The next step, Carleton said, is to work out how the claustrum talks to the cortex, and how that exchange keeps a held memory from degrading.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Communications.
