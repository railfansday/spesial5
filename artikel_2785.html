Bumping knuckles: The Indigenous roots and enduring spirit of the faceoff

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

At the start of every California Redwoods game since 2022, TD Ierlan has stood at the center of the field, waiting for the first faceoff.

He used to feel a sense of pressure in these moments. As he's gained more experience, though, he is more even-keeled. Much like Ierlan's mindset, the technical aspects of taking faceoffs evolved over the years. Rule changes have affected the types of grips players can use, whether they can carry the ball in the back of the stick and even whether they can put their knee down on the ground.

Despite these changes, the spirit of the faceoff – a core element of lacrosse since the game's inception – has remained consistent.

Ierlan loves the history of the sport and its connection to Indigenous heritages, which have given the sport different monikers. Many refer to it as "The Creator's Game" or "The Medicine Game." The Haudenosaunee also call it "Deyhontsigwa'ehs."

That roughly translates to "they bump hips" or "they bump knuckles."

Whether it is Ierlan taking faceoffs for the Redwoods, or Drew Simoneau and Greg Gurenlian before him, or even what Redwoods head coach Anthony Kelly did so well during his career in Major League Lacrosse, the faceoff is and always has been about bumping fists and bumping hips.

"It's cool that it's been part of the game forever," Kelly said. "Obviously, it's evolved with more scoring than back in the day when the fields were endless, and they would play for days on end and maybe have a goal at the end of it. I think it was probably an integral part being that there was probably one faceoff to start the game, when the ball is thrown up in the air. I think it was probably pretty impactful on the game to get that first faceoff and have control of the ball, and obviously, now, it's a huge piece of the game."

While faceoffs in the PLL begin with the ball on the ground, in traditional Haudenosaunee games, the ball is thrown upwards. All the players have their sticks up in the middle – similar to how teams break their huddles – and an Elder who is helping organize the game will throw the ball on the first faceoff.

Dr. Thomas James Reed, an assistant professor of American Indian Studies at California State University, Long Beach, and a member of the Oneida Nation, said the faceoff was not just a way to start the game, but to celebrate it, as well.

"As the ball goes straight up, as much as possible, you throw it as high as possible, straight up," Dr. Reed said. "Everybody, with your sticks in the air, you let out like a big war 'whoop.' You let people know that we're playing this game, that we're still here as Native people, that we're still playing the Creator's Game.

"We're playing this Medicine Game for all those who are playing, for all those who can hear us, all those who cannot hear us, all those who can see us or not see us, but we're letting people know, everyone in the area, that we're playing this Medicine Game, that we're playing this Creator's Game for everyone and for the Creator."

The first faceoff in traditional Haudenosaunee games was an important one, evident by the Elder throwing the first ball and the loud war cry. Ierlan said whenever he wins the first faceoff, he hears the team shouting from the sidelines.

For Kelly, the first faceoff sets a tone; he even goes as far as saying the team needs that first one. If his team can get the first possession and score, even better.

Gurenlian and Ierlan, however, felt more pressure and struggle come with the opening faceoff.

"I laugh about it now, thinking about the Lizards games when I was at home," Gurenlian said. "They'd always be like, 'Greg 'The Beast' Gurenlian,' and you're like, 'Oh my God. I've got to win the first one.' Getting that first one out of the way, especially at a night game when you've been sitting around all week, and that feeling that adrenaline dump, whether you won or lost, that whole sweat happens after the first whistle, and you're like, 'OK, God, I got it over with. Now let's go get the second one.' The first one was always the toughest one."

"Usually, I don't win the first one very much," Ierlan said. "It's not for lack of effort. I definitely try, but for me, a lot of it is feeling out the point of how the game's going, what they're doing as a game plan, and try to get better as the game goes along. I think when I was younger, I would put such an emphasis on starting strong and coming out, and I would end up putting too much pressure on myself to win every faceoff, and in reality, that's just not attainable."

One area where the faceoff is different from its origins is how much the position has specialized in recent years. Kyle Harrison – a future Redwoods teammate of Gurenlian – starred at Johns Hopkins in the early 2000s. While he was known more for his scoring prowess and remarkable split dodge, he also is third all-time in the Blue Jays' record book in faceoff winning percentage, winning 61% of his 537 career faceoffs.

When Gurenlian was in college, he was still relatively new to the sport and figured the guy who took the faceoffs just happened to be the guy on the team who was best at it. No one on his recruiting visits ever used the term FOGO. At Penn State, Gurenlian took faceoffs, played man-up and played on defense.

It wasn't until Gurenlian reached the professional level that he realized he needed to specialize at the position.

"It's really hard to have the energy to go out there and crush 30 faceoffs – your stick gets mangled and warped – and then also have the energy to be a consistent midfielder because not only are you playing offensive midfield, but sometimes, you've got to sprint back on D," the Professional Lacrosse Hall of Famer said. "By the time I got to the pros, it was very weird for guys to face off and play midfield because it was just too – especially at the pro level – it was just too taxing on your body."

Though today's game is more specialized, and specialists take the vast majority of draws, the faceoff still features a team element, much like it did in traditional games where multiple players vied for the ball.

The faceoff specialists work as a unit with their wings. Gurenlian said he doesn't like when faceoff athletes go to the stripe with their head down, "in their own world." He said he studied more film on Scott Ratliff, Brodie Merrill, Joel White and Matt Bocklet than he did on his faceoff adversaries. He also gave a lot of credit for his success to his own wings, such as Kyle Hartzell, Kevin Unterstein and Pat Harbeson.

Ierlan is often flanked by 2026 George Boiardi Hard Hat winner Brian Tevlin and Brodie Merrill Long-Stick Midfielder of the Year finalist Jared Conners, whom he said are "definitely bumping hips out there to make our lives easier."

"Tev knows where I like to exit and where I like to faceoff," Ierlan said. "I know Tev is really good at finding that soft space and getting open if I do have the clamp. With Jared, he knows if I don't have the clamp, and we give him that range to roam because he's earned it. I just feel like we're always on the same page."

Though there have been changes to how it looks and the rules governing it, the faceoff remains as essential to the game today as it was when the Haudenosaunee first played it.

Gurenlian values it so much, he suggested the faceoff even be used away from the fields and settle all types of "tribal disputes" throughout the United States, adding, "I think we can get a lot done."

The position being connected so closely to the roots of the game, though, is something Gurenlian cherishes.

"I already had a lot of pride in the fact that we're playing the Creator's game, and we're playing the first true North American game," he said. "So, on top of that, still having one of the positions have the warrior mentality in sports, I wore that on my sleeve, and I have a lot of pride for it."
