Slack and Teams have a neurotypical bias. Here’s how to fix it

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Text-based workplace communication platforms like Slack, Teams and email can create challenges for neurodiverse workers. But most have easy fixes—and the solutions can benefit all workers.

According to an analysis by BRYGE AI, an online tool that helps make messages more neurodiverse friendly, most workers fail to recognize the ways in which their communication style poses challenges for recipients with autism, ADHD, dyslexia, or other neurological or learning differences.  

For example, research suggests that it takes most people an average of over 23 minutes to return to a task after being interrupted, and that workers are interrupted every two minutes by an email, meeting or notification. Getting back on track, however, can be especially difficult for those with ADHD, who often struggle with task paralysis, executive dysfunction and a more fragile working memory.

“Slack and Teams have the green dot, there are typing bubbles, read receipts, received and sent signals, all built into it—so now, there’s an implicit pressure to respond right away,” explains BRYGE AI founder and AI engineering lead for Uptrend Motion, Rebecca Koniahgari. “We took these flexible tools and turned them into a stopwatch, where everybody is being judged by visibility.”

Working across communication channels can also make it hard for anyone—but especially neurodiverse workers—to find a needle in a haystack of information. When answers are not easy to find, or when requests are not made explicit, those with autism or ADHD can quickly become overwhelmed.

“Messages get spread across different channels—email, DMs, Slack, Teams, iMessage, WhatsApp—and nobody knows where the decision was made,” Koniahgari says. “Sometimes you don’t respond because you don’t know what it is the other person is asking. And then they read it as ‘this person’s a no-show’ or is disengaged.”

Workplace communication tools can also create what Koniahgari calls “urgency ambiguity,” which occurs when a message inadvertently creates panic due to its lack of detail, such as a “Can we talk?” message from a manager. “One person reads it as, ‘Great, we’re going to have a good conversation later.’ And another person thinks they’re getting fired,” she says.

Text-based communication platforms also often lack the richness of context that comes with real-world interactions, leaving recipients to fill in the blanks on their own.  

“For neurodivergent people, there are a lot of gaps that need to be filled in to understand what a message means. All this ambiguity adds to the cognitive load,” Koniahgari says. “Now they have to manage the extra invisible cognitive load on top of the real work that they have to do.”

Why bridging the gap often falls on neurodiverse workers

The switch from mostly real-world to more online and asynchronous workplace communication hasn’t been all bad for neurodiverse workers.

For example, ADHD workers, who often struggle with short-term memory, can search through past conversations for information they can’t recall, while autistic workers who struggle with eye contact may find it more comfortable to communicate by text.  

At the same time, Koniahgari says online platforms also inherited the broader workplace expectation that communication practices default to neurotypical norms, putting the onus on the neurodiverse workers to adjust their approach. That is what autistic sociologist and social psychologist Dr. Damian Milton refers to as the “double-empathy problem.”

The theory, Dr. Milton says, was developed in response to accusations that those with autism lack social skills, which he believes is only true when considered through a neurotypical lens. Those perceived communication shortfalls, he says, often disintegrate when the same individual is interacting with autistic peers.

“Breakdowns can happen between any two people. However, in the autistic/non-autistic setting, the autistic person is constructed as being impaired,” he explains. “This can often lead to autistic voices being devalued.”

Communication breakdowns, he suggests, are more common between groups or individuals whose brains are wired differently. Yet only the autistic group is believed to lack communication skills.

Dr. Milton adds that the same is true in the workplace, as the onus typically falls on the neurodiverse worker to adapt to a more neurotypical communication style. According to a study by neurodiversity non-profit Understood.org, 69% of neurodivergent men and 75% of neurodivergent women feel pressure to conform to neurotypical behaviors at work.

While the double empathy problem has been primarily studied in the context of autism, Dr. Milton says he believes it would also be applicable to other forms of neurodiversity.

Finding fixes that work for everyone

Small changes to how we use workplace communication tools can make a significant difference for neurodiverse employees by reducing their cognitive load, removing unnecessary distractions and making instructions and tasks easier to follow.

“There’s such an easy fix: just add a few words,” says Sarah Greenberg, a licensed psychotherapist for neurodivergence nonprofit Understood.org. “It doesn’t take very long, and it can make such a difference in someone’s day. Like instead of ‘Can we talk?’ say, ‘Do you have time to talk? It’s not urgent. A question just came up that I think you’re the best person to answer. Let me know if you have time before tomorrow.’”

Providing a clear subject for the conversation, a clear timeline and some added context can turn a message that might derail someone’s day into the innocuous request it was intended to be.

“I would rather have my team focus on the important work that we’ve hired them to do than have them spend their time twisting and turning to try to translate what it is I mean,” Greenberg adds.

A little extra effort at the outset can also help reduce the overwhelmed feeling that can result from having information scattered across platforms and conversations.

“Accessibility is strongest when it is informed by lived experience. By bringing neurodivergent perspectives into product development, Microsoft can better understand where experiences may unintentionally exclude users, learn from diverse ways of thinking and working, and create solutions that ultimately benefit a much broader audience,” says Neil Barnett, Microsoft’s Chief Accessibility Officer. (Fast Company also reached out to Slack for comment, but didn’t hear back by time of publication.)

“When we create space for neurodivergent voices to shape our products, we don’t just improve accessibility, we build better technology for everyone,” Barnett’s statement continues.

Simple strategies, like starting a long message with the ultimate request or assignment rather than burying it within, making key details explicit, numbering points or questions to be answered or addressed in sequence, including supporting materials or clear instructions on where to find them can all go a long way.

Furthermore, giving workers permission to disappear from the Slack or Teams channel while engaged in their work, and designating a clear protocol for managing actual emergencies, can also help reduce distractions for all workers.

“One of the best things you could do as a leader—and certainly as a leader of neurodivergent individuals—is being really clear on what the goal is and when it’s needed,” Greenberg says. “If you can do that consistently, it will make a huge difference.”
