Lab-grown chicken is finally becoming affordable. Will consumers bite?

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

There’s something new on the menu at Epic Steak, an upscale restaurant across the street from Google in San Francisco: “Cultivated” chicken, made partly from chicken cells grown in bioreactors, is now offered to diners right alongside staples like porterhouse and Wagyu.

Epic is one of eight California restaurants that serves the food, along with some corporate and college campuses, including Stanford University. The Bay Area startup behind the cultivated chicken, Upside Foods, says it has reached a new milestone in scaling up its production—and that has helped bring the price down in the range of premium farm-raised chicken. Its new product includes a blend of cultivated chicken and plant-based ingredients like soy and olive oil.

At Epic, “Southern Fried Upside Chicken” with kimchi slaw and hot honey aioli sells for $12. Waterbar, a neighboring seafood spot with the same chef as owner, sells a five-spice version of Upside chicken for $15. At Chimmelier and Jilli, popular Korean fried chicken restaurants in Los Angeles and San Francisco, K-Pop Chicken made from the new meat sells for $19.

“We can put it at a price point on the menu where it can be available for everybody,” says Parke Ulrich, executive chef and owner at Epic and Waterbar. Two of his other Bay Area restaurants, Mersea and Hurrica, will also serve dishes made with Upside chicken.

Upside, which launched in 2015 and has raised around $600 million, has spent years on R&D for its cultivated meat—meat produced without raising animals or using slaughterhouses, using real animal cells that grow in tanks the company calls cultivators.

The company was originally called Memphis Meats, but rebranded in 2021 with a name that it saw as more consumer-friendly. It’s one of more than 140 companies working in the space of cultivated meat. A handful are selling products on the market, including Vow, which sells cultivated quail and fois gras in Australia, and Wildtype, which sells cultivated salmon at a few U.S. restaurants.

In theory, producing meat this way could help reduce the enormous environmental footprint of animal agriculture, though that depends on how it’s made, including how much energy is used.

The space has been slow to take off, and some other companies have faced difficulties. Good Meat, another Bay Area-based cultivated meat brand, stopped commercial production in the U.S. in 2024. Two other startups, Meatable and Believer Foods, folded late last year.

Over the last several years, “the biggest challenge was the external skepticism that this would have worked,” says Dr. Uma Valeti, cofounder and CEO of Upside Foods. The company first unveiled a commercial facility in 2021, though it didn’t initially use its large-scale bioreactors. (Wired reported that it struggled to use the equipment; the company says that its early R&D challenges have been fully addressed.)

In 2022, when the company got regulatory approval of an earlier version of its chicken, it was produced at a small scale. Customers had to make reservations for the dish, available only in limited quantities, at a restaurant that sold it temporarily, or find it at one-off events.

Now the company says large-scale production is underway. Upside has built a much larger production line, with 20,000-liter tanks in addition to its earlier equipment. Valeti says the two-story-tall tanks “are the largest of their kind in the world,” including anything ever used in the pharmaceutical industry. (The company had previously planned to build a factory in Illinois, but retrofitted its Emeryville headquarters with the giant equipment instead.)

In a year, it claims that it has the capacity to produce more than a million pounds of Upside Chicken on less than half an acre of land. One cultivator produces 1,500 to 2,000 pounds of meat a day; the next “harvest” will begin later this week. Inspectors from the U.S. Department of Agriculture and the Food and Drug Administration work on-site, using a new regulatory process that was developed for this type of food.

Scale helps bring down cost. The company makes a key ingredient for its cell feed, something that was expensive to source previously. The chicken itself has also been redeveloped and now includes some plant-based ingredients. That helps improve the texture and nutrition of the food, Valeti says, but also reduces cost. The company declined to comment on the percentage of the food that’s made from cultivated chicken cells versus plant-based ingredients, saying only that cultivated chicken is the second ingredient after water.

For restaurants and cafeterias, the chicken comes in whole cuts, cubed, or shredded varieties. Ulrich, who has been developing recipes for his restaurants with Upside Foods over the last several months—experimenting with everything from soup to a barbecue chicken sandwich—says the chicken flavor is subtle, since the product is a blend. But the texture is “fantastic,” he says. It “peels apart like a chicken breast would. It has those strands. And it’s just very silky in texture.”

Upside wants to show demand at the restaurants and cafeterias it works with now, and then will scale up production more and potentially begin retail sales. With more scale, and production facilities built outside the expensive Bay Area, the cost will come down even further.

The big question: How many consumers will be willing to eat it? The market for alternative meat has been struggling for a few years. Plant-based meat sales in the U.S. are roughly half of what they were in 2021. As consumers increasingly look for “clean” ingredients—simple, recognizable, and natural—cultivated meat’s production methods are a harder sell. Even as the taste gets closer to conventional chicken, the food is still likely to be perceived as different.

Texture is key, chef Ulrich says: “My focus has been trying to get the texture there, because I think people eat with their eyes, and it’s almost more important sometimes than how they taste it. Because if it doesn’t look like chicken, if the look isn’t there, their taste buds automatically go somewhere else.”
