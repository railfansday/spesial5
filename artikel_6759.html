The Body and The Teen Drama Fashion Machine

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

The Body, Overcompensating, Off Campus… Just when you thought pop culture had reached peak teen drama, the streaming giants release another slew of bingeable shows to capture the hearts of Gen Z and Gen Alpha viewers, and new audiences for their sponsors.

Taking the lion’s share of views at the moment is The Body, a coming-of-age psychodrama set in a Catholic school, which has quickly made its way into fashion fans’ For You pages. Just this week, the Netflix show has amassed 9.5 million views, marking its second week in the platform’s global top 10. It’s also currently the number one show in 39 countries.

Costumed by Mirren Gordon-Crozier, the series features iconic ‘00s designs, including the Dior Saddle bag, a Louis Vuitton x Takashi Murakami Pochette, a John Galliano-era “J’adore Dior” T-shirt, and various Juicy Couture accessories.

Fans have responded online, applauding the depth and details of the costuming. “This is why the Y2K clothing in The Body was so accurate,” reads one TikTok with 106,200 views. Another TikTok splices glamorous scenes from the series, showing big sunglasses, velour tracksuits and flip-flops, soundtracked to Gwen Stefani’s 2004 hit, “Rich Girl” (257,000 views).

“With a show set in 2004, the pieces had to feel real — not like a costume of the era,” says Gordon-Crozier, recalling that a lot was sourced vintage and last-minute finds in local Toronto thrift shops. “The ‘J’adore Dior’, the Juicy, the City bag, and the Murakami were the status language of those girls.” Since the show aired, Gordon-Crozier has received hundreds of messages on Instagram asking where pieces are from, along with compliments on the styling. “Y2K fashion was loud, optimistic and completely unembarrassed,” she says. “It was pre-Instagram, so there was no algorithm telling you what to wear.”

True to the period, Gordon-Crozier even recreated tabloid clapback tees, including ‘Team Aniston’ and ‘Team Jolie’ tees for the identical twins Bailey and Britt Bradley. “That generation grew up on new, flashy pop stars like Britney Spears, Paris Hilton and Christina Aguilera, who were all about excess and fun,” she adds. “Today, there’s so much curation. Quiet luxury I’m sure is a response to that in a way.”

“Teen dramas have become Gen Z’s lookbook,” says Rick Jackson, marketing agency Pion’s head of B2B content. “Unlike a traditional ad, a show places a product inside a character, and a lifestyle young people already aspire to.” As such, the City bag or the Juicy Couture tracksuit isn’t just shown, but lived, he explains. “Because Gen Z watches with their phone in hand, an outfit can go from screen to TikTok to checkout within hours,” he adds. This is a big draw for brands, and arguably a far stronger marker of credibility than paid media.

Balenciaga’s City bags were sourced especially for the The Body.

Many brands are wise to this already. H&M consistently ties its collections to Netflix youth hits, channeling the mood of each series into capsules: ’80s retro for Stranger Things, romcom prep for To All the Boys and bold retro looks for Sex Education. This consistency is key. Based on Pion’s recent report into Gen Zalpha (16-24-year-olds), cultural relevance needs ongoing presence in youth culture — on screen, through sport, music and movements — instead of a single, one-off moment.

“Placement gives brands cultural relevance with a generation that’s sceptical of traditional advertising,” says William Weeks, Pion’s chief marketing officer. On the primary market, he explains, interest converts fastest when there’s an accessible entry point. That might mean a discount, a dupe-friendly price point, or a smaller item from the brand. Merch can also fill this gap.

But resale will reap the rewards first, experts agree. Luxury pieces can generate traction as resale offers young fans a way to tap into the moment at a fraction of the price. “For brands, these series are less about a direct ‘buy this exact item’ moment and more about spotting cultural momentum early,” says Sophy Vanner, a strategy partner at creative agency Amplify. “Often, secondary platforms get the first wave of demand because fans want the hunt and the specific reference point.”

“The smartest brands play both,” says Weeks. “They win the cultural moment on screen and make it affordable for young fans to buy into straight away.”

Vanner argues that another way primary brands can still win is by leaning into the codes, lore and IRL spaces around those moments: “Turning on-screen energy into real-world hangouts, collabs and shared rituals rather than just another product push.”

Euphoria has served as the playbook here, offering everything from pop-ups where guests can have their makeup done, DJ-ed events and talks. In 2022, A24 also launched a makeup line with Euphoria's makeup artist Donni Davy called Half Magic. Globally, the number of TikTok videos posted using #EuphoriaMakeup increased by 1,051% year on year between November 2025 and October 2026, TikTok shares with Vogue Business.

The Half Magic brand was founded by A24 and Euphoria makeup artist Donni Davy.

In February next year, Amazon will air the second season of Benito Skinner’s Overcompensating, a comedy about a closeted ex-footballer. The show has quickly become known for its very 2010s costumes. Already, the first season (aired in 2025) has built a cult following around its preppy fare. Mall prep brands such as J.Crew, Polo Ralph Lauren, and even L.L.Bean have become associated with the male characters.

“We were trying to tap into archetypal ways that people represent themselves — the way that kids try to fit in by overdressing in the version of the thing that they want to be,” says the show’s costume designer Hanna Puley. “For the girls it was a lot of fast fashion. I think we pulled a fair bit from H&M and stuff that was readily accessible and felt familiar. It’s that Forever 21 aesthetic.”

The costuming tracks with a shift that’s been underway for a while among young consumers. Mall brands are having a comeback, accessible luxury is performing (Ralph Lauren revenues climbed 10% in the last quarter), and nostalgic pre-loved designer accessories continue to captivate. It’s exactly why brands should look to these love-triangle, first-kiss dramas to position themselves in the zeitgeist.

Consider also Amazon’s Off Campus, which launched in May and follows a men’s ice hockey team in their heterosexual dalliances. Aside from the usual plot analyses that flood TikTok, the show has also built a strong online contingent of fans wanting to discuss the outfits. Leading female protagonist Allie Hayes, played by Mika Abdalla, appeared in one episode wearing a recreation of Jennifer Lopez’s Donatella Versace-designed dress for Halloween. The piece went viral on TikTok, with creators sharing behind-the-scenes footage of costume designer Charlene Akuamoah at work and, of course, their own reactions. One video shared generated 258,200 views.

“I think part of what makes Off Campus resonate so strongly from a fashion perspective is that the clothing strikes a balance between aspiration and relatability,” says Akuamoah. “I was intentional about mixing contemporary brands, vintage pieces, and different price points to create wardrobes that felt authentic to each character. Younger audiences are increasingly turning to television for fashion inspiration, and social media allows them to immediately identify, share and shop what they see on screen.”

Off Campus star Ella Bright attends the Miu Miu Womenswear Spring/Summer 2027 show.

Across the board, the teen drama genre remains a well for fashion inspiration. Past contenders include US first-love hit, The Summer I Turned Pretty, Spanish private-school thriller Elite and A24’s Euphoria. With them come merch opportunities for the streaming platform and commercial careers for actors beyond the big screen. A24 is no stranger to merch, and after Euphoria, starring actresses Zendaya and Sydney Sweeney have both gone on to become style icons in their own right, closely followed for their press tour wardrobes and brand ambassadorships with Louis Vuitton and Miu Miu, respectively.

“These shows used to be about putting products on screen, but today the real value sits with the cast themselves,” says Alison Bringé, Launchmetrics’s chief marketing officer. She points to the average media impact value (MIV) of an Instagram post from some of The Summer I Turned Pretty’s leading stars: Lola Tung ($387,000), Christopher Briney ($256,000) and Gavin Casalegno ($354,000). “Their fandoms don’t just watch: they follow, recreate and share, which turns these actors into commercial voices with real staying power,” Bringé adds. “That’s why we’re seeing brands sign them as ambassadors rather than treating them as a one-season moment.”

Similarly, Ella Bright, an Off Campus star, commands an MIV of $757,000 per Instagram post. Selena Gomez’s Rare Beauty also announced Bright as its first-ever brand ambassador in July. The news culminated in a MIV of $4.6 million within a week and lifted the brand’s overall performance by over 138% the same day, with the top post driving $815,000 in MIV alone. In September, Belmont Cameli, another star from the series, generated $943,000 in MIV in one week for Saint Laurent through a single post.

Devin Barrett, General Idea’s copy director for social and culture, says that teen dramas offer brands a direct link to vibrant cultural conversations and the opportunity to align with future breakout talent. He points to The Body as a fictional portrait of the affluent 2004 teen consumer, explaining that it proffers a new style inspiration source beyond the oversaturated ’00s paparazzi imagery. “Shows like this bring Gen Z and Gen Alpha into an era they didn’t originally experience, orchestrating a richer world around Y2K aesthetics,” he says. “It unlocks the potential for new brand ambassadors with an authentic connection to products like the Dior Saddle bag.”

Of course, fashion has long been a fan of teen dramas, both on television and in the movies. Millennials and Gen-Xers might remember Grease, The Breakfast Club and Mean Girls as historic style inspirations. Shows, however, offer a more enduring marketing avenue for brands to tap into throughout a storyline. “These shows freeze a fleeting, perfect moment — the first love, a friendship group that feels like the whole world,” says Katy Lubin, Lyst’s VP of brand and communications. “The characters are flawed and human. For a brand, being part of that feels genuine in a way a campaign shoot just can’t.” As she sees it, specific pieces become associated with specific character traits, much like the Fendi Baguette gave a cool, New York yuppie association through Sex and the City’s Carrie Bradshaw.

Benito Skinner, director and lead in Overcompensating, attends the Burberry Spring/Summer 2027 show.

Ultimately, because viewers follow shows for long periods across seasons, it means the fashion choices can also shift and evolve, presenting new opportunities for brands. Competitors will need to be agile. Indeed, next season, Overcompensating is leaning into a different vibe, Puley tells Vogue Business. She’s interested to see how viewers react. “We’re not veering so mainstream. There’s a lot more niche stuff,” she says.
