Researchers tested what happens to student grades when schools completely ban cell phones in classrooms

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Students who dropped their phones in a box before every lecture earned higher grades, and the phone ban helped struggling students most.

Schools around the world are taking phones away at the classroom door, mostly in the belief that such a ban has to help. Almost no large randomized experiment had tested whether it does. Now one has, and the phones went into wooden boxes.

College students in eastern India who dropped their phones in a box at the start of every lecture finished the semester with slightly higher grades than classmates who kept theirs. Attendance didn’t change, so the researchers read the gain as better attention in class rather than more time in it.

Alp Sungu of the Wharton School at the University of Pennsylvania led the study. His co-authors were Pradeep Kumar Choudhury of Jawaharlal Nehru University in New Delhi and Andreas Bjerre-Nielsen of the University of Copenhagen.

The trial covered 16,683 students, 742 teachers, and 2,305 courses.

In an interview with Earth.com, Sungu said the gains were largest for lower-performing students, and he was careful about what that means for schools elsewhere.

“That doesn’t guarantee the same ban will work everywhere,” he said. “Banning phones is not a silver bullet, but it is plausibly a simple, low-cost policy lever worth considering.”

The experiment ran at 10 colleges and universities in Odisha, an eastern Indian state of more than 40 million people. There, more than 70 percent of people aged 15 to 29 own a smartphone.

The team had about 1,000 wooden deposit boxes built from recycled materials and put one in every classroom. A full-time research assistant at each campus kept the protocol running.

Departments were split by year of study into 420 groups, and a random draw assigned 198 of them to the ban. In those groups, every student handed over their phone before each lecture for the whole spring semester of 2024, from February to May. The policy wasn’t optional.

Students with a documented emergency or caregiving duty could tell the teacher beforehand, and everyone else went without. Students in the other groups kept their usual access.

The boxes stood in their classrooms too, so the only difference between the two arms was whether a teacher enforced the rule.

The participants averaged 19 years old, ranged from 16 to 22, and about a third were women. Grades came from end-of-semester exams that outside examiners wrote and marked, with no devices allowed for either group.

Across everyone in the trial, the ban raised grades by a small amount. It came to about a third of the difference that a very good teacher, rather than an average one, makes over a school year.

That puts it in the same range as large curriculum reforms and teacher training programs, which the authors use as the benchmark.

The average hides where the change happened. Students whose earlier grades fell below the middle of the pack improved, and so did first-year undergraduates.

Students who were already doing well, postgraduates, and students in science, technology, engineering, and math fields showed no measurable change.

Because the weakest students moved most, the spread of grades grew less in the banned classrooms than in the others. Attendance, measured from the colleges’ own records, didn’t shift in either direction.

The grades themselves are hard to inflate.

Under Odisha’s rules, 60 to 80 percent of each course mark comes from external exams graded off-site by people who don’t know the student. The ban’s effect was largest in non-STEM courses, where that outside share is 80 percent.

So teachers going easy on their own students is an unlikely explanation, though the authors couldn’t rule it out entirely.

A subset of 2,557 students answered two surveys over the semester. Students who had spent the term under the ban became more supportive of phone restrictions than those who hadn’t.

They also rated the restrictions as more beneficial and were less likely to want a classroom with no rules at all.

Given five options, from an all-day ban to no ban, the banned students favored stricter policies than the control group did, with the biggest shift toward an all-day ban. The trial only ever tested a lecture-only ban.

Asked by Earth.com what surprised him about how students felt, Sungu pointed to that shift in support. “That’s encouraging for implementation, since policies students support are easier to sustain,” he said.

The surveys found no change in how distracted students felt, how much they thought they were learning, their well-being, or their motivation. Students under the ban reported a mild rise in the fear of missing out, and no change in how much time they spent on their phones.

Women in the banned groups backed the policy more strongly, while men reported a small rise in missing-out feelings and in screen time.

Research assistants also made random spot checks, glancing into each classroom through a window or doorway and ticking off a list of behaviors without interrupting the lecture.

In banned classrooms, they recorded less disruptive behavior and less chatter unrelated to the lesson, and teachers who were busier keeping order and more engaged with their material.

The checks also caught something the team hadn’t planned to measure. “To our surprise, our random classroom spot checks showed that teachers also used their phones in class, and the ban reduced their phone use as well,” Sungu told Earth.com. Teachers were never subject to the rule themselves.

One reading came out the other way. To the observers, students in banned classrooms looked more distracted than students who had their phones.

The authors suggested that with the phone gone, a student’s attention goes to whatever is next most noticeable, such as background noise. That kind of restlessness is easier for an observer to spot.

The students in this trial were in college, having already passed entrance hurdles, and the policy fight in most countries is about younger children.

Since first-year students and weaker students gained most, the authors think the effect could be larger at earlier ages, but that remains to be tested. The study itself ran for one semester in one Indian state.

The ban covered lectures only. Whether a bell-to-bell ban, phones locked away through breaks as well, changes how students treat each other or use their phones at home is an open question.

So is whether the result holds in richer countries, where students often have other devices alongside the phone.

Sungu’s next question is about a different technology. “There have been recent proposals to ban AI in classrooms, but such bans could be a double-edged sword, and we need more scientific evidence to guide policy,” he said.

He also named delaying young people’s exposure to social media and AI companions as an area worth studying.

Until a trial like this one runs in a middle school, the evidence for phone bans rests on a semester of wooden boxes in Odisha. It also rests on students who ended that semester more in favor of the rule than their classmates who kept their phones.

The full study was published in the journal Science.
