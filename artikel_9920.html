NASA Mars rover and orbiter suffer glitches

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

It isn't only systems on Earth suffering technical problems

It's been a bad few weeks for technology on and off the planet, as NASA reported problems with its Perseverance Mars rover and Odyssey orbiter this week.

For Perseverance, the problem seems to be a relatively straightforward communications issue that has paused science operations because of low data rates. The Odyssey orbiter, however, is facing an issue that could end the mission.

The 2001 Mars Odyssey reached orbit around Mars in October 2001 to search for evidence of water and ice on the surface. The mission was supposed to last two and a half years, but the spacecraft's operations have been extended for a quarter of a century.

In that time, it has had its fair share of ups and downs, including the failure of one of its four reaction wheels in 2013. Three reaction wheels are needed to orient the spacecraft; otherwise, it must fall back to its hydrazine thrusters.

Earlier in November, between the 4th and 6th, Odyssey dropped into safe mode, which engineers determined was caused by one of the remaining reaction wheels stopping spinning. Science data stopped flowing as the team worked to recover the component; the good news was that the wheel moved when commanded. The bad news is that if the thrusters need to be used, only a few months of fuel remain.

More bad news is that the spacecraft is part of NASA's Mars Relay Network, and was responsible for sending back the first post-landing images from the Phoenix lander and the Spirit, Opportunity, and Curiosity rovers. Its loss (should it come to that) would therefore be keenly felt.

In the event the orbiter is indeed approaching the end of the road, the European Space Agency's Mars Express (MEX) and Trace Gas Orbiter (TGO) could pick up some of the load although, as a former ESA insider put it: "MEX is a science mission that can do relay where TGO is a relay mission that also does science." According to NASA, TGO already sends the most relay data from Mars to Earth.

The challenge facing the Perseverance trundlebot is one of communication. On October 30, the rover's main computer detected problems with the primary radio (a series of "heartbeats" from the device did not arrive as expected) and automatically switched to a redundant backup, placing itself in a protective safe mode.

So far, so expected. Engineers brought the rover back from safe mode, analyzed the data, and made an unpleasant discovery. The backup radio was not performing as it should – it was operating at approximately half of its expected power output.

Both radios were therefore deemed inoperable – at least until the team has got to the bottom of the issues – and the rover has used X-band communications over its high-gain antenna to talk to Earth ever since. The problem here is that the connection provides a significantly lower data rate, meaning science and instrument operations are on hold.

Engineers are working first to return the backup radio to operational status, then to address the primary. ®
