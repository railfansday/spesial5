How artificial intelligence is offering new hope for stroke patients in the Last Frontier

It was just after lunchtime when the doctor’s phone started to vibrate and chime. On this summer Wednesday, a neurointerventionalist in Anchorage got the alert: Three hundred miles away, at a hospital deep in Alaska’s interior, a clot in a major artery was blocking the flow of blood to a patient’s brain. He needed it out — and he’d have to come to the state’s biggest city to get it done.

Time is never on a stroke patient’s side. Every minute without blood flow, 2 million brain cells die. And in Alaska — a state more than double the size of Texas, most of it inaccessible by road — it can take several hours to arrange a plane, airlift a patient to Ted Stevens airport in Anchorage, and deliver them by ambulance to one of the five neurointerventionalists in the entire state who can extract clots.

In his mid-60s, the patient on the doctor’s phone had arrived at a nearby emergency room after a morning fall, suddenly weak on his left side. Like most patients with stroke symptoms, he got a head CT, which helps diagnose a stroke, whether it’s caused by a clot or a brain bleed, and determine whether that clot can be broken up with drugs or needs to be extracted. Normally, once the scan is complete, it could take 45 precious minutes just to send those thousands of two-dimensional brain snapshots to Anchorage for review — 90 million brain cells before a doctor could accept his transfer.

But this summer day, the patient had a new type of technology on his team. Stroke is the fourth-leading cause of death in the United States, the majority caused by occlusions that block blood flow to the brain, and survival rates are significantly worse for rural patients. In recent years, states have built up stroke networks, including telestroke care and automated artificial intelligence analysis, to try to improve transfer times for patients without access to specialist neurological care. One of the latest additions was in Alaska — where, starting in 2023, a small network of hospitals had agreed to use the same AI software to triage stroke patients and get them to the right care, faster.

Nearly instantly, the tool, from RapidAI, analyzed his CT scan for signs of a large vessel occlusion, or LVO, a type of blockage that often needs to be mechanically sucked out or retrieved with a stent. Through an app used by other hospitals with the technology, the results went out to his local team of doctors and the faraway network of neurointerventionalists — one of whom was on call at Providence Alaska Medical Center in Anchorage.

The app alerted the doctor with the results just seconds after the scan was complete: suspected LVO. Less than a half hour after the patient arrived at his local ER, the team had called Providence for a transfer — two minutes before their on-call radiologist even delivered their official CT report.

Four hours and 23 minutes later, the clot was out. Without the AI assist, it would have taken at least an extra hour.

In 2026, it’s not rare for a hospital to use AI to triage stroke patients. Eight years ago, clinical trials showed that automated image analysis could extend the clot removal window for stroke patients as late as 24 hours after they were last well — before this, the window was just six hours. At the same time, the Food and Drug Administration approved the first AI to detect large vessel occlusions.

Those “were the wonder years,” said Stanford neurologist Greg Albers, co-founder of RapidAI. “Stroke centers scrambled to access the software.” Developers started to bundle different stroke algorithms in one package, driven by vendors like Viz.ai, Aidoc, and Brainomix, along with RapidAI.

As with most new medical technology, though, that adoption has been uneven. At the forefront of the deployment were large metropolitan hospitals, including those categorized as comprehensive stroke centers.

“Maybe it’s because they had more advanced workflows in place. Maybe it’s because they had the funding to acquire this AI software,” said Casey Pelzl, a researcher at the Harvey L. Neiman Health Policy Institution who studied some of the technology’s early adoption. “But what we know about stroke is that a lot of patients seek initial treatment at noncomprehensive stroke care centers, and these are the places that could potentially benefit from it the most.”

That’s the promise that a small group of stroke coordinators and neurointerventionalists in Alaska saw in 2023, when they established a volunteer nonprofit called the Alaska Stroke Coalition in a bid to improve stroke outcomes in a state that throws up obstacles at every turn.

“The distances that we have to cross are enormous. The weather that we have to face is awful. Resource availability is so low,” said Kelly Isaacs, Providence’s stroke program manager. Sometimes Alaskans arrive at their village clinics by snow machine or dogsled. The state doesn’t have consistent reporting of stroke outcomes. And in the summer months, when cruise ships wind up the coast, a seaside hub’s population can swell nearly tenfold, often enriched with older passengers. Jaded hospital workers sometimes call them the “stroke boats.”

Against those unfavorable odds, one thing that could improve patients’ chances of survival after a stroke is getting them to the right care as fast as possible. “‘Time is brain’ is still very much the case, and the more quickly you can start a treatment — whether it’s the drug to break up the clot or if it’s a big clot, to transport them where they can get the clot taken out — that’s going to make a big difference for people,” said UC Health neurologist and stroke researcher Joseph Broderick.

By 2023, a few hospitals in the state, mostly in Anchorage, had started using RapidAI’s software, but barely any regional hospitals in the state’s interior and on its far-flung coasts did. Even for those hospitals that had the tech, “the challenge was we existed in all these different systems, but we weren’t coordinated,” said David Stoffel, RapidAI’s chief business officer. Each hospital had its own software license and didn’t share information within the app, making it difficult to coordinate decision-making for patients who might have needed a transfer.

So the newly formed Alaska Stroke Coalition, whose founders included Isaacs, tried to build a solution. Lucy He, a neurointerventionalist in Alaska, found funding from a family foundation to buy RapidAI for six additional hospitals spread across the state, at a cost of about $450,000. The resulting network spread from Alaska’s very northernmost tip in Utqiagvik, all the way south to the state’s capital of Juneau, which can only be accessed by air or water. When the tech threw up an alert about possible LVO in a patient, the message wouldn’t just circulate within that hospital team — it would go out wide, to the neurointerventionalists in Anchorage who might be able to help.

In return for the coalition paying for the AI tool, the health systems agreed to report their performance over the next three years, to see if the technology could really make a difference.

Day to day, the stroke coordinators and doctors accepting patients from across the state saw the technology changing their decision-making. It wasn’t always about quickly transferring patients for treatment — sometimes it was about making the hard calls to stay put.

Kevin Myrick, one of the coalition’s founders and Providence’s stroke program coordinator, pointed to a patient transferred to Providence this summer: a man in his 80s with a large vessel occlusion and severe symptoms. Patients like him are usually paralyzed on one side and lose speech. When strokes are this bad, it is far from given that a patient will regain function after treatment: Dead brain tissue is dead, forever. And restoring blood flow to dead tissue can do more harm than good, reintroducing toxic metabolic byproducts into circulation and causing swelling and bleeding.

“We did not put this [technology] in to transfer every patient to Anchorage,” said Myrick. If a patient doesn’t have enough chance for recovery, it can be preferable to keep them closer to home — to save them expense, keep them near their support network, and avoid using limited EMS staff time and planes.

In these cases, it’s a head CT with a contrast agent showing detailed blood flow in the brain that can help doctors tell what intervention is worthwhile. In the Rapid app, that image looks like a series of brain slices colored in neon-bright, Lisa Frank shades of green and pink. Pink tissue is gone. Green is brain that has the potential to be salvaged if blood flow is restored in time. “In Alaska, where you’ve got those big distances, having that imaging is going to be really important about deciding who to send and why to send,” said Broderick, of UC Health.

For the patient in his 80s, the CT perfusion images that came through the app showed far more green tissue than pink, their volumes neatly tabulated in milliliters by the software. “This patient was definitely a go,” said Myrick. He traveled 750 miles — about the distance from New York City to Chicago — and within a few minutes of arrival was shuttled into Providence’s catheterization lab, its team ready and waiting.

A CT scan after the clot removal shows his brain in profile. Blood fills the main arteries and tangled branches that supply oxygen throughout the entire brain; in his first CT, nearly half of that field was blank, its roadways cut off by the clot. Still weak and with impaired speech after the mechanical thrombectomy, he has a long way to go, said Isaacs. But “when he got sent home, he’s got a much better chance of recovery than he would have otherwise.”

The technology isn’t perfect. Like most radiology algorithms, there are false positives — scans that the app will say show a large vessel occlusion that actually don’t. It takes a good image to work well, said Broderick: “Sometimes, when you look at the pictures, if the person moves or the study wasn’t done right, the data is not good.” That means a doctor still reviews every output, and that official read is what drives the call to transfer or not. And for the biggest returns, it requires a hospital to actually perform CT perfusion imaging, which hasn’t been uniformly adopted in hospitals that don’t remove clots themselves.

But despite the limitations, the hospitals that have received the technology through the coalition’s grant are enthusiastic about connecting more of Alaska with the same alert system. “In rural Alaska, at all these tribal health organization hospitals, I think that that really can change the narrative here,” said Dylan Latif, stroke coordinator at Alaska Native Medical Center, one of the program’s grantees, at a meeting of the coalition in early October.

“If we do this with a bunch of different hospitals, then we create, you know, the Skynet of stroke in Alaska,” said He, the neurointerventionalist, at the same meeting.

The stroke coalition hasn’t yet published research about how the technology has impacted stroke care for Alaskans. But early data point to a recent increase in the number of thrombectomies delivered in Alaska, which could be driven in part by the program. And once patients arrived in Anchorage, hospitals with cath labs saw about a 30% improvement in the time it takes to start threading a device in to retrieve a clot.

“Logistically, every cath lab in America during normal business hours is booked 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. all day, every day,” Isaacs explained. With coordinated alerts from referring hospitals, it can be easier to prepare for stroke patients’ arrival. “We’ve got our little flight tracker up, and we’re like, OK, they’re 30 minutes out,” she said.

As is so often the case with AI deployed in health care, it will be longer before Alaska can show whether the new technology is changing the outcomes that matter the most: rates of long-term disability and death from strokes. It’s especially challenging in Alaska, where detailed data has been scarce; more complete stroke intervention and outcome recording began at the same time the coalition expanded the use of AI.

“Can I give you statistically significant information that RapidAI has made a big difference in the state of Alaska?” said Isaacs. “I can’t, really.”

For now, some doctors who care for stroke patients in Alaska are optimistic. “Being able to take these people who are from way the heck out of the middle of nowhere, they got 45 minutes to the local scanner, and now they are deemed eligible for an intervention — it has saved countless numbers of patients,” said Ryan Wilson, a neurologist at Alaska Native Medical Center, to his colleagues during the October meeting. “They’re gaining their brain back.”

As the members of the Alaska Stroke Coalition face down the end of their three-year grant period, that enthusiasm is translating into action: Each one of the six hospitals that got free access to RapidAI has said it wants to keep and pay for it. And there’s hope for even more significant changes to the landscape of stroke care in the state.

“Coincidentally, three things happened that help us further that mission,” said Isaacs.

The first happened this February, when the American Heart Association and the American Stroke Association released new stroke guidelines. The previous guidelines suggested narrow windows in which a patient could be treated with clot-busting drugs: They had 4 1/2 hours to get the medication. But in the updated guidelines, depending on how much salvageable tissue a patient appears to have in one of those pink-and-green CT perfusion images, they can still receive the drugs nine, even 24 hours after symptoms started.

That could dramatically increase the number of treatable stroke patients in Alaska — especially those who can be treated in place by clot-busting drugs. With RapidAI in more hospitals, Isaacs hopes that perfusion imaging will become more common, helping to make those calls about whether a patient falls in those extended windows. “Stroke used to be way more depressing to take care of, because our treatment options were very limited,” she said. “The windows were very short, and now with increased technology and increased time in our guidelines, it’s not quite as sad as it used to be, honestly.”

The second boost came in June, when Alaska enacted a law that will help establish statewide standards for strokes and heart attacks, like it already had in place for trauma. “We always sort of looked over at what trauma had, and we were like, ‘Isn’t that great? They’ve got designations, they get site visits, they’ve got data collection. We need these things,’” said Isaacs, who, along with several other coalition members, voiced support for the bill.

And in October, the final piece of the puzzle fell into place: The coalition got word that the work it’s been doing is primed to expand. “They got proof of concept that they can achieve this equity of care in Alaska for stroke,” said RapidAI’s Stoffel, and leveraged it in an application to the five-year, $50 billion federal Rural Health Transformation Program.

In the first year of funding, the Alaska Stroke Coalition received $3.2 million of the state’s funding to start building a statewide AI imaging network.

With grant-funded hospitals and independent purchases combined, Alaska will soon have 21 acute care hospitals and two CT scan-enabled clinics with RapidAI’s stroke system in place. “This is phenomenal for Alaska,” said RapidAI’s Albers. “There are financial issues for some of these smaller hospitals, and I think they’re even more significant in Alaska than elsewhere.”

The group hopes to receive funding for at least two more years — first, to build support infrastructure that allows for rapid decisions to transfer or keep patients, and then track the financial impact of the technology on hospitals.

“As a girl who was born and raised in Alaska, this is the most Alaskan story that you can get,” Isaacs said to the room of her colleagues in the coalition’s October meeting. Earlier that day, she had gotten the email confirming the grant funding. “It’s like, ‘Oh, you don’t want to do it? Fine, we’ll go do it ourselves.’ And here we are.”
