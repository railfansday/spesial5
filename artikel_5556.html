Top tips for flying like a pro

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Travel like a pro with practical air travel tips for smoother airport visits, smarter packing, and better seats

Air travel has a funny way of testing your patience. One delayed flight can throw off an entire itinerary, a packed carry-on can turn security into a stressful obstacle course, and somehow the gate always seems to change right after you've found the perfect place to sit.

The good news is that most travel headaches aren't completely unavoidable. Small habits—checking in early, packing smarter, choosing the right seat and knowing what to do when plans change—can make the entire experience noticeably smoother. The best travelers aren't necessarily the ones who fly the most. They're often the ones who've learned a handful of practical tricks that save time, reduce stress and help them stay flexible when airports get chaotic.

Reader's Digest has rounded up some of the simplest habits that can make flying easier, from handling cancellations more effectively to avoiding common airport mistakes before they happen. Many of these tips don't cost anything, and several take just a few minutes to put into practice before you leave home.

Whether you're heading out for a weekend getaway, a business trip or a long-awaited vacation, these practical strategies can help you move through the airport with a little more confidence—and hopefully spend less time dealing with travel frustrations and more time looking forward to your destination.

When a flight gets canceled, most passengers instinctively head straight for the gate desk. According to Reader's Digest, a better strategy is to join the line while calling the airline at the same time.

Gate agents are suddenly responsible for helping dozens—sometimes hundreds—of frustrated travelers all at once. Phone agents are often able to access the same reservation system and may reach your booking before the line moves very far.

This approach gives you two chances to solve the problem instead of relying on one.

If the phone agent finds another flight first, you can step out of line. If the call takes longer than expected, you're still holding your place at the counter.

It's one of those travel habits that becomes especially valuable during storms or other widespread disruptions, when every minute matters. Rebooking quickly can mean the difference between getting another flight the same day and waiting until tomorrow.

Reader's Digest points out that staying proactive during cancellations usually works better than waiting for the situation to sort itself out. The earlier you start exploring alternatives, the more options you're likely to have before other passengers claim them.

Checking in before you arrive at the airport does more than save a little time.

According to Reader's Digest, checking in early can also improve your chances of getting a better seat and may even help you earn bonus miles, depending on your airline.

Many airlines release or reshuffle available seats as check-in opens, so waiting until you arrive at the airport can leave fewer desirable options. If you're hoping for a window seat, an aisle seat or simply a little more legroom, checking in as soon as you're eligible gives you more flexibility. It also makes the airport experience simpler.

Instead of standing at a kiosk or waiting for an agent to print your boarding pass, you can head directly toward security if you're traveling with only a carry-on. Reader's Digest notes that this is one of the easiest travel habits to build because it takes only a few minutes and can pay off before your trip even begins.

Sometimes the smallest tasks have the biggest impact.

Few things slow down a security line faster than digging through your suitcase for a tiny bag of liquids.

Reader's Digest recommends keeping travel-size toiletries in an easily accessible pocket of your carry-on instead of burying them beneath clothes and shoes.The same can apply to electronics if you need to remove them during screening.

Having everything within reach makes the process smoother for you and for everyone waiting behind you. The standard liquid rules still matter too: containers should be 3.4 ounces or smaller and stored inside a quart-sized bag when required.

This becomes even more valuable when you're rushing through a busy airport or trying to make a tight connection. A little organization before you leave home can save several minutes, and a surprising amount of frustration.

A carry-on that fits one airline may not fit another. According to Reader's Digest, airlines use different size limits for overhead bags, and a suitcase that technically fit on your last trip could become a gate-check surprise on your next one. Even if you know your suitcase's advertised dimensions, it's worth measuring it after you've packed it.

A fully stuffed soft-sided bag can expand beyond its listed size. Checking the measurements ahead of time helps you avoid unexpected fees, last-minute repacking and the stress of wondering whether your bag will fit into the sizing frame at the gate.

It's a quick step that can prevent an expensive travel mistake.

First class may sound appealing, but it's not necessarily the smoothest place to sit. Reader's Digest explains that passengers who experience motion sickness often have a more comfortable ride near the middle of the aircraft, where movement tends to feel less dramatic.

Think of the airplane like a seesaw. The farther you are from the center, the more noticeable the motion can become. If turbulence usually leaves you feeling uncomfortable, choosing a seat closer to the wings may help make the flight feel steadier.

You may not eliminate turbulence entirely, but you can make it feel a little less overwhelming.
