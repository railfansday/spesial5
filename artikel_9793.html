Jensen Huang's Nvidia Exit Is a Wake-Up Call for Startup Founders

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

This morning, November 12, 2026, Nvidia's board said Jensen Huang had resigned as President, CEO, and director, effective immediately. CFO Colette Kress is now interim CEO. The board also named Harvey C. Jones as its first-ever chair, which tells you it had been a one-man show for 33 years.

The board's word is "resigned." Most headlines went with "ousted." Days earlier, CBS had reported the board was preparing to remove him. You decide which word fits.

Here's the short version. A New York Times report alleged multiple affairs, including one with a Singapore data-center executive whom U.S. officials reportedly suspected of ties to Chinese intelligence, at a firm that bought billions of dollars of Nvidia hardware. Another alleged affair was with a senior executive who reported directly to him. Huang had already admitted infidelity in a memo to employees on November 9. Then came national-security questions, demands from lawmakers, and a stock that fell roughly 10%, wiping out hundreds of billions in market value. After that, the end came quickly.

Set the morality aside for a minute, because the board didn't need to settle it. What I want founders to notice is the mechanics.

Jensen Huang co-founded Nvidia in a Denny's in 1993. He bet the company on GPUs, then on CUDA, then on AI, and grew it into a company worth more than $4 trillion. He is about as close to a founder-god as Silicon Valley has.

And he had no structural protection whatsoever.

Nvidia has a single class of stock: one share, one vote. Its board was made up of independent directors plus Jensen, and it could remove the CEO with or without cause by simple majority. Mark Zuckerberg can't be fired. Larry Page couldn't be fired. Jensen Huang, the man who made the AI age possible, could be shown the door in a week.

If that doesn't make every founder reading this check their charter, nothing will.

Founders don't usually lose control in one dramatic boardroom scene. They lose it gradually, through a series of small and individually reasonable decisions.

Act I: One share, one vote. For about sixty years the NYSE refused to list companies with dual-class shares. The idea was that voting power should track economic risk. Ford's 1956 IPO, where the family kept super-voting shares, was the famous exception. Media dynasties like the New York Times Company got a pass on the grounds of editorial independence.

Act II: Google changes the rules. In 2004, Larry Page and Sergey Brin took Google public with Class B shares carrying 10 votes each. The public bought it anyway. Facebook, LinkedIn, Snap, and Alibaba all followed. Hong Kong held out, lost Alibaba's $25 billion IPO to New York, and changed its rules in 2018. By 2021–22, about 47% of U.S. tech IPOs were dual-class.

Capital intensity makes it worse. If your company works with atoms (chips, robots, biotech), you burn two to three times the cash of a SaaS company and sell more of the company at every round. Jensen is the hardware founder's cautionary tale: decades of capital-hungry growth, and nothing left to vote with when it counted.

The lesson: you don't lose control when the board votes. You lose it, one term sheet at a time, years before.

Every founder knows this story, and every founder assumes it couldn't happen to them.

In 1985, Steve Jobs was 30, a co-founder of Apple, and the person most responsible for its products and culture. He had no super-voting shares. The Macintosh launched to great reviews and disappointing sales. Jobs's "my way or the highway" style wore everyone out. John Sculley, the Pepsi executive Jobs had personally recruited with the famous "sugar water" pitch, moved to take away his operating authority.

Jobs tried to get Sculley removed instead. The plan leaked, the board backed Sculley, and Jobs, with no structural power at all, resigned and went off to start NeXT.

What followed was a twelve-year slide that brought Apple to within about 90 days of insolvency in 1997. The board, out of options, bought NeXT, mostly to get Jobs back. You know the rest: iMac, iPod, iPhone, and the most valuable company on Earth.

Dual-class advocates like to say that if Jobs had held super-voting stock, Apple would have skipped a lost decade.

Maybe. I'll give you the more uncomfortable version. The Jobs who got fired was probably not the Jobs who saved Apple. NeXT and Pixar humbled him and taught him things, and he came back a better CEO. Getting fired may have been the most valuable thing that ever happened to him, though it's hard to see it that way while it's happening.

Adam Neumann got the thing Jobs never had: 20 votes per share. The board couldn't fire him, investors couldn't overrule him, and he ran the company accordingly.

When the S-1 came out in August 2019, public investors saw $47 billion in lease obligations against $4 billion in committed revenue, plus governance that made Apple's 1985 board look like the Federalist Papers. The IPO collapsed and SoftBank had to fund a bailout. Neumann was out, and in 2023 the company filed for Chapter 11.

Here's the lesson I care about most. Super-voting stock does two things: it protects you from the market's panic, and it protects you from your own mistakes. The first is valuable. The second can kill the company. The same structure that would have saved 1985 Jobs from Sculley also saved Neumann from anyone who might have told him no.

So what should you actually do? Here is the playbook, roughly by stage. If you can, negotiate these terms when you have leverage, which means before you need the money.

The academics have a useful framework for this. Agency costs are the damage a founder does when nobody can stop them; that's WeWork. Principal costs are the damage investors do when they're in charge: panic, short-termism, and a buyback where an R&D budget should be. That's 1985 Apple.

Law professors Zohar Goshen and Richard Squire argue the right structure minimizes the sum of the two. A visionary founder in a 20-year bet like AI, VR, or rockets should get more insulation. A mature company with a founder who has drifted should get less.

The data supports this. Dual-class firms trade at a premium at IPO, because markets value the founder's vision. Around seven to ten years later, that premium tends to become a discount as the founder's economic stake shrinks while their votes don't.

So the goal isn't maximum control. It's control that fits where your company is in its life: enough insulation to make long-term bets, with a working mechanism for someone to say "stop" if you lose your way.

Jensen had plenty of the first and none of the second, which was fine for 33 years until this week. Neumann had too much of both. Jobs had none, and it took a lost decade to fix.

Here's what I expect over the next five years.

1. The Jensen effect on fundraising. Founders raising in 2027 will bring up Nvidia in every board-composition negotiation, and investors will bring up WeWork. Both are fair points.

2. Trust-based structures as the new middle ground. Anthropic's S-1 shows a new model. The founders hold a Class F share that gives them 50.1% of ordinary votes, while an independent Long-Term Benefit Trust with no economic stake elects a majority of the board. The founders control the shareholder vote, and the trust controls the board. OpenAI's foundation keeps exclusive power over its PBC's board even though Microsoft owns 27% of the equity. These are deliberate checks and balances in a company charter, and you'll see others copy them.

3. Sunsets become standard. Perpetual founder control is ending. Index providers, proxy advisors, and institutional investors all agree on that. Expect seven-to-ten-year sunsets to become the price of a dual-class IPO.

4. The jurisdiction wars continue. Delaware vs. Texas vs. Nevada. Your state of incorporation is now a strategic decision, not a box your lawyer checks.

5. Character as a governance mechanism. This is the uncomfortable one. As founders gain more structural protection, the remaining risks come from conduct: personal behavior, conflicts of interest, national security. Those risks don't care how many votes your shares carry.

My closing advice to every engineer-founder reading this:

Fight for control early, when it's cheap. Protect it with structure, not just goodwill. Give it an expiration date before someone else does. And above all, build a company and a character that a board would never want to fire.

Jensen Huang built one of the greatest companies in history and still lost it in a week. Your share structure can't fully protect you from yourself, so you'll have to manage that part on your own.
