CoreVault Exploit: Spot Price Fallback Drained 259 BNB

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Posted on Nov 4 • Originally published at panda-security.xyz

Originally published at panda-security.xyz

At 21:50:20 UTC on 3 November 2026, a single transaction on BNB Smart Chain drained 259.09 BNB from the reserve behind CoreVault, a BNB vault system tied to the OSK token. The attacker kept 160.26 BNB, which CertiK valued at about $112,200. There was no stolen key and no reentrancy. The attacker borrowed about 207,000 WBNB, dumped most of it into PancakeSwap's WBNB/USDT pool, and then asked CoreVault to redeem a position while the pool showed BNB at roughly one percent of its real price. CertiK reported that the redemption came out about 33 times larger than it should have.

The interesting part is not that a spot price can be moved with a flash loan. Everyone knows that. The interesting part is that the protocol reportedly did have a multi-source oracle, and the attack went through the path the oracle used when its safer inputs were not ready yet. This post walks through what the chain shows, where the reported root cause sits, and what to test in any vault that pays out of a reserve.

We pulled the receipt and the surrounding state from public BSC nodes. The transaction is a contract creation: the attacker deployed a helper at 0xc28cfeee...f387e2 and ran the whole attack from its constructor, so there was no separate setup transaction to front-run or flag.

The numbers reconcile. The round trip through PancakeSwap cost the attacker 195,000 minus 194,901.165, about 98.83 WBNB in fees and slippage. Take that from the 259.09 BNB that left the reserve and you get 160.26 BNB, which is what landed in the attacker's wallet. Onchain Lens reported a gross withdrawal of 260.21 BNB through CoreVault and a net depletion matching the roughly 259 BNB we see, and Telem's write-up reports the same pair of figures.

Right before the attack block, the PancakeSwap pair held about 15,345,479 USDT and 21,917 WBNB, a spot price of about $700.15 per BNB. After the 195,000 WBNB dump and the USDT that came out, the reserves were about 1.55M USDT and 216,917 WBNB, which puts the spot price near $7.16. That is roughly a 98x drop, held for the length of one transaction.

CertiK reported the redemption was inflated about 33x, not 98x. We do not have the verified CoreVault source to explain the exact ratio, so treat this as our reading rather than a confirmed fact: a figure between 1x and 98x is what you would expect if the manipulated spot price was blended with other price inputs instead of used alone. Blackpaper's summary says the redemption logic averaged a same-transaction spot price into a short sampling window. Either way, a partial dilution of a 98x move still left a 33x error, which was more than enough to empty the reserve.

The most detailed public description comes from Telem, and since CoreVault has not published a post-mortem, the function names below are as reported there and not something we have verified against source code.

According to that report, CoreVault's redeemPrincipal pays BNB for a USD-denominated principal. To convert, it uses a value called wbnbPerUsd, how many WBNB one dollar buys. That value comes from an OracleLib helper that takes a median of three sources. When the TWAP or average-price windows are not fully populated, the median falls back to the PancakeSwap spot price. The payout then flows through Treasury.payoutBnb into InsurancePool.withdrawReserve.

Dumping WBNB makes WBNB cheap, so wbnbPerUsd goes up, so a fixed dollar principal is converted into far more BNB. The attacker did not need to fool the oracle forever. It needed one transaction in which the only source that counted was the one it could move.

A median of three sources is a reasonable design. It only works if all three sources are live and independent. The reported fallback breaks both conditions at once: when the windows are empty there is effectively one source, and it is the one an attacker can move with borrowed money.

Fallback paths are easy to miss in review because they look defensive. Someone wrote "if the TWAP is not ready, do not revert, use what we have" so the product would keep working right after deployment or after a quiet period. That is a liveness decision. In a function that sends value out, it turns into a security decision, and the safe default for a payout is to stop, not to guess.

The second design problem is where the money came from. An insurance reserve is supposed to absorb losses. Here it was wired directly into the redemption path, so any pricing error in redemptions was paid from the backstop. That turned a pricing bug into a reserve drain, and there was no per-transaction or per-period limit in the way.

Make every oracle fallback fail closed in value-moving paths. If a TWAP, median or external feed is not ready, revert redemptions, withdrawals and liquidations. A paused vault is an annoyance; a vault that prices from spot is a donation.

Test the cold start. Many TWAP implementations need a warm-up period after deployment, after a pool migration, or after a long gap in observations. Write tests that call every payout function in that state and assert that they revert.

Use spot only as a sanity check. Comparing spot to a TWAP and rejecting large deviations is useful. Letting spot set the price, even inside a median, is not.

Bound reserve outflows. Rate limits, per-transaction caps and caps relative to the reserve's size would not fix a bad price, but they turn a full drain into a partial one and buy time for monitoring to react.

Model the flash loan budget. Here roughly $143.6M of WBNB was borrowed against a pool holding about $30.7M of total liquidity. Your threat model should assume an attacker can borrow several times the depth of any pool your contracts read from, for free, within one transaction. Our earlier guide to flash loan attacks covers the general pattern.

Treat "derived from balances" and "derived from spot" as the same smell. The Moonwell MAMO exploit inflated a value through direct token transfers; CoreVault inflated one through a swap. Both read a number an attacker could set. Our smart contract security checklist is a good place to start when reviewing a vault.

CoreVault did not lose its reserve because it trusted a single DEX price on purpose. It lost it because a fallback written for convenience became the only price that mattered for one transaction.
