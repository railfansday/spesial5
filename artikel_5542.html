4 ways to handle your 401(k) after losing a job

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

What to do with your 401(k) after a layoff comes down to four options — leave it, roll it into a new plan or an IRA, or cash out

A layoff ends a paycheck. It does not end ownership of the money already sitting in a 401(k). Every dollar an employee contributed through payroll, along with the investment earnings on those dollars, belongs to that employee regardless of how or why the job ended.

What can shrink is the employer's share. Matching contributions may follow a vesting schedule, which ties ownership of company money to length of service. Any portion that has not vested by the last day of work is generally forfeited. Before choosing a next step, check the plan's vesting records or the most recent account statement to see how much of the balance is fully yours.

Once the vested amount is clear, the decision about what to do with your 401(k) after a layoff becomes a question of where the money should live next. Leaving a job makes it possible to move the account. There are four main paths. The balance can stay in the former employer's plan. It can be rolled into a plan at a future employer. It can be rolled into an individual retirement account, or IRA. Or it can be withdrawn as cash.

These four options are a selection of common choices, not a ranking. Each one fits a different set of circumstances, and the right fit depends on age, cash needs, job prospects and tax situation.

One term comes up repeatedly in any 401(k) rollover after losing a job: the direct rollover. In a direct rollover, the old plan sends the money straight to the receiving account. It never passes through the account holder's hands. This is also called a trustee-to-trustee transfer. Because the money is never paid out to the individual, a direct rollover triggers no income tax and no withholding. The alternative, in which a check goes to the account holder first, carries rules and risks that a direct transfer avoids.

The timing of a layoff can add pressure. Severance may be short, unemployment benefits may not cover every bill, and a large retirement balance can look like a ready source of cash.

The slides that follow take the four options one at a time. Each covers how that path is taxed, which rules govern access to the money and what trade-offs to weigh before filling out any paperwork.

Many 401(k) plans let a laid-off worker keep the balance where it is. The money stays invested in the plan and remains tax-deferred until it is withdrawn.

For balances above $7,000, staying in the plan is the default. Nothing moves unless the former employee asks for a transfer or a withdrawal.

Smaller accounts follow different rules. Under the SECURE 2.0 Act, plans may force out balances of $7,000 or less for distributions made after Dec. 31, 2023. The earlier cutoff was $5,000. A balance of $1,000 or less may be paid out in cash. A balance above $1,000 and up to $7,000 may be moved automatically into an IRA. Anyone with a balance near those thresholds should read the plan's notices closely after leaving.

Staying in the plan comes with limits. A former employee can no longer contribute to the account. Investment choices are confined to the funds on the plan's menu, and the account holder cannot add outside options.

That menu can cut both ways. Some plans offer institutional-class funds or stable-value options that an individual investor may not be able to buy elsewhere. Comparing the plan's lineup and costs with those of other accounts shows whether moving the money gains or loses anything.

The account also keeps its legal shield. The Employee Retirement Income Security Act, or ERISA, gives 401(k) assets strong protection from creditors. That protection continues while the money sits in a former employer's plan.

The Rule of 55 can make staying in the plan worth more to older workers. A worker who leaves a job in or after the calendar year they turn 55 can take withdrawals from that employer's 401(k). Those withdrawals avoid the 10% additional tax on early distributions.

Ordinary income tax still applies to the money withdrawn. For certain public safety workers, the qualifying age is 50 rather than 55.

The rule covers only the plan of the employer the worker just left. It does not apply to IRAs. Rolling the balance into an IRA would give up that penalty-free access. That trade-off matters for anyone in their mid-50s who may need the money before age 59½.

Required minimum distributions also apply to money left in an old 401(k). The starting age depends on birth year under current law, so account holders should confirm their own date with the plan.

Once you start a new job, a direct rollover of your old 401(k) into the new employer's plan keeps the money tax-deferred and puts your retirement savings in one account. This path opens up only after you have been hired and the new plan is ready to take the money.

That timing matters for anyone who has just been laid off. A person who is still looking for work has no new plan to roll into. Until a job and a plan are in place, the balance has to sit somewhere else, such as the former employer's plan.

The new plan also has to accept rollovers. Not every 401(k) plan does. Some plans also set a waiting period before a new hire can participate in the plan. The plan administrator or the plan's summary documents can confirm both points.

Requesting a direct rollover keeps the move free of income tax and withholding.

Each type of money keeps its tax treatment. Pre-tax contributions and their earnings go into the pre-tax side of the new plan. Roth 401(k) money goes into a Roth account, but only if the new plan offers one. A worker with Roth savings should check for that feature before starting the transfer.

Money held in an employer 401(k) plan has strong protection from creditors under ERISA, the federal law that governs these plans. Rolling into a new employer's 401(k) keeps that protection in place.

Loan access is a separate question. If the new plan permits loans, consolidating the balance there can preserve the ability to borrow against retirement savings. Not every plan offers loans. Whether borrowing remains possible depends on the new plan's rules.

The investment lineup changes, too. Once the money moves, the new plan's fund menu and fees apply to it. Comparing the new plan's menu and costs with the old plan's shows what the switch would change before any money moves.

A direct rollover of pre-tax 401(k) money into a traditional IRA is not a taxable event. It also usually gives the account holder a far wider choice of investments than an employer plan's menu offers.

This path does not depend on landing a new job. The account holder chooses the IRA provider, not an employer. Fees and services vary widely from one provider to the next. The account holder can compare those differences before the money moves.

The route the money takes shapes the tax result. A direct rollover moves the balance with nothing withheld. If the plan pays the money to the account holder instead, it must withhold 20% for federal income tax.

That indirect route starts a 60-day clock. To avoid tax on the full balance, the account holder must deposit the entire original amount into the IRA within that window. The deposit has to include the 20% the plan withheld, which means covering that portion from other funds. Any shortfall not deposited in time is taxed. A direct rollover avoids both the withholding and the deadline.

The type of IRA also shapes the outcome. Pre-tax 401(k) money moved into a traditional IRA stays tax-deferred. Pre-tax money moved into a Roth IRA counts as a conversion. The converted amount is added to ordinary income for that year and taxed as such. Money already held in a Roth 401(k) can move into a Roth IRA without tax on the rollover itself.

Creditor protection also changes once the money leaves an employer plan. ERISA, the federal law governing employer plans, gives 401(k) assets strong protection from creditors. IRAs carry federal protection in bankruptcy, but that protection is capped. Outside bankruptcy, the shield around an IRA depends on the law of the account holder's state.

Early access works differently, too. Withdrawals from an IRA before age 59½ generally face a 10% additional tax unless an exception applies. A worker who could have used the Rule of 55 to draw from a former employer's 401(k) loses that option once the money sits in an IRA.

Cashing out a 401(k) turns the balance into ordinary income for the year the money arrives. Before age 59½, the withdrawal generally also carries a 10% additional tax unless an exception, such as the Rule of 55, applies.

The 10% charge sits on top of regular income tax, not in place of it. It is often called an early-withdrawal penalty, though the tax code treats it as an additional tax. Even when an exception removes the 10% charge, ordinary income tax is still owed on the withdrawal.

The check a person receives will be smaller than the account balance. The plan must withhold 20% of a cash distribution for federal income tax. That withholding is a prepayment, not a final figure.

The actual tax owed depends on the person's total income and tax bracket for the year. It may be higher or lower than the amount withheld. If it is higher, the difference comes due at filing time. If it is lower, the excess comes back as part of a refund. The 10% additional tax, when it applies, is settled on the tax return as well. State income tax may also apply, depending on where the person lives.

The timing of a layoff affects the math. A year cut short by a job loss may bring lower wages than usual. A large withdrawal still adds to that year's income. It can push part of that income into a higher bracket.

A cash withdrawal also ends tax deferral on whatever amount comes out. Money left in a plan or rolled over keeps growing without current tax. Cashed-out money does not. Federal rules allow 60 days after a distribution to roll it into an IRA or another plan. Once that window closes, the money can no longer be rolled back into a tax-deferred account.

Plan loans add another layer. An outstanding 401(k) loan usually comes due or is offset after separation from service. An offset means the unpaid loan balance is subtracted from the account and treated as a distribution. That amount is taxable like any other cash withdrawal. Rules in place since the 2017 tax law give extra time when the offset happens because of leaving the job. In that case, the person has until the tax-filing deadline for that year, including extensions. Within that window, the offset amount can be rolled into an IRA or another plan to avoid tax on it.
