Some shoes do not have a sexy reputation. Say “stability shoe” to a certain kind of runner and they’ll look like you just suggested they run their next marathon in Crocs. Stability shoes have a reputation problem, and it’s not entirely undeserved. The stability shoes of the early 2000s were clunky, rigid bricks that treated pronation like a disease to be cured with a slab of hard plastic (looking at you, Saucony Grid Azuras). If you ran through that era, I get why you might have a negative, visceral reaction to the type.

But here’s the thing: Stability shoes got better. Today’s versions are lighter, softer, and not for anything? Cuter. And they are some of the best shoes you can lace up—not just for overpronators, but for anyone whose form gets sloppy when the miles pile up. I’ve known runners who white-knuckle their way through marathon blocks in neutral racers when a little guidance would have saved their knees, their IT bands, and frankly, their sourpusses. Needing support isn’t taking an L. It’s biomechanics.

At its simplest, a stability shoe is designed to limit excess inward rolling of the foot (overpronation) after your foot strikes the ground. A little pronation is normal and even beneficial; it helps distribute the shock of the impact. But when your ankle collapses too far inward with every step, that torque travels up the chain to your shins, knees, and hips, and over hundreds of miles it can screw up not just your stride, but your long-term health.

Neutral shoes let your foot do whatever it wants. Stability shoes help guide your foot into a more correct placement keeping your body more aligned.

With any piece of running equipment, comfort should be your foremost deciding factor. Here are a few thing to consider before your next shoe fitting:

1) The shoe should have enough room for your toes to splay in the toebox, about a thumbnail’s width from the front of the shoe to the tip of your longest toe.

2) The shoe shouldn’t feel too tight or loose around the midfoot area. There are lacing techniques to help modify your shoe’s width, but you should also consider different widths instead of the standard D (men) or B (women) width.

3) A secure heel is also paramount, as slippage can cause blisters. The heel-lock lacing method—threading each lace in the etra eyelet next to it to create a loop, then crisscrossing the opposite laces through the loop to create a “lock”—can give you a more secure fit.

4) Give the shoes a run at the store or check website’s return/exchange policies. Besides trying on the shoe, the best way to know if it’s comfy is if you run in it, too.

Some runners want maximum cushioning underfoot while others want less padding to experience more ground feel. New foam technology has given us the best of both worlds with plush, high-stacked cushioning that’s lightweight, too. Because of these high stack heights, stability tech is added to help runners run course tangents—and not twist their ankles while doing so.

Still, shoes with thicker cushioning and a high stack (36mm or more) tend to be squishier, making them less stable. Shoes with lower stack heights (32mm or less in the heel) provide a closer-to-the-ground feel and better feedback.

You don’t have to give up max-cushioning if you’re an overpronator, however. Besides looking for shoes with built-in stability features, like guide rails and stability planes, consider shoes with wide, exaggerated soles, particularly under the heel, and a wider midfoot. Raised sidewalls (the interior padded sides of the shoe) create a snugger fit that will also enhance support, ensuring optimal stability and comfort.

Besides looking for shoes with stability features, you should also consider the type of workouts you’ll be performing in these shoes.

For speedwork, like tempos and track workouts, lightweight trainers (less than 9 ounces for men or 8 ounces for women) with a curved “rocker” midsole help you surge forward. For everyday runs and longer runs, slightly heavier shoes with more padding provide the comfort and durability that you want on a daily training run. Or if you’re interested in venturing off-road, it’s key to have a trail shoe made for rough, undulating terrain.

Stability shoes are specifically designed for overpronation, a type of gait pattern when your foot rolls too far inward upon landing. Flat-foot, low arch runners can also benefit from this extra padding in shoes.

Not every flat-foot runner or overpronator may need the extra support, however, especially if their feet only have a slight roll. We strongly advise having your gait evaluated by a professional, such as a podiatrist, physical therapist, or movement specialist. They can confirm if you need motion-specific shoes or an orthotic insert.

The best stability shoes focus on enhancing comfort and promoting a natural gait rather than imposing a harsh, corrective feel. While extra padding may sound like a plus, it can be too prescriptive for some runners. Worst-case scenario: Overcorrecting a neutral stride could increase your chance of injury, rather than make you more comfortable.

If you’re a new runner unsure of your gait pattern, we recommend running in a neutral shoe, like the Hoka Clifton 11s, first.

When should neutral runners consider wearing a stability shoe?

Some shoes with stability tech lend support to late-onset overpronators, i.e., neutral runners whose form becomes erratic midrun due to fatigue. Brands like Brooks and Altra, for instance, use denser foam on the medial and lateral sides of the midsole to help control excessive movement and promote knee and foot alignment, resulting in a more efficient stride.

Is there an adjustment to running in stability shoes?

As with any running shoe transition, like trying zero-drop trainers for the first time, you’ll want to gradually make the switch if you usually wear neutral shoes. Start with shorter runs and then slowly ramp up mileage or runtime as your body adjusts to the new shoes. Read your body for cues; if you feel some discomfort you may need a slower process before fully committing to stability shoes. Muscle fatigue and pain can also be non-shoe-related and be attributed to overuse. If this is not the issue and it is the shoes, discontinue running in them and talk to a specialist for further evaluation.
