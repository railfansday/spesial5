Even in November, backyards can look like they got some time left, perhaps with a few late-season tomatoes still clinging to the vine and container tropicals green on the patio. But all it takes is one hard freeze, dropping overnight temperatures with a day or two’s notice. Suddenly, tender leaves turn black and crops are ruined. What’s worse is that much of the damage stays hidden until spring.

You’ll only discover a split copper pipe when you turn on the hose in April, or realize you lost your prized dahlia tubers when green shoots fail to emerge. Sure, not every chore needs to be tackled now. Routine pruning and bed edging can wait until late winter. However, certain tasks carry a hard deadline set by the weather: this includes anything involving overwintering plants, as well as plumbing fixtures that hold water.

In some yards, this deadline has already passed, and in others, it's coming up this week. Completing the following tasks before temperatures plunge can save you hundreds of dollars in expensive winter damage, and keeps everything in the best shape for spring. Here’s how to make sure your garden emerges strong and healthy next year.

Understanding the difference between a light frost and a hard freeze can give you the lead time you need. Growers sometimes talk about frosts and freezes interchangeably, but the differences matter. A light frost occurs when air temperatures hover right at the freezing threshold (32°F / 0°C), forming ice crystals on leaf surfaces. While tender annuals like basil and coleus blacken quickly at this temperature, cold-tolerant vegetables like kale shrug it off (and even taste better).

A hard freeze is more aggressive. It occurs when ambient temperatures drop to 28°F (-2°C) or lower and remain there for several hours. At this depth of cold, ice crystals form inside plant cell walls, bursting cellular membranes and killing unhardened woody stems. A hard freeze also freezes water inside pipes, outdoor faucets, and irrigation lines. True frozen ground, where soil hardens into an impenetrable crust, usually develops weeks later after sustained sub-freezing weather.

Northern gardens are often done by November, while milder regions may still have several weeks. Because first-freeze dates are geographical moving targets, your regional location dictates your timeline. Gardeners in northern USDA hardiness zones 3-5 often face hard freezes by mid-October, while those in milder zones 7-9 may not see temperatures dip below 28°F until late December or January. This can help prevent panicking too early – or waiting too late.

Don’t wait for a freeze warning to assemble your gear. Keeping insulated spigot covers, garden forks, frost fabric, and storage crates on hand now ensures you can secure your yard quickly when the first blue whistler rolls in. To help you track things, it’s also a good idea to have a thermometer on standby. One frost can ruin tomatoes, peppers, eggplants, and basil. Even near-freezing temperatures in the mid-30s can cause chilling injury to peppers and eggplants, dulling skin quality and introducing rot spots. So strip your vines of all mature crops before freezing weather arrives. Use sharp scissors or snips like Fiskars Micro-Tip Garden Snips from Amazon.

Gather every ripe tomato, along with full-sized green tomatoes that have developed a glossy, pale-green sheen. Placed in a cardboard box indoors at room temperature, mature green fruits will ripen off the vine over the next few weeks. Leave small, hard fruits behind, as they lack the maturity to ripen. Pull up basil plants to process into pesto or dry indoors.

However, you can leave cool-season brassicas and root crops where they are. Collards, Brussels sprouts, kale, carrots, and parsnips tolerate temperatures well below 28°F (-2°C). The cold triggers these hardy crops to convert complex starches into plant sugars, which can help them taste sweeter after frost.

In regions with cold winters (typically zones 7 and colder), tender summer-flowering bulbs, tubers, and corms won’t survive in freezing ground. Leaving dahlias, cannas, calla lilies, caladiums, gladioli, and tuberous begonias in the soil too long, and the fleshy storage organs will freeze, decompose, and turn to mush. Dahlias and cannas benefit from waiting until an initial light frost blackens the foliage. (Tuberous begonias are more tender and often need to get lifted earlier as the leaves yellow.)

Once that light freeze hits, don’t wait for the ground to freeze solid. Cut black stems back to 4-6 inches (10-15cm). Carefully insert a garden fork into the earth, at least 12 inches (30cm) from the main stalk to avoid impaling the tubers. Gently pry the root clump upward. Shake off loose soil, and allow the tubers to cure in a dry, frost-free garage for a few days.

Then gently pack in cardboard boxes filled with dry peat moss, vermiculite, or wood shavings. You can buy Plantonix Premium Vermiculite Bliss from Walmart to help protect dahlia and canna tubers against humidity spikes and fungal rots. Store in a dark, cool space between 40-50°F (4-10°C). Where winters are mild (zone 8 and warmer), dahlias can stay in the ground, provided soil drains freely and is protected with mulch.

Plumbing and outdoor water features carry the highest financial risk when a hard freeze arrives. Water expands by about 9% when it turns to ice. Even if your home features modern "frost-free" outdoor hose spigots, leaving a garden hose attached prevents the internal valve from draining properly. The pipe inside the wall can split, unnoticed until it leaks in spring. So uncouple every hose, stretch them out on a gentle incline to drain residual water, and roll them up for indoor storage.

If your home has an interior shutoff valve for exterior spigots, close it now. Open the outside tap to bleed the line, and then secure an insulated foam faucet cover over the fixture. You can get Frost King FC1 Foam Faucet Covers from Walmart in double packs. Drip lines can usually be drained by opening the end caps. In-ground lawn sprinkler systems require compressed air to blow all standing water out of the pipes, manifold valves, and pop-up heads. Too much air pressure can crack pipes and heads, so if you don't know your system, winterizing sprinklers is better left to a pro.

Anything holding standing water is at risk. Drain cast concrete, ceramic, and stone water fountains. Cover them with waterproof tarps to prevent pooled water from freezing and cracking the masonry. For bird baths, stone bowls will split under hard freezes, so store decorative basins indoors. To support backyard birds in winter, add a designated heated bird bath such as the Gesail Heated Bird Bath from Amazon. You can attach this to a decking or balcony and it’s a great depth for smaller songbirds. Alternatively, float a clean rubber ball or rubber duck on the surface of frost-safe metal basins to keep surface water moving.

Container-grown plants are far more vulnerable to cold than specimens planted in garden beds. Cold air surrounds the container on all sides, dropping potting soil temperatures up to 20°F lower than garden beds. An outdoor shrub rated hardy to your zone might still freeze and die if left unprotected in a pot. Roots in a pot sit above ground, with cold reaching them from the sides as well as the top. They're also less cold-hardy than the stems above them.

Tropical plants, potted citrus, pelargoniums, and tender patio perennials should be moved indoors once nighttime temperatures hover around 50°F (10°C). Before anything goes inside, inspect leaf undersides and stem junctions for hitchhiking pests like aphids, mealybugs, and spider mites, as well as webs or anything sticky. Wash the foliage with a gentle stream of water. Use Bloem Plant Caddies from The Home Depot to roll heavy container citrus plants and tropicals indoors. Keep them apart from houseplants until you're sure nothing hitched a ride.

Pots left out need plants rated a zone or two hardier than your area. For hardy perennials, boxwoods, and dwarf conifers in large patio containers, group pots along a sheltered, south-facing foundation wall. Elevate on pot feet to ensure drainage holes remain ice-free, and wrap in burlap or bubble wrap to protect root balls from freezing solid. An unheated garage is fine for many hardy potted plants: just water them now and then so the soil doesn't dry out completely.

Fall is an ideal time for planting perennials and shrubs, but specimens tucked into borders within the last 6-8 weeks have not had long to root and establish. As freeze-thaw cycles strike, frost heave can push shallow perennial crowns and strawberry plants out of the ground. Mulch can help, but you need to time this right. Wait until after the first hard freeze has cooled the ground.

Once the top inch of soil is cold, apply a 3-4-inch (8-10cm) layer of coarse, loose organic mulch to new plantings and strawberry patches. Good options are shredded pine bark, clean wheat straw, or chopped leaves. You can buy Timberline Pine Bark Nuggets from The Home Depot. Keep it pulled back 2-3 inches (5-8cm) from plant crowns to prevent moisture buildup and winter rot.

Vulnerable shrubs require above-ground shielding. Broadleaf evergreens like boxwood, rhododendron, and cherry laurel continue to lose moisture through their leaves in winter. Combined with winter sun and wind, this can cause foliage burn. Water deeply before soil freezes. Then drive stakes into the ground around vulnerable shrubs while the soil is still workable, and fasten some burlap to form a windbreak. On old-wood bigleaf hydrangeas, use these screens to protect fragile flower buds.

Porous containers left out in freezing conditions are a common source of costly winter loss. Unglazed terracotta, ceramic pots, and composite clay planters absorb water during fall rains. When temperatures plunge below 28°F (-2°C), the moisture trapped within the clay walls expands, causing pottery to crack and split. This won’t always be obvious until spring, when a planter crumbles as soon as it is lifted.

Before freezing weather arrives, empty all unused potting soil, as a damp potting mix retains water that accelerates cracking. Scrub the inside walls with a stiff brush to remove mineral salts. For a good set of stiff bristles, buy the OXO Good Grips Heavy Duty Scrub Brush from Amazon. Allow the pottery to dry in the sun, then store the containers upside down in an unheated shed, garage, or dry basement. If planters are too heavy to move inside, empty the soil, turn them upside down on wooden blocks off the ground, and secure a waterproof tarp over them.

Take a few minutes to drain all watering cans, sprayers, and outdoor hoses, storing them where residual water cannot freeze and split their seams. Finish by scraping dried soil off spades, trowels, and hoes. Wipe the blades with a rag dipped in mineral or vegetable oil. This will prevent rust while tools sit idle over the winter months.

Some common fall chores can wait until well after the first hard freeze. In fact, tackling certain jobs too early can do more harm than good. Hold off pruning deciduous shade trees, fruit trees, and summer-blooming shrubs like panicle hydrangeas until late winter or early spring. Pruning now opens fresh wounds that cannot form protective callus tissue before a severe cold sets in, increasing the risk of injury and infection.

General border clean-up and leaf raking can also be paced out. Leaving dried flower heads, ornamental grasses, and hollow perennial stems standing in early winter provides structural interest in snowy beds. More importantly, these standing seed heads supply winter food for foraging birds like goldfinches, while hollow stems provide shelter for overwintering native bees and beneficial insects.

Finally, mulching hybrid tea roses and established perennial crowns can wait until several hard freezes have pushed plants into deep dormancy and chilled the soil. Rushing to mulch warm soil traps ground heat, delaying dormancy and encouraging tender shoots. Focus your immediate energy on plumbing, tender crops, and cold-vulnerable tubers; the rest can be tidied over the coming weekends.
