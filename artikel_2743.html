Cardiff's Kronk? The gym where Welsh champions train

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

In the red corner, a two-time world champion staying sharp as he waits for his return.

His opponents, a succession of boxers readying themselves for an undercard bout that could be a key step in their careers.

And out the back, the fancy dress climax to a Halloween boxing camp for local kids that mixed the sweet science with arts and crafts.

Welcome to Llanrumney Phoenix ABC, situated on the eastern edge of Cardiff as the suburbs rise up and away from the city centre of the Welsh capital.

It is both six kilometres and a million miles away from the bright lights of the Principality Stadium, the focus of the boxing world on 11 December when it stages the heavyweight showdown between Anthony Joshua and Tyson Fury.

But it is also the place where the steps towards it are made.

Some are further along than others, such as Joe Cordina, whose image complete with world title straps is one of several champions that hang high above the main sparring ring, flanked by a plethora of punchbags.

Or former featherweight champ Lee Selby, now a bareknuckle boxer but lending his experience to the sparring as Gavin Gwynne gets ready for his own battle with Zelfa Barrett, a bout that would ultimately end in a fifth-round stoppage for the Welshman.

Then there are those of the next generation, priming themselves for a primetime audience with this week's BBC-broadcast show on the undercard of Lauren Price's title defence against Michaela Kotaskova at Cardiff's Utilita Arena, the exciting pair of Brandon Scott and Rhys Edwards included.

The closeness of the fight night and the amount of fighters creates a tangible buzz between the buzzes of the timer that pings into life every three minutes. Words of advice and tips from their own fights won – and lost – are shared between rounds, between the jokes at each others' expense.

Eleven pros throwing countless punches, all adding up to the sense this is one of the centres of the Welsh boxing world. Cardiff's Kronk, if you like.

Gary Lockett turned to training after a middleweight career that saw him win the WBU title as well as challenge WBC and WBO champion Kelly Pavlik in 2008 [Getty Images]

"You've got every level here, from former world champions to guys who dream about that, British title level, prospects – but everyone is treated the same," says Brandon Scott, who has recently returned closer to home following a spell training in Sheffield.

"I had a trial and trained with the boys and it felt I'd been here for years. I was asking Gary to please train me the next day."

Gary is Gary Lockett, the former middleweight world title challenger, recently inducted into the British Boxing Hall of Fame and who has coached almost ever since hanging up his gloves in 2008.

"I came here in 2018," says Lockett, who accepted an invite that year from the club's founder and proud local, Tony Richards, to set up camp in the gym that doubles as a community hub in one of the more deprived areas of the city.

As well as running classes for aspiring young amateur boxers, it has won recognition for inclusion activities and the support it offers.

"We obviously have our guys training for their fights, but we're also part of this community. That's important," Lockett says.

"We're not Premier League footballers who are on a different planet to most people. "And we make sure there's no bad eggs here. We want them to be good people first and foremost, as well as good boxers.

"My first aim is always to educate to be the right kind of person out of the ring."

That's obvious. But it's also clear that it's what could be earned inside the ring that inspires the electricity in the air this October morning.

The thought of a sell-out fight night in the city looms large, with Price's rising tide as a boxing star helping to lift Lockett's stable in terms of opportunities.

"I like to think of Cardiff as a fight town, but we do struggle in Wales with the big promoters coming here," says Lockett, who also manages around 20 fighters, including the Enzo Maccarinelli-trained Willy Gilheaney and Josh Kelly who will also appear on the Price undercard.

"It's very hard to build these guys on small-hall bills, so we need platforms like this. They know they need to take the opportunity, sell as many tickets as you can, because this could be your shop window and the chance to be signed by a big promoter."

Brandon Scott - who is yet to be beaten in his nine pro fights - joined Gary Lockett's camp earlier this year [Getty Images]

Swansea's Scott has had the experience of that, earning an early-career deal with promoter Eddie Hearn's Matchroom.

Injuries and some personal issues saw him take time away before – fittingly – arriving at the Phoenix under Lockett.

"Everything is back to normal," says the 23-year-old, who next faces journeyman Mexican Erik Omar Lopez.

"I had a lot at a young age and perhaps I needed a bit of time to mature, and now mentally and physically I'm in the right place.

"I feel like I'm starting my career over again and, coming here, I've giving myself the best chance to go again."

The boxer with a Spiderman tattoo and who called himself the "baddest nerd on the planet" says he's "still a silly goose" but Lockett says he's learning more and more, including an "eye-opening" time training in Japan.

His sparring session with Edwards is fascinating, several fighters taking a break from their own workouts to offer tips as the similarly styled featherweights enjoy the endurance test.

Rhys Edwards is one of several Phoenix boxers who will feature on the Lauren Price undercard [Boxxer]

In fact, a smile never seems to be off the face of Edwards, whose fight with former world bantamweight title challenger Lee McGregor serves as the main support act to Price's headlining fight.

"I'll probably sell 30 tickets before leaving the house," laughs the 26-year-old, one of 11 siblings from Penygraig who previously admitted to sleeping in the Llanrumney gym close to fights to avoid the chaos at home.

Not afraid of social media (a TikTok of him dodging shots in a pillow fight hit more than 10m views a few years back) and admitting to feeling like a minor celebrity such is the extent of the support he gets from his valleys hometown, Edwards is aware of the serious business fast approaching as he puts in the work.

Having recovered from a first professional defeat in December 2024 – losing on points to Peter McGrail having taken the Riyadh fight with four days' notice – he missed out on a chance of a British title in a draw with Gully Powar in Cardiff in April.

"It's kind of make or break – anything less than a win will push me back a year or two," Edwards says. "A big win sets me up, so the stakes are high."

As is the opportunity, something the Llanrumney gym is all too aware of as the hits come and the hard work continues.

And all hope it leads to something more down the road where the bright lights beckon and championship fights call.

But it all begins in places like Llanrumney.
