Inside the new life of 'conflicted' Arsene Wenger after he turned down the fight against Gianni Infantino: His 'divorce' from Arsenal and why he could still walk away from FIFA's gravy train

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Published: 12:00 EST, 16 November 2026 | Updated: 12:00 EST, 16 November 2026

Next week Arsene Wenger will be in Qatar for the second staging of FIFA's 48-team Under-17 World Cup.

He described last year's inaugural event using one evocative word. 'Paradise,' he said. 'Basically, 25 per cent of the football world was here.'

At the end of last month – as the fall-out from FIFA president Gianni Infantino's foiled plan to sell off parts of the men's senior World Cup rumbled on – Wenger was in Azerbaijan at the hosting of the very first Under-15 version.

An eight-a-side tournament with the emphasis on technique and quality, it was – in his role as FIFA's head of global development – another of his own ideas. According to those who know him, this is Wenger's happy place.

'Arsene's vision is very simple,' one long-time ally tells Daily Mail Sport. 'It has always been that as many boys and girls as possible can participate in the game under as good a quality of coaching as is physically and financially possible.

'That is it, basically. That is why he works for FIFA. He wants that direct influence on the game. He is 77 but fundamentally he is a coach at heart. In his own mind, he still has his whistle in his mouth and his stopwatch round his wrist.'

Arsene Wenger is FIFA's head of global development and many thought he'd challenge Gianni Infantino for the presidency of the organisation. Those hopes were misplaced

Wenger, the former Arsenal manager, is still a coach at heart according to those that know him well

Viewed in this context, it is perhaps not a surprise that Wenger is no longer expected to stand as a rival to Infantino at next March's presidential elections in Rabat. The deadline for nominations is on Wednesday.

Wenger's fury at what he saw as Infantino's World Cup treachery was limitless. Some close to him feared he may quit in protest at an organisation he joined seven years ago. Others urged him to challenge Infantino's presidency, hoping it would serve to cleanse FIFA's reputation for sleaze.

But even though as recently as last week Wenger's intentions remained unclear to many people within the organisation, the conflict raging inside his own head was crystal clear.

'Arsene is an idealist and a romantic,' explains another source who has known him for 20 years. 'And the head of that organisation is not a place for someone like that. FIFA presidency is about glad-handing and politics and – let's face it – money.

'Say what you like about Infantino, he's good at it. It's his natural habitat. He knows the rules. Arsene? He would lie awake at night wondering where all the money was going. He would be fretting about whether it was being spent in all the areas the different confederations told him it was.

'On the one hand, Arsene would be a perfect figurehead for the global game. A purist. The total antithesis to Infantino's perceived chicanery. But in reality, how much could he change? Many of FIFA's reputational issues are deeply embedded.'

The sub-plot to Wenger's emotional conflict regarding a direct challenge to Infantino is that inside FIFA it has had an all or nothing feel to it. The former Arsenal manager's contract in his current global development role expires at the end of this year and for all that the organisation would very much like him to renew, there remains the possibility that he may walk away.

If Wenger doesn't – as we now expect – stand in opposition to Infantino, will he have the appetite to continue working underneath him?

Wenger was furious with Infantino's foiled plan to sell off parts of the men's senior World Cup

Wenger with the Premier League trophy in 2004. 'Arsene would be a perfect figurehead for the global game. A purist,' says a source who knows him well

Wenger, for example, was a noticeable absentee when other FIFA leaders gathered to support Infantino at a management committee meeting in Morocco in August. The two men were not seen together again until they stood side by side at the Under-15 tournament last month.

'I think that (Wenger's future) is a very pertinent question,' adds another ally. 'It's on his mind and it's a big decision. To a much lesser degree, his age may well be a factor too. But he has the fire in his belly. He has energy. I know that.

'He had a certain aim and certain targets when he started with FIFA. And he hasn't met them yet.'

Under Wenger's stewardship and guidance, FIFA have established 60 academies in previously under-resourced football backwaters such as India, Uzbekistan and New Caledonia.

'We provide the structure, the finance and – by way of top coaches – the expertise,' explains a source from the governing body. 'For example, we have Spanish coaches leading the way in India. We basically have coaches coaching the coaches, if that makes sense.

'Arsene believes every child in the world should have a pathway if they have the talent. India, for example, have never been to a senior men's World Cup. Arsene wants to change that.'

At last month's Under-15 World Cup, 500 players were involved who had emerged from Wenger's FIFA academies. The governing body's stated aim is to have established 75 by the end of next year.

'In Arsene's mind, the number would actually be 100,' laughs his associate. 'And he knows he isn't there yet.'

Spain, watched by Infantino (left), raise the Under-15 World Cup in Azerbaijan last month

The Under-15 World Cup was one of Wenger's ideas and is an eight-a-side tournament with the emphasis on technique and quality

Wenger is evangelical about it all. Some would – and do – say he remains a dreamer at heart. He also remains a small details person. For example, the reason he has established the Under-17 World Cup as an annual event is that he doesn't want a single talented footballer to fall through the cracks and miss out simply by being born a year too early or too late.

The Frenchman is not, however, without his contradictions. Once asked privately how a father of a daughter could justify a FIFA decision to hold the 2034 World Cup in Saudi Arabia – where the women's rights issue remains deeply contentious – Wenger suggested that he wished only to 'effect change from within'.

Just as pertinent is the impact Wenger has undoubtedly had – for better or for worse – on football's global calendar. You don't have to have a particularly sharp memory to recall the many times Wenger railed against the dangers of too much travel and playing-time on his top players when he was in charge at Arsenal.

Of all the top English clubs, Arsenal were the last to commit to arduous pre-season tours to places like America and the Far East and that was simply because Wenger was the manager. He always preferred a week in the Austrian mountains to a schlep round the world.

Now – eight-and-a-half years on from his departure from the Emirates – Wenger has been firmly attached to FIFA's gravy train for some time, a driving force behind the expanded summer Club World Cup and a key advocate of a plan to hold the World Cup every two years that ultimately didn't get off the ground.

Wenger is on record on the matter. 'The welfare of the players in the last 20 years has increased dramatically,' Wenger has said. 'When you look at injury prevention, recovery work, nutrition and advances in medical technology. It is unrecognisable from what it used to be.

'Also VAR has helped with the protection of players, as players know they cannot escape from making bad tackles that cause injury.'

Club coaches across Europe may raise eyebrows at that. So would many of those who opposed the expansion of this year's World Cup to 48 teams. But alternative theories are also in play.

'Arsene was seen as poster boy for the two-year World Cup and, yes, he put his weight behind it,' explains our FIFA source. 'But in truth we were being asked that question by some member associations. It was FIFA's duty to look into it.

'The mistake some people make is to look at everything from a European perspective. European football is blessed but not everybody is. Many countries just don't play as much competitive football and Arsene is strong on the global view.

'He listened to the pushback but wants to give opportunities to clubs from smaller nations in things like the Club World Cup and a bigger World Cup. It's not just about England, France, Germany, Argentina and Brazil.

'When you are a club manager you are paid to be essentially selfish and when he looks back he recognises that. His horizons have been broadened. Some of the smaller nations don't have the calendar congestion that exists in England, for example.  His principles remain the same but he has just seen another perspective.'

By his own self-effacing admission, Wenger has always led a rather monastic existence. Splitting his time between Zurich – where FIFA have their HQ – and his home in the north London enclave of Totteridge, he still endeavours to run 5km every morning. After that, it's football.

'If there is football on TV from one of the leagues then I will watch it,' he told Mo Farah on a recent episode of the Olympic Champion's podcast. 'It's simply what I like to do.'

It is not too simplistic to say that the sport remains Wenger's obsession. It is hard to imagine him without it at the core of his life. Equally, it is difficult to imagine a return to club football in any meaningful capacity.

The wounds caused by what he felt was a rancorous departure from Arsenal have healed but that, nevertheless, remains a chapter closed.

'Arsene was seen as poster boy for the two-year World Cup and, yes, he put his weight behind it,' explains our FIFA source

'Sir Alex Ferguson was capable of staying tight to Manchester United but for Arsene and Arsenal it was different'

'It would be disingenuous to say it wasn't all a bit distant and uncomfortable for a while,' says a source with knowledge of the situation. 'Arsene made a decision to step away from it completely. Sir Alex Ferguson was capable of staying tight to Manchester United but for Arsene and Arsenal it was different.

'The ending was different for a start. So it was a bit like a divorce. You need some distance for a while before you can remember what you liked about each other back in the first place.

'But that's where it is now. It's thawed. There is semi-regular contact. He's been back to a game, even if it was just the one.'

For Wenger, the pull of what he sees as unfinished business with FIFA may remain strong. Equally, beyond the rather waspish and phlegmatic public face has always run a river of steel.

'You don't go toe to toe with Fergie all of those years and have all that personal c**p poured on you by Jose Mourinho and come out the other side in one piece if you haven't got some proper b***s,' says one of our sources.

'Some of us were surprised how long it took him to come out and speak publicly about his opposition to Infantino's World Cup sell-off. It was discussed. But he got there in the end. The bottom line is that he has his principles and he already has his relevance. Infantino constantly has to fight for his. Arsene does not. If he isn't comfortable where he works, he will always find something else.'

It's a view thoroughly endorsed by just about everybody in Wenger's orbit. 'Whatever he does next – whether with FIFA or somewhere else – it will be in football,' adds an ally. 'The next chapter will be his decision. He is very focused. And there is already a legacy.'

Wenger and Infantino will in all likelihood be side by side in Doha next week as the Under-17 jamboree gets into full swing. Teams from Tanzania, Mozambique and Vietnam will make their debut in the 104-match tournament. In Qatar, FIFA will present a thoroughly united front with Wenger's avowed principles at its core.

'You can play with an orange or you can make a ball with paper,' Wenger told The Independent in a rare interview three years ago. 'That's why football is so popular. But today if you want to get to a good level of education in football, in some countries you don't get it.

'I find that not right because, in many countries, there are some private initiatives, but the parents have to pay to get the children in there and play. For me, it doesn't matter if you're rich or poor. If you are good, you come in.

'Our academies are free, just open to talent. You have to adapt and find a solution in every country.'
