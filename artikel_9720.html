Satellites Have a Ground-Level Cybersecurity Problem

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Weaknesses in the ground-based infrastructure supporting satellite services are becoming more consequential as a new space race gathers pace.

The satellites circling Earth may seem far removed from the reach of cyberattackers, but the ground-based networks and infrastructure supporting them have long offered plenty of ways in. That exposure is now becoming increasingly consequential as the number of satellites in orbit and the services depending on them grow rapidly.

In a new analysis of cyber threats emerging around the booming space ecosystem, Recorded Future's Insikt Group warned that in 2025 alone, organizations and nations around the world placed 4,517 satellites in orbit; 87% of them are commercially owned, contributing to a $686 billion space economy that is growing rapidly.

Satellite services now underpin a growing range of everyday services and critical operations, from communications and navigation to shipping and infrastructure monitoring. As dependence on them grows, so does the potential fallout from a cyberattack that disrupts them, Recorded Future researchers warned. The growing militarization of space and the emergence of orbital AI data centers, such as those that Google and SpaceX are exploring with Project Suncatcher and Starmind, respectively, introduce another layer of risk, the company said.

Recorded Future pointed to several incidents where attackers exploited weaknesses in the ground-based systems supporting satellite communications to cause widespread disruption. One of the most consequential examples was in February 2022, just before Russia invaded Ukraine. Russian attackers used a destructive, data-wiping malware, dubbed AcidRain, to disable tens of thousands of modems connected to Viasat's KA-SAT satellite broadband network, after first gaining access to the network through a misconfigured VPN appliance. The disruption affected Ukrainian military communications as well as ordinary customers across Europe. In Germany, some 5,800 wind turbines lost their remote monitoring connections.

A similar pattern emerged in attacks against Iran's commercial shipping fleet in 2025, when a group calling itself Lab-Dookhtegan disabled communications aboard dozens of tankers and cargo vessels. The attackers reportedly gained access through Fanava Group, an Iranian IT provider serving the fleet, and obtained administrator-level control of shipboard Linux systems. They used that access to disable satellite communications software and software for identifying and tracking ship movements.

Recorded Future also pointed to an incident last August, where an oil tanker lost communications for about 30 hours after attackers seemingly compromised the ship's onboard network. Investigators focused on the satellite terminals aboard the ship and the remote-maintenance systems connecting them to shore as possible points of entry, the company noted.

"In none of these cases did anyone touch an object in orbit. The attackers went for the vendor, the terminal, the management plane," Recorded Future explained.

Even as geopolitical rivalries fuel a new space race, the basics remain broken. "Every satellite has a terrestrial address: the ground stations that command it, the links that carry its telemetry, the user terminals at the edge, and the software supply chain behind it all. That is where attackers go, because that is where the doors are," according to the firm.

Its report dovetails with research by teams at the University of California, San Diego, and the University of Maryland. Using a satellite dish costing just $800, the researchers found that roughly half of the geostationary satellites they could observe were transmitting data unencrypted. The exposed traffic included telecommunications backhaul, corporate communications, and military data.

The US National Security Agency has also warned of the expanding attack surface created by the proliferation of low-Earth-orbit (LEO) satellite networks. In a joint advisory with the Australian Signals Directorate and other international partners, the NSA examined security risks across the satellite communications ecosystem. This included satellites, ground stations and control centers, user terminals, end-user devices, radio links, and third-party suppliers. The agencies highlighted threats ranging from radio-frequency jamming, signal spoofing, and interception to weaknesses in ground infrastructure and user equipment.

"The ground segment is highly exposed to cyber threats due to its extensive connectivity with terrestrial networks," the NSA and its partners warned. "Ground segments are typically the most interconnected and vulnerable points in a space system." Highlighted risks included malware injection, social engineering, DoS attacks, identity-based attacks, and poor user practices.

To address these risks, the advisory recommended continuous monitoring of ground systems, stronger endpoint security and access controls, and technical safeguards such as frequency-hopping signals, backup communications paths, and anti-jamming antennas to protect satellite links.

Recorded Future's own recommendations are for all stakeholders to begin treating space infrastructure as critical national infrastructure, holding vendors to stronger security standards, and implementing basic measures like encryption for satellite communication.
