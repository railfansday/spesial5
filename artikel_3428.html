What Happens If We Break Letterboxd?

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Earlier this month, the Trylon in Minneapolis gave audiences six chances over three days to see “Kanał,” Andrzej Wajda’s 1957 film about Polish resistance fighters retreating into the sewers of Warsaw. After opening night, programmer John Moret saw enthusiastic reviews on Letterboxd.

The next evening, he says, ticket sales picked up.

Moret could imagine the calculation for somebody still on the fence: “Oh, these people I trust went to go see this movie, and they loved it. And it’s just playing for two more days.”

On November 5, the New York Times reported that its parent company was in exclusive talks to buy Letterboxd for roughly $330 million. Under the deal being discussed, the popular film-logging site would likely keep operating on its own but use the Times Company’s advertising technology. For theaters like the Trylon, what matters is whether cinephiles who went on opening night can still reach those who might go tomorrow. In 2026, that handoff is working remarkably well on Letterboxd.

Five days before “Kanał” opened, Ken Russell’s “The Devils” sold out at the Trylon on election night. Moret also co-founded the national Programmers’ Jam with Bret Berg, theatrical sales director at the American Genre Film Archive, and Dan Halsted of Portland’s Hollywood Theatre. When the Jam met in Los Angeles in May, Berg told IndieWire that programmers had access to more restorations than ever before; plus, more places to show them and a new audience coming through the doors.

We helped declare it the Golden Age of Repertory programming this spring. And when we spoke with Berg again this month, he said Letterboxd still drives “the FOMO of needing to be conversant in a movie of the moment.” For at least a few days in Minneapolis, that movie was “Kanał.”

Berg can see why a press company might be a good home for Letterboxd. He also remembers what the last sale made plain to him: Something cinephiles had spent years building was ultimately “just an asset to be bought and sold.” In the r/Letterboxd thread about the talks, one commenter saw an upside to a newspaper owning a site that rates movies. They also worried about paywalled features and ads, but liked the prospect of a ratings system free of “studios putting their fingers on the scale.”

If you come home from seeing a rep movie and write what you thought about it, your friends get a recommendation, the theater gets a little local press, Letterboxd itself gets something to put an ad next to, and as a matter of historical record? Years later, a “Kanał” fan who’s never even been to Minneapolis can land on the same page and read what it was like to see it with your crowd.

That’s a powerful engine for word-of-mouth, particularly among the very online. Moret keeps his own movie diary in a paper notebook these days, and told us he actually stopped posting what he watched on Letterboxd for a while because Trylon customers kept asking if that meant he was going to program it. “Sometimes a guy just wants to watch a movie,” he said.

Moret still regularly checks Letterboxd after screenings and uses that data to help shape his local scene. “I can see that night exactly how they responded,” he said. Moret said he’s seen Trylon patrons meet theater volunteers and follow them on Letterboxd afterward. He’s also heard from people who knew one another by their Letterboxd handles before they started going to showings together. Their community has real taste, and Moret’s job is to help subtly steer that momentum in exciting artistic directions.

He did have an ulterior motive for December’s Dede Allen series.

Moret explained to IndieWire that he wanted to show “Mike’s Murder,” James Bridges’ 1984 mystery starring Debra Winger as a bank teller trying to find out who killed her tennis instructor, and realized a Letterboxd user could make a pretty good list out of movies you didn’t know shared an editor like Allen. So, he put multiple of the craftsman’s offerings on the calendar — effectively allowing Trylon visitors to use a popular classic like “Dog Day Afternoon” on December 11 as an avenue toward “Mike’s Murder” on December 18. (Of course, both play in 35mm.)

On the supply end at the American Genre Film Archive, Berg reads the reviews from one town with the next town in mind. A joke review with thousands of likes might tell him very little about who’d pay to see a movie. But five people leaving a one-off screening at a DIY microcinema and feeling compelled to write about it could tell him quite a lot.

For someone deciding which films in the archive’s catalog deserve another look, that’s “some interesting free data,” and even Berg comes across movies he’s never heard of. Three and a half stars on Letterboxd is enough to make him give one a closer look. Then, he wants to know what kind of time people had. “How many audience moments are there? That’s what I care about.”

For a horror movie, he might be counting jump scares. With Herzog, he’s thinking about how many iconic images there are. (Referencing Les Blank’s “Ship of Dreams,” I personally suggested that the nation craves the sight of a steamship being pushed up a mountain. “Oh, it’s true,” Berg said.)

Berg also named Spectacle in Brooklyn and Whammy! in Los Angeles as places where he could count on hearing from a few people whether a movie made an impression. He said he can look for the same things he would on any research viewing, but with the added benefit of a crowd that makes a significant difference. Was everybody there to see a new restoration? How full was the room exactly?

Berg could always pick up the phone and ask someone like Moret how a screening went in Minneapolis. But Letterboxd gives them both a way to see and respond to the same information, connecting what happens in one theater to what programmers are noticing hit elsewhere.

Berg also thinks about who’s had the chance to write film books previously, and who has been taken seriously as a critic on a national stage. He singled out trans critics among the writers who can find an audience on Letterboxd without first persuading an editor to commission a review. In the end, the person who finally gets you to watch a movie might write three sentences half a century after the original reviews ran. They might also be the only person you encounter who’s heard of it.

If the people writing those Letterboxd reviews scatter across half a dozen sites, Berg could still go looking for them but it would take longer. And if Letterboxd imploded altogether, he’d expect “a return to gut feeling” that wouldn’t be bad — but might give the audience less of a voice.

Berg has some experience with the decision to leave. He still uses Vimeo for his day job, but the price helped persuade him to move his own project, Museum of Home Video, to a cheaper service. Finding another place to stream was manageable, and the Letterboxd equivalent of that transition is doable for most users too. First, it would mean finding another place to put your film log and reviews. Then, you’d have to find the people whose retro picks and ideas got you to sign up in the first place.

In that same Letterboxd Reddit thread, one person was already looking for the exit. “Any other apps people know about with similar list-building functionality?” they asked.

Andrew Masyk, the founder of rival tracking site Simkl, says its importer can bring over your watchlist, ratings, and the full text of your reviews, and custom lists can come over using a separate tool. That said, the comments and follows stay behind. “People have to find each other again, and that’s the hardest part of moving,” he wrote.

Masyk has every business reason to make switching easy. Even so, he figures people who have spent years keeping a movie diary on Letterboxd are mostly inclined to stay put. “What moves people is the product changing under them: core features going behind a paywall, heavier ads, or getting your data out becoming harder.” His test for a buyer is whether they keep that exit open. “The export button matters more than who owns the company,” he wrote.

Yes, the export button can save a Letterboxd review but the friend who used to see it in their feed still has to find it — and that can get more difficult long before anyone actually leaves Letterboxd. If a buyer gave sponsored posts more room in the feed, for instance, you could still log every film you see while missing the three sentences from a friend that would’ve gotten you to your local theater in time.

If Letterboxd breaks, the Trylon loses one way to fill a seat, and a programmer in another town loses one way to tell whether “Kanał” might fill a few seats there, too. As for regular film fans? Hear about “Kanał” on Monday, and you can go. Hear about it on Wednesday, and you’ve missed the boat.
