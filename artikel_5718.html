Can Tonsillectomy Help for Non-Asian Patients With IgA Nephropathy?

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

— Study showed more achieved early remission when adding surgery to immunosuppression

Immunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN), a chronic autoimmune kidney disease, is driven by mucosal immune activation that produces galactose-deficient IgA1, forming immune complexes that deposit in the glomeruli and trigger progressive kidney injury.

While tonsillectomy combined with pulsed corticosteroids has long been an established treatment in Japanese practice, international guidelines have explicitly steered nephrologists away from surgical intervention in non-Japanese patients due to a lack of evidence outside Asian cohorts.

However, a recent prospective comparative study in Nephrology Dialysis Transplantation suggested that pairing tonsillectomy with immunosuppressive therapy may substantially improve remission rates and curb kidney function decline in white patients at high risk of IgAN progression.

"One could not be surprised at all, considering IgAN as the kidney's response to aberrant activation of MALT [mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue]," study author Vladimir Dobronravov, MD, PhD, of Pavlov University in St. Petersburg, Russia, told MedPage Today. "It would be logical to assume that removing the trigger concurrently with targeting of glomerular inflammation might lead to a reduction in disease activity and better outcomes."

In the single-center, nonrandomized study, Dobronravov and a co-author assessed 221 white patients with primary IgAN at high risk of progression -- 85 who underwent tonsillectomy alongside immunosuppressive therapy and 136 who received immunosuppression alone.

Nearly all patients (94.6%) received renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, and 72.3% were treated with glucocorticoid monotherapy (intravenous methylprednisolone induction followed by a 6- to 12-month oral prednisolone taper). Average patient age was 35 years, and 52% were male.

High risk of disease progression was defined as proteinuria over 1 g/24 hours with hematuria, or proteinuria under 1 g/24 hours with hematuria plus either an estimated 5-year renal replacement therapy probability above 11% or at least one active histological lesion.

Over a median follow-up of 33 months, patients who underwent tonsillectomy were significantly more likely to achieve early overall (complete or partial) remission (HR 3.36, 95% CI 2.30-4.92) and early complete remission (HR 6.32, 95% CI 2.62-15.22) compared with those who received immunosuppression alone. Results remained consistent after 1:1 propensity-score matching to account for baseline differences between groups.

Nearly all (95.2%) in the combination group achieved early overall remission (vs 72.6% of those on immunosuppression alone) and 33.9% achieved complete remission (vs 11.3% on immunosuppression alone).

"Such a substantial rate of early remission can unlikely be expected at the 30% to 62% decline in baseline proteinuria, as reported in recent and ongoing studies of targeted-release budesonide, complement inhibitors, and anti-[B-cell activating factor/a proliferation-inducing ligand] therapies," said Dobronravov.

Disease progression -- defined as at least a 40% decrease in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) from baseline, an eGFR below 15 mL/min/1.73 m², or initiation of renal replacement therapy -- occurred in three patients (3.5%) on combination therapy compared with 39 patients (28.9%) in the immunosuppression-alone group (adjusted HR 0.13, 95% CI 0.04-0.43).

"In contrast to the current guidelines, the combination therapy may represent a reasonable and safe practical approach to the improvement of long-term prognosis in the selected subpopulation of white patients with severe IgAN," said Dobronravov. "The study results raise a broader question about how the control of other potential MALT activation factors, beyond tonsillectomy, might influence the course of IgAN."

Despite the promising findings, Dobronravov noted that "we are far from setting the suggested approach in opposition to others," particularly given the rapidly evolving field of novel IgAN therapies.

"The future of effective IgAN management is likely to be based on individualized, mechanism-based treatment selection," he suggested. "Ideally, the predominant pathogenic MALT pathways should be identified in each patient and therapeutically addressed at multiple complementary levels."

George Bonifant, MD, training program director for the nephrology fellowship at Northwell Staten Island University in New York City, who wasn't involved with the study, urged similar restraint, stating he would "place [tonsillectomy] in the category of a biologically plausible intervention that deserves further investigation, but not alongside established medical treatments for non-Japanese patients."

"The tonsils and other upper-airway mucosal lymphoid tissues are considered important sites of IgA1 production, so removing tonsils was proposed to reduce a source of pathogenic IgA production," he told MedPage Today. "Thus, the rationale for this treatment choice is immunologic."

However, Bonifant said it is too early to recommend adding tonsillectomy solely to improve IgAN outcomes on the strength of this single study.

"What would be persuasive is a multicenter randomized trial in non-Japanese patients, with both groups receiving contemporary background treatment. It should measure sustained proteinuria reduction, kidney function decline, kidney failure, surgical complications, quality of life, and total exposure to immunosuppression," he noted. "The clinical question is not just whether the urine looks better after surgery; it is whether patients keep their kidney function longer with an acceptable overall treatment burden."

"This is encouraging research, but it is not yet a reason to have your tonsils removed to treat your kidney disease," said Bonifant.
