Opinion: More surgeons in Africa isn’t enough when only 10% are women

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Vadset is a research collaborator at Harvard Medical School’s Program in Global Surgery and Social Change and a fourth-year medical student at Albert Einstein College of Medicine.

A voice cuts through the commotion of the Kigali operating room — calm and resolute, directing the staff as they prep the patient. Standing at the head of the table is Dr. Françoise Mukagaju, the first female plastic surgeon in Rwanda. Beside her is Dr. Victoire Mukamitari, the soon-to-be second.

I watched this film from a theater in New York, half a world away, during the 81st United Nations General Assembly, a week filled with speeches about human capital and health systems built to last. “The Making of a Surgeon” follows Mukamitari through the operating room, as well as her training, her family, and the expectations of both. I saw what the week’s speeches kept missing: Global surgery cannot grow its workforce without women, yet the systems built to grow it are not required to measure them.

A recent analysis of the surgeon workforce in 12 of the 14 member countries of the College of Surgeons of East, Central and Southern Africa (COSECSA) found that women make up one-tenth of the surgical workforce. This number has gone unchanged since 2015 despite a 42% increase in the total number of surgeons in the region. Meanwhile, 5 billion people worldwide lack access to safe and affordable surgical care.

I was 15 when a friend’s mother told me I would never be a surgeon. Fortunately, I live where many female surgeons have paved the way for hopeful women and young girls — a luxury many across the globe do not have. (In the U.S., about 30% of surgeons are female.) One international study of women surgeons found that 56.4% had been told they could not be a surgeon because of their gender.

When the film’s moderator asked Mukagaju how she earned the respect of her male colleagues, her answer was simple: be excellent, and the respect will follow. Excellence is worth striving for. But that answer carries a quiet condition: that a woman must first prove herself exceptional before she is afforded the same respect and opportunity as her male colleagues. More importantly, though, excellence cannot be demonstrated without opportunity. Before a woman can prove that she belongs at the table, someone has to consider her, so perhaps the question is not simply, “How can women earn a seat at the table?” but rather, “How do we make sure women are given the opportunity to take one?”

But you cannot widen the door you refuse to measure. When the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery set its six global indicators for measuring surgical systems, specialist surgeon, anesthesiologist, and obstetrician density was defined, but with no breakdown by sex. A gap that goes unmeasured cannot be fixed, and what can’t be fixed won’t be funded. The vehicle to resolve this already exists: National Surgical, Obstetric, and Anesthesia Plans (NSOAPs), which serve in part as the platforms whose data drives funding decisions. Writing gender-disaggregated indicators into NSOAPs can bring visibility to this disparity. The method itself is not missing — only the decision to look.

Some will argue that gender equity in surgery is a soft target, and that the real crisis is the sheer shortage of surgeons. They’re half right: The shortage is the crisis. That same 42% growth in surgeons raised density by just 0.06 surgeons per 100,000 people. At that pace, the region will not train its way out of this gap by recruiting from the same narrow pool.

Women comprise roughly 40% of recent medical graduates in sub-Saharan Africa, yet they remain that same one-tenth of its surgeons because of barriers along the pipeline. Research suggests they’re discouraged from choosing surgery in medical school, harassed during residency, and face gendered assumptions and family pressures once they’re in practice. The low percentage of women among surgeons is not an aside to the workforce shortage. It is one of its causes. You cannot close the surgical gap while drawing surgeons almost entirely from the same narrow pool of men in each graduating class: mostly men.

The will is not absent. The Pan African Women’s Association of Surgeons connects women surgeons across the continent for mentorship, and regional colleges like COSECSA, among others, run their own initiatives. But mentorship networks and good intentions alone will not garner the money while the gap they address remains invisible in national plans.

Uplifting women in global surgery belongs in every one of these conversations, from NSOAPs to residency programs to the U.N. General Assembly. It is not the sole answer to a 5-billion-patient crisis. But it is a lever we can measure, fund, and act on now — and a promise to the women and girls who are still being told this work is not for them. More women standing at the operating table, like Mukagaju and Mukamitari, is how health systems are built to last.

Taylor Vadset is a research collaborator at Harvard Medical School’s Program in Global Surgery and Social Change and a fourth-year medical student at Albert Einstein College of Medicine.
