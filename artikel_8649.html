Bike to Work 3 Days a Week – Savings Can Cover Your Gear in Months

If you live within about 10 miles of work and have a safe route, bike there two or three days a week instead of driving. Each day you ride saves gas, car upkeep, and any parking fee, so a used bike and basic gear can pay for themselves within a year where you pay to park.

Every mile you bike instead of driving saves about 29 cents, AAA’s 2026 average for a new car’s gas, maintenance, repairs, and tires.

You don’t have to ride every day for the savings to add up. Say you live 6 miles from work, about a 30-minute ride, and bike there three days a week for 50 weeks. You’d otherwise pay $5 a day to park, and the bike needs about $150 a year in tune-ups and flat repairs. Those rides take 1,800 miles off the car, worth about $520 at 29 cents a mile, and skip $750 in parking, so you keep roughly $1,100 after upkeep.

With $1,100 a year in savings, $500 spent on a used bike, lights, a helmet, a sturdy lock, and a rain jacket pays for itself in under six months. After a year, you’re about $600 ahead. Where parking at work is free, you keep about $370 a year, and the same gear takes about 16 months to pay for itself. The six rides also give you three hours of exercise a week.

Keep the ride to about 10 miles each way, or ride to a train or bus for part of a longer trip. An e-bike lets you ride farther, but it often costs $1,000 to $4,000. Portland State University researchers track the state and city programs that help pay for one, and some give lower-income buyers $1,000 or more.

Before your first commute, test the route on a weekend. Pick bike lanes and quiet streets over the fastest roads, and leave a change of clothes at work.

Then ask HR what your employer offers bike commuters, such as a bike room, showers, or a taxable stipend. A carpool or a remote day can cut your commuting costs the rest of the week.

Ride the route you tested one day next week, then add a day each week until you’re riding three.
