Most of the tax decisions that matter for 2026 have to be made by December 31. After that, the year is closed, and the return you file in April mostly reports what already happened.

That’s especially true this year. Recent regulations have changed several rules that hit physicians directly, from how catch up contributions work to what a charitable gift is worth. Some habits that worked last year now cost more.

Below is a year end tax planning checklist built for physicians: the retirement and giving sections apply to almost everyone, and the practice section covers owners and anyone with 1099 income.

Work through what applies to you and bring the list to your tax team before the holidays take over December. Every year our tax team sees the same pattern: the plan was sound, but one deadline slipped past because you only hear from your CPA in April.

Start with these before you check any boxes:

The deferral limit for 401(k), 403(b), and governmental 457 plans is $24,500 for 2026. The catch up for those 50 and older is 8,000(11,250 if you turn 60 to 63 in 2026), and the IRA limit rose to $7,500 (IRS Notice 2025-67).

If you’re 50 or older and earned more than $150,000 in FICA wages from your employer in 2025, your 2026 catch up contributions must go in as Roth (IRC §414(v)(7); IRS Notice 2025-67).

The SALT cap is $40,400 for 2026, but it shrinks by 30 cents for every dollar of modified AGI above $505,000. It bottoms out at $10,000 around $606,333 of MAGI, which is where some physician households land (One Big Beautiful Bill Act, Pub. L. 119-21; IRC §164(b)(7)).

Itemizers can only deduct charitable gifts above 0.5% of AGI (OBBBA, Pub. L. 119-21; IRC §170(b)(1)(I)). If you’re in the 37% bracket, the tax benefit of your itemized deductions is limited to roughly 35 cents per dollar (OBBBA, Pub. L. 119-21; IRC §68).

The dependent care FSA exclusion rose from $5,000 to $7,500, or $3,750 if married filing separately (OBBBA, Pub. L. 119-21; IRC §129(a)(2)).

Employee deferrals to a 401(k) or 403(b) have to come out of a 2026 paycheck. Check your total so far this year, and raise your election for the last few pay periods if you’re short of $24,500.

If you also have a 457(b), it carries its own $24,500 limit on top of your 401(k) or 403(b).

457(b) plans at nonprofit hospitals and other tax exempt employers work differently. They are unfunded, so the money stays an asset of your employer and is exposed to its creditors if the employer runs into financial trouble.

Their distribution rules also differ, so read the plan terms before you defer more.

If you’re 50 or older, check that your plan moved your catch up to Roth if the new rule applies to you. If your plan has no Roth option, high earners can’t make catch up contributions at all in 2026.

Moonlighting, locums, expert witness work, and side practices can all fund a Solo 401(k). The catch is that the $24,500 employee deferral limit is per person, across every plan you participate in.

If you’ve already maxed your hospital 403(b), your Solo 401(k) is limited to the employer contribution, which is roughly 20% of net self employment earnings. If you don’t have a plan set up yet, ask your tax team now, because the deadlines for opening a plan and electing deferrals are different.

The pro rata rule looks at your pretax traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances on December 31. A pretax balance sitting there at year end can make part of your backdoor Roth conversion taxable.

The usual fix is rolling those balances into an employer plan that accepts them, and finishing the rollover before December 31. This is one of the most common backdoor Roth problems we catch, usually an old rollover IRA from a prior job that nobody remembered.

A conversion counts in the year it’s completed, and it can’t be undone. Physician on FIRE has a thorough breakdown of when Roth conversions make the most sense, especially in the years between retirement and required distributions.

S-Corp owners have to run their reasonable W-2 salary through payroll by year end. Set it too low and it won’t hold up to the IRS.

Set it too high and you give back payroll tax savings, so true it up in the final payroll runs.

If your practice elected PTET, the payment has to leave the practice account by December 31 to be deducted on your 2026 return.

Wages paid to your children for work actually done in 2026 are deductible to the practice. Up to the 2026 standard deduction of $16,100 (Rev. Proc. 2025-32), your child owes no federal income tax on those wages, assuming they have no other income.

In a sole proprietorship, or a partnership owned only by the child’s parents, wages to children under 18 also skip Social Security and Medicare tax. Corporations don’t get that exemption. Keep timesheets and run the wages through payroll.

OBBBA permanently restored 100% bonus depreciation for property acquired after January 19, 2025, so qualifying equipment can be fully deducted in the year it’s placed in service (OBBBA, Pub. L. 119-21; IRC §168(k)).

Equipment, software, and vehicles only count for 2026 if they’re placed in service by December 31. Buying in late December and installing in January moves the deduction into 2027. We see it every January: equipment ordered before the holidays that didn’t get installed until after New Year’s.

If you own your practice through an S-Corp or another entity, home office, cell phone, and mileage costs reimbursed under a written accountable plan are deductible to the practice and tax-free to you. Process 2026 reimbursements before the year closes.

Sole proprietors don’t need an accountable plan, since they deduct these costs directly on Schedule C.

The 0.5% floor means that with $500,000 of AGI, you get no deduction for the first $2,500 of gifts (OBBBA, Pub. L. 119-21; IRC §170(b)(1)(I)). Add the roughly 35 cent limit for filers in the top bracket (IRC §68), and small annual gifts are worth less than they were.

Two year end tax planning moves work better under the new rules:

Bunch your giving. Concentrate several years of gifts into one year, often through a donor advised fund, so more of the total clears the floor.

Give appreciated shares instead of cash. Donating shares held more than a year generally avoids capital gains tax on the appreciation, and the gift still counts toward your deduction.

If you take the standard deduction, you can now deduct up to 1,000ofcashgifts(2,000 married filing jointly) without itemizing (OBBBA, Pub. L. 119-21; IRC §170(p)). It covers cash gifts to qualifying charities, but not gifts to donor advised funds or most private foundations.

The last 2026 estimated payment is due January 15, 2027. If your 2025 AGI was over $150,000, paying 110% of your 2025 tax through withholding and estimates protects you from an underpayment penalty (IRS Publication 505).

If you’re W-2 with outside income, you have an extra option. The IRS treats withholding as if it were paid evenly throughout the year, so raising your December withholding can cover a shortfall from earlier quarters.

Open enrollment is happening now at most employers. If you pay for child care, you can elect up to $7,500 for 2027, up from the old $5,000 limit.

Dr. Lee is a hypothetical hospitalist, age 52, married filing jointly, with $380,000 of W-2 salary and about $60,000 of 1099 income from moonlighting. Here’s how the checklist plays out for her:

Her 403(b) deferrals are on pace to reach $24,500 by her final paycheck. Her 2025 FICA wages were over $150,000, so her $8,000 catch up goes in as Roth.

Because her 403(b) used up her deferral limit, the Solo 401(k) on her moonlighting income takes only the employer contribution, roughly 20% of her net self employment earnings.

She has a $40,000 rollover IRA from a prior job. She moves it into her 403(b) before December 31 so the pro rata rule doesn’t make most of her backdoor Roth conversion taxable.

She usually gives $6,000 a year. With AGI around $400,000, the first $2,000 isn’t deductible. She bunches three years of gifts, $18,000, into a donor advised fund this December. She loses the $2,000 floor once instead of three times, so $4,000 more of her giving counts toward the deduction.

No tax was withheld from her 1099 income. She raises her December withholding to close the gap, which the IRS counts as paid evenly across all four quarters.

Nothing here is exotic. It’s the tax version of closing out your charts before a long weekend: routine work with a hard deadline.
